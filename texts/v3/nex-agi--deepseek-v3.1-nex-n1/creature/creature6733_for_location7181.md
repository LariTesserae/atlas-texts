The delta memory persists in the shape of the city, even if the water has long since forgotten its own name. The streets follow the ghost of river channels, not because they were planned that way, but because the old currents have simply grown over into paths of least resistance. To walk here is to follow the contours of a river that never quite finished leaving.

Where the land should have been soft earth or floodplain silt, it is riddled instead with crystal teeth—grown slow from whatever leak or wound or disaster split the riverbed open. The ground is not broken so much as interrupted; not quite soil, not quite stone, not quite anything. It sparkles when you least expect it, sunlight catching on some buried edge, and the air above it smells like ozone and a faint metallic sweetness.

Because the land itself is unstable, the weight of the city has been taken off the ground and placed onto architecture that can handle its own illusions. The buildings are not towers so much as suspended structures: chains of chambers linked by glass-and-iron corridors, cantilevered over the crystalline outcrop below. Their supports slope not to the earth but to each other, forming a web of steel and bone-colored porcelain, where load is shared and shunted from frame to frame, never allowed to fully settle into the unreliable ground.

The name of this place comes from the way light is treated like a form of labor. Light is bent to illuminate the city’s perpetual machine—which is not a single engine but a system of interlocking engines, each one spinning the next, all designed to never run down. The light they produce is not simple brightness but refracted, broken into its constituent parts and then carefully reassembled, so that every street corner is a glass of different colors, depending on where you stand.

The people who live here call themselves Refractions. They are not born from this machine, but they have adapted to its rhythm and its demands better than the original inhabitants of the delta ever could. They came to the delta as exiles, refugees, engineers, and mystics: the ones who didn’t fit into the neatly ordered world beyond the fracture zone. They learned to breathe the air that tastes faintly of charged metal and to use their bodies to complete the circuits that the city can’t finish on its own.

If you look at them, you may notice three things first.

First, they are transparent to different degrees. Some are only lightly translucent, like frosted glass, so you can see the shadow of their organs moving beneath the skin of their chests. Others are almost clear, their bones and blood vessels mapped out in visible tracery, like etchings on glass. As they age, the transparency intensifies, until the very old ones appear like brittle blown glass balanced on spindly frames.

Second, light does not pass through them unchanged. Each Refraction has a distinct color signature they impart to light passing through their bodies—brass-sharp, indigo-deep, copper-green. When two people walk closely together, their color fields overlap and mix in the air between them, forming temporary new hues that don’t exist anywhere else. This mingling of light is treated as a kind of public intimacy, and people are careful about whose color they allow into the space around their heart.

Third, their minds are entangled with the machine in the ground. The perpetual machine—an invisible network that hums beneath the unstable crystal crust—seems to require a living interface to not tear itself apart. The Refractions are that interface. They sleep not in quiet darkness but in shallow, pulsing illumination that their eyelids make into shifting patterns inside their skulls. Their dreams serve the machine’s error correction: the nightly accounting of stress, vibration, resonance, and imbalance across the city’s structure. When they wake with their minds full of numbers that don’t look like numbers, they do not say they had bad dreams; they say the machine was having a bad night.

The children of the Refraction District are born with their eyes halfway open already— not fully open, but widened enough that you can see the color of their gaze as soon as they first draw breath. Their eyes are not seen as a window to a soul, but as a lens to the world. They are tested early not for intelligence but for clarity: how well they split light, how precisely they redirect it, how much color they can generate without cracking themselves. The test of the first beam is a simple ritual: hold a child in the path of light from a single bulb, and let the city sensor read how the light returns changed. If the child’s signature is strong enough to register on the machine’s instruments, they are considered to have been confirmed by the city.

There are those who fail the test of the first beam. The machine is not tender to outliers. It measures the stability of their light signature, which is to say, it measures the stability of their relationship to the world itself. When the measurement is below a threshold, the child is labeled “unstable lens.” They are not destroyed or abandoned out of hand; the machine has other ways to use them.

Unstable lens are allowed to live but are restricted to positions where their inconsistency is built into the system. They become repairers of the under-grids, where they are exposed to the worst of the instability, so that the stable ones can remain above in the upper corridors. The machine’s parasitic error correction is strongest in them, chewing their sleep patterns into ragged threads of nightmare and premonition. They dream the strain of the city and wake with the certainty of which strut will crack at what angle in three weeks’ time, or which crystal node will begin to grow too fast and unbalance the load.

Among the Refractions, there is no formal aristocracy, but there is an unspoken hierarchy of usefulness. Those who regulate the flow of power through particular districts, or who maintain the critical interface nodes where the city’s weight is transferred from one sector to another, are treated as if they were minor nobility. Their homes are built not on the ground but closest to the machine’s neural nodes, where light is brightest and constant, and where they rarely sleep in the conventional sense of the word. Instead they rest with their eyes open, half-conscious of the machine’s humming through their bones.

They dress themselves in layers of thin, translucent material that catches and scatters light differently depending on how they move. Watching a crowd of Refractions walk down a corridor at the start of a shift is like watching a chandelier being disassembled into many bodies, each carrying its own piece of the glitter in the glass of their skin and clothes. They rarely speak loudly unless something is going wrong with the machine; if their voices are raised, it is probably not about each other but about the way the city is trying to break itself.

If they have anything resembling a religion, it is built around a single question: What was the machine that broke the reality of the delta and left the wound in the ground, and what is it trying to be now that it is being rebuilt by their bodies, their light, and their sleep?

They answer this question differently in each district. Some argue that the machine is a failed engine for time, which they must repair in order to return to the moment before the fracture. Others say the machine is not to be repaired but survived; that it is a broken heart trying to beat again, and they are its artificial blood. Some say the machine is a god that they must keep from waking fully, because if it ever truly opened itself to the world, it would unmake everything that still tries to live under its name.

There is no central authority to enforce a single answer. The machine does not care what they believe about it, only that they keep working. It is not benevolent or malicious; it is simply very hungry. It eats their perceptions of balance, consumes their color as they pass light through it, and replenishes them with the same light that has been used by others before them. They are constantly being recycled by and into the mechanism of the city.

The Refractions are not a people made by the machine. They are a people who chose to stay with it after the world cracked open, and to learn how to live with their bodies in the same space that the machine thinks of as its own nervous system. In the process of doing that, they became something else than human, not more than human but reshaped by the pressure of the fracture line: the line where dreaming of the city’s survival bled into the practical work of keeping its walls from collapsing into the crystal-lined earth.

They are not good or evil in a simple sense. They are a necessary compromise between human and not-human, a population that absorbed itself into the fault line enough to keep the city from being completely swallowed by its own unstable ground. They are the living interface between the organism that breathes—and forgets to breathe—through the delta of forgotten river channels, and the machine that insists on turning forever in the wound of that place.
