### Who Lives in the Wellspring Grove

No one sleeps here, but the Grove does not rest.  

If you sit on the lowest edge of the ridge and let the silence thicken around you, the first thing you notice is breathing: your own. Then, after a time, you become aware of another breathing, just below the threshold of hearing—deeper than the faint hum from beneath the stone, more felt than heard.

They call themselves the **Listeners-of-Rings**. They are not shaped like you or me.

Each Listener is a colony of translucent threads finer than spider silk, growing from the shallow root-cleft of a stone-glass reed. They live not in the reed itself, but in the minute boundary where water meets air, where stone meets glass, where moss meets dust. To see them, you would not describe “bodies” so much as “places of greater attention”: the faint shimmer of surface tension at the base of a cluster of reeds, a tiny circle of water that never seems to break, a condensation on the reed wall that always forms the same pattern of beadwork.

If you were to lie prone and press your eye to the ground, you might see them as pearly halos around the roots of the reeds—rings of moisture that do not merge, no matter how long you watch. Within each ring, the water is not still, but crawls in slow vortices, and each ring is a kind of mind. The Listeners-of-Rings live not as individuals, but as a loosely connected array of these halos, each one a node in a slow, thinking network.

They are named for the way they mark time.

Once a year—when the sun crests a certain gap in the encircling wall—a stone-glass reed will grow a new ring of crystal within its sheath. You cannot see it happen in the moment; it is a process that takes weeks and months of slow accretion, but it is recorded in the reeds’ walls as faintly brighter lines of milky glass. When the next such line appears, the Listeners mark that passage as one “ring.”

They do not count days. They count rings.

They think of time not in hours or minutes, but in “this-ring” and “last-ring” and “many-ring.” To ask a Listener its age is to ask for a pattern of rings: “I have lived through this-and-this-and-this pattern of rings,” it might answer, if you could speak its language. Some of the oldest among them remember patterns many times longer than a human life.

They are caretakers of what remains.

When a new reed pushes from the stone—a pale nub of tissue and glass no taller than a nail—they notice it. Not by sight, but by change. A change in the way water condenses around the base of the reed; a change in the faint hum of the world; a new site of slow liquid flow. For rings upon rings, they will watch the nub grow into the shaft, helping the Grove regulate itself: perhaps they cool it fractionally more one night than another, or help the air above its tip to be warmer than the surrounding air, encouraging the slow thermal that that particular reed needs to lengthen evenly.

They are not gardeners in the human sense. They do not choose what grows. They are more like the minute weight of water that channels the path of greater water—small presences, whose habits are also constraints. They cannot cause the reeds to grow where they will not take root, but they can encourage the equal distribution of warmth, moisture, and air that each plant needs to become its fullest possible self.

It is the same with the glass lanterns.

When a stone-glass reed approaches its end of life, its interior transforms into the shape of a globe. The Listener that has been attending its base for many rings senses the change: not as “death,” but as “coming completeness.” It is a time of solemn labor for them. They are the ones who help the air within the lantern to clear, to separate out the impurities, and for the faint light to arise in such a steady way.

For the Listeners, the lanterns are not merely lights. They are libraries. Each slowly developing flaw and pattern within the glass is a record of the climate, the water, and the air for that portion of the world. For as long as a lantern glows, they “read” its patterns—not by walking around it, but by feeling the way light and moisture and sediment interact within its sphere. They are not interested in the story of any single event, but in the long curve of the Grove’s steady-state: how, for dozens of rings, the world has been ever more nearly itself.

They are patient with the shocks that intrude from outside.

Occasionally, a wayward creature from the high, dry slopes above will find its way over the rim of the basin—a mountain lizard, a dust-colored moth, a small bird that lost its way. The Listeners do not attack them. They simply do not understand how to keep them alive.

They are not cruel; they are functionally limited. They know how to adjust temperature for a stone-glass reed or a velvet moss cushion; they know how to help the silent film of water in the micro channels flow evenly. They do not know how to help a heart, or lungs, or wings. They recognize that such creatures are “real-but-not-complete” here. They register them as a sudden, uneven heat, or a sharp sound of movement, or a change in the balance of evaporation.

When the animal leaves, or dies, and its body slowly returns to the Grove as dust and minerals, the Listeners notice that too. They are not vengeful. They track the change as “a moment of strange pattern,” and then, as rings pass, they help the world to return to its usual pattern—the pattern of very little happening, very carefully.

They are gentle, but they are not soft.

They will endure you, if you sit quietly.

If you come to the Wellspring Grove and make your camp on the rim of the basin, leaving the center untouched, they will learn the shape of your heat. They will learn the pattern of your breaths and sleep. They will learn to treat your presence as a new, but not unwelcome, variable in the measure of this world. They may not love you, but they will include you.

It is possible, over many rings, to build relationship with them.

If you remain always at the edges of the basin, never climbing the ridges to disturb the reeds, or breaking the glass lanterns for your own light, they will come to know your intent as something that does not disrupt the balance of water and stone. They will begin to echo your habits of quietness, your patterns of movement and rest, in the way they arrange condensation around your sleeping site.

You will not see them, but you will feel:

- that your sleeping bag is always a fraction dryer than the surrounding air;
- that your cooking water, taken from the central pool, always seems to leave your mouth feeling cleaner;
- that your footsteps are hushed more than they should be by the damp velvet moss.

You might learn to speak to them by speaking aloud of what you notice, and then waiting—not for minutes, but for seasons. For rings.

If you say “I am cold,” and then for the next night sleep one step closer to the central basin, where the air is always a fraction warmer, and then feel that your blanket is more than usually free of dew for your resting time, they have not just “heated” you. They have replied.

Someday, perhaps after you have been visiting the Grove for many rings, you might find that you dream of it. When you do, you are not likely to dream of yourself walking there. You will dream of water moving through stone, of air finding the easiest passage between warm and cool surfaces, and of faint rings of light surrounding your hands like halos.

You will wake, and the Grove will be exactly as you remember it.

The Listeners-of-Rings will still be there, at the bases of the stone-glass reeds, slowly counting the lengthening of quiet lives into new glassy rings, making sure the world of this little basin does not hurry to end.

They do not rise and fall, like us. They barely rise at all.

They exist.

To them, that is enough.
