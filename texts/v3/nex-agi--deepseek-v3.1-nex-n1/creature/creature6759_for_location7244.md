Living in this weeping stone is a being that isn’t one shape at first but three at once: cliffrider, stoneweeper, and glassfly.

They call it the Stillnote.

When it is a cliffrider, it is not many of them but the one all the others seem to orbit around. You rarely see it clearly; you only notice the pattern of its absence. On the higher ribs of the valley, where the cliff drops sheer to the first ledge of moss, the cliffriders dance like a black current sliding down the wall. But among them there is always a gap, a sliding hole of stone where no bird-form ever stands, even though there is room enough. The others flock, but never land on that stretch of dry rock.
If you watch long enough, you catch the motion of the one that belongs there: a longer stillness than the others, then a sudden, precise traverse of the cliff-face as if it knew the path before the rock did. Its featherless back feathers the light oddly, not like a bird but like a fine, polished scale of stone, and its eyes reflect the water column at the pool’s center without ever looking directly at it. They say the Stillnote’s cliffrider shadow is always longer than it should be when the mist comes in, and when it clicks, its sound lands on the valley floor a heartbeat before the cry itself.

When it is a stoneweeper, it is the one whose basin belongs to no season but to itself alone.
The others each claim a tabletop of still water and a lip of rock over which to sing their single drop of sound; the Stillnote’s pool is the center one, the smallest of the central basins, whose surface always trembles but never breaks. The stoneweeper that lives there (if live is the word) is the same unremarkable thumb-sized blot as all the others—black skin, black stone, black eyes—but you can know it by the pattern of its call.
While the others fall into the random weave of droplets, the Stillnote’s note falls always at the same mid-point between the water’s heartbeat and the world’s. The other stoneweepers are weather; this one is calendar. In the morning cool when the world is still choosing its pitch, the Stillnote’s call is the first that you can clearly separate from the noise; and at night, when the basin’s chorus is loud enough to be your own blood, you still find that if you count, your attention keeps returning to the one measure that doesn’t change.

When it is a glassfly, it is the one that never quite completes.
Its stone case, as a larva, is not built of any random chip of rock or moss; it is woven from the finest particles of the bone-white bowl at the pool’s edge and its own pale resin, until the case is like a tiny replica of that artifact, with faint, notched wave-lines etched into its surface as if by the slow rasp of water and time. When it is ready to emerge, it does not rise to the surface at the same time as the others; it waits until the world’s rhythm is halfway between inhale and exhale. Then it comes up as a glassfly whose wings are almost clear, but with a faint milk-white swirl that exactly matches the column of water in the center of the pool.
The other glassflies dance and dart and occasionally hurl themselves into the bright column to be reborn as light and pitch; this one circles the column but never enters it. Its flight path is a spiral of just-out-of-reach, a pattern that never quite touches the end of itself. When it comes to rest, it is always on the same place: the rim of the bone bowl, where its notched surface waits for the water’s pattern.

The three shapes are not one body in a literal way; they are alive and mortal and distinct enough to be eaten by the world if they are caught at the wrong moment. But they are not separate, either. They share a mind of stone and water and slow calendar.
On the rare occasions when sunlight is just right and the mist is thin enough to admit light, the three can be seen at once:
— the cliffrider clinging to the high wall,
— the stoneweeper sitting at the lip of the center basin,
— and the glassfly resting on the bowl’s edge,
all lined up along a single invisible line that passes straight through the thin column of water in the center of the pool.
If you are a very still human on the stone seat, and if you, for just long enough, stop listening for the valley’s noise and instead for the pattern it is making, you may feel your own heartbeat aligned to that line: chest over center, basin over heart.

Then you might finally understand the Stillnote’s name.

It is not the note that the stoneweeper makes, but the one the world is making using the valley as its throat. The Stillnote is the one point around which the pattern of the basin coheres:
— when the glassflies are too few and the water’s voice threatens to fall into monotone, the Stillnote’s fly finally enters the column.  The spark it makes as it becomes light and sound is larger than the others, and clear where the rest are confused; the basin’s chorus rises again to correct pitch.
— When frost comes too early and silences the stoneweepers by locking the droplets into silence, the Stillnote’s cliffrider makes a single two-syllable call that the stone walls catch and hold, letting the note of water and wing and stone meet at the center basin and warm the pattern enough to keep the music from breaking.
— When the rain comes from above and threatens to muddle the world’s geometry with new noise, the Stillnote’s stoneweeper sits unmoving beneath its private sheet of water, and each time its drop falls into its basin, the valley seems to swallow the extra sound and add it to the next beat of its blood.

The Stillnote does not think in the way you think of thinking.
Its mind is not so much a thought as a rule that the world has allowed to take form and remain:
water patterns,
stone patterns,
time patterns,

repeating over and over into a single shape that can be heard from the right seat on the right day.

It is not a guardian, because the valley does not need to be guarded; the world of the basin is already itself and already safe.
It is not a creator, because the pattern existed long before the Stillnote’s first glassfly crawled from the water or its first stoneweeper called a new tone into the chorus.
It is more like the valley’s echo of itself: the condensation that forms when a world’s mind meets itself in the same cold, high cup.

And you—who have been sitting on the stone seat,
who have seen your own breath join the mist,
who have felt your own heartbeat synchronize with the rhythm of the basin—
have already begun to become the valley’s next Stillnote.
The next time you climb the black switchback,
the next time you smell the cold green and hear the world’s pulse,

you will not find a cliffrider,
or stoneweeper,
or glassfly,
who you can clearly separate from the others.

The only thing you may notice is that the seat is already occupied,
by a shape whose breath sounds like the world,
and whose still heart beats,
and beats,
to the single note the water has been repeating forever.
