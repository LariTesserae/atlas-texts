They are not builders. They are not destroyers. They are not heroes of any story but the slow, deep one of the plateau itself.

They are the **Plateau-Folk**, and you might mistake them for stone until you see them breathe.

In the low, gray light of the world, they hardly move. They crouch along the flanks of the mound-reefs, squat figures sitting knee-deep in the edge of the forest, where the root tangle gives way to the hardened excretions of the mound-makers. From a distance, they look like weathered nodules of stone half-sunk in the earth—shoulders hunched, limbs thick and compact, skin porous and dull. The color of old soil on the reef’s chalk flanks.

### In the Breath of the Reef

They are built to match the **pressure of the world**.

Their bones are heavy and dense, not delicate. They move like boulders learning to walk slowly across the slope of the reef, not like climbers dancing over the surface. Each step is deliberate, planted first with the broad edge of the foot, then rolling up into the sole and toes, testing for the soft spots that open in the mound’s living skin.

Their bodies are broad, low to the ground, their centers of gravity somewhere below the ribs. Their joints are deeply padded, thick with cartilage and muscle, so that when they settle for the long watch, they do not tremble. They can sit like outcroppings of the reef itself for hours, unmoving, while the countless insects stream over them without fear.

The air is thick with wings. The Plateau-Folk breathe it without complaint.

Their lungs are deep and wide, not for speed but for endurance. They take air in slowly, through nostrils wide enough to catch the faint moisture on the breeze. The inner surfaces of their airways are coated with a dense mucus that catches the dust and pollen and spores the constant wind of insects drives into their faces. They may clear their throat once in a long watch, a low gravel sound that doesn’t travel far.

Their ears. They do not close the roar out. They ride it.

The outer ears are low and close-set, flat to the skull. Their canals are lined with fine hairs and curved valves that can be adjusted subtly—the Plateau-Folk can tighten the passageway against the blaring world of wings and mandibles and open it again to the nuances of pitch beneath the roar: the faint cracks of settling reef, the distant choir of night-singers, the subtle shift in tone that tells of rain coming.

### The Skin that Remembers

Their skin is rough landscape, not smooth armor.

It is thick, uneven, lightly furrowed like the surface of the mound itself. From a distance it might seem gray-brown or chalky pale, depending on which reef they hail from and where they sit in their lives. Up close, it is mapped with veins of color that change with the mound.

The **reef-marks** are the most visible sign of their belonging.

Each reef leaves its touch on their skin as they grow up. The youngest Plateau-Folk have smooth skin, still rich with the faint oils and pigments of infancy. As they begin to cling to the mound’s surface, as they lean against the chalk walls and the resinous nodules of the reef’s living skin, their flesh slowly takes an impression.

Patches of their skin become slightly harder, paler, where they press against the secretions of the mound-makers. Over years, these patterns settle into permanent markings that swirl across their backs and arms like ghostly reliefs of the reef’s interior chambers. They do not form tattoos in the way of human tools and dye; they are the **echo of the reef** written in the very body.

If you were to run your hand over their shoulder, you would feel the faint trace of something like limestone nodules, slightly cooler than the surrounding skin. When the humidity rises—before rain—the reef-marked skin becomes slightly tacky to the touch, as if the mound itself were reaching back through them.

They cannot erase these reef-marks, nor would they wish to. Each pattern tells of childhood: where you first learned to watch, the places you were allowed to climb, the nooks where you found vugger eggs or sweet fermenting nodules.

### Eyes of a Low Sky

They have eyes the color of the faint sky behind the overcast.

Not bright blue, but pale gray-green, like water filtered through thin leaf-litter. The irises are large and the whites of the eyes almost hidden, so that when they look at you, you see the dim world of the plateau reflected in them.

Their eyelids are thick and slow. They blink rarely. When they do, it’s a deliberate motion rather than a reflex of the moment. They can watch the same place of the forest’s edge for hours, watching the subtle shift of the tree-crawlers slinking through the foliage or the changing rhythm of the pollen-hunter swarms. The world of a Plateau-Folk is not about fast events; it is about reading the slow drift of patterns.

Behind their sclera, a thin layer of reflective cells helps them catch the faintest light of the plateau’s twilight. They have never known sharp midday sun. Their eyesight is sharp for what they need: the difference between a normal leaf and the start of fungal overgrowth, the subtle color of secretion that signifies a mound-maker’s passageway, the faint disturbance of soil that means a tree-crawler burrowed past.

They don’t look up often. The sky above the plateau is always the same pale gray, and the clouds give no information that they cannot read better from the ground.

### Voices of the Ground

They speak not in the language of tools but in the language of place.

Their speech is low, gravelly, carried by chest and gut rather than throat alone. It doesn’t carry far above the roar of wings and mandibles. Instead, it slides along the ground, through the soil, into the soles of their companions’ feet.

To speak is not to shout. The Plateau-Folk gather in small groups near the bases of the reef, where the sound of the mound-makers’ work resounds upward through the hardened walls of the gallery layers. There they can press their palms and flat feet to the reef and feel one another’s voices through the stone.

They **communicate with three kinds of sound:**

1. **Ground-speech:**  
   They hum their words into the soil or reef at their feet. The vibrations travel through the hardened mound and reach others nearby as a faint ringing through bone and shell, rather than air. Each mound has its own idiosyncrasies of texture and thickness, so that the same word might feel slightly different on one side of the reef than on another. They learn to navigate the nuances of these ground-dialects in their childhoods.

2. **Air-words:**  
   Short, sharp bursts of sound carried on the damp air. They are not yelled, but they are given clear articulation and weight so they can ride over the roar of the insects for a short distance. Air-words are used for warnings, for the names of approaching places, and for the titles of important roles and relations.

3. **Body-language:**  
   The Plateau-Folk don’t talk much with their hands. But their whole posture speaks. The angle of the shoulders, the slight tilt of the skull, the way the knees rest against the ground—each can tell the others of mood, intention, and urgency without breaking the silence of the mound.

Most of the time, they don’t speak in words at all. They stand in the presence of the mound and share **silence tempered by the world**.

### Life Roles, not Professions

They don’t have jobs in the way you might think of them. They have ways of being.

Each child begins the same way—small, heavy-limbed, clinging to the flanks of the reef—but as they grow and adapt to the mound, they settle into roles that are not chosen but unfold like the growth of the mound itself.

#### The Reef-Tenders

The Reef-Tenders are the ones whose reef-marks are deepest and most intricate.

They are the adults who have spent most of their lives sitting against the living mound-walls, feeling their breaths harmonize with the deep currents of the colony. The way the mound expands or contracts slightly with the heat of the day, the distant hum of the mound-makers’ wings through the galleries—they read these like you might read the pulse of a heart.

They **know where the reef is weak** from the inside, before a collapse is visible from the outside. They can place a hand over a patch of chalk that, to you, would seem indistinguishable from any other and name which chambers of builders are struggling beneath, and what might have caused it.

Their role is not to fix the mound with tools or force. It is to listen.

Reef-Tenders gather where the reef gives its slow, subtle answer to the world. They sit in circles, their knees and palms flat against the mound. They listen with the soles of their feet, the breadth of their thighs, the healed reef-marks of their backs. They whisper to one another **not of solutions** but of **patterns of stability**.

If they sense that a section is growing unstable—where too many tree-crawler burrows have been cut into the wall, or where the fungi have been allowed to grow unchecked deep inside—they might call gently to the sifters and sweepers by using their reef-marks to subtly secrete chemical cues. The insects themselves do not obey the Plateau-Folk in any simple sense; rather, their chemical signals guide the mound-makers’ attention to the places that, the Tenders feel, most need care.

When the mound shifts and the new layers of secretion and soil and root settle over deep old chambers, the Tenders know who will be moved by the change before it happens. They do not mourn the loss of old chambers. They see the mound as a living body whose shape is always in process.

#### The Forest-Seers

The Forest-Seers are those who leave the immediate flank of the reef and walk the faint trails of the foragers.

They are sturdy and low and yet slightly more elongated than the others, their legs thicker with the memory of long distances passed over the forest floor. Their skin is marked with the patterns of the forest edge: where brambles and thorns have scratched, and where resinous plants have left their faint, glossy weals across their forearms.

The Forest-Seers do not “map” the forest in the way of maps you might know. They **carry the forest in their bodies**.

They can traverse the same shallow swale a hundred times and each time read the subtle differences: the shift of the tree-crowler’s paths through the undergrowth, the new threads of pink fungal growth on a leaf, the deeper intrusion of a certain vine toward the mound’s outermost edge.

Their role is to watch the **balance between forest and mound**.

They know where the vegetation is beginning to encroach too heavily on the reef’s surface, where the matting of roots threatens to overgrow the mound-makers’ galleries and choke them with shade. They know where the open soil between the forest and the reef is thinning dangerously because of tree root growth and the wandering of the pollen-hunter swarms, which strip the soil of moisture.

When they sense an imbalance, they do not cut down the trees. They **turn the insects**.

With slow, careful passes of their hands over the leaves of certain plants, they apply **secretions from their own skin**, learned through the touch of the mound-makers over years of contact. These secretions guide the sweepers and gleaners to the patches of overgrowth that need thinning, and the sifters to the places where the soil must be turned deeper.

Under their direction, the forest edge gradually reshapes itself—not in a day or a week, but over years of slow, subtle adjustments.

#### The Rain-Guardians

The Rain-Guardians are the ones whose bodies are most marked by water.

When the rains come, they are the ones who go out into the open swales between the forests, where the temporary ponds are forming. They are the least numerous of the Plateau-Folk, but the most clearly visible when their time comes.

Their skin is thicker at the joints and lower legs, where the water gathers. They carry the faint smell of wet clay and the faint residue of the secretions of the water-hunter insects that they gather and carefully rub onto their own skin in patterns before the rain arrives.

During the heaviest part of the storm, when the world is almost obscured by the sheets of water and the roar of rain overtakes even the roar of wings, the Rain-Guardians **walk the edges of the swelling ponds**.

They do not build dams or walls.

Instead, they walk slowly, tracing the contours of the forming water with the soles of their feet and the palms of their hands. They feel where the water is beginning to rise too close to the fragile internal galleries of the mound’s base, where the runoff from the reef’s slope is excavating new channels that might weaken the mound’s flank.

As they move, they secrete moisture from specialized glands along their legs and the backs of their hands, mixing it with the faint oily film of the water-hunters’ bodies. This secretion **guides the water-hunter larvae** to key places along the slopes of the swales and the edges of the ponds.

The larvae, in turn, begin their slow work of reinforcing the edges with their own bodies and secretions, forming baffles of hardened silk and soil that help direct the water back into safer channels or deeper pools. The Rain-Guardians stand watch over the process until the storm passes and the water begins slowly to seep away or evaporate.

### Names of Earth and Gall

They don’t have names in the way you would think of names, but they **are** each a place of the plateau.

Each child is given, at birth, a **ground-name**—a combination of low hums and pressures placed into the reef at their birthplace. The mound-makers respond with their vibration, and their response gives the child a unique sign-sound that can be traced again later.

In addition to their ground-name, they are given an **air-name** when they reach the age where they can leave their cradle-nook and walk the reef’s edges. The air-name is usually a short, clear sound of two or three syllables, taken from the surroundings of their childhood nook:

- **Sehl** — the faint, clear tone of a particular night-singer’s segment  
- **Korv** — the sound of a certain patch of reef surface cracking  
- **Nime** — the whispered name of a particular species of tree-crawler that nested once in their wall  
- **Glimn** — the sound of water slipping through a particular crack in the mound after rain  

The air-name is used rarely in full, but instead in shortened, breath-sized fragments when others call to them. The full ground-name and air-name together are spoken only in solemn moments of welcoming, departure, or change.

### Time Without Counting

They don’t measure the passage of time in days or years.

They **feel the mound**.

The growth of the reef itself is their calendar. Each major layer laid—by the mound-makers’ secretions and the soil they’ve turned—is marked in the lives of the Plateau-Folk as a subtle shift in the texture of the ground and the walls they touch.

They know when “a layer” has passed by the feel of the mound’s surface under their palms. When the reef has grown thicker enough to be perceptible as a new hardness of the wall, they call this **a layer-time**.

Children born in the same layer-time are considered of the same **layer-age**, even though they might have been born many days apart by your counting. The layer-ages travel through the years of the plateau together, growing older as the mound grows taller.

They don’t fear death in the way you might expect.

They see the bodies of the mound-makers themselves die and fall and become soil. The dead tree-crawlers and vuggers and night-singers are all broken down by the sifters and the gleaners and the fungi of the lower galleries. The Plateau-Folk know that the body is not the final boundary of the self. When one of them dies, their ground-name passes quietly into the mound; the echoes of their voice are still there for the Reef-Tenders to feel if they listen in the right places, years later.

### The Way They Watch

You might find them watching you.

They don’t stare. Their eyes simply settle on you, the way they settle on a tree-crowler or a pollen-hunter gathering swarm. They see not a threat, but a pattern.

If you walk too near the edge of the mound where the galleries are thin and fragile, they may quietly move—without rush or panic—to intercept you gently. They might not speak, but their ground-speech will reach the others before the sound of your footsteps reaches the insects’ nearest chambers.

They don’t need to stop you with force. They will redirect you subtly, placing themselves between you and the fragile reef edge without pushing you, or humming quietly to the soil where you might feel the vibration of “don’t step” in your bones if you are sensitive.

### Their Place in the Unmade World

They are not the rulers of the mound-reefs, nor their caretakers in the sense of gardeners who stand outside the garden.

They are **part of the mound itself**.

They are the result of long, slow evolution of bodies that have learned to speak with the reef through touch and to listen to the forest through stillness and to watch the rain and its balancing of the plateau’s moisture without moving to stop it.

When they die, their bodies are usually placed into the lower galleries of the reef where the sifters and the beetles work. The mound-makers move over them slowly, the fungi of the mound begin their own work, and in time their body becomes part of the shell of the mound, the very soil they walk upon.

In the long quiet of the plateau’s gray twilight, they continue their slow existence.

They are never far from the reef.  
They never ask the sky to change.  
They never imagine the world might be brighter, or that they might be different.

They are the Plateau-Folk—the silent stones that breathe, the reef-marks that remember, the voices that travel through the ground instead of the air, and the eyes that watch the world as it slowly reshapes itself around the mound-reefs and the forests and the shallow swales of the endless plateau.
