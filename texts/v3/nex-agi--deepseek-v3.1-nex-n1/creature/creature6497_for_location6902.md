They call themselves **Listeners** here, but not the way you’d use the word in a quiet room.

Theirs is a listening of the body, of the stone, of the pressure gradients in the air and the faint harmonics of the engine below their feet. Listening to the city is not a choice for them; it is a state of being.

They are tall and slender, not fragile but thin-wired like ferns growing toward a low sun. Their skin has taken on the color of the rock they live within and around — a pale, bone-white shade that never tans, never darkens, only faintly flushed at the joints where blood is closer to the surface. Their hands are long-fingered, with an extra joint between knuckles, giving them a subtly segmented, graceful motion when they adjust the channels or the conduits.

Built from scarcity and patience, they have learned to breathe with the world, rather than against it.

### Shape and Movement

They are built for water and stone and quiet.

Their bones are dense but not heavy, closer to the mineral weight of rock than to fragile bird bones. Their joints move smoothly, with barely a sound, even when they climb the sheer ledges around the bowl. They don’t climb like you might picture — no frantic scrambling or hauling — but rather slide along the stone as if their weight has been negotiated carefully with the cliff itself.

Their clothing is simple, almost an extension of the stone around them. They wear layers of thin, grey cloth woven from some pale fibrous plant that you never see growing whole — only in threads that they harvest from the edges of the channels where it clings like persistent algae. They don’t dye it or add patterns. There are no bright colors — no banners of rank or clan. The closest they come to decoration is the faint, almost invisible pattern of stitching where the fabric overlaps, which looks like the lines of moisture channels on a map of the city.

When they walk, they are unhurried. Their shoulders are relaxed, their chests slightly open, as if they are always in the middle of a deep breath that never ends. They don’t stride; they *flow* along the paths, with a small rolling motion of the hips that keeps their center of gravity low and balanced over the damp, uneven stone of the terraces.

You rarely see them run. There is no need to run here — the world unfolds at a slower cadence than the panic of sprinting would suggest.

### Senses

Their eyes are large compared to a human’s, but not in the way of night creatures. They’re more like the eyes of someone who was born to read faint text by low light. The irises are pale — almost colorless in the dim, or faintly green like shallow water over stone. They don’t blink often. When they do, it’s a slow, deliberate closing of the eyelids, as if they’re testing the darkness behind each blink.

They are adapted to the flat, evenly-lit sky and the faint veins of light in the stone. The world to them is not a place of harsh shadows and sharp contrasts, but a subtle gradation of tones and moistures and pressures.

Most strikingly, they hear *more* than sound.

They are attuned to the subtle vibrations of the engine and the water channels and the stone itself. Their ears are only slightly larger than a human’s, but structured differently — the outer folds are not random curves but carefully shaped funnels that channel sound into patterns of pressure across their tympanum. They can feel the difference between a small leak in the channels above them and the slow settling of a supporting beam under the next terrace over, just from the way the air vibrates differently.

Every morning of their cycle, they press a bare foot or hand to the stone of the bowl floor or the wall behind their sleeping platform and “check the machine’s breath.” They are not praying; they are taking the world’s pulse.

They are, one and all, mechanics of the hidden system that keeps them alive.

---

### Voice and Silence

The Listeners are not quiet out of shyness but out of necessity.

The hum of the engine under the stone, the slow drip of water, the faint shifts of metal under the streets — these are the baseline sounds of their world. If they were to shout, or even speak loudly and often, they would lose the thread of the machine’s state. They have become, in practice, a quiet people.

They do speak, but in a language of low tones and small pauses. The words are short, most of them, and many of the same syllables are used to talk about water channels, engine load, stone stability, and the subtle patterns of moisture on the walls. Their speech is precise not because they love grammar but because they love *information*.

The faintest misphrasing might be the difference between “there is rust beginning in the lower right channel” and “there is rust beginning in the lower left channel.” In a world of precious water and hidden faults, that is a gap wide enough to matter.

When they are among themselves and not attending to the machine, they fall into long comfortable silences. They are not uncomfortable with quiet; they are shaped by it.

### Work and the City

There are no distinct classes of rulers here — no kings of the bowl, no council of elders swathed in robes. Instead, there is a hierarchy of *attention*.

Whoever can sense the city best holds sway over small decisions: *this channel needs cleaning before dusk, that section of wall is weeping too much and must be guided into a new basin, the engine beneath the plaza is running slightly off the rhythm of the last rain cycle, and the pressure must be adjusted.*

They are ever-attending, the Listeners.

Their hands are often stained with faint green from moss or darkened with absorbed moisture from the stone they touch. They carry the tools of the city’s tending: slender probes of metal and bone for testing the hardness of mineral deposits in the channels, flat stones carved with faint lines that they press against the wall to listen to the vibrations behind it, small containers of translucent gel that shimmer when exposed to moisture and light — indicating where water is hidden that they cannot sense directly.

They move along the terraces in the same pattern every day, but not because of any rigid rule. They simply follow the same path because the city needs the same care. The channels are still young; their edges are precise but not yet self-sustaining. One day, perhaps, the stone will be worn enough to guide its own water without the need of fingers adjusting clamps and angles. But today is not that day.

---

### The Engine Below

The engine at the center of the bowl is not a god to them, but it is not merely a machine.

To them, it is the single most responsible thing they know.

It never breaks down, not fully. It only… falters. Sometimes it slows slightly, or a different harmonic overtakes the usual hum for a cycle. They have learned to read these variations as symptoms of the world: *too much moisture gathered in the northeastern basin, the metal under the plaza is beginning to stress, there is a new fault in the stone under the lower terraces that has not yet shown on the surface.*

They are like the white cells in a living body who are beginning to understand the heart they move around.
