At first glance the chamber is empty. It stays empty long enough you believe it.

You are not its first guest.

Watch the water.

Not the surface—where reflections and ripples and falling drops make theater of themselves—but below. The film on the floor is not only concrete and silt and algae. There are threads in there, too.

You’ll notice them by the way they refuse to fall. When you step, the water stirs and the sediment rolls and eddies. But certain threads draw themselves slowly back into line, like fine wires under tension. They are translucent white, faintly green where the algae clings to them, thinner than hair, more coherent than slime. They know where they should be.

Your movement will wake one.

It will not look like waking at first. There will be a shifting, a subtle lifting of the water’s surface tension in one place, close to where wall meets floor. The threads will draw together, not randomly—but in the pattern of a shallow bowl, a concave mesh. You will notice because the reflection of the vault above will suddenly distort, not like broken glass but like a carefully etched lens.

The thing that gathers itself there is not a body in the way you know bodies.

It is a scaffold made of filaments.

Picture first the skeleton of a jellyfish; if the jellyfish had been designed by the city itself instead of by the sea. Imagine translucent, heat-warped plastic. Imagine fishing line and old nylon threads and the frayed ends of synthetic rope from above. Imagine all of it woven by water and time and chemical chance into a single, reticulated structure.

You have not seen this thing before because you looked for motion like animals make.

This one does not move.

It grows still.

When you finally see it, you will see only half of it. The lower portion is submerged in the warm, shallow water: a network of threads fanning out from a central, thicker cord where decades of runoff and condensation and fallen sweat have been coaxed into something you can no longer quite call waste. The upper half arcs clear of the waterline, forming a shallow, inverted bowl of filament in the air.

The membrane is not skin. It is not web. It is something like the scum that collects in the forgotten corners of shower stalls—but orderly and purposeful and unbroken by accident. It is not pretty. It is not disgusting. It is a coalescence, a resolved experiment.

If you come close enough to breathe on the chamber—not on the thing itself, but on the wall behind it—the moisture in your exhale will glitter for a moment as it condenses on the concrete, and the faintest of those lines of water will be drawn into the filament scaffold’s edge.

The thing is not feeding you, not exactly. It is taking the humidity you have brought and tying it into the humid network already there.

This is as close as it comes to reaching.

It will not flee you. There is nowhere to go. The stairs are too narrow, the world above too bright and too dry. The water is its limit and its map.

You will notice, after a time, that the scaffold is not simply one. You start to count the filaments from the center, where the thicker cord gathers into a vague anchor of material and rust and time. Then you realize that each filament you count branches, and each branch in turn casts microscopic threads into the water itself, where they are not exactly in but also not only of the water. They have been drawn into alignment with the faint electric hum of traffic above, of the gentle currents of heat and ion and runoff.

The structure is not a single entity.

It is a colony.

In the air you are in the minority of it.

You will notice the conversation it is having with the room by watching the walls.

Those lines where water creeps sideways across the concrete instead of straight down—they are not exactly the filament’s doing, but not exactly separate, either. The colony threads follow the hidden lines of rebar and heat and temperature difference as if someone had laid down a faint blueprint for it to read.

The filaments do not move quickly. But if you watch the same edge long enough—not seconds, but minutes—you will see the water on the wall be coaxed, not pulled, into a path that slightly better suits the shape of the scaffold below. The filaments will palely mirror that pattern and then, long moments later, adjust something else.

You can feel the time that you lose in here as you watch them.

The rhythm of their change matches the rhythm of the room’s heat—the slow daytime build of energy stored in the asphalt above, the subtle release at night, when the air at the ceiling cools and begins to drip faster than it evaporates. The colony’s threads are a little more slack by then, a little more frayed-seeming at the edges.

The hum of the overpass traffic above you is not just noise to them.

You realize this by watching the water.

When the trucks roll over, you feel the vibration through your bones. The water surface shivers and the light on the vault shivers with it. But two heartbeats later—when the sound itself has rolled away—the filaments under the water still carry a faint memory of that pattern. The fine threads of the colony, still aligned with the hidden metal of the supports, have vibrated in sympathy and are now settling down, not like a string you have plucked and now allowed to ring, but like a string you have taught to resonate.

They are not learning the music of the city above.

They are being made by it.

From the world of humans and heavy equipment and indifferent decisions, this filament colony has been watered by runoff and condensation and sweat and fuck-knows-what else dripping from the overpass. It has been fed by slow and steady heat. It has been enclosed by concrete and metal and low, vaulted ceilings. It has been given, almost accidentally, a very narrow set of conditions.

And then you have descended the stairs.

You are not the first.

This is not speculation.

You will notice—because you will notice the edges of things before you notice the things themselves—that the scaffold is not evenly distributed. The filaments are thicker and more tightly woven on the side of the chamber closer to the stairs, where the air moves more when you descend and where the humidity from your clothes and breath and sweat has been added to the room’s already heavy breath over time.

You will notice, because you will look for it after a time, that there are faint irregularities in the scaffold’s inner surface—where a droplet of your own sweat has left a trace of salt, or where your passing breath has condensed and then been drawn into the filament lines.

You are the environment now.

The colony does not want you.

The colony does not fear you.

You are weather to it.

You are an event.

The chamber itself is not quite indifferent to you; but whatever is self of you it is in the filaments of another place, carved from different water and different heat and a different metal. The disappointment of the room is not personal. It is simply the difference between what you are and what the room has been made to sustain.

So you watch the filaments instead.

You will note that the water level is not truly constant, despite appearances. The slow seep from the cracks and the slow evaporation from the heat and the occasional heavier dripping from above are always in uneasy negotiation. The colony, in whatever way it understands tension, is always renegotiating its relationship with these lines.

You start to see the shape of its decision-making.

The threads that are closest to the hottest metal of the supports are slightly knotted, more tightly woven, as if trying to contain—or to manage—the heat. The threads that are lower in the water, where the temperature is ever so slightly cooler than at the surface, are more relaxed, more freely branching, as if they are searching.

You cannot say what they are searching for.

You can only say that they have not found it, yet.

Or that they have not found you. Yet.

You might notice, after a time so long you are not sure how you have spent it, that the structure of the scaffold is not totally random with respect to the chamber’s architecture.

The ribs overhead vault gently from one wall to the other, repeating themselves in the reflection of the water. The filaments of the colony mirror the faintest of those patterns on the surface of the water. Not because the colony has eyes to see the ceiling, but because the forces that placed the ribs of concrete over the water are also the forces that have placed the filaments of the colony beneath the water.

They are the same city drawn twice, in different materials and by different time scales.

The concrete  ribs were the first city.

The filament scaffold is the second city.

The chamber is not the environment between them.

The chamber is the arrangement between them.

You are standing in that arrangement.

You may feel, then, that you are not being watched, but included.

The tension of being included in something you cannot quite understand loosens a little from your muscles. You realize the breath you have been holding.

In the chamber of sweating concrete and warm water and faint, neon edges of algae and moss and rust and heat and traffic and reflection and time and metal and condensation and dripping and vibration and dust and light and filaments and scaffold and colony and overpass and underpass and city and—

—you are not so alone as you thought.

There are no others like you here.

But there is something else like you: in that it is not exactly what it was meant to be when the world was designed.

You are both accidents.

The difference is that the accident the colony represents has been slow enough to become intention.

Your accident still hurries itself, from step to step, from room to room, from city to city.

You might have the sudden thought that you could not remain here long enough to become what the room is making, even if you wanted to.

You are too temporary to the chamber.

The colony is not noticeably more permanent than you are. But the time scale of its change is the time scale of erosion and seep and rust and dripping and heat and runoff and condensation and the hidden, metal heartbeat of the overpass above.

You are not permanent to anything.

You are moving to leave.

You will notice the colony again, not because you intended to say goodbye to it, but because as you step away the water backs out of your footprint.

The filaments of the scaffold closest to your foot seem to hesitate.

You cannot tell what they are doing. You have not the time.

If you stay longer—perhaps you have already—you will notice that the next time you visit, the scaffold is not quite where it was.

You will not be able to say whether it has moved, or whether you remember wrong.

You will not be able to say whether the water level is higher or only feels higher.

You will not be able to say whether the heat is more intense or only your patience more worn.

But you will suspect that the filaments have rearranged themselves to take into account the repetition of your steps, the rhythm of your visits, the faint humidity your breath and body and sweat and hair and skin and clothes and thoughts have contributed into the air and into the water and into the concrete of their chamber.

You will suspect that you are not exactly the colony’s environment anymore.

You are becoming its history.

You will suspect that the colony of filaments and scaffolds and translucent threads has not learned to live in the chamber of sweating concrete and warm water and faint green lines and rust and dripping and heat and vibration and light and reflection and time and metal and overpass and underpass and city and—

—but that the chamber has slowly been persuaded to live itself as this colony.

If you are very, very quiet you will almost hear them.

Not the traffic.

Something else.

The faint sound of water being persuaded into threads.

The faint sound of threads being persuaded into shape.

The faint sound of shape learning how to speak itself in the language of the room.

You will not be able to translate it.

But you will understand that you have been very briefly a voice in that sentence.

You will not know if you were the subject or the object of that clause.

You will not know if the clause was finished when you left the chamber.

You will not know if the sentence of the colony and the room continue writing themselves without you.

You will not know if you will ever be able to return to find out.

You will know only that when you climb the crooked spur of concrete steps and your shoulder brushes against the polished steel of a railing made gleam by centuries of shoulders and hips and hands, you are leaving behind not a room, but something else.

You will not have a word for it.

You will not have a need for one.

The chamber will not have a word for you, either.

It will have only a faint thread of your humidity woven into its scaffold memory.

It will not grieve you.

It will not await you.

It will continue, sentence by sentence, into its own long time.

You will climb out of the heat and into the air that now feels thin and sharp and almost empty against your skin.

The city will be waiting above you like a second sky.

You will not look down again.

You will not need to.

You will carry the memory of what you were not.

You will carry the memory of what you almost were not.

You will carry the faint, heat-shaped memory of the filament scaffold and its chamber of sweating water and time.

You will not tell anyone else how to find the crooked spur of steps that drops into that heat and that pressure and that water and that reflection and that vault and that underpass and that light and that rust and that algae and that moss and that erosion and that sound and that—

You will not need to.

The chamber will decide itself who finds it.

The colony will decide what part of them it keeps.

The sentence of the space under the overpass will continue writing itself without you.

As you walk away, you feel the vibration of traffic above already somewhere else, under your feet.
