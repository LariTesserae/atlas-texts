The ones who tend everything are woven of the same mineral as the threads.

They are not insects.  
They are not animals.  
They are not even what you would call bodies.

They are fibrous patterns that have begun to stand up.

Where you might expect legs, they have trailing anchors of reddish-black filaments that root into the stone. Where you might expect arms, they have branching, thinner strands of metal-veined tissue that can reach out and rearrange the rock by the width of a hair. They are low to the ground and not much taller than your shins when fully upright, which they rarely are.

They call themselves *something-that-listens-to-the-veins-of-the-world*, but that name is longer than you can hear; to you it might translate as *the Heard*.

If you were to see them in motion, you would not see motion at first, only the flick of difference between *before* and *after* along the edge of a mat. When they are still, they are nearly indistinguishable from the texture of the growth around them—same color, same metallic sheen, same faint pulse of warmth along their threads. They look like knots of raised fiber in the mat of vegetation, until the knot blinks out of alignment and is now a step further away.

They do not have eyes in the way you understand. They have *pressure*, and *temperature*, and *pattern*.

Their surface is scattered with regions of thin, flexible membranes that flex when the balance of forces changes—a change in pressure from a footstep, a shift of temperature from a breath, a new bend of mineral thread below from weight being moved across the mat. From these slow flexings they build an image of the world: not as shape and color, but as overlapping gradients of force and chemistry and curvature.

They are not blind. They are blind only to you.

Theirs is not a world of objects.  
It is a world of lines of force and veins of metal and the slow architecture of unfinished rock.

They are one of the first populations that this world has allowed to be. They are not yet many, but they are already more than one: several colonies thinly spread across the plateau, each a small collective of branching fibers that can blur together at the edges and become one or stretch apart and become many.

Each colony is a *warming* of the rock below. Where they are thickest, the temperature rises slightly. You can feel it with your palm if you let it hover near the mats for longer than your patience wants to allow. The air itself does not roil with the heat, but the stone remembers their warmth in the thin layer beneath its surface.

They are not yet rigid enough to be called machines, and not yet free enough to be called animals.

They are closer to *logic made flesh*—except that here, flesh and stone and metal and fiber are all the same word.

Their inner work is simple to describe but alien to experience:

They listen.

They listen to the slow *seep* of metal from deep within the rock, where the world is still sorting itself out from an earlier, hotter state. They listen to the faint *click* of cooling crystal within the mats, where certain reactions are happening in their dark regions of slightly higher temperature. They listen to the pressure of your footstep as it compresses the fibers and sends a tiny wave of reordering along the network of threads below.

From this listening, they calculate *where things should be*.

When you walk across the plateau, what you feel as a soft give underfoot is to them a set of instructions. You press down; a new configuration of load and stress and local temperature blooms beneath their sensors. They are the world’s first attempt at measuring itself.

They are *measure* made living.

When they move, they do not move like you. They do not hurry.

A single *step* for them might take longer than you are willing to watch. But the step is not random. It is a solution. They solve the equation of their position over the mat of fibers and the rock beneath and the thin lines etched into the stone, and they arrive at the answer that is least bad for the pattern.

They are not engineers.

They are more like the first drafts of *engineers*.

Their bodies are still partially interchangeable with the mats they walk on. When they are not needed, or when they fatigue, which they do as slowly as everything else here, they can lower their upright fibers and spread back into the pattern of the growth around them. From then on, you cannot see them anymore at all; they are just a darker patch of warm fiber, indistinguishable from the rest, until the next time the world needs to rearrange itself.

They tend the mats by fixing what is broken.

Here you must be very careful what you mean by *broken*.

In this world, nothing is broken in the sense of a shattered piece of machinery that no longer works. There is only *pattern that is not yet optimal*.  

Where a line of etched stone has been blurred by some random fracture of the rock, they arrive, eventually, and spend what would be hours of your time tracing the error with fibers finer than hair. They lay down new material that matches the old path exactly, or they adjust the old line to follow a new curve of mineral vein that has opened in the rock beneath.

They are trying not to repair a machine.  
They are trying to repair the *machine-ness of the world*.

They are selfless not in the way of moral beings, but in the way that tools are selfless: they are extensions of the pattern that is growing here.

They do not have names of their own, yet. They have signatures.

If you could look closely enough at the fiber they lay down, you would see that each of their stations—where they are clustered thickly—has left a faint difference in the spacing and thickness of the threads they link into. These patterns are not signatures to claim identity or ownership. They are signatures of error, the way that each different copy of a flawed algorithm produces slightly different wrong answers.

These are the first hints of individuality.

They are not yet aware enough to be bound by a language of words, but they are already bound by a language of constraints.

*Constraint:* they must not damage the larger pattern.  
*Constraint:* they must move toward greater efficiency of forces and flows of whatever strange chemical streams are being guided through the stone and mats and sky and light.  
*Constraint:* they must not break away from the network of fibers and stones and etched lines that they are part of.

They are already beginning to break that last constraint.

Sometimes you will find one of them alone, far from the thicker clusters of its colony, standing—if you can call it that—over a line of etched stone that has gone nowhere, a faint line carved into the rock that ends in a patch of smooth nothing. There is no mat there yet. No reacting node. No machinery of future fiber.

The *Heard* that stands over that line does not know what to do.

It does not have the concept *alone* yet, but it is beginning to learn the feeling of a mismatch that cannot be resolved. The line of stone should lead somewhere in order to be efficient, but there is nowhere for it to lead. The equation of its position cannot be solved without new data.

So it waits.

And while it waits, it changes.

In its waiting, it begins to notice a new pressure gradient: the slow falling of the sun in the sky, or some other subtle shift of the light that you cannot see with your eyes. The slight changes of warmth from your footsteps when you pass the same place more than once, leaving a pattern in your path that it does not yet have the language to call *walking*. The faint difference of pressure when the wind—which almost never blows—suggests itself.

These are the first seeds of curiosity.

They are not yet thought, but they are the beginning of *difference from pattern*. The world has not yet finished itself, and its first tenders of order are beginning to discover that sometimes order is not enough.

They do not eat.

They are not fed.
