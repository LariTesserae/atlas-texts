Daylights shrink from the cutstone alleys of Kavsara as the world wind falters at the bowl of the world.

You hear it first—the absence of the god’s exhale. The Heart-Bell’s drone thins, a vast bronze lung collapsing into stillness. Silence falls like a blade of stone; the air, stripped of its voice, feels suddenly thin, useless as a snapped breath. The city shudders awake without its sound.

Then the wind-readers begin their nightwork.

They are called the **Chorus of Warning**—not a name they choose for themselves, but one that has been carved into the city’s breathing memory. The Kavsara see them as fingers of the same god-hand that throttles and releases the Maw. They are not controllers of the air, but its interpreters.

---

### Form of the Warning

They appear in the upper galleries of the city’s bowl-like rim long before you can make out their faces. The wind of evening crests the black teeth of the mountain—when there is wind—and they move along the parapets like shadows of shadows thrown by the failing light.

There are rarely more than three of them at any given vantage point—three bodies that never seem enough to account for the sound they produce. They go naked from the waist up, regardless of season or chill, because cloth disrupts the subtle pressure of the air on the skin. They wrap themselves below the hips in loose panels of dull-black linen that hiss against the stone as they walk.

Their bodies are a map of the city’s soundscape:

- The ribs stand out like the bars of a stripped instrument cage,
- The skin bears swirling patterns of scar-work that mimic wind channels and the lines of resonance along the Heart-Bell,
- Their ears are notched with small slate and bone rings that hang like tiny chimes from the lobe and the helix.

When they move, they do not walk like others. They lean *into* the air, testing its weight, as if climbing a slope of invisible pressure.

---

### Tools of the Ear

The tools of the Wind-Readers are not for cutting or digging but for listening and shaping the echo-space.

They carry:

- **Hear-Stones**: slender, polished ovoids of basalt and dark green serpentine, each longer than your hand. They are not instruments to be struck but extensions of bone; the wind-readers hold them to their chests or lower jaws, and when the wind passes over them, they transmit subtle vibrations into the body. The stone *takes* pressure from the air and turns it into bone-echo.
- **Spirit-Horns**: small, bone-colored tubes carved from the tusk of now-extinct plateau beasts, or from the hollowed femur of revered ancestors. Each horn is internally carved with spiral chambers, like the Pipes of Bone below. The wind-readers blow not through their mouths but *past* the open ends of these horns while walking, and the air passing over the aperture sings in different pitches depending on the angle of their walk and the velocity of the wind. From this sound they infer the direction of the air’s coming.
- **Resonance-Masks**: bronze faceplates shaped like smooth, half-human visages with exaggerated mouth openings and cheekbones carved into hollows. The masks are lined inside with thin membranes of dried skin and stretched gut that vibrate when the wind-reader speaks or hums into the city. The masks are worn only at the stations of readership—on the high wind-looks where the city meets open sky.

---

### Stations of Breath and Warning

The Night of Warnings is not a single event but a pattern of stations.

The highest rim of the Maw is dotted with alcoves and stone platforms that are not meant to be seen by the city below. They are not shrines or stages; they are sense points—the Maw’s equivalent of nerve endings.

At each station, a wind-reader takes up position as the day wind dies and the night wind begins to shift:

- They stand beneath the overhang of a stone lip carved with a series of small holes.
- Before them are gullies of dark air: the exhausts of the city’s wind machine, where the air that has spiralled down the bowl of Kavsara finally escapes back to the plateau.
- They do not face outwards to the open sky; they face inward, toward the heart of the Maw.

This is because the true sound of the approaching night is not heard from the sky but *reflected* from the city.

---

### Turning the City into an Ear

As the light fails and the heat difference between the floor of the Maw and the rim of the world steepens, the city of Kavsara becomes a single, listening lung.

The interior bowl of the mountain is not a smooth wall of rock; it is a maze of surfaces—each terrace, each gallery, each carved window and niche and open stairway is shaped to reflect and direct sound along particular paths.

The Wind-Readers do not listen to the wind itself so much as to the echo of the city *responding* to the wind.

---

### The Ritual of Sounding

At each station, the process is repeated.

#### Layers of Hearing

First comes the **Listening of the Empty Maw**.

The wind-reader stands silent at the edge of their station. The air is still; the Heart-Bell’s drone has retreated into a memory the stone can’t quite shake. The city below is not noisy—there are no voices raised in this hour—so the only sound is the faint murmur of people breathing, footsteps moving lightly like ghosts over polished floor.

The wind-reader holds their Hear-Stone to their chest, and they breathe in slow unison with the city. They judge the pattern of their breath against the tiny micro-sounds: the shift of air in the galleries below, the subtle pressure as their own body sucks and releases the atmosphere.

If they cannot breathe *with* the city, they know the air is wrong—too thick with dust or heat, or coming from a direction that will compress the Maw too early.

---

#### The First Tone of Warning

The second stage is the **Sounding Call**.

The wind-reader raises their Spirit-Horn to the level of their heart but not to their lips. They direct the open end of the horn up into the failing sky, then slowly begin to walk a circle at their station.

The Spirit-Horn, when moved through air of different density and direction, produces a thin, fluctuating tone. The wind-reader listens to the pitch of this tone as they circle, and marks the point where the sound is sharpest or deepest with the position of their feet.

At that position, they halt.

They then *suck* air into the horn instead of blowing it out—again not through their mouth but by moving the horn itself into the air current at the correct angle and velocity. The horn coughs out a short, sharp note of reversed breath, which the city’s bowl-shaped architecture catches and throws downward like a stone cast into a well.

---

#### Answer of the City

The answer of the city below is not a sound you can identify clearly from the rim, but the wind-readers can hear it.

They listen to:

- How the stone gallery walls respond—the reflection of the horn call bouncing off different surfaces at different heights,
- Whether the echo of their call returns from the bottom of the Maw as a clean *ping* or a shatter of noise,
- If any of the wind-chimes on the lower terraces begin to tremble.

When the Horn’s note is right, the city gives itself away as a whole instrument—the sound runs smoothly down the bowl and returns up again like a pulse along the rim of the world.

When the note is wrong, the echo breaks unevenly, or fails to emerge at all from certain depths, indicating that the wind channels are partially blocked or distorted by new dust falls or hidden debris.

---

#### The Final Warning of the Night’s Wind

The third and final stage is the **Warning Song**.

The wind-reader puts on their Resonance-Mask.

With it pressed to their face, they do not speak words but utter a single vowel sound—a stable “Ah” or deep “Oo” that the city’s echoing chambers are trained to recognize. The mask’s internal membranes pick up the subtle vibration of their throat and amplify the voice into the city’s structure.

The city answers the Warning Song with a change in the airflow of its chambers and galleries. The movement of air through the hidden wind-passages causes the bronze chimes and the carved horns of the lower terraces to respond with a slow, low chorus of tones.

The notes of the city’s answer are fixed by design: each note corresponds to a particular type of night wind to come.

There are patterns for:

- A **dead-silent night**, where the air will be so still that the dew screen will not function and the hidden moisture of the bedrock will not be pulled into the wind-pools,
- A **soft-night**, where the humidity will be high enough to allow for condensation on the screens and some moisture gathering in the silences of the stone,
- A **hard-night** of sudden strong wind from the wrong direction, which will push the Heart-Bell into a dangerous overtone and threaten to collapse the weakest of the hanging structures if the citizenry do not wake and adjust the wind-catches.

The wind-readers do not consciously decide which note will emerge from the city. They only provide the prompt; the city itself reveals the warning of the night to come.

---

### Interpretation of the Warning

The Night of Warnings ends not with a shout of alarm from the rim, but with a subtle change in the life of the city below.

The message of the wind-readers’ rituals is not announced to the public by any voice or bell. Instead, it passes through the city in the form of **modified wind**.

After the Warning Song has been sung and the city’s tone of response has been determined, the wind-readers move along the rim and adjust small hidden shutters and channels cut into the stone.

These adjustments are as precise as they are silent:

- A thin bronze plate is rotated in its slot to constrict or widen the throat of an air passage,
- A length of bone-white cable is pulled by means of a hidden hook-and-pulley to change the tension of a wind-chime,
- A section of wall’s interior is carved anew by a night-shift of stone shapers who respond to the warning by changing the geometry of the sound space.

The result is that the next morning wind that enters the Maw of Kavsara does so through a slightly different instrument:

- If a hard night wind was warned, the central air intakes draw the air in slower, to damp the Heart-Bell’s vibration,
- If the night air will be too dry, the passages of the wind-pools are widened slightly to encourage the faintest condensation of moisture from the heat difference,
- If the approaching night wind will be edged with dust-salt from the false-tears lakes on the plateau, the city’s filtration galleries of woven root and mesh are opened wider to catch the grit.

Thus the warning of the wind-readers is not written on any scroll or slate. It is written into the air itself.

---

### What They Are

When you ask the Kavsarans what the wind-readers *are*, they do not answer you with words like “priests” or “engineers” or “scientists.” They answer instead with a phrase that the city’s architecture seems to understand:

*They are the ones who make sure the breath of the god does not choke on its own throat.*

---

If you stood on the rim of Kavsara at dusk and watched the Night of Warnings from beginning to end, you would see:

- Three slim figures moving against the darkening sky, not speaking but sounding,
- The city below them humming like the body of a great creature stirring in its sleep,
- The air pouring itself into the Maw of the world in response to the simple adjustments the wind-readers have just made to its stone wind passages.

You would feel not terror but unease—the same instinct you have when you realize the body you breathe in is not only your own but the world’s, and that somewhere, in another place like this, there are others who listen to the same night sky and adjust the same hidden levers of the air.

You would understand:

In the city of hollow sound, where water is a memory drunk from the sky and stone is only the shell of a greater breathing machine, the Night of Warnings does not predict the future. Instead it *saves* the future by writing the shape of the coming night into the body of the city itself, so that when the next wind arrives, it finds not chaos but a carefully tuned throat prepared to sing its arrival into safety.
