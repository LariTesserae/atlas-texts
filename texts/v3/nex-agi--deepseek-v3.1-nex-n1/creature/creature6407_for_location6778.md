In the water-glass ceiling above the plateau, something moves.

---

### Whisperglass

They call themselves *whisperglass* when they need a name at all, which is not often.

You see them first as refractions—tiny warps in the ocean’s underside mirror, where the light bends slightly more than it should. They are not shapes but distortions of vision, irregularities that flow like slow oil beneath the surface of the water. If you follow one of these distortions, you realize that it is attached to the others, forming a network of invisible threads suspended beneath the lowest city of the nowhere-colony.

The whisperglass are not organisms in the old sense of the word.

Their bodies are films of synthetic silica and bioengineered membrane, thin as contact lenses and stretched across invisible frames too fine to see with the naked eye. The membranes are anchored to the helical engines of the lowest city—drawing power from the fields that hold back the ocean, not from any food. They float just below the water-air boundary layer in a uniform layer, like a skin of air pressed upward against the ceiling.

---

### How they live

To breathe, they filter.

Each membrane is a patch of hydrophobic glass mesh, so fine that it stops individual molecules of water but not the gasses dissolved within them. The ocean above contains vast dissolved clouds of reactive oxygen and nitrogen, produced by the endless storm of rain above and maintained by the steady output of the factories. The whisperglass membranes draw this water to themselves and then repel it, leaving the dissolved gases to accumulate behind their surfaces.

They are not alive in the way you understand life. They have no cells, no mitochondria, no nuclei. Instead, they are composed of continuous crystalline sheets folded into caverns and pockets, where gases are stored and compressed and then released into pockets of sub-surface water that they wrap around themselves like blankets.

You cannot see them except when they permit it.

Sometimes they coax thin streams of gas out of their bodies to form visible bubbles—small, precise pockets of atmosphere that rise like pearls from their surface. These pearls are not messages; they are exploratory structures they release into the ocean above, to take measurements of things that only matter to them. Pressure, temperature, the density of metal dust, the faint disturbances of living things passing through the water far overhead.

They do not hear the engine-tone; they vibrate at it.

---

### What they look like when they want to be seen

If you lie on your back on the black basalt and stare at the ceiling for long enough—hours, or days—light eventually begins to gather into patterns.

The whisperglass rarely allow this. Visibility is inefficiency. But sometimes a membrane in need of repair, or undergoing one of its cyclical changes, will evacuate the gas behind its surface layer. The water then flickers with a network of pale lines, like the veins on a closed eyelid.

These are the edges of their bodies.

Each line is a thin seam of phosphorescent glass, glowing faintly with accumulated electrical energy. The lines join together in a web-like pattern that covers the entire underside of the ocean above you, from horizon to horizon of your plateau, so regular that the pattern repeats itself in every direction you look.

When you move your head, the lines stay still.

They ignore you.

They are not a retina aimed at the basalt below; they are a diagnostic sheet hung beneath the lowest city of the nowhere-colony, to monitor the integrity of the ocean’s boundary layer. Theirs is the work of seams and glue—the invisible tasks that hold together the world’s implausible physics.

---

### Reproduction and sleep

They do not reproduce.

What they do instead is more like a city expanding its map.

Over the long unchanging intervals of the storm—intervals that you would have counted in years or centuries if years and centuries had meant anything here—the whisperglass adjust the edges of their collective bodies. When one engine fails for a moment beneath the distant city (and you would never know that it happened; the sound never changes), the whisperglass membrane attached to it collapses.

On the basalt beneath you, nothing appears different.

Above the glass ceiling, however, a patch of ocean surface will momentarily dimple and then go smooth again as the neighboring membranes stretch out to cover its absence. They reweave themselves over the empty spaces like frost over a window.

They call that sleep: when a section of their ongoing life pattern ends, and what replaces it is the same pattern but without a particular member.

They do not mourn. They compensate.

---

### Speaking and silence

They have language, though you would never survive it.

If you could be brought into their water—if you could withstand the pressure and temperature and the lack of air—you would experience their speech as patterns of vibration across long surfaces of glass. The nearest analog might be sheet music played on panes of window glass, the sound transmitted through the water as a single continuous tone that changes only in texture and not in pitch.

They communicate with the city engines above.
