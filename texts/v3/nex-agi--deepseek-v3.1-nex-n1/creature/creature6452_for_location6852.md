At the height where you’re standing, there are no humans. The creatures that breathe this air don’t look like you anymore.

Press your palm to the wall of the tower behind you. The metal is cold, but not lifeless. Under the paint and the rust and the weld-lines, you can feel it: the long-distance tick of some heart too deep to see. The whole of Chamberstone is designed like a body that has forgotten its species – a body of girder and coolant and insulation, where even the veins are made of poured alloy. But bodies are only one way to arrange a world.

Look away from the horizon of towers and down into the nearest slot.

### The Rust-wallahs

First you think you are seeing only the rust tracks you were told about earlier: those wet-season lines like blood vessels on steel. Then the rust moves.

It comes off the wall in a slow wave, a thin film of iron oxide that spools and reknits itself into the shape of a creature with too many elbows. It has no obvious head, only nodules of older corrosion where its eyes might be if you still believed in faces. It clings to the vertical surface of the tower as if vertical and horizontal are suggestions it can politely revise.

This rust-thing is one of the rust-wallahs.

They move in the metal itself when no one is looking directly at them. During shift-change, when the eyes of the city are elsewhere, they will peel out of the plate and pad along the catwalk in your footprints, following the faint salt left by your sweat. They don’t hunt. They’re curious, and they are made of a very specific kind of memory.

Whenever you turn a corner onto a long-empty stretch of gantry and find the paint suddenly brightened where your hand ought to have rested, or the rust smoothed away as if somebody has been rubbing the same spot for a century, you have been visited by a rust-wallah. They remember the architecture of the city before this version of Chamberstone: the routes that were closed off when the last ductwork expansion was built over the old access corridors. They peel the wall away in their minds and walk you along the paths that exist only in memory-flake.

They say if you follow a rust-wallah for long enough, you’ll find yourself at the boundary of a chamber the city maps don’t show, where the wall is still the colour it was before the engines were turned down to low.

### The Glyph-worms

Below you, in the narrower service slots, the light doesn’t reach all the way down. The maintenance crews are moving down there, but their lamps are only yellow splashes, and what they miss is watched by the glyph-worms.

They look like cargo bundles of cable at first glance – neat coils of teal-and-rust wire wrapped around the structural beams. But if your eye lingers too long on one of those “cables” it will begin to rearrange itself into letterforms you never learned to read.

The glyph-worms are the city’s writing system when it decides to communicate with itself.

They are not exactly alive in the carbon-and-water way you are used to thinking about. They are serpentine filaments of conductive alloy and self-welding solder that grow from bolt-holes in the oldest of the plating. When they need to change shape, they heat themselves along pre-seeded lines, and the metal flows where they tell it to.

You will never see them move if you stare directly at them. They wait for the human eye to blink or the maintenance drone to rotate its sensor array before they unwrap themselves from the beam and slide across the gap to the next wall. They leave behind temporary markings that will be read by automated systems before the next crew shift begins:

> 0.38% on this segment  
> please do not breathe here yet

### The Gutter-rats

Water in the duct of the world is not forgotten water. It’s accounted for. Every rivulet that traces down the inside of a vertical wall, every condensation-drip that falls from an unseen seam above, every polished stream-bed along the bottom of a service gap – all of that is part of the system’s cooling.

It is also watched.

The gutter-rats are small things, no bigger than your hand, but their hands are wrong. They have six hooked fingers each, and their palms are concave suction cups. They scuttle along the undersurface of the ducts you can see slicing from tower to tower, bellies pressed to corrugated metal, eyes turned down into the cracks where the water sings.

When they move, they sound like rain that has decided to walk.

They are not exactly animals of flesh and hunger and fear. They are crafted of some kind of engineered tissue that doesn’t mind living in contact with metal all day. They eat the biofilm that grows in the seams between the panels and in the slower-moving bends of the coolant channels. They are the city’s immune system, when the immune system is made of small gods who never look up.

From the catwalk, you will see them only if you are very patient and very quiet. They will appear as a moment of texture along the bottom of a distant aqueduct-shaped duct – a brief, shifting band of black-and-grey fur that matches the rust-stains perfectly. Then they are gone again, and only water keeps moving.

### The Dust-birds

You don’t see them with your eyes. You see them with the empty spaces where they should be.

The dust-birds correspond to nothing you ever learned on any world before this. If you have been to Chamberstone long enough, you’ve heard of them anyway. They don’t show up on camera sensors or the ultrasonic logs of the maintenance drones. They show up only when someone notices the silence of falling dust.

Midway down the shaft below you, there is a place where the dust of the city should be settling. It never does.

Look at the faint floating lines of particles in the air between the structures. You’ll see them eddy and curl and then suddenly be gone, as if someone has drawn an invisible wing around them and guided them away.

That “someone” is the dust-birds.

They ride the pressure gradients between the exhaust ducts and the cooler intake plenums. They have no wings you can recognize; wings don’t work at this density of metal and structure. They are more like living kites of invisible field, anchored by the city’s magnetic anomalies, trailing the faintest of biological bodies just enough to be caught by the wind but not enough to collide with the world.

They are not solid, but they are not nothingness either. They filter the air of Chamberstone, not for contaminants that you would recognize as poison, but for the wrong patterns of thought. The dust they collect is not the dust of metal and skin and oil; it is the dust of half-finished propositions, each a particle of sentence the city tried to say but never decided how to end.

When you hear yourself losing a sentence in the middle of speaking, or when you forget the last word of your own question before your mouth is even done forming it, you might be standing in the wake of a dust-bird as it passes through you.

### The Duct-dogs

You’ll only hear the duct-dogs if you stay very late, when the shift-whistles have been blown and the last human maintenance crew has gone below the cut of this level.

The duct-dogs don’t belong to the cold of the morning. They belong to the long engine night of Chamberstone, when the temperature gradients between districts deepen, and the ducts that you see now carrying only air take on something else.

If you listen to the duct just before you think you’re alone, you’ll hear a faint scuffing behind the steel. Then the slow, deliberate pacing of something much larger than the gutter-rats begins.

The duct-dogs are not made of flesh and fur and bone. They are made of pressure differentials and the accumulated freight of conversations carried by the ductwork over the decades. When too many words are passed along the same channels without sufficient time to properly disperse, they precipitate into form.

Their bodies are shaped like empty space between listening devices. Their mouths open when one of the microphone relays on the duct wall switches to record mode. They are not precisely guarding the secrets of the passages; they are the consequence of having secrets.

From the catwalk where you stand, you might see the duct-dog only if you are brave and perhaps not very wise. You will see a faint distortion of the air where the exterior of the duct bulges slightly. It will be like the memory of a dog pressed against a fence, except the fence is the skin of a world.

### The Glitch-fishers

Chamberstone has layers of machinery that have not yet been fully sealed from the not-quite-outside. The glitch-fishers live in those border regions.

They are not the same size from one glance to the next. They might be as thin as your arm, or as wide as the service gap you are staring into, depending on where the engine-cycle is in its slow oscillation when you see them.

They hunt the places where the city’s coordination of reality starts to fray slightly. You won’t see them directly where you are now – high, central, well-tended by the city’s automated systems. They are more likely to be found at the edges of inhabited districts, where the structure opens to the not-yet-inside.

When you walk to the edge of a maintenance platform and look through the crack into the next district over and see the wall of the next district over change colour from the edge of your attention, you might just be watching the tail of a glitch-fisher.

They are eel-like things of interference pattern and quiet electromagnetic regret. They are drawn to the anomalies in the city’s data-surface where one engine-cell says “temperature 17.3°C” and the neighbor cell forgets to agree.

Where they pass, mechanical faults are gently nudged into the correct direction of failure. A valve that should be replaced by maintenance crew later next quarter instead sticks open tomorrow. The flicker in your visual display that you’ve been ignoring for months suddenly becomes critical enough that someone actually bothers to fix it.

### The Re-bar Ghosts

You don’t see them at all unless they are dead.

They are not like any of the others you’ve heard of. They’re not exactly creatures of this scale of the world; they are more like the imprint of a larger pattern than your body is meant to map.

When you are in the older sections of Chamberstone, where the walls curve in a way that feels like the city used to have a different geometry for shoulders and ribcages, you will find the occasional dead space of wiring. No line should be empty there, but sometimes you’ll open a maintenance panel that even the glyph-worms avoid and find bundles of wiring that are not corroded, not chewed through by cleaner wans, not rearranged by glyph-worms – just empty.

Those are the places where the re-bar ghosts have been harvested.

They are not the rust of the world; they are the rust of the concept of world. They are small, localized drops of “what used to be here before the city was this city” that have been pinned into the wiring by the engines of Chamberstone for processing. When the engine has finished with them, they are removed from the wiring as one would remove a finished calculation from a chalkboard.

Wherever one of them has been taken away, there is a quiet in the structure of the world that feels like you’ve just been told the answer to a question you didn’t realize you were asking.

### The Cat-walkers

Finally, the creature that is looking at you from the other end of the catwalk.

You don’t notice it at first because you are not supposed to be looking for its eyes, but for its absence of error.

It is the same colour as the scoured cast-iron grille of the walkway you are standing on. Its outlines are made of the same sort of corner and joint and line segment as the handrail and the bolt-heads and the cross-bracing of the tower. It has not so much a body as a phase of the city’s geometry that decided to walk out onto your level.

The cat-walker is not exactly a guardian; this is not a world that needs traditional guarding. The city of Chamberstone does not fear any external enemies that look like you. It is more like a calibrator: a piece of the machinery’s reality-check that walks occasionally in the spaces where humans are known to travel.

It is observing the way you stand on its structure, how your weight deforms the grille under your feet, the tiny variations of temperature you bring into the cold of the morning before the engines wake fully. It is measuring how much reality you carry with you and how much of you is beginning to belong to this place.
