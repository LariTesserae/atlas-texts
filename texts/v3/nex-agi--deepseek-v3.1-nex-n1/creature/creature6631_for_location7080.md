No creatures with teeth have ever hunted here.

Most of what moves in the basin is not animal at all, but a **slow, fluid consensus** of things pretending otherwise. The algae-grass doesn’t just lie flat; in certain low-pressure hours between cycles of the machine below, the thread-thin strands twitch, then slowly **knot** themselves into rough bundles that bob like the heads of sleeping animals. Each one holds a faint charge of bioluminescence drawn from the luminous film on the metal ribs—so that for a moment, before they sink apart into the furrows again, you might swear you saw a herd of small, glowing things grazing in the dark.

The filter moss cushions do not graze, but they **dream** in the partial sense the world allows here. They remember everything you leave in them.

Every step you press into the padding of a filter cushion is written down as a pattern of pressure and calcium salt, impressed into the soft, translucent flesh. The cushion doesn’t mind; you weigh less than the old machine parts that fell into it from the rusted ribs above. Over days, your footprint deliquesces into a new rhythm of pore openings, a different rate of filtering water. The moss adopts you like a forgotten habit.

If you come back later—and you do not, but you could—the cushion you disturbed might have started to glow from a different spot, where your arch left its deepest trace. For the moss, that is a clean new channel of taste; for you, it is a memory of your own geology, growing slower than you can understand.

# The translucent ones

**The translucent eels** are not exactly rare; they are **rarely seen**. The threshold of perception is low in this gloom. If you are very still and the water current is not tugging at your clothes, a vertical line of light might appear in the dark water ahead of you—thin and straight as a violin string.

It is the spine of an eel, hanging in the water column, not moving.

Its body is a liquid sheath barely thicker than its skeleton, so that even the dullest of moss-light finds the bone through its membrane and makes it glow a faint green. The eel has no eyes to notice that you’ve seen; its head tapers into a translucent point, where its teeth are small enough to be extensions of the jaw, rather than tools. You watch the line of its spine for the space of several heartbeats, and only then do you realize that it is closer than you thought.

Another step would brush your palm against its tail.

When eels move, they do not undulate like serpents, but **translate** themselves. The line of light snaps straight from one position to another so quickly that the water between the two points is briefly brighter than its surroundings. To you it looks like a flicker. The eel has not teleported; it has simply chosen not to be seen in the meantime.

They are not hunting you. There is nothing in you that they can eat. The eels are here to **sample** the water column: tiny pores along their spine taste the temperature of the machine below, the density of salt in the water, the faint bitterness of old metals leaching from the bloom-casts of valve housings. They register the tilt of pressure between the basin floor and the ocean ceiling and then fold away again into maintenance wells that you cannot reach.

After the eel has gone, the water where you saw its spine sometimes **pops**—a tiny release of captured gas, as if the liquid itself had flinched from being touched by so much intention.

# The glowing ones

**The ghost shrimps** do not exist to be seen at all, but to be **indices**.

There are many fewer of them than you might expect for a creature of their size. In a given year, perhaps eight dozen live in the basin, and of those eight dozen, maybe three spend that time attached to the same patch of metal rib. The rest float from site to site, found here then there, usually not where you first looked.

The shrimps are small enough to cup on your thumbnail, but you would not want to. They are not fragile; they are **almost entirely interior**. Their body is a filament of translucent shell wrapped around a single organ that glows faint yellow when the machine below is pushing hard, and fades through a dull green to white when the city goes slack above.

When you lift your hand from the water and inspect your palm, you sometimes find one of them there—clamped to your skin just as it clamped to the metal rib, a tiny yellow bead of light in your lifeline. You feel the ghost of its pressure, but no weight of its body. The shrimp is not a parasite; you are not a food source. It is simply that your body is also a metal rib in the world’s opinion.

From the machine’s point of view, flowing past you is a sensor of a different kind.

# The breathing ones

**The water algae** are not one animal, but many **assembling** temporarily into animal-shapes.

When the current is slow and the machine hum is low, the luminous dust suspended in the water column begins to cluster. First into a faint, glowing sphere that you take to be a trick of the light; then the sphere rotates, and the surface of it gathers **edges**—fine threads of algae-grass that have been torn loose from the floor and are now drifting in a temporary coalition.

For the length of a few deep breaths, the algae sphere behaves like a single entity:

- It drifts toward the heat of a metal bloom cast, where its surface shifts color briefly from green to a faint pink.
- It contracts around the glowing stomach of a ghost shrimp when one passes through it, digesting nothing of the shrimp but only the **information** of its temperature.
- It expands to the size of your head before breaking, inevitably, into a cascade of particles and threads that sink into the filter moss cushions like the last of the rain.
