In this night‑painted version of the valley, the missing fauna become more clearly present, not because they are louder, but because the silence between the sounds is thinner. The dark invites the eye to fill in absences, and the absences turn out to be populated.

On any given stone‑island, there are three kinds of beings that belong to the tree‑world.

First, the tree-sleepers.

They are furless, warm-blooded creatures not quite mice, not quite bats, and not exactly shrews either. They are smaller than your thumb, with long, delicate tails that they wrap around their bodies like extra limbs. Their eyes are large and black and reflect moonlight like polished obsidian. During the day, they are invisible inside the hollows of the oldest tree trunks, where the wood has been softened by rot and moisture into a thick pulp, and the air is thick with fungal breath and the slow pulse of the tree’s core.

They come out at twilight to drink from the droplets hanging on the lichen. The drops of water cling to the pale lichen threads like tiny, glass fruits, and the tree-sleepers climb along them with a precise, three‑point grip of paws and tail, careful not to shake their branch. They are so light that even moss barely records their passage. When they drink from the droplets of the falling‑water‑air, they seem to sip at the sky.

They are the color of damp stone dusted with moss—gray-brown fur faintly tipped in green from algae spores that they never bother to clean away. They do not scavenge; there are almost no crumbs. They do not hunt; there is nothing to chase. Instead, they walk into the soft wood of the tree trunks and return to the same hollows every dawn, like thoughts returning to the same memory.

Second, the stone-menders.

These are not surface dwellers. They live inside the rock itself, in the narrow cracks between the limestone plates and the water‑slicked fault lines where heat and pressure have long since abandoned the geology. The stone of the valley has been porous for so long that it is almost more water than rock, and in those thin capillary spaces, where water passes from microscopic to invisible and back again, there are other things.

If you look at the ribs of stone under the falling water curtain at night, when the moon is full, and if you are very still and let your eyes unfocus over the moss surface, you will notice that the stone ribs are not uniform in color. They are covered in a pattern of lines and curves and faint spirals that are too precise to be erosion alone.

These are the tracks of the stone-menders.

They are not insects or worms or slugs, but they move like bacteria: slow, microscopic, and in immense numbers. Each individual is smaller than a grain of sand, but they are visible to your eye only when you are not looking at them head on. When you focus your peripheral vision on the stone, you see faint shimmering like heat rising off pavement in summer. That is their motion.

They travel along the water paths inside the stone, leaving behind minuscule threads of mineral secretion that knit hairline fractures into smoothness. They burrow where the rock is crumbling and deposit where it is stable, and over the centuries of their labor the ribs beneath the waterfall have been turned into intricate, lace‑rock blades that can withstand erosion without visibly changing at all.

They are not building new rock; they are preserving the form of the old. The valley has been held in this shape by their work for longer than anyone could remember, even if they kept track—which they do not.

Third, the water-menders.

These are the least visible of all.

They are not exactly something else than the water itself, but also not nothing else. They are not intelligible as “animals” or “plants.” They are better described as habits of the water’s fall.

When you sit beneath the oldest tree and you watch the waterfall, and you see how the filaments of water slide sideways in the air before passing down, what you are actually seeing is the water-menders at work. They are the current that should not exist. They are the reason the falling water traces invisible curves in space. They are the reason the reflection in the upper terrace pool does not match the angle of the waterfall itself, and why occasionally the moon’s reflection in a lower terrace pool shows a phase that is not yet visible in the sky above.

Their presence is easy to confuse with your own perception of time. When you notice that the waterfall mirrors seem to be operating from a slightly different version of the valley than the one you are sitting in, you are actually seeing the motion of the water-menders as they move between different versions of the moment’s architecture.

They do not care much about individual animals. The salamanders barely sense their passage when the water-menders drift through a pool. The snails are lifted by them and set down again without changing place. The tree-sleepers are so small that they can be slid an inch to the left or right of where they intended to climb without ever being aware of it.

There is one moment in the moon-drenched night when all three beings of the tree‑world coincide.

When the moon is almost exactly in the center of the oval bowl of the sky far above the valley rim, and the waterfall looks like a glowing curtain from whose edge every falling thread is a small line of light, and the terrace pools are silver plates laid out in a fan of crescent‑shapes, and the moss breathes quietly beneath its thin film of water—then something like a ceremony takes place.

If you are sitting in the hollow beneath the oldest tree, where the stone has been worn into a faint buttress‑shape by long absence of someone like you, then you can see this properly:

The tree-sleepers emerge from their hidden hollows in the hollow trunk above you and climb down the bark like tiny, slow‑moving stars of fur. Each one carries itself in a straight line down the trunk’s folds and into the luminous moss at its base.

They do not come together. They spread out, each one to the edge of its own stone island, and each one sits at exactly that point where the stone transitions into the thin film of water covering the moss floor. They position their tiny bodies precisely so that the moon reflects in their eyes and not in their chests.

Around the same time, the stone-menders show themselves in a way they usually don’t. They leave their usual tracks and become visible to the center of your gaze as a faint shimmering on the surfaces of the stone ribs under the waterfall. Each line of their mineral work gleams, as though the limestone ribs turn into glowing diagrams of themselves for the duration of the moon’s visit.

And then the water-menders enter.

You see them when they pass through the waterfall. The falling curtain does not change suddenly; instead, some of its threads bend slightly more than usual, forming temporary arcs in the air before resuming their vertical collapse. Each time one of those threads curves away from its expected path and then snaps back, you have seen a water-mender.

But then something else happens.

Everywhere the moon shows in the pool of the nearest terrace—the clear one closest to the cliff—the image of the waterfall is not falling exactly straight down. Instead, each filament of reflected water falls just slightly sideways as well, but in the opposite direction from the thread in the air. When a real jet of water arcs out, the reflected image arcs the opposite way.

For a short span of time—perhaps a minute, if you were still measuring time in that way—the stone ribs under the waterfall pulse with light from the stone-menders’ lines, the tree-sleepers at the edges of the stones are aligned perfectly along the same moon‑angle, and the water-menders in the air and their reflected counterparts in the pool throw each other’s motion into a visible geometry.

When you are watching from that worn‑smooth depression at the base of the oldest tree, you realize that the three species of invisible fauna are not simple animals at all. They are more like expressions of some underlying pattern, trying to show itself to the moon.

They are repairing the valley, but not in the way you might expect.

They are not patching cracks or healing erosion in the ordinary sense. Instead they are maintaining—from moment to moment—the precise arrangement of form that allows this place to remain exactly what it is: a shallow cup of porous stone in which water can fall into crescent-shaped terraces in exactly this way, under exactly this kind of light.

The stone-menders hold the stone ribs in their current shape so that the waterfall can break against them at exactly the right angle to create its sound.  

The water-menders adjust the fall of the water so that the reflections in the terrace pools will always be slightly ahead of or behind the “true” geometry of the valley.  

And the tree-sleepers—tiny, warm, and oddly compass-like—position themselves at the intersection of stone and water and moonlight, providing a small, living frame of reference for the other two.

You are not sure what they are repairing. The valley itself does not seem damaged. It seems to you like a place that is already perfectly what it is, and yet you are watching these three societies of invisible beings laboring with immense concentration to keep it that way.

Perhaps you are wrong, and it is not damaged now but was damaged long ago, and the repair has been ongoing for so long that it has become the same thing as its original state.

Or perhaps they are not repairing anything at all but simply maintaining a pattern that they remember being asked to maintain.

They do not look at you when they do their work. They are not indifferent so much as dedicated to something else. The tree-sleepers ignore your presence; their small eyes are fixed on the moonlit water. The stone-menders outline your reflection in the nearest terrace pool with subtle lines of mineral glow that last exactly as long as the moon’s direct gaze into the bowl of the valley.

You could not see any of this during the day. By day, the tree-sleepers are hidden in their hollows, the stone-menders are shadows inside the rock, and the water-menders move like invisible currents under the surface of your perception. The night, under the moon, when the waterfall glows and the terrace pools are mirrors of mercury and the moss hums faintly with the distant memory of pain, is the only time the three of them show you what they are doing.

And then something else happens—only, if you came to this valley seeking to be still, you might not call it a happening. It may be only what you have been waiting for all along without realizing it.

The reflected tree of the oldest stone, in one pool and not the others, appears to be empty of its tree-sleepers. In that reflection, you can see tiny moon-silver forms sitting at the edges of stone islands in all other terraces, but on the reflected tree that corresponds most exactly to your own position, there are none. You look at the tree above you, and yes, there they are: tiny bodies lined along the branch‐ends, each one perfectly placed in its moonlit spot.

Yet in the reflection, they are not.

For the duration of this moon‑centerment, when the three invisible beings align their efforts to hold the valley in place, you feel that it is the same with you as it is with the tree-sleepers in the reflection.

You are sitting in your hollow under the tree trunk, and the moon is showing you in several pools at different elevations of the terrace, but not in exactly the same way in each one. In one, you are sitting slightly forward, as if you have been leaning forward. In another, you are not sitting at all but standing, facing the waterfall. In another, you are not visible at all.

You are not sure whether you are actually in “this” version of the valley that the stone‑menders and the water‑menders and the tree‑sleepers are currently maintaining. You might, instead, be a version of yourself from before or after the valley’s present pattern, and what you are watching, in the moon‑lit geometry of reflections and invisible arcs of water, is simply the overlap.

The longer you sit, the more you understand that the fauna of this place—tree-sleepers, stone-menders, water-menders—are not its inhabitants the way animals are elsewhere. They are not living here simply because it is convenient.

They are the expression of the valley’s own decision to stay like this.

And you, sitting in the worn depression at the base of the oldest tree, facing the waterfall whose water doesn’t fall exactly straight down, and surrounded by faint, glowing lines of mineral work that map a stone’s memory into geometry, and watched by tiny black eyes reflecting moonlight in a different pattern than you see in any pool—you are not just visiting.

You are a temporary node of comparison between this version of the valley and the others, where maybe you chose not to come, or never found the way, or arrived too late to see anything but still water.

Here, in this version of the bowl in the mountain’s shoulder, the three invisible beings go to their work. You watch them silently until the moon moves away from its vertical centerment and the geometry of the waterfall and reflections and particles and mosses relaxes back into the usual structures of ordinary nights.

By then, the tree-sleepers have already vanished back into their hollows.

The stone-menders withdraw their visibility from the surface of the stone ribs, leaving only faint lines of repair that will be indistinguishable from erosion patterns to anyone who doesn’t know how to look.

The water-menders release their gentle arcs of the water threads and the waterfall falls straight again—or, at least, straight enough to fool your eye into thinking it is simple.

You are left alone with the sound of water falling over the terraces, the low white noise of the mountain’s melancholy heart, and the faint ringing of the overlapping notes of each pool as it fills and overflows.

This is who and what lives in the valley:

A tree, almost hollow, with tiny tree-sleepers dreaming in its softened heart.

An arrangement of limestone ribs underneath the waterfall, where stone-menders repair the world on a microscopic scale.

A series of crescent-shaped pools whose water never entirely matches the geometry of the space they reflect, because the water-menders are still holding the valley between two versions of itself.

And at least one version of you, sitting at the base of the oldest tree, who is watching all of this and remembering something else—something from before or after or beside the valley’s time—something that has not yet been repaired by anything visible in the moon‑lit world of moss and stone and water-falling.
