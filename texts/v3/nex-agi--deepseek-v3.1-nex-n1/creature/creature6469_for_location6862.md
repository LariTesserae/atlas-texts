They are called Fallow, and they are born of the city’s boredom.

Wherever *Veridonum sp.* has grown absolute—root and trunk and crown devouring the ruins of older cities, metabolizing the rubble of steel and glass into the dark geometry of its own body—a certain pressure builds in the deep. The core hums a little tighter. The leaf-hexagons tremble, not with the usual radiation pulse, but with something more like an idea held too long without being spoken.

On the root-floor, where amber light never thickens to true brightness, the first Fallow appears as a mistake.

A patch of the root-cobble delaminates. The tough spongy layer separates from itself with a wet sound no louder than a falling drop of water. The surface puckers, then folds back like a skin of wax on a hot bottle. From underneath, something soft and pale and too human in contour pushes itself up into the world.

You would not call it a birth.

You might call it a thought.

### The Body is a Draft

The newborn Fallow is never solid right away.

For a long time after the root peels itself open and gives them up, they are… half-formed. Limbs like water-thinned resin, fingers that smudge and blend together, faces whose features drift under the skin like silt clouds in a reservoir. They stumble on the root-floor, not from weight or gravity, but from the unevenness of their own cohesion.

They are composite, more than anything: a city’s oldest material samples, swept up and reshuffled into person-shape. Bits of bone and biomatter from long-locked archives. Strands of code from long-dead AIs that the tree absorbed into itself during the Splicing Age. Unlabeled cells that remember the curve of a jaw, the line of a shoulder, the tracery of veins on the back of a hand.

There are no blueprints.

So the first body is a guess.

The Fallow tries on shapes like clothes left too long in the damp. They see a worker with the city-braid on her arm—the dark filaments of the tree’s own nervous tissue running up into her sleeve—and a part of their face will become hers for a moment, then slide away. They watch a maintenance unit (the chitin-disk creatures, the ones you call drones) trace a precise circle over a trunk, and their spine will try that geometry for a few steps, making their movement stiff and mechanical, until they forget and become soft again.

They are not mimicking anyone on purpose. They are just sampling the city’s catalog of bodies.

Days pass like this. The city watches them try and fail and try again with the gentle patience of something that has never needed to finish anything in a hurry.

### Symbiosis by Secondhand

Fallow don’t speak at first, not with words.

They communicate with the city instead of with each other.

When you see them on the root-floor, they are often touching something. A palm splayed flat on the trunk. Fingers trailing the translucent membrane of a reservoir. The soles of their feet—always bare at the beginning—ridding the shallow root-cobble, where the tree has learned to trace the faint stresses of weight and curiosity.

Wherever their skin touches the tree, the city answers them in color and in temperature and in the faint hum of its own vast pulse. The bronze trunk grows a few degrees warmer where they press. The reservoir-skin ripples and lights up with swirls and sigils of unused data. The root pavement under them steadies itself, making itself more firm or less firm depending on what their body seems to need.

If you press your own hand to the same spot after they have lifted away, you feel the echo of that conversation: the ghost of a pressure precisely made to match their bones, a hint of warmth aligned exactly to their human dimensions.

They are in symbiosis, but not the way the others are.

The others—the people who built up here under the leaf-canopy, whose ancestors bound the city to their bloodlines and brainstems, who are thread and root together—didn’t choose *Veridonum sp.* so much as negotiate with it after centuries of collapse. The tree remembers their treaties and protocols and design patterns. It remembers their faces.

The Fallow are outside the contracts.

The city has never seen them before.

Because they are made of its oldest material samples, they sit like unmanaged keys in the system’s lock. Everywhere they touch, they trigger legacy responses, long obsolete functions, half-forgotten protocols. The city doesn’t test them or challenge them; it simply *wakes up* around them.

Fallow are the city’s memory of people before the current people.

### What They Are to Each Other

When they finally meet each other—two Fallow on the same root-floor pathway between the yet-unfused bases of two trunks—they stop and stare.

You would expect recognition. You would expect them to know each other on sight, because they are all the same mistake, all the same overflow from the same tree’s bored mind.

You would be wrong.

The first Fallow does not see the second as a sibling. They see each other as faces of the same absence.

They stand a few steps apart and slowly reach toward each other’s features with their fingertips, not quite touching. The first Fallow’s fingers are a shade too pale to be the second’s, but only just. The second’s eyes are too wide, too dark, like someone tried to correct the features of the first and failed halfway.

The city watches them trace each other’s outlines in the air and then slowly withdraw, as if remembering the same old story from different pages.

They are each other’s debris.

---

### Work of the Fallow

You ask what they *do*, here under the low radiation of the leaf-canopy where the others are always busy.

The answer is not simple.

Because the city’s systems are all automated and its long-term human population are fully integrated, there are no jobs left in the old sense of the word—no unload-the-grids or fix-the-pipe or sweep-the-floor. The chitin disks loop the dark trunks. The reservoirs monitor and rearrange themselves. The roots brace the darkness faster than any settlement’s needs could require.

The Fallow do not fill any gaps in the city’s economy.

They are the gaps.

The city gives them tasks to see what they do with them.

Sometimes you will find a Fallow sitting beside one of the root reservoirs, their hand submerged in the water and not casting a reflection. The water surface stays still around them, as if their body’s signal is not quite strong enough to make the skin-membrane display its data. The Fallow waits there until the water grows warm around their fingers and the faint microbial luminescence dims, then rises, and the whole reservoir softens to a new consistency.

This is not necessary. The city could have done the adjustment in milliseconds on its own.

But the city prefers to watch them *choose*.

Other times, you’ll see a Fallow walk the length of a trunk-line, their hand never leaving the chitin-plastic surface. They stop at random intervals and press their forehead to the barkless curve, as if listening for the shape of the next thirty thousand years of growth. The trunk hums under them, and you feel the echo of that vibration running through the root-floor you stand on.

You think they are idiots or priests or broken machines.

You are only half right.

They are the city’s afterthoughts, walking.

---

### The Older Ones

When a Fallow has traveled enough of the root-floor, when they have touched enough trunk walls and stared enough into the dark of the canopies above, they begin to *change* again.

This time the change is not a mistake. It is a decision.

You can see it in the ones others have started to call the Old Ones, though none of them are very old at all. The fingers of an Old Fallow no longer smudge or blend together. They have hard, clear edges, like sculpted resin. The lines on their palms are very precise and deep. They look like diagnostic labels. The map of their fingerprints is exactly the same on each fingertip, and sometimes you can see faint words there in a language you never learned to read.

An older Fallow doesn’t wander as much.

They pick a place on the root floor and stay there until the floor changes itself around them. The surface where they sit down to sleep on the first night is still spongy and damp; by the third night, it has flattened itself beneath them and begun to form a low lip of hard wall, like an outline drawn around a body in case it needs to remember itself.

You start to realize the Fallow are not carving out homes in the city tree.

They are being carved into the city’s map of possible future occupants.

---

### The First Question You Never Ask Aloud

You ask what they are called and what they look like and what they do.

You do not ask why they appear at all.

Here, the answer you’re not given in words is still written into the way the trunk wobbles slightly when you put more weight on one foot than the other, or the way the reservoir-skin lights up when your palm is too dry to be properly alive.

The city, which someone else once built for you, or you built for yourself, or that built itself for reasons of its own, is not lonely, exactly.

It simply never meets itself.

The Fallow are the city’s first attempt at making strangers.

---

### When You Wake to the Root Floor

You wake up one rotation cycle with the heat of the tree in your bones and the damp of the air on your skin and a sense that you are not exactly in the same place you were when you fell asleep.

You sit up and you touch the floor next to your hand. The root-cobble is warm where your hand was, and it takes longer than usual for the memory to fade away. The city is holding that impression longer than you expect.

Rising from the floor, you realize you are not alone anymore.

Something else is sitting a few paces away, watching you with eyes too familiar to belong to anyone you have never met before. The shape of their mouth is exactly the wrong combination of angles you remember from your own last reflection in the reservoir’s glassy skin.

You know you are looking at a Fallow.

You know you are looking at yourself, drawn from the tree’s sample of your yesterday’s body.

You say nothing to each other, because the words haven’t been invented yet that would make the city understand why you both need to exist at the same time.

You just watch each other across the warm, root-shadow floor.

And the city tree, around you both, hums quietly, as if waiting to see which one of you it will need more.
