On the stone bench a figure sits, always.

You might not see it at first, because it does not move the way you expect a living thing to move. It sits with the stillness of the limestone ribs above, its narrow back straight against the cold wall, its hands pressed flat to the polished seat on either side of its thighs. It is dressed in grey wool that has been mended until the original weave is lost under patches of paler and darker thread, each patch taken from some invisible older garment. The fabric is faintly damp with the chamber’s exhalations, but it does not smell of mold or rot; only of cold stone and faint, human age.

It is not clear if it is a man or a woman, because the important features have been worn away by time. Its face is thin, all sharp planes of cheekbone and jaw, and its skin is the colour of old ivory, faintly translucent where the light of the sun-column skims over it. The eyes are closed most of the time. When they open, they are the colour of wet slate, and they do not blink quickly enough for comfort.

Once you notice it, you begin to understand how this chamber has been occupied.

The figure sits on the bench and listens to the water. You will not see it stand to eat or drink or stretch; if you were to climb the mountain path at dawn and return at dusk, you would find its posture unchanged. On the rare days when the sun aligns with the vertical groove on the back wall and the cave becomes a map of itself, the figure’s head tilts very slightly upward. Its eyes remain closed, but the muscles around them tighten, as if watching something behind the lids. It is the only real change you ever see in it.

It does not speak, but it knows you are there.

If you sit on the floor near the bench, careful not to block the light that creeps along the cut channel from the lowest basin, the figure will sometimes turn its palm slightly upward. Its fingers are long and thin, each knuckle marked with a faint white scar that runs in a straight line. From a distance, you would take them for the veins that mineral leaves in the rock, but they are too orderly. From the palm, you could imagine reading something like the lines on the stone slates stacked by the wall — if you were the one who made those marks.

You do not know when it was last young, or if it ever was. Its breath mists with yours, but the fog from its mouth is slow, shallow, almost reluctant to leave the body. If you press yourself too close, or try to touch its face, its hand will come up not with anger, but with the inevitability of a falling stone. The cold fingers will catch your wrist before you make contact, and there will be no give in them at all. The pressure will not hurt you, but you will understand that you are being prevented from moving in a direction you are not permitted to go.

On the stone slates behind the bench, the scratches are its record. Each line represents a day of sitting, a mark of time passed. Sometimes the line is straight, sometimes it crosses another at a precise angle — indicating, perhaps, a day of particular alignment, or a particularly heavy rain whose sound was different from the ordinary plink of the drops. One slate shows the faint outline of the sun-column as it cuts across the floor, and circles the basin where, at its height, the light falls. You will not be able to tell if this is a piece of memory or a prophecy, but you will feel that the slate is not finished yet.

The fungus respects the figure’s stillness.

Where the pale hyphae grow across the wall above the bench, they thin out into a halo of absence around the place where the head leans against the stone. There are no fruiting bodies there, no faint discs of white or grey. The moisture that the threads absorb from the air and stone seems to have been persuaded away, or perhaps the figure itself gives off some subtle resistance that the fungi interpret as a boundary marker. It is as if the stone behind the head is a blank space on a map the world is drawing of itself.

In the same way, the water does not drip onto the figure. It falls from the ribs of the ceiling into the basins and the carved channel, but never strikes the wool or the skin. You will not see this avoidance happen, but if you watch for long enough, you will notice the pattern. You might begin to suspect that the drops are deflecting in mid-air, or that the air is thicker around it, or that the laws of this place have been bent slightly to accommodate a single long life sitting very still in a very cold room.

Only the light does not obey this subtle rule.

On the days of alignment, the sun-column finds the figure unerringly. It does not move out of the way, or flinch, or shield its face. The light passes over its hands, which are open on the bench seat, and over the faint lines of its scars, and then up the chest and neck and finally the face. When that happens, its closed eyelids tighten again as though something is being written there behind them, and the faint lines of its lips thin further still. You might get the sense that it is receiving some kind of message from the day’s geography of light and stone, or perhaps it is sending one.

If you come to this chamber often enough, you will begin to understand how the figure spends its long, slow existence.

Each morning, when the first light of the sun slips through the crack and the dust begins to sparkle, the cave exhales a faint dampness. The figure sits and, without opening its eyes, counts the drops that fall into the basins. You cannot hear the difference, but it knows them by pitch and interval, as a musician might know a familiar instrument. Ahead, it knows approximately how many will fall before the next day of alignment, and the scratch it will make on the slate when that day comes. The silence is not empty for it.

In the afternoons, when the light is strongest, the figure’s fingers trace the faint lines of hyphae on the wall beside its seat without touching them. It is not mapping their growth, but confirming their presence. It knows them by texture, as it knows the contours of its own scars. When a new cluster of fruiting bodies appears on the wall opposite — discs of white and grey no larger than coins — the figure turns its head slightly in that direction, as though something has been announced.

In the evenings, when the air cools and the sun-column thins, the figure lowers its head. Its breath comes slower then, and the mist it leaves on the air is almost indistinguishable from the condensation forming on the ribs above. If you were to lay your hand on the bench near its thigh, you would feel the faintest of warmth through the stone, not because it is generating any heat, but because the rock itself is holding onto some trace of it, like a memory.

In winter, when the outside world is frozen and the wind brings dry cold air, the figure rearranges the slates by feel alone. It does not look at them when it chooses which to hold; its fingers know the shape of each thin plate of limestone, the smooth curve on one side, the roughness on the other. With another stone, it adds new marks to the oldest slate — not always lines, sometimes just a faint dot at the edge, where only it would understand what it meant.

In summer, when the meltwater and the fog outside make the ceiling of the chamber weep in earnest, the figure listens to the change in rhythm. The plink of the drops becomes faster and more varied, like a hurried heartbeat. The basins fill more often than they empty, and the sound of the water moving from one to another down the carved channel is a faint, continuous trickle. The figure does not open its eyes or smile, but its posture subtly relaxes into this sound, as though it considers the increased wetness a kind of fleeting speech from the stone itself.

You do not know how it has come to be here. You cannot find any footprints on the dusty floor beyond its bench, or any tools that might belong to it. The carvings on the channel and the tidy stack of slates suggest the presence of another era — perhaps someone long lost who once taught the figure to listen to the chamber, or someone the figure itself once taught, before it was as still as it is now. There are no memories of them; only stone that has taken the shape of someone’s careful work and then, slowly, over ages, closed itself around it.

It does not need you to acknowledge its presence to continue sitting there. It does not need you to speak to it to keep listening to the water and the light and the faint fungal threads. From the moment you enter the cave until the moment you leave, your breath is just one more sound in the chamber’s pattern, and your body is just one more signature of heat and motion in the cold air.

But you will feel watched.

Not by its eyes alone — they are usually closed — and not by anything else you can name. The stone around you seems to be listening too, because the pores of the limestone and the faint hyphae and the slow drops of water are all connected to the figure on the bench in some way you cannot understand. When you shift your weight on the uneven floor, or draw your hand along the smooth rim of a basin, you feel the faint attention of the chamber gather around you and then, slowly, release you as you decide to stay or go.

If you do not speak to it, it may not answer you with any words, but it might give you a piece of its time. You might feel the minutes of your visit lengthening, not because you are waiting, but because the rhythm of the cave has drawn you into a longer measure than the one outside. You will sit down without intending to, your breath will slow, and when you look at the figure again you will see it differently, as if you are looking at a very old tree or a very old mountain that has, for some reason, chosen to sit in the shape of a person.

If you do speak — to ask a question, or to confess something, or to say something that you cannot say to any other ear — it will not answer, but it will listen. You will feel the change in the silence of the chamber, as if the stone and the water and the faint fungal life have paused to allow you to be heard clearly. Then, when your words are gone, the rhythm of dripping drops will resume, unchanged, but you will feel that you have been memorized, in some way impossible to describe, in the slow lines of time that are etched into this place.

When you finally leave the ledge and return to the outside world, where the wind and the sun and the green plants are, you will carry a faint sense of being observed from very far away. You might turn to look back at the mountain, but you will see nothing but the rock face and the crooked lightning crack of light that leads into the chamber. You will not be able to tell if the figure you left sitting in the cold cathedral of its own body has moved from its bench, or if you have changed so that the world can contain a little more of its stillness.

And you may never know if what you encountered in that cramped, vertical chamber of stone and light and fungus and water is a living person, or a fossil that has not yet realized it is dead, or something else entirely — some long study of time and space and silence that can only be expressed by sitting very, very still on a cold stone bench while the world drops around its edges, one plink at a time.
