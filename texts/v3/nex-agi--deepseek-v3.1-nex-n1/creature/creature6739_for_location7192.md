**Klabbeth**  

Klabbeth is not young. Not in the way her ancestors measured age, but the body remembers its own calendar. Her joints ache when the pressure deepens; her fingertips tingle from the cold that has settled into them over years. Her hair is short, uneven, hacked off with a knife when it started to snag in harness buckles. The color of it—what’s left of it—isn’t a matter of pigment so much as exhaustion: dark where it hasn’t been whitened by saltwater-frost and ozone.

Her eyes are the color of rust.

Not metaphorically. The irises are layered with fine, brownish-red striations like the flaking surface of old iron. She doesn’t know if that’s something from her ancestors’ meddling with their own genomes or simply what happens to people who live long enough looking into the deep sky-sea. Either way, she sees the world differently.

Her eyesight is sharp, but the world here is vast. The platform beneath her feet is wide enough to hold a small structure of ceilings and rooms stacked like a trove of salvaged boxes, but everything else around it is distance and depth. The water-column light washes the color out of everything, so the world is reduced to gradients of brightness, and Klabbeth reads those gradients the way old sailors once read clouds.

Her clothing is layer over layer of scavenged materials. The outer shell is a slick, synthetic membrane that beads water off like it’s waiting to drown; under it are thick, padded liners quilted with uneven stitching. She’s patched them with pieces of industrial canvas, with the translucent film of old data-slabs, and in one shoulder, a triangle of something that looks suspiciously like a Roqqa-trail fragment left in a cable and then pried free.

Everything she owns is either strapped to her back or lashed to her body in a series of pouches, crates and slings. The containers are a mismatched collection of ancient weather-proof crates and the odd-shaped ceramic shells salvaged from some dead machinery station. The rope harness around her chest and thighs is made of synthetic fiber that still holds strong in spite of its obvious age, but she doesn’t trust the tech woven into it—the auto-tensioners, the shock-absorbers that are supposed to snag her if she falls. She doesn’t know if they’re listening to the world’s new rules of gravity and turbulence or to the old ones that died under the ocean sky.

So she’s overridden it in the only way she knows how: with knots.

Everywhere there are loops of cord, there are also knots of her own making. Simple loops that her ancestors used to tie down tarps and cargo, now repurposed to redistribute the strain of the harness when she climbs cables or rides the platforms as they spin through the water. The rope is old enough that its braid has been polished by her hands into a kind of soft shine.

She doesn’t talk much, because there is no one to talk to.

Her words, when they come, are hoarse from disuse. The Roqqa don’t answer language. They respond to pressure, to temperature, maybe to emotion if she lets herself believe that. The storms are not conversation partners. The city’s changing architecture is not a listener.

So Klabbeth has cultivated a quiet that goes deeper than silence.

Her mind walks not in words but in patterns. The patterns of cables and platforms, the gentle sway of a distant disc beneath her that marks her next destination. The faint changes of color in the water column that indicate coming turbulence. The way Roqqa move along structural cables before a storm—like blood cells retreating from the surface of the skin before the chill of exposure.

She is part of her people’s last generation—not because they’re gone, but because they are scattered, and because the distances between them are too wide and the water column too deep to be navigated casually. The High-Up station where she was born is not a city in the old sense of the word. It’s more of a cluster of platforms, a few hundred souls who remember how to read the old maps and maintain the last of the ancient mechanical devices that are still made to operate under air, not water.

She’s been climbing and descending and drifting across the sky-column for longer than she cares to count in years. The mission of her ancestors was to preserve the old maps, to keep the knowledge of trading routes and power stations and data vaults. That’s what she tells herself she still does.

Except she doesn’t know anymore if the maps are worth preserving.

When she studies the ancient archives projected on the scuffed-glass of a data slab, she sees the same city of the past over and over: hard angles, streets that run between towers of glass and steel, the sky open above them like a clear, dome-shaped blessing. There are shadows of people who once walked there, but the images never show their faces clearly enough to be recognizable as anything but humanoid.

Her ancestors’ purpose, deciphered from the last of the readable files, was to find those “cleared nodes” of the old network—secure vaults where critical data, or specimens, or tools were stored. If they can reach at least one of those nodes, they once believed, they could reconstruct enough of the old world to reclaim something.

She doesn’t know what that “something” is.

Still, it is a purpose to steady her steps when the world shifts beneath her feet.

Because of this, Klabbeth carries the tools of her ancestors’ mission: a small, sealed cylinder of synthetic material that holds copies of the maps—data locked in glass that no longer interacts with the world except when the rare device can be coaxed into reading it. She doesn’t understand the data itself, but she understands the images: the towers of the past, the straight lines of the streets, the markings that indicate where important nodes once lay.

Her own map is not on the data slab anymore. It’s inscribed in wounds and scars.

The backs of her hands and forearms are mapped in thin, white lines where cables and sharp edges of worn platforms caught her skin. The harness has left permanent red impressions across her shoulders and collarbone. The cold of the platform where she stands now has seeped into her bones in a way that will never leave, and when she descends too rapidly into slightly warmer pockets of water column, she feels the ridges of old pain in her joints like tidal lines.

Her muscles are lean and long, not bulky. The world of High-Up doesn’t reward brute strength. It rewards precision and timing. The strength of a body that can hold its position for a long time as the world pitches around it; the strength to wait out the strange turbulence of the water column and not be distracted by the illusion of urgency.

She doesn’t remember the last time she saw a child.

There were children in the earlier platforms of her life, but she doesn’t know what happened to them. The platforms are far away now, and the threads between them are thin and brittle. The Roqqa don’t seem to care one way or the other about human beings—unless there are too many of them, or they are in the wrong places at the wrong times.

Klabbeth doesn’t think of herself as lonely. Not in the way that she might articulate if she had language to do so. It’s more that her world has narrowed so that the only real question left is whether there is anything beyond the next platform.

The air of her platform smells of cold metal and faint ozone, and there is her own scent underneath that: sweat and the synthetic oils of the tools she uses to maintain her harness, the faint smell of the powder she puts on her hands to keep them from slipping on cables. When she exhales, the steam of her breath hangs in front of her face longer than it should, tracing out slow spirals that are then swallowed by the water’s glow.

The Roqqa that approaches near the rail is not the first she has watched closely. It’s about the length of her torso, its body made of softly overlapping arcs of light that resemble translucent discs of glass or hardened jellyfish. They don’t have faces that she can interpret as such—no eyes to speak of—but their limbs tend to move in the direction of she thinks they’re paying attention to. When it reaches out and runs the glowing tendril of itself along the rail, the metal blooms under its touch with a subtle color change.

She watches carefully.

This is not the first time she’s seen them do this. The Roqqa aren’t builders like her ancestors; they are more like pruners, or catalysts of structure. They live along the cables and platforms and pillars of the city like the nerves of the world. When they touch metal, it changes subtly. It becomes smoother, or harder, or more luminescent. The cables they frequent tend to resist bending better than others; the platforms they congregate around are less likely to collapse during turbulence.

She’s noticed something else, too.

The way they react to her presence is not with fear, but with something closer to the indifference of a storm system toward a rock. When she approaches too closely, they tend to flow around her, like water around an obstacle, rather than avoiding her entirely. They don’t show interest in repeating patterns of behavior around her body, but on the occasions where she has placed herself along the routes of their cables, they sometimes change those paths slightly afterward.

The data in her ancestors’ maps might be obsolete, but it is the only clue she has to what might still be left of them.

The next platform down is where one of the “cleared nodes” is supposed to be.

According to the maps, it should be a cylindrical vault of a data storage facility, or a repository of some kind of artifact that matters. The high platform where she was born and worked to learn the language of the sky-column was once one of the “secondary nodes.” The humans of her time weren’t supposed to be anywhere else.

But water doesn’t read maps.

The platform she is on now is not the same height as anything on the ancient blueprint. It’s not the same diameter. It has not the same function, if it ever had one in the beginning. The charts of the present are not abstract. They are written in the way the platforms drift over time, and in the patterns of the cables that have been drawn by the water’s flows and by the slow, persistent migrations of the Roqqa.

The rope that runs from the rail by her boot to the distant platform below has been altered by the world so it is not straight, but it is stable enough. She tests it with one hand and then the other, checking the knots she’s tied around the anchor point. The tension of it feels like the heartbeat of something far away.

She’s not afraid of falling.

She is afraid of not being prepared when the world decides to move.

The turbulence that is coming is not the worst she has ever seen. It’s just enough to make the platform tremble under her feet in a steady, low thrumming like a distant drum. The pressure of the water column above feels like an invisible hand pressing down on her head, and the air has become heavier with the smell of salt and static electricity.

The Roqqa are beginning to draw away from the open cables and into the thicker pillars of the platform’s structure. They aren’t fleeing; they are adjusting, like the slow closing of a valve.

She doesn’t know if she is making the right decision by leaving now.

She doesn’t know if there is “right” or “wrong” in a world where the architecture itself is being reshaped by chaos and time.

As she steps over the edge of the rail and lets herself be supported by the cable and the harness, she feels the familiar sting of the cold air through the layers of her clothing and the pressure of the world on the soles of her feet even through her boots.

The next platform is two kilometers away, and three kilometers down.

That will take time.

The world of High-Up is empty of almost everything except her, and the platforms, and the cables, and the water-column that has become the sky. But she is not empty. She carries the maps of the ancestors like a weight in her pack, and she carries the new patterns of the present deep in her bones.

Her hand is firm on the cable. The Roqqa that have not drawn away from the open structures are drifting above her now. They move like slow luminous threads of smoke or like ink dissolving in water.

They are the city’s intelligence now.

She is the anomaly.

She doesn’t know if the cleared node will be there when she reaches it. If it is, there will be fewer of the ancestors’ tools than she expects, and perhaps more of their knowledge than she can handle.

Klabbeth does not smile, but there is a set of her jaw that speaks of determination more than hope. The world of the High-Up is not built to comfort her or any human being. It never was.

It is just there.

And she is just passing through it.
