They call themselves the **Condensation Consensus**, though no single one of them has ever said the words aloud.

They are small—most of them would fit in your cupped hands—and almost invisible unless they choose not to be. Their bodies are built from what the machine discards: flaked patina, shed heat-sink foil, descaled coolant residues, and the ever-present water. Over generations, they have learned to knit these into shells, carapaces, limbs.

Viewed up close, an individual looks like a walking piece of the chamber itself: a tarnished brass crab whose joints weep slow tears; a many-legged fragment of corroded ductwork with a faintly glowing belly; a soft-bodied constructor whose skin shifts color with the temperature around it. They are not beautiful by any design. Their beauty is in how perfectly they fit the place that shaped them.

They move along the railings and beams you see, but they rarely walk where you can follow. Their paths run underneath catwalks, along condensation channels, inside hollow supports where moisture has eaten tiny worm-trails through metal. When they need to cross open space, they spin gossamer lines of coolant-strand and biofilament and swing, pendulum-like, from rib to rib. From a distance, if you caught the motion at all, you’d mistake it for loose cable swaying in an updraft.

### How They Live

Their lives are patterned on the Regulator’s breath.

When the great cylinder inhales—drawing warmth and vapor up toward the distant ceiling—the Consensus retreats into high crevices and hollows. They nest in clusters around warm pipes and heat lamps, bodies pressed to metal that thrums with rising temperature. Their shells drink the gradient; internal reservoirs shift density; memories are written as patterns of pressure and salt.

When the cylinder exhales—pushing warm damp back down into the depths—they descend. The younger ones ride drafts like airborne seeds, spreading out along new drip-lines to establish footholds. The elders remain near the heart: the hanging cylinder and its orbiting heat sinkers. There they make their most important decisions.

Home for them is not a single place but a network woven through your cathedral-machine. A favored condensation channel becomes a thoroughfare; a seam between misaligned floor plates becomes a market junction; that thicket of turquoise moss you noted becomes archive and orchard both—its stiff strands store information in cellulose patterns its tenders can read by touch and taste.

They drink what falls: dew from moss-fronds, rivulets from overhead pipes, condensate beading on their own bodies when humidity climbs too high. They eat what grows: engineered biofilms on hot surfaces; micro-thickets on induction coils; sometimes each other’s carefully cultivated symbionts after negotiation and trade.

There is no waste here that does not become someone’s tool or story or shell.

### Sense Without Sound

They do not perceive sound as you do; their world is too loud for auditory nuance.

Instead, they sense vibration through feet and joints: the low roar of heat pumps as a steady baseline hum in their bones; each drip as a distinct tap along specific veins of metal; every click of relay valves as sudden pressure changes traveling through supports. When something large moves—a maintenance crawler cleaning a new stretch of railing, say—they feel it coming minutes before you would hear anything at all.

Their communication is tactile-thermal-chemical: patterns tapped on metal or filament, shifts in body temperature used as emphasis, pheromone trails laid down in condensation channels like ink in moving water. A long debate among elders might look like nothing more than clusters of small bodies pressed together on a warm beam for days at a time while their shells cycle heat back and forth in structured waves.

The machine’s “olfaction” is literal for them. They read chemical gradients like maps: this corridor tastes of overheating insulation; that patch of moss carries news from three levels down; this railing bears traces of your sweat and skin oils from seasons ago. Their world is written in scents dissolved in water.

### Mind Made Many

No single individual holds more than fragments of knowledge or memory.

An elder carries perhaps ten years of detailed sense-impressions: every drip-line it tended, every thermal anomaly it traced to its source, every negotiation with crawlers or flies it mediated. A juvenile carries mostly instinct: follow warmth upward on inhale, moisture downward on exhale; avoid open air when drafts grow strong; cluster when temperature spikes unexpectedly.

But together they form something larger than any one body or lifetime.

When two groups meet at a junction seam or shared condensation channel, they exchange more than greetings. They press close enough for sensory organs to interlace—fine hairs dipped in shared fluid reservoirs—and their recent memories merge into consensus-history. A drought three levels down is passed upward as subtle chemical codings that change how this group allocates water storage. Your passage along certain catwalks is recorded as patterns of trace metal and salt left behind on railings where you lean.

In this way they maintain continuous awareness spanning depths below your lattice floor and heights above your sight-line into unseen ceiling workings. The Regulator’s slow breath-cycle is writ large across their distributed mind as shifts in collective behavior over weeks and months rather than seconds or minutes.
