In the pale, bone-smooth hollows of this mountain lung, the Kith have learned to live without edges.

They are small people, as compact as the chamber that holds them. Adults stand no taller than a human child, their bodies rounded and dense, built for bending beneath low fronds and crawling through root-thick tunnels. Their shoulders curve forward in a permanent, comfortable stoop; their spines have long ago accepted the ceiling as their true horizon. Skin the color of old ivory and bruise-purple moss clings tight to wiry muscle, mottled in patterns that echo the phosphorescent nodules above—irregular constellations that shift faintly with age and health.

Their faces are broad and close-set, eyes large and dark as beetle shells, pupils swallowing what little light there is. They blink slowly, deliberately; there is no sun to dazzle them, only the dim glow they cultivate on their own walls. Their noses are flat and wide, nostrils flaring with every breath as they taste the air: damp soil, warm fur, the faint cinnamon-resin of their homes.

They dress in layers of living cloth—thin membranes peeled from certain fungi-like growths or woven from hair-thin plant fibers pulled from the mats that coat every surface. The fabrics feel like soft bark or tough moss under your fingers, still faintly damp, still slowly growing at the edges until someone trims them back with a sharpened shell blade. Children go nearly naked until they are old enough to weave properly; their skin is already patterned by faint scratches from squeezing through narrow gaps.

The Kith do not think of themselves as prisoners here. They know no “outside” beyond stories whispered by elders who heard them from elders before—tales of a bright burning emptiness where water falls freely from open sky and light does not need coaxing from living things. To most, these are myths told to explain why they must be careful with every drop of moisture and every flicker of light.

Their language is soft but layered—a constant murmur beneath the hum of insects and creak of vegetation. Consonants click like chitin on stone; vowels are low and rounded in throats used to breathing thick air. They speak close to one another’s faces so words can be felt on the skin as much as heard through ears half-full with ambient noise. There are no shouts here; sound carries too well in such intimacy.

They move through their city like water finding paths it has followed for centuries. Children scamper ahead on bare feet toughened by packed soil floors and woven mats; adults follow more deliberately, often walking with one hand brushing wall or stem for balance on uneven ground—and for reassurance that everything remains where it should be.

Homes are hollowed hummocks reinforced by living latticework walls grown into deliberate shapes over generations: low domes barely taller than an adult sitting upright inside them when fully grown up from infant mounds only knee-high at first birth day celebration (they mark age by how much higher they must stoop). Entrances narrow down tight enough to squeeze through sideways—protection against larger creatures who might wander out of deeper cracks or upper canopy layers but also simply efficient use: why make big openings when bodies can adapt?
