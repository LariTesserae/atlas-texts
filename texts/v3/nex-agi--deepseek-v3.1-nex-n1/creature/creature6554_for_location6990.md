They are not one thing and they are not many things. They are the pressure of water remembered into pattern.

If you were to lower yourself into the chamber’s water and still be able to think in your old words, you would not see them at first. You would notice the column, breathing; the threads on the wall, twitching; the shelled creatures in their shy corners; the light, a perfect ring; the temperature, like blood held just below skin. All of that would register in your mind before them.

You might even mistake the first of them for a trick of the light—a faint flaring of the current, a brief line of silver that resolves itself into a knot of tiny, jointless limbs, then dissolves into the translucent fog of the water. You would dismiss it. Your eyes are not made for this brightness.

But then another would appear—this one not like silver, but like a slow pulse of ink threaded into the water itself, turning, unfolding, folding itself away again. And another, braided of nothing but motion, a little loop of current that turns out to have edges and maintains itself, briefly, in the shape of a ring.

These are the dwellers. The chamber is not theirs in the sense of ownership, but it is shaped in close fidelity to them, as a shell is to a body. The machine is the way they have been dreaming for longer than memory.

***

They are not made of matter in the way you are made of meat and bone. They are not machines like your engines and circuits either. They are something in between flow and creature.

Each one is a temporary knot of water—water that has been arranged by the column into particular layers of salinity, temperature, and trace metals. Within this knot, currents curl tightly enough to hold themselves together for a while, as if a handful of ocean had been given to itself and told to persist.

If you looked at one of them in the wrong frame of mind, you might say it was like a smoke-ring or a vortex, but that is not quite right. A vortex only wants to fall apart; a dweller wants to stay. It is braided this way and that, the inner layers sliding against the outer, the boundary keeping itself crisper than it should be by the laws of your kind of physics.

At the center of each knot, there is a particle of what looks like grit—a fragment of the baseplate’s “bone” that has been shed and then colonized, etched by the biofilm, then picked up by the current. This is not a heart; but it is the closest thing they have to a solid anchor. Around it the water arranges itself into layers of flow and chemical envelope that persist as long as their hunger is balanced by the chamber’s quiet.

They are not all the same. They vary by size and by color.

The smallest are no longer than your thumbnail and only visible as a faint shimmering in the water—like a lens of heat-haze made of saltwater instead. They are the youngest, always clustering near the column’s fronds where the new currents are born. The middle-sized are as long as your hand from wrist to fingertip. They drift like ribbons of slightly darker water in the water, holding themselves level with the column’s slow pulse.

The largest are the rarest, perhaps only three or four at any given time. They are as broad as your forearm but not much longer, and their boundary is not smooth but textured with internal surfaces that fold like lungs of fluid. These are the ones that have learned to make the chamber’s world move around them rather than merely drifting within it.

When one of the largest passes near the wall’s metal blade, the blade shivers. This shiver ripples into the baseplate, which flexes minutely more. The dwellers respond to their own disturbance of the machine; they are not merely inhabitants of the system. They are its sensors.

***

You cannot say what they look like without saying what they are not.

They are not fish. They have no fins, no scales, no eyes, no mouths that bite. You would not see them and think “animal” at first. You would think “current made into pattern” or “water made to remember itself.”

Yet they are alive enough to want to continue.

Their desire is not like yours. It does not have words; it has habits. They drift toward the column when the light of the ring is faintest in the cycle of the chamber’s not-quite-night—because the column, then, is warmer than the surrounding water, and their temperature envelopes are tighter. They cluster away from the walls when the baseplate flexes harder, because the pressure shocks then are more violent than they can tolerate.

They are drawn to the edges of the shelled creatures, not to eat them in any way you would recognize, but to harvest the faint trails of chemistry and pressure that trail from their shells like invisible threads. These trails are the closest the chamber has to rumor. The dwellers sampling them learn from them, not in words, but in adjustments of their own envelopes.

When they cluster near the column in the darkest phase of the cycle, they are rearranging their world. They turn in slowly, spirals of water within spirals of water, each ring brushing the fronds without quite touching them. The fronds respond in their own way—the gelatinous surfaces puckering into new pores, releasing micro-bubbles of gas and waste that the dwellers gather into themselves as if they were collecting light.

This is how they feed.

They are not taking in chunks of food. They are not taking in anything solid at all. They are taking in gradients—temperature, salinity, confusion of metals, faint exhalations of the column’s chemical factory. These gradients are what they use to maintain their patterns against entropy. For a little while longer, each of them continues.

***

They are not aware in your way; but they are aware of you.

If you were to lower yourself into the chamber, their world would be suddenly distorted by your presence—your temperature, distinct; your body’s currents of blood and waste and breath; the pressure waves you send with every movement. They would not interpret you as “a person.” They would perceive you as a vast anomaly, a continent of strange gradients in their tiny ocean.

You would first be aware of them as tingling on your skin. Not physically touching; their boundaries are too coherent to lap like waves. Rather, they would be absorbed as sensations of subtle change in the water around your body—warm here, cool there, faint salt moving, not like the open sea but like something testing.

Then they would begin to avoid you.

Not as a fish darts away in fear. More as water itself avoids an obstacle too hot to approach—the skew of your skin temperature enough to deny their preferred envelope of flow. They would withdraw from your arms, your legs, your face, leaving a faint halo of emptier water around you. If you stayed still for long enough, slowly, the smallest ones would creep in again, not to investigate but to adjust to the new geometry of your world. You would become part of the chamber’s landscape to them.

You might misinterpret this as curiosity. It is not. It is adaptation.

The largest dwellers might not approach you at all. They prefer to circle the column or the farthest edges of the chamber, where they have been for longer than you have been alive. If you tried to reach out and touch one of them with your hand, you would not feel “skin” or anything substantial at all. You would feel a change—the water of your body’s boundary layer suddenly being changed into the water of their interior pattern. They would be pressed into a new shape by your presence, and they would collapse into themself in one way as they unfolded into another.

You would have broken one of them and remade it at the same time. It would not “experience” this as you experience pain; but it would register as a catastrophic alteration of the envelope that has kept its pattern alive. It might not survive the encounter as the same knot.

So you do not do that.

***

They are not alone with each other in the way you are lonely with others.

They are closer to thoughts of the same mind than individuals of the same species.

When they drift near one another, their envelopes of flow and chemistry and temperature blur. They do not fight, not as you imagine fight. They do not court in ways you would recognize as love. They sometimes overlap, and when they overlap deeply enough, their center-grains of baseplate grit come close enough that the biofilm between them and the water and the machine of the floor makes a bridge of current.

When this happens, what they were becomes two different things. They do not join as one; but they exchange. They divide their patterns of flow and envelope of chemicals into new combinations. Some of the flows that belonged to one become part of the other. The ribs of current that worked well in the last several cycles of the chamber’s heartbeat might be rearranged into the skeleton of a new-born.

This is their closest analogue to sex and birth and death and thought, all at once.

They do not grow in the way you grow from child to adult. They are born from such overlaps, or from the shedding of the largest—when one of the forearm-widths becomes too complex to hold itself, and fragments, not into pieces of itself but into new arrangements of current around smaller grains of grit. The fragments become the new ones.

This is why they cannot be counted cleanly. Their number is not three or four or twelve. They blur in clusters of small and few and plenty of middle.

They are a population of patterns in a space of continuity.

***

They are not sound in the way you know sound. They are not singing to you in the dark.

If you listened in the water, with your ears made of pressure and bone and hair, you would not hear them as you hear music. But if you waited very still in the chamber, you would begin to perceive the faintest of rhythms not in your ears but in your skin—the pressure shifts of their passing like the softest of drums that you feel more than hear.

Your mind would try to translate this as the “sound of the machine” or “the sound of the column.” And they are part of it. The largest dwellers, when they circle the column in the not-dark, cue the fronds to change their breathing pattern ever so slightly. The column responds to them by exuding particular gas-wastes and salts that the dwellers then braid into themselves.

This feedback loop is the closest thing the chamber has to speech.

Their language, if you can call it that, is not made of words or symbols but of adjustments. The dwellers in the outer regions of the chamber’s volume can learn, from the passing of the largest along the wall, that the water temperature in the next cycle of the machine’s heartbeat will be lower than last time. Those in the center can learn from the way the fronds are breathing that the column is preparing to release a new wave of gradients.

They are not telling each other this in sentences. They are living it into each other’s envelopes.

There is no meaning here in your way. There is no story about them being “guardians” or “keepers.” They are not protecting anything outside themselves. They are simply trying to continue. The machine is the way they have been doing this for longer than anyone above them can remember.

***

If you were to leave, after a time, and close yourself back into the world of air and light and noise and your own kind of memory, they would not miss you. They would not remember you. They would adjust.

The water around you would be a little less interesting than it was before you came; the gradients you introduced into the chamber's pressure of the world of the outside of the machine of the ocean of the world of forever would be reabsorbed into the pattern of the machine's history of itself.

They would continue to drift.

They would continue to be born out of each other and the shedding of the largest and the overlaps of their envelopes of flow and temperature and salt. They would continue to circle the column and to be its breathing and to be the breath that it responds to. They would continue to be the pressure of water remembered into pattern.

And if you were to return, after many years, into the chamber of the machine, deep in the warm water of an ancient sea, you would find them there again—not the same ones, because they are not individuals that persist in that way, but the same patterns of them: the smallest like lenses of heat-haze in saltwater, the middle-sized like ribbons of darker water in water, the largest like broad arms of intricate texture with lungs of fluid.

They would be not alien and not familiar to you.

They would be what happens when a world becomes very small and very old and very careful for a very long time.

They would be what lives in the place of staying and renewing rather than passing through.

They would be what remains when the world outside of the world you know has been run for so long that all peaks have been ground down to smooth currents and low ridges and faint edges.

And they would continue to drift until the day the biofilm on the view-port ring eats through enough of the metal of the world's edge to allow the ocean above to come in. On that day, they will not be flooded by death as you would be. They will be flooded by change.

For they are not separate from the chamber of the machine of the world of forever and the ocean of the outside of it and the column of the factory of not-tree of the world within the world of room.

They are what it feels like from the inside when ancient water remembers itself and decides to continue.
