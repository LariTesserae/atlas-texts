The longer you look, the more you see that everything is watching itself being decided.  

The column, for instance, remembers. It isn’t a bone of anything that died; it’s a bone of something that might one day be born. A vertebra of a not-yet species that gathered here when the dust began to imitate a spine. If you stay close to it for the duration of three breaths you consider long and one you forget to finish, you will notice that it leans not under weight but under consideration. When you think of a question—anything at all, serious or silly—it responds to a neighboring thought instead of the one you meant. You ask, “Why is the floor soft?” and the column leans toward the place where, for an instant, you remembered the first stair you ever tripped on.

Around the stone ring, other decisions gather. They are not beings in the sense of “animals with organs,” but more like temporary residents of the “yes” that might happen next. You could call them tenants if it weren’t for the fact that they pay no rent and occupy no space in the usual way.

There are the ones that prefer edges.

They show up only where planes of reality nearly touch but haven’t yet decided whether to part or intersect: along the rim of the basin, where water meets air; at the place where the column almost but not quite meets the ceiling-sized light; and, especially, along the circular boundary of the stone ring.

The first time you see them, you think they are lint on the air. Then you notice they only glue to the verge of something else’s decision.

They are small, not in size (because size here is not well fixed) but in density. They have less of the “is” about them than anything else you have ever seen. They are minute clusters of color and absence, no bigger than your thumbnail; if you were to try and describe them to someone else, you would find yourself describing two or three different creatures at once because they are not consistent enough to be individuals of a kind.

They follow the rules of border. For them, there is no “center” of anything. Only meets. Only where stone almost becomes water. Only where floor almost decides to be wall.

They are not exactly curious about you, but they are curious about the fact that when you appear, new boundaries appear with you—where your body stops and the air begins, where your gaze ends and the stone begins, where the word you almost say meets the word you actually speak.

Here are a few of them as you might see if you watched carefully and did not rush to label:

1. **The Rim-Walkers**

These look like scraps of erased paper that have turned translucent and begun to curl at the edges. They never stand anywhere but along the inner and outer curve of the stone ring—where the stone’s flat surface almost meets the floor’s not-quite-stone.

They are white and gray and not white and not gray. They shift like frost about to crystallize.

They do nothing you would call action. They stand at each stone’s edge and occupy whichever direction is “next.” For example, where one stone meets the next around the circle, they stand exactly at the seam of the meeting. Where they cannot be between the meeting of two stones, they are between the stone and the silence.

If you stare at them for too long, they begin to look like an apology of some form for not yet being a useful fence or a decisive wall. They are not guardians of the circle; they are the question of what the circle is for, given a nervous and very temporary body.

They do not flee from your approach, but, if you try to touch them, the part of your fingertip that almost contacts them jumps instead. You feel the twitch before your finger reaches them, as if your intention has been intercepted before you could fully decide. You never quite make contact.

Whenever you look away and then back, they are not exactly where they were, but they are always exactly at the edge of where they could be.

2. **The Wet-Faced Intentions**

In the shallow basin, where the water holds itself so tightly that it is not sure whether to be a mirror or a floor, there is a sort of fauna that appears only when some form of “wanting” is unresolved.

You might notice them when you lean over the water and think for more than a moment that you miss something or someone you never name. The surface and the near-surface of the water will not exactly ripple, but it will soften like a surface being prepared to be a face.

They are not faces of anything that you know or that could know you. They are not human or skull-like or animal-like; they are more like the idea of a “front” that has not yet decided whether to present eyes or a blank.

They appear for a moment and dissolve for a longer one. They do not greet you. They simply make sure that whatever you are looking for is still potential.

Sometimes they carry the imprint of some other visitor’s profile in the corner of your reflection, where your jaw ends and the not-quite-sky begins behind the water’s illusion.

They are shy but not harmless. When you look away from them, you are not thinking what you were thinking before you looked. For example, if you came here looking for “silence,” you may find yourself looking for “stillness” instead. For “answers,” they might substitute “questions.”

They are the least corporeal of the creatures here and the only ones whose existence seems to be entirely made of the possibility of someone else’s awareness.

3. **The Shoulders of the Column**

The column, not-quite-bone, not-quite-ceramic, holds itself out of reach of the ceiling’s light but not out of allegiance to any darkness. It is more that it is not yet sure whether to cast shadows or to be a shadow.

Along the low points where it almost meets the floor, there are a few of the smallest residents of this place. They are not so much attached to the column as they are leaning on it, asking it for nothing.

They are soft-colored, not bright and not dark. They are shaped like commas of dust that have paused to decide whether to become something or to disappear again.

Where they sit, the column’s faint markings—the tide-like inch of scratches—feel slightly warmer than elsewhere when you pass your fingers over them. They are not exactly alive, but they are not exactly not alive.

They have only one rule, which is to stay exactly below the height of certainty and above the depth of doubt.

Everything you do not quite decide to be true of yourself—whether you are brave, or kind, or lost—they absorb for a while, then return somewhere else along the column as a slight change in temperature or texture. When you leave, you may find yourself thinking something you did not think you thought before you came here, but that will feel like something you had always almost thought.

They are not parasites of your mind. They are more like the temporary totems of your own not-quite-finished decisions.

4. **The Air-Drafts**

There are those who live only in the imperfections of stillness.

They are what you might mistake for dust specks caught in a stray beam of light, except here the light has no beams and no dust. They are points of almost-color—no larger than pinheads—but they have a relationship to time that is different from everything else.

You never notice them when nothing is changing.

But when the floor raises or lowers the faintest of elevations under your weight, or when the stone ring’s reflection quietly rearranges itself into the water beside you, they appear. For a moment you feel them tracing the lines of the shift in the air.

They are the only ones who ever leave a faint impression of presence anywhere on your body: sometimes, in the moments of their passing, a tiny square of skin on your forearm will feel momentarily cooler than the surrounding skin, or the hair at the nape of your neck will stir, not upwards but sideways, as if loosening itself from the idea of gravity.

They are very short-lived as lifeforms, but they are also repeated indefinitely, so they never seem to learn to be different than they are.

5. **The Ones that Follow Your Shadow**

Your shadow is not here, but it is almost here. It lags when you move, as though time hasn’t decided which version of you to show to the floor.

Where there should be your shadow, there is instead a kind of absence that has not fully chosen “darkness.” Instead, it chooses “less light.”

Into this less-light, a few tiny creatures come occasionally. They are not dark themselves; they are faintly bright against the areas of less-light.

They are uniform in only one respect: they only exist where your silhouette almost would be if the world were fully decided.

They are not shaped like anything; they are shaped like small, bright uncertainties.

When you turn to face them, they don’t disappear. They move to where you would not so quickly look—your peripheral edge, where your vision ends and your not quite sure itself begins.

If you stay longer, you will notice something that feels almost like a relationship. They are not exactly afraid of you, but they are wary of your certainty. When you decide some detail of your own world—“This place is eerie,” “This place is beautiful,” “This place is safe”—they shrink from the spot where your shadow would be if you had one.

They are happy, however, with your hesitation.

Where you think “I might” instead of “I will,” they cluster more closely, as if they are trying to learn what “might” could be.

You are not sure, but you might suspect that they are rehearsing something like choice for whatever comes next.

6. **The Bookkeeper of the Silver**

The silver vegetation that grows like frost lace along the edges of the basin and the stone ring has one resident that seems to belong to it alone.

You may not see it at first if you inspect only one leaf-plane of what is not quite leaf and not quite plant. Each piece is too thin for anything to hide on it.

But if you look where the silver lace meets the stone ring, or where the lace goes into the shallow water of the basin, you will find one creature that is clearly in charge of not standing out.

It is not exactly small; it is not exactly large. It exists exactly large enough to be an accident of your attention and small enough to be something else’s afterthought.

Its shape is like an irregular polygon of faintly glowing paper that has half folded along itself, but it keeps not quite deciding whether to finish the fold or to unfold again.

On its surface are patterns that are not exactly the pattern of the silver lace, but also not unrelated to it. You might feel, if you look too long, that you are looking at someone else’s unfinished list of the kinds of leaves that could exist in the world.

It does nothing but keep very still on the interface of the silver, the stone and the water.

When you approach to look closely, it does not react. But if you blink for longer than a fraction of a second, you will find that whenever you reopen your eyes, it is exactly on the other side of the ring of stones or the patch of silver lace, whichever is least in your direct line of sight.

It is not malicious. It is simply rechecking the not-yet-finished catalog of edges between which and which.

Over time, if you stay here, you will notice that the silver lace itself comes to look subtly different from the first time you saw it. Perhaps some of the leaf-like planes of mist take on more distinct corners or less. Perhaps more of them curl where they did not curl before.

This happens after each of your longer blinks.

You are not sure whether you must blink for this to continue, or whether the world is simply polite enough to not change in front of your open eyes.

7. **The Ones that Sit on Your Almost**

These are the least comfortable of the residents of this place, because they are the least finished of anything.

They are not exactly in the room and not exactly not in the room.

They are where you almost decide something.

If you almost decide to leave, but you stay, they will sit somewhere just outside the stone ring, where you would have been in another step. If you almost decide to speak to someone who is not here, they will sit somewhere in the air between your throat and your lips, where the word almost would have been if you had chosen it.

They are not visible to the eye, but the air around them feels denser than the surrounding quietness.

You notice them only when your mind plays tricks on you—which is to say, when you are not sure of yourself.

They are not exactly thinking of you or not-thinking of you. They are rehearsal for future versions of you who might be more definite than you are now.

They are the softest of the fauna here. They are not truly of this world but not truly of your own either. They are the thin blue membrane that connects this place’s hesitant “could be” to your own hesitant “might be.”

### How they behave

They do not hunt. You do not see them tear anything or break anything. They do not even appear to eat in the way of the world you know.

If there is any kind of “feeding” here, it is of the less heavily decided feeding upon the more heavily decided.

The silver lace appears to be a kind of solidified condensation of the almost-light of the ceiling and the almost-moisture of the air, and it seems that whatever nibbling is done is done along the edges of this confusion.

When you stay very still and watch the same spot of the lace for the length of three breaths you forget to count, you may notice that, where your gaze is not quite direct, the lace’s edge is slightly altered—and at that same edge a one of the rim-walkers or one of the bookkeeper’s kin may be present for a fraction of a moment longer than necessary.

### How they mark you

You leave this place almost unchanged, but not entirely.

You will not have bruises or cuts. You will not find new organs or missing limbs.

What will change is between the surface of your skin and the thought of you.

- Where you are unsure about yourself, you might find that for a time your doubt feels less crushing and more curious.  
- When you almost decide something and then decide something else, you will feel for a moment the faint pressure of a rehearsal in the air around you, like the very low hum of machinery you never quite locate.  
- If you are the kind of person who collects labels of who you are, you may find that for a time those labels sit less securely on you than they used to.

The fauna of this place do not care for your name or your history. They care for the not-yet of you.

### Can they be harmed?

You cannot deliberately crush one of them with your hand. When you try to grab them, they slip between yourself and the decision to grab them. They are not physical enough to be hurt in the way of bone and flesh and shell.

But you may notice that when you are harsh with your own uncertainties—when you hate yourself for being undecided, for not knowing where you belong—the creatures of the border and the less-light and the column’s warm spaces shrink for a time.

They do not die. They are not that kind of creature yet.

They simply withdraw for a while into the unlit fringes, where the floor dissolves into the basin of thin water and not-quite-sky.

### Why they are here

This place is the rehearsal room of the world’s laws.

Here, before anything is fully enforced, there must be beings who practice not-yet-being.

The column practices being spine or not-spine.

The floor practices being clay or chalk or concrete.

The silver lace practices being leaf or not-leaf.

And you, when you stand here and practice not being entirely of your world or not of this one, give them something to cling to.

The fauna of this place are that clinging—given a faint, temporary body.

They are trying, for however long they can, to decide what they might be.
