In the Cortical Sea, no one is born.

They condense.

They thicken out of the medium like a thought that has repeated itself too often, acquired weight, and refused to dissolve. Over spans you would call centuries they become a coherent density, a “someone” the world can notice. The process does not start with a body; it starts with a habit—a recurring pattern of pressure in the memory-fluid, a repeated way of noticing itself.

First there is only drift: faint currents that prefer certain paths, certain inclinations. Maybe it is a persistent curl around one of the memory-spores, as if the medium “likes” to remember that particular lost world. Maybe it is an attraction to the smooth flank of a buried structure, where some ancient argument once left a scar in reality.

Over time this preference gains definition. It begins to hold together when the weather changes. A color storm of meaning passes through, toying with local laws, and afterward this pattern finds it easier to return to its previous arrangement than to be rearranged. It remembers how it was.

From there it begins to acquire an inside and an outside—not as sharply as flesh and air distinguish themselves on younger worlds, but as a difference in density: what is “self” feels slightly more viscous than what is “sea.” The boundary is soft, negotiable, like smoke in water.

A self thickens.

At some point—there is no moment one can point to—this density becomes stable enough that it begins to experience time as sequence rather than mere drift. It notices before and after. It notices that when it moves in certain ways through the fluid the world responds differently—warmer here, colder there; heavier here, lighter there. Curiosity stirs.

Curiosity is the closest thing this place has to birth.

When curiosity becomes persistent enough, it draws matter from the sea into a more definite form: something roughly elongated, translucent and glassy like everything else here; something capable of slow movement through the heavy medium. The being does not break off from the world like an infant torn from a womb; rather, it gathers itself out of shared substance so gradually that for most of its early existence it cannot tell whether it is one creature or several overlapping currents.

It learns pain when it first tries to move against the faint insistence of gravity or against one of those “winds” of meaning-pressure that stagger through the depths. The world resists not with force but with reluctance—as if asking gently if this movement is really necessary. The cost of change registers as ache along its surfaces: lines of dull heat where intention rubs against memory.

It learns fear when it first encounters erasure—a region where meaning-threads thin out so much that self threatens to unravel back into undifferentiated medium. To pass through such a place feels like losing words for what one is; one’s edges blur, and for a moment there is danger of becoming just another patch of background nostalgia.

Mostly they learn stillness.

The beings who live here have no distinct names because they have no distinct species-feelings—no us versus them at their deepest layer. They are all made from the same oversaturated memory-water; they differ only in how tightly they hold their pattern together.

When they need to refer to each other (which is rare), they do so by direction and texture rather than by labels: “the colder current that lingers near the old fracture,” “the slow pulse behind me,” “the one whose surface remembers metal.”

They do not sleep in your sense but enter phases where their boundaries loosen and they allow more of their internal history to be rewritten by the surrounding sea. In these states they are less individuals than temporary thickenings in an ongoing conversation between older worlds’ memories and present intentions.
