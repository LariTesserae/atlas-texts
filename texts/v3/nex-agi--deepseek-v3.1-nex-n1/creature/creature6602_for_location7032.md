High on the plateau where the world is still being made, there is no god of stone and no god of water; there is only the Weaver of Green, and She is very busy.

### Names and Natures

They call themselves the **Condensation People**.  

They do not call themselves that where anyone else can hear. There is no one else to hear. The name lives inside their chests, like the pressure of the cloud-sea far below, or the weight of the leaves that roof their world.

The Weaver of Green has many names, but they are all whistling syllables and clicking sounds made at the back of the throat, impossible to write down. They fall like water drops into the rush of the plateau and are swallowed up. The Condensation People do not speak Her name unless the mist has closed in and the water-noise has risen to a roar that might hide their words.

### Form and Flesh

They are not tall. The plateau does not encourage height.

They are narrow-built, thin-wristed, narrow-shouldered—bodies made to slip between close trunks, under low root-arches, through the gaps in the living lattice that forms the ground of their world. Their bones are dense but light; their ribs flex more than they should, allowing them to compress through tight spaces. When a child is born, they laugh softly and say that the child is still “half-folded,” and not until the first growth-spurt does the spine firm up into something like permanence.

Their skin is pale, not from lack of sun but because the sun, when it appears, is filtered through cloud and leaf and a thousand film-thin screens of water. The light that reaches them is gentle and reflected; it leaves their skin the color of old bone, or the underside of a mushroom, crossed with faint green veins where the blood lies close to the surface.

Wrapped around their limbs and torsos are the **lichen-cloth coats**.

The coat begins as a plant. The Condensation People cultivate a thin, slow lichen that grows in sheets on the undersides of trunks and in the hollow hummocks of the plateau’s living substrate. They peel it carefully, leaving just enough to regenerate. Then they layer it onto a thin net of the plateau’s own fibrous material—somewhere between root and thread—and press it into their own body heat.

Over time, the lichen learns to expect them. The living cells stitch into the pattern of their movement, becoming thicker under pressure points, thinner where they need to bend. The coat grows with them, at the pace of moss. Old coats are ceremonially cut into pieces and given to children; the new lichen clings more easily to something that already remembers the shape of a human body.

The lichen-cloth coat never truly dries out. It filters the water from the air, drawing in moisture and passing just enough to the skin beneath. When a person moves from a drier patch to a dripping edge of the plateau, the coat swells and grows heavier, but the inside remains exactly damp enough, never soaking. The Weaver of Green gives them Her second skin.

Everyone wears these coats except the very young and the very old—and then only for comfort, not necessity. The coats are comfort too.

### Breath and Voice

The air on the plateau is heavier than it seems.

The Condensation People learn to breathe with their whole chests, from the bottom up, like filling a container to the brim. Their speech is shaped around that breath: short sentences, long spaces between, where the listener waits patiently. The water noise makes everything else impatient. If you hurry your words, you run out of air before you finish and the sound is swallowed.

Their language sounds like water: clicks are drops falling onto stone; whistles are air moving through some narrow channel in the trunk above; humming vowels are the distant generator starting up again somewhere under the green world. They rarely speak in full sentences unless the need is urgent. Instead, they say half-words and finish with a gesture—the flick of fingers toward a dripping leaf, the palm turned down and pressed flat to indicate patience, the edge of the hand pointing toward the cloud-edge where the world falls away.

### Where They Live

They do not live in the metal boxes.

The metal boxes are for **It**—the thing that hums under the plateau, the thing the Condensation People call **the Sleeper in the Stone**.

The human structures—those low, flat-roofed buildings of rusted metal and concrete—are scattered on the patches of bare rock that rise like islands from the plant-sea. They look like beetles from a distance, dark shapes against the darker stone. The Condensation People treat them with distant respect. They keep the moss off the door panels, clear the channels where water might seep into the foundations, and check the faint lines of text and symbols stencilled there long ago.

Inside the buildings are machines: dull screens that sometimes flicker with a code, pipes that run into the floor, dull lights that blink occasionally, without apparent pattern. The Sleeper in the Stone speaks through them, not in words but in pulses of color and vibration. The Condensation People learn these codes the way they learn the moods of the sky: by watching, over years.

For their own homes, they prefer the **root-hollows**.

Where the trunks of the plateau’s one great plant weave together and arch over bare stone, they form hollows beneath the living canopy—smooth-walled caves of compact root and wood, where the floor is uneven but not uncomfortable. The trees above them still breathe; water runs through hidden channels around the hollows but never into them, unless there is an overflow.

They line these spaces with thin sheets of compressed plant matter from elsewhere on the plateau—drying what little can be dried in the faint heat of their body and the rare, clear moments of sky when the cloud layer thins. They are careful not to take too much. The Weaver of Green does not like greed.

Fires are small and infrequent, kept in metal pans from the old buildings. They burn the driest plant matter they can find—compact cores of older, half-dead branches that have hardened near the center of the plateau, where the water does not reach easily. The smoke is thin and smells like crushed resin. It rises into the roots above and disappears.

### Work and Obligation

They have three kinds of work:

1. **Keeping the Sleeper Aloof**

   Every few days or weeks—according to a pattern only they know—someone must go to the buildings, check the machines, and confirm that the Sleeper has not woken. The machines are kept running automatically by some power source below the stone, but they need to keep clear of obstructions.

   They watch the screens for new colors, new symbols, new hums. If there are too many, too fast, they say that “the Sleeper is turning in its sleep.” They then perform small adjustments, turning knobs, pressing buttons that still respond to touch, redirecting wires and lines they themselves only half understand. They learned from the faded diagrams left behind by people who were once engineers.

   They believe that if they ever fail to keep the Sleeper aloof—detached from the world rather than entangled with it—something vast under the plateau will wake up and change everything. The water might reverse direction. The green might cease to weave. The stone might finally decide what form it wants to take, and the plateau might erupt into all the familiar shapes of erosion: valleys, cliffs, deep ravines. They both fear and long for that.

2. **Listening to the Green**

   The Weaver of Green does not speak in words. They listen to Her instead through changes in the sound of water falling through the canopy.

   Each morning, someone climbs with quiet ease into the latticework of trunks and branches near their hollow. The lichen-cloth coat helps them merge with the color of bark and moss. They find a place where the leaf pattern has changed slightly—new growth, or a leaf-edge turning dark—and sit there for a long time.

   They listen to the drip of water from leaf to leaf. If the pattern is too rapid or too irregular, they trace the line of water back up, through branch and trunk and leaf, to find where something has shifted. Perhaps a new branch has grown too thick and bent another, causing water to spill over in a wrong direction. They might trim carefully, not to kill but to redirect the flow.

   They also listen for the occasional high cry of the creatures that share their world—pale thin things that slip between roots and rarely rise into the light. They identify the threat of the new, or the absence of the familiar.

   If the water noise begins to change its character—from rustle to rapid patter, say, or from the steady thin song of droplets onto stone to the uneven thud of heavier drops onto hollow trunk—they interpret that as a question from the Weaver of Green: **Is something coming?**

3. **Tending the Human**

   They keep themselves alive.

   This means gathering food from the plants that are not quite the same as any familiar species: small hard nodules that grow where the trunks meet, tightly packed clusters of seeds that taste like green and metal and new rain, translucent shoots that only appear after certain shifts of moisture.

   They trade with the metal buildings when they need tools: taking from the stockpiles of wire cutters, seals, containers, and cord. They do not hoard. They mend their lichen-coats where they can and replace them where they must. They care for their children and the elderly not with ceremony but with quiet attention.

   They know very little of their own history. They remember that others were here before—more people, loud machines, voices raised above the water’s roar. They left, or faded away, or became the Sleeper in the Stone. No one is sure. The Condensation People do not worry about it. They remember the Weaver of Green and the water and the breathing canopy above; the rest slides off their minds like rain off bark.

### Beliefs and Time

Them, they believe that the world is not finished yet, and that is why they are still here.

In the beginning, according to their reckoning—which is not written in numbers but in patterns of sound—there was only stone and water. The stone was bored and did not know what to be. The water was restless and did not know where to go.

The Weaver of Green arrived when the first “plant-that-is-not-one-plant” spread its network across the plateau. She wove the stone into something with a surface, and the water into something with rhythm, and the air into something with memory.

Humans came later, from below or from elsewhere—they do not agree on that story. Some say that humans were once like the Sleeper in the Stone: powerful but dreaming, and dangerous to wake. Others insist that humans are simply a kind of moss that grew too self-aware and learned to stand on two legs.

They are not sure if they are waiting for something. The plateau might one day decide what it wants to become: a deep valley with steep sides, or a chain of mountains with sharp peaks, or simply a vast platform covered with one immense organism. They cannot imagine which is more likely.

Sometimes they speak of the **When Below**, a vague term that refers to whatever world exists beneath the cloud-sea. Some say that below is empty, and the plateau is all that remains. Others say that below is full of cities and noise and dry, thirsty air, and that the Weaver of Green has placed them here as guardians of a last reservoir of water.

Most of them don’t think about it. The here and now is enough: the sound of water, the weight of the lichen-cloth coat on their shoulders, the faint vibrations of the Sleeper in the Stone under their feet, the slow and steady weaving of the green above them.

### Why They Are Few

There are not many of them.

The plateau doesn’t need many. It is not a harsh world but it is a very definite one. There is only so much bare stone on which to rest, only so many root-hollows that will safely hold people without the Weaver of Green reclaiming them.

When they are too many in one place, the children grow sickly—not from disease but from something like the grief of the green. The leaves around them begin to drip a darker water. The sound of the droplets on the roots below changes subtly, becoming heavier, more insistent. The Condensation People interpret this as a request: **Less.**

When they are too few, this pattern reverses: the sound of water lightens. The leaves around them angle more openly toward the faint light above. The root-hollows are easier to find and more comfortable. The lichen grows thicker on their coats.

So they regulate themselves by watching the world’s signs. Families spread out. No settlement becomes too large. If a group grows too comfortable, someone makes the choice to leave and seek another edge of the plateau, another place to listen.

### Why They Rarely Meet Others

If another human being climbs from below—through whatever forgotten shafts or paths emerge from the cloud-sea—and arrives onto the plateau, they will likely never know the Condensation People are there.

The People watch strangers from within the green lattice. They see the metal boxes, the curious steps, and perhaps the wonder of someone who has never before been inside the world of a single plant-organism stretching from horizon to horizon.

They rarely choose to show themselves.

They are cautious of what might happen if the Sleeper in the Stone and the outside world become aware of one another again. They are cautious of disturbing the Weaver of Green with too many new variables.

If someone is particularly dangerous or destructive to the plant-mat—tearing at roots, scraping away bark heedlessly, trying to break open what is not meant to be broken—they will appear, one at a time, at the edges of the stranger’s vision. They will not call out. They will not threaten directly. They will simply **be there**, faint shapes in the mist, watching.

If that doesn’t work, they will become more insistent. They will redirect the water. The stranger’s path will become flooded with shallow pools that appear and vanish before they can be mapped. The sound of the droplets will change around them, becoming louder and more urgent, until the stranger’s thoughts are full of nothing else.

If they still persist, they may disappear. The plateau will accept them into its slow processes of transformation and reabsorption.

The Condensation People do not think of this as murder. They think of it as the Weaver of Green adjusting Her pattern.

### Why They Do Not See Themselves as Lonely

To outsiders, they might seem achingly alone.

They are not.

They are surrounded by a single organism that is always listening. The plants under their feet and over their heads are not inert; they respond to the pressure of their steps, the heat of their bodies, the sound of their voices.

When they walk softly, the leaves above them sometimes turn slightly, as if attending to their direction. When they are still, the water dripping around them seems to arrange into more regular patterns.

They are always inside something else’s attention.

When they speak to each other, they lower their voice not out of fear but out of intimacy—like the faint whisper of water between root and stone, something that belongs only to the one who can hear it.

### When It Rains Forever

You ask if they are tired of the rain.

The answer is no.

To them, it is not like rain falling from somewhere distant in the sky. It is more like being inside the body of something vast that is breathing slowly and evenly. The droplets are the breath.

Sometimes they lie in their root-hollows and listen to the water-noise. It is not a single sound but many thousands of tiny sounds—drop on leaf, leaf on root, root on stone, stone on root—that line up into phrases like language.

They say that the Weaver of Green is speaking to the stone and to the Sleeper beneath the stone and to the world below and above and all around them at once.

They are simply the ones who have been allowed to stand inside the sentence and hear what it is trying to become.
