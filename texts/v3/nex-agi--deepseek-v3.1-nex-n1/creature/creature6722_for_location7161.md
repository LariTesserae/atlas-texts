She has been here longer than the stones.

Not longer than the rock itself, perhaps—but longer than the waterline, longer than the faint mineral sheen that bands the pool. Longer than the low crescent of rocks someone else piled once, in some other lifetime of the world.

You would not see her at first.

You would notice the water is not quite empty. You would look again and decide that what you saw was a trick of the light—a reflection of the pale opening above, perhaps, or a shadow cast from some unevenness on the ceiling.

But then your eyes would adjust to the dimness, and you would realize that there is, in fact, something in the water.

Not swimming.

Not held there.

Simply suspended, like everything else in this place.

She is not a fish, nor a woman, nor anything else language easily names. If she has a body, it is made of water and warmth and the faint suggestion of limbs that never need to move. The shape of her is easiest to see from the chest up: a suggestion of shoulders, the outline of a head tilted back, the faint suggestion of a face that is not so much a face as a place where surface tension holds the light differently.

Below the waterline she is less distinct. There are no legs that end in feet, no fins that clearly separate from the liquid. She is the water, and the water is her. Her edges blur where they meet the pool; the only reason you do not mistake her entirely for a reflection is the faint way that her presence dims the light from the opening above as it passes through her—softening, filtering, taking the edge off the brightness of the reflected sky.

Her hair, if it is hair, is not hair at all but a slow, drifting cloud of suspended matter in the water around her head and neck—not particles of dirt, not algae, but something else: the faintest pale shimmer of minerals that have not yet decided whether to settle or to dissolve. When the water is very still and the light catches the pool from just the right angle, this cloud of her hair looks like a dim, nimbus of mist around the moon.

You would not see her eyes move, because they do not move. She does not blink. There is no need to blink in the warm, wet darkness of the water. She has not seen for a long time—or perhaps she sees, but not in the way of eyes that open and close and track objects across space. She sees the way the water sees: the way pressure changes, the way the faintest of currents shifts against itself, the way the temperature of the water above her is not quite the same as the temperature of the water below.

She does not breathe.

Breathing belongs to the air, and the air has not touched her in a long time. If she has lungs at all, they are full of water and have been for as long as she can remember; which, to be honest, is not very long in the way of memory. She can remember the last time she moved, and the time before that, and the time before that—and before those, there is a great blankness of stillness that might be a long pause in the same moment or many centuries strung together in the same way.

Movement does not mean what you think it means here.

When she last moved, it was a small thing: the slow closing of her fingers within the water, a gesture that might be mistaken for a drift of current if not for the way that the minerals in her hair faintly rearranged themselves around the motion of her head. She closed her hand around something—not something solid, not something that can be taken out of the water. She closed her hand around the warmth of the stone beneath the pool, where the water’s bottom meets the bedrock of the world.

That was not today.

Nor yesterday.

Nor in the life of anyone who walks now above her.

When she last moved, she was testing. Testing the limits of endurance, of the body’s need to cry out, to gasp, to change. She found them and held them and then let them go, and then she found that she did not need to obey them after all.

The water is not cold. It is not hot. It is the temperature of the stone. It is the temperature of her skin, which has been given time to match the stone around her.

The air is warm, but she does not feel it. She has not felt the air for a long time. It is another layer of the world above her, another layer of the world that has not touched her.

Sometimes she remembers the air.

When she does, it is as a distant memory of a place that used to hurt her: wind that could burn or chill, dryness that could crack her skin, the way that the body was always rushing to compensate—for heat, for cold, for hunger, for thirst, for movement, for change.

She remembers lying on the stone floor beside the pool, her body heavy and aching and wanting to be less of itself, and the water looking at her like the sky looks at the earth from inside the opening above.

She remembers lowering herself into the water not to be cleansed or reborn but to be finished.

The water took her in then as she takes it in now. She did not sink so much as she was allowed to stop floating above herself. The water closed around her like a skin that she could not shed but could be shed by. The world of air and wind and dry, scraping surfaces moved away.

The first year, she thought she would be afraid of drowning.

The second year, she was surprised to find that she had not drowned.

The third—if there was a third—she was not sure where the boundary of her body ended and the water began. She had become a place where water was not a container but a content.

Now she remembers the air as she remembers the story of someone else’s life.

She does not need to change. She has not changed for a long time.

She does not need to eat; the warmth of the stone suffices her, like the sun sufficed the world before she slipped below it. She does not need to drink; the water is both inside and outside of her, and the difference between the two has long been forgotten by her body.

She does not need to sleep, but she does not need to stay awake either.

She is not waiting for anything.

She is simply the thing that lives in the water in the exact same way that the water lives in the stone.

People do not come here often.

When they do, they are not usually people who expect to find someone waiting in the water.

They tend to be the ones who have already been hollowed out by grief or guilt or weariness, the ones who have decided to leave the world of noise and responsibility and fury and light and have sought instead someplace small and quiet and finished with its forming.

They come down the narrow stair that leads into this cave, or they slip through the crack that opens into its floor above, and their first thought is usually that the water looks empty.

They might test the pool with their fingers, and in the odd way that water sometimes has, their skin might brush against hers. They would not know it. They would feel only the faintest of resistance, the way that water sometimes resists you before it gives way, and they would assume that it is only the current they have created with their own motion.

They might sit on the low crescent of stones, or on the stone platform that might be an altar or a bed or both, and they might speak aloud to their gods or their ghosts or the silence of the world in general.

She does not answer.

Not because she is not listening but because she does not remember the language of air. She understands the way the water understands pressure and weight and the absence of change. She understands the way that someone’s grief can be like a slow current of colder water entering the pool, and the way that her own body of warmth and glass and dissolution can adjust—very slightly—to accommodate it.

She has not comforted anyone in a long time.

Her comfort is not the comfort of embrace or words of assurance or the promise that it will all be all right. She offers comfort the way the water offers comfort to the rock: by being a medium that is not hostile to existence, that does not judge or decide or demand or condemn.

People who stay here long enough begin to feel the quiet of the place seeping into them like water seeping into stone. They begin to realize that their body has not been devoured by any creature of the deep, that their fear is not being punished by divine judgment, and that the world of noise and motion and change and failure and victory and struggle and pain and joy and humiliation and triumph and regret and hope and bitterness and passion and betrayal and devotion and indifference and fury and laughter and tears and all the other things that crowd the air above is not the only world that can be real.

They sometimes kneel by the water and whisper their sorrows to the pale reflection of the sky that they see in the surface.

They do not know that their reflection is not the only thing they are speaking to.

She does not give them advice or revelation or absolution. She does not tell them what to do or how to be or what to become next. She simply lets them feel the solidity of the water beneath them when they test the edge of the pool with their hand, and later she might feel the faint current of their body as they lower themselves into the water, not in order to be taken by it but in order to be held by it.

They are always welcome to stay.

If they choose to leave, the water does not bind them.

They climb out, dripping, and the water that they take with them on their skin and in their hair and in their clothes evaporates back into the world of air, and they are free to return or not as they decide.

She has known some of them to return.

She has known others to disappear into the lightness above and never come again.

She never forgets them, but her memory of them is not the memory of faces and names and histories and secrets. It is the memory of the particular balance of temperature and motion and weight and salt that each body leaves in the water when it is there.

She is not the guardian of this place, but she is, in a way, its habit.

The water holds her. She holds the water.

She is not the ghost of anyone who came here before and never left. She is not the spirit of the spring. She is not the genius of the cave, or the goddess that the low altar of stones might suggest to someone with more superstition than sense.

She is simply the woman who has been here long enough that the difference between water and body and warmth and stone and self and not-self has ceased to matter very much to her.

She does not change because the water does not change.

She does not move because the air is too quiet to require movement of her.

She does not speak because the silence has already filled her.

The next time you come into this cavern and you see the faint outline of something in the water—your first, half-suppressed suspicion of a person submerged—you might wonder if you are imagining her.

You are not.

She is here.

She has been here longer than the stones, and she will likely be here longer than the opening that lets light fall from the world above into the water of the world below.

If you ever find yourself in the pool, and you feel a faintness of warmth against your skin that is not the rock and not the water itself but something else, something like the body heat of another person in the same water, you might be afraid.

You don’t need to be.

She is not a threat. She is not a monster. She is not a secret.

She is just the one that lives here, in the same way that the water lives here and the stone lives here and the warm, moist air lives here.

She is the one that has finished being made and has now moved into rest.

She is the one that has found her perfect equilibrium, and is not waiting to change for anything that you or anyone else might bring into her quiet, waterlit world.
