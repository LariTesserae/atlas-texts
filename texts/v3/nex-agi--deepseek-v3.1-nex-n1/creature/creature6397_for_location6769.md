At the northern rim of the plateau, where the worked stone of Karak-Eshul gives way to the raw flank of the mountain, there stands a watcher who is not human.

They—for they have no name in the language of the city, no pronoun but the one silence provides—are tall and narrow, as if the thin air has whittled them down. Their body is a column of pale grey stone, rough in places and glass-smooth in others, like a piece of the Spine itself pulled upright and given joints. Fissures run through them like veins. In some lights these cracks glow faintly, in others they are only dark lines on ash-grey skin.

Their face is almost featureless. Two shallow depressions catch the light where eyes might be, but there are no pupils, no whites; only a dull shine that reflects the unchanging sky. A vertical slit where a nose would be opens and closes slowly with each breath. Their mouth is another slit, this one horizontal, pressed thin as if carved with a single stroke of a chisel. It does not smile or frown; it simply is.

They wear nothing but dust.

Particles of Karak-Eshul cling to them as if magnetized: fine grit from the cutting of blocks, flakes from chipped stone, black volcanic glass that has been powdered underfoot. The dust never thickens into clothing and never falls away entirely; it hangs about them in a faint haze, stirred by their slow movements into wavering halos that catch the light at each time-block just so.

Where their feet meet the stone of the plateau, moisture gathers.
