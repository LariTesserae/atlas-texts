The maintenance engineers call them “drips,” but that word is too small for what they are. In the logbooks, they’re listed as *Condensata symbiotica*, a name chosen by someone long dead who clearly enjoyed the sound of Latin more than the sight of living things. The engineers who come up once a decade to recalibrate the regulators and flush the gutters treat them as part of the infrastructure, like valves that happen to breathe. If you live here, though, you know better. The drips are not infrastructure. They’re the reason this chamber can be lived in at all.

You don’t see them at first as individuals. You see a shifting film on every surface—a pale scum on the steel, a breathing patina on the pipes and conduits. Only when you stare at one patch do they resolve: dozens of small, sloped bodies pressed shoulder to jointed shoulder, each no bigger than your outstretched hand.

Their bodies are segmented plates of translucent chitin over softer, pulsing tissue, like porcelain armor laid over something unborn. Inside, you can see the dim glow of their nodules: faint ember-orange points that never brighten or dim. The nodules cluster along their spines and around what might be a head—a blunt wedge with no mouth you can find, only two dark pinprick eyes set deep under clear skin.

They cling by clusters of short legs tipped in fine, hookless pads. The pads secrete a faint tackiness; it leaves no visible residue on metal but lets them hang upside-down from gantries or crawl along vertical walls without effort. When they move—which is seldom—they flow rather than step: a slow wave passing through the mass as each body adjusts by half an inch and then stills again.

They breathe water straight from the air. Condensation beads on their carapaces faster than on bare steel; droplets gather along the seams between plates and vanish inward, wicked away by some internal meshwork you can’t see. They cluster thickest where the drip is heaviest: under cold junctions where condensation falls in steady threads, around trumpet-mouthed basins, along gutters where water runs thin but constant.

They aren’t eating it, not really. They’re *harvesting* it.

Their bodies drink just enough to keep their tissues from desiccating in the cold, then shunt most of what they catch into narrow channels that run beneath your feet and behind the walls—channels that shouldn’t be there according to any blueprint I’ve ever seen. If you press your ear to the floor late in a quiet shift (as quiet as this place ever gets), you can hear it: a soft *shush* under the engine thrum and machine whirr. Not water dripping anymore; water moving with purpose toward unseen tanks far below us.

I think that’s why they were allowed here—if “allowed” is even the right word for something no one admits to designing. The city needs this chamber to regulate temperature and humidity across half a dozen levels beneath us; it needs every drop wrung from warm factory breath to be sent back down as clean condensate for cooling towers and boiler feed systems.

But water doesn’t like our geometry.

Left alone in this much metal and cold, it films every surface evenly; it beads and falls where gravity wants rather than where efficiency demands; it rusts joints we can’t afford to lose; it seeps into housings and shorts connections we need intact. The drips fix that.

They are shaped coaxers.
