On Túrnakmol you learn quickly that nothing belongs only to itself. The moss is half lichen, half fungus, half algae; the trees are more root than trunk; the seabirds move in currents that are both air and water. Only one creature here insists on being singular.

They call themselves the Keepers of the Still Point. You will never learn their true name, if they ever had one. They are the ones who laid the boardwalks and painted the orange-red cairns, who maintain the rusting rail and the harbour station bolted to the cliff. They live in the ridge between the two summit domes, in a low complex of stone and glass dug into the saddle so that only its spine shows above the peat.

From a distance, their settlement looks like another outcrop of basalt vertebrae. Up close you see it is all angles and joints: walls made of dressed columns fitted together without mortar, roofs sloped just enough to shed water into hidden cisterns. The windows are small and triple-glazed against the wind. Condensation beads endlessly on their inner faces; someone is always wiping them clear with a cloth that leaves faint grey spirals.

There are never many of them — perhaps thirty at any given time — but their presence feels larger than their number. They dress in layers of oiled wool and canvas, colours chosen to disappear into rock and moss: charcoal, iron-grey, deep peat-brown. Their hands are always slightly damp, their cuticles softened by constant moisture, their knuckles reddened from cold. Even indoors you see them flex their fingers unconsciously, as if trying to wring out a memory of warmth.

They speak softly and infrequently. Their language has no polite small talk about weather because weather is not a topic here; it is a condition of being. When they say “the sea is rising,” they do not mean it as metaphor or future projection; they mean that today’s high water will reach another millimetre up the tide gauge bolted to the harbour wall.

Most of them keep their hair short or tied back tightly from their faces; loose strands would plaster themselves across cheeks and lips within minutes outdoors. Their eyes are light-coloured — grey or green or faded blue — widened slightly from years of staring into fog and low cloud. You notice how often they pause on walkways or at doors to look out at nothing in particular: a blank wall of vapour, a smear of dark rock emerging for ten seconds before disappearing again. They are not restless; they have simply learned to take what glimpses they can.

They move differently from visitors: with slow precision rather than caution. Visitors clomp and slip and curse under their breath; Keepers place each foot with an almost meditative deliberation, never fighting the slick wood or moss-slick stone but riding it like a current. They never run unless something has gone seriously wrong.

You learn quickly not to ask where they’re “from.” The question confuses them more than it offends. A few were born here — a startling fact when you first hear it — but most arrived at some point in adulthood on supply ships whose names they no longer remember clearly. Some mutter about previous lives on continents or tankers or research stations elsewhere; others simply say “I came up out of the sea one day,” as if they were seabirds hauling themselves onto land for good.

Time works differently for them than for you. They measure it in equipment cycles: generator overhauls, beacon bulb replacements, corrosion inspections along the cliff ladders. Calendars exist on clipboards behind glass doors; pages curl gently despite dehumidifiers humming day and night. Birthdays are small affairs: extra rations of tea or chocolate if stores allow it; someone might read aloud from one of three battered books shared between them all.

Their days follow tidal patterns more rigidly than any clock could impose:

Before dawn someone walks alone along high paths above surf-noise so constant it becomes silence after ten minutes’ exposure—a kind of auditory snow-blindness—to check anemometers and rain gauges bolted into outcrops where spray lashes even when waves appear calm below.

By false dawn others gather in dim rooms lit by humming strips while instruments print lines across graph paper like seismographs recording some distant catastrophe forever occurring just beyond horizon’s edge.
