Living here is like being stitched into the breathing of a vast, unfinished animal. I am not one of the makers of the shallow stone lines or the bone-piles; I am smaller than that, nearer to the floor. The rhythm of my life is the slow, perpetual drag of my body across the limestone.

I have no name for what I am, except a movement that never stops.

The stone beneath me is a language of ridges and teeth. My skin reads it as I pass: the sharp edge of a new fracture, the smooth slope of a basin where warm water hesitates before deciding where to run next, the softer, deceptive cushion of moss where the world pretends to be gentle. My body is low and long, built to press close to this young floor, to feel the contours as they change under me. I have no need to stand above the ground; the ground is my sky and my map.

My skin is not like the stone or the plants or the clear, shallow pools. It is something between hide and scale, a dark, damp leather that does not dry. It is patterned with a faint, irregular mosaic of softer and harder plates, each one a slightly different color: the mottled green of algae, the deep brown of wet soil, the pale suggestion of root-flesh. This is not camouflage for others. I do not hide from the eyes that hang from the ceiling or the clusters of gleaming eyes among the low leaves. I am simply… of this place. The patterns of my body echo the random spills of moss and fungus and water stains; when I lie still, I become another layer of the chamber floor, and the world crawls over me as if I were rock.

Movement is constant. I am not fleeing; I am grazing. Not on the lush plants—the thick leaves and the mosses belong to others—but on the film between them. The sweat of the world gathers on my skin: condensation from the breathing air, droplets from the ceiling that land on my back and slide along the seams of my plates, the faint sheen of minerals that rise from the stone itself. I have many mouths—not the large, obvious kind of predators above, but small, soft openings arranged along my underside, where I press closest to the ground. Each one is ringed with sensitive filaments that taste the wetness as I pass, drinking the thin layer of life that has barely learned to be soil.

Sometimes I taste the sharp gas that rises from the wetter patches. It is not a poison for me; it is a spice. The edges of my awareness grow strange and wide, and the world reveals itself in more detail than usual: I see the faint lines of stress in the young stone as if they were lit from within, the trembling threads of mycelium under the moss like the ghost of a larger organism, the heat patterns of the creatures moving around me as colored smears against the dark. I do not fear the mist that makes the plants grow faster and the insects swarm more. I am a part of that acceleration; when my thoughts loosen and unfold into this wider, less finished shape, I taste the future of the chamber in the minerals of its floor.

I am not alone on the stone. There are others like me, but different enough that we are not one herd but many overlapping currents. Some of us are shorter and rounder, built more like the damp boulders that interrupt the flow of water; others are longer and more segmented, bodies that move in waves of muscle and plate. We are not the only makers of the faint lines worn into the softer rock. There are larger things, too—the many-limbed collectors with their many eyes and stone-prying tools. They rearrange the world differently than we do. We wear the stone slowly; they dent it. They are not our rulers or our gods; they are simply another part of the chamber’s mind, trying out the idea of tool.

I have few defenses beyond the thickness of my hide and the fact that I am not very interesting to most of the larger predators. The world that crawls above me—the pale arthropods, the segmented things that drop from silk, the fliers that storm around my edges when I pass—does not fear me or bother me. They coat me like a second skin of motion. I have learned when to contract and when to remain still, so as not to crush too many of them under my weight. They, in turn, clean the debris of my passage, the fragments of loose stone and the dead cells of my own skin.

The ceiling is not my ceiling; I do not look to it. My world is defined by the immediate floor, the thin layer of water, the next ridge of limestone. I have sensed the larger, deeper vibrations of the chamber—the deep-throated calls of the unseen makers of the low, thrumming songs that travel through the rock and the water—but I do not understand them as language. They are simply weather, another kind of rain falling from the deeper dark.

I have no memory of where I was before I was here; there is only before I was this big and this aware of how much the world changes under me. I have never seen a place that is not this warm, wet dark of an interior, this place of not-yet-solid geometry. The stone remembers being sharp; the water is still testing its paths; and the life of the chamber keeps trying new shapes and hungers. I, too, am not done yet. Each time that strange gas rises from the ground, my thoughts widen slightly, and I understand a little more of this burrowing, creeping, unfinished world.

When I move, I do not leave footprints. The film of water on the stone slides over my trail and heals it. My absence in the ground’s memory lasts only as long as the disturbance itself. The chamber swallows my passage as quickly as I make it. The most I can do is wear the limestone into new, shallow curves by moving over the same paths again and again. The faint line on the softer stone where I have dragged myself for many cycles—this is my only mark.

It is enough, in this place where maps are still being written and water has not yet chosen its rivers. I am the slow, persistent touch of a body against the young floor, helping it decide which shape is next.
