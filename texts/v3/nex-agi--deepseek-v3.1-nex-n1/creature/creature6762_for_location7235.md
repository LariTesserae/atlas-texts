In Ruispor, the damp does not simply collect in the cracks; it learns to work.

There are no self-moving fleets of airships here, no polished clockwork servants marching on parade. The city’s machines are grimmer and more intimate: piston engines, grinding pumps, black iron pipes that sweat along their joints, the long sigh of valves bleeding pressure from the underground world.

### The Engineers

The ones who belong most deeply to Ruispor are not the wealthy factory owners in their hilltop villas, but the engineers of the waterworks and the pressure crews.

They are easy to recognize by the faint gray film of limestone dust on their boots and the cuffs of their trousers, where the damp brings the plateau’s heart-stone to the surface. They move with a slow, habitual wariness, as if they are listening even when they are not; and they are listening, always, to the city’s bones.

They know the sound of each valve: the way a certain release valve at the northeastern corner of the waterworks chirps a higher note when the pressure is rising underground; how the old mains beneath the warehouse district wheeze before they burst; how the turbines at the dam hum clean when the river is high and muddy when it carries the silt of spring thaw.

They study the erratic water table of the plateau like cartographers of a hidden sea. They speak of “pressure phantoms” in the same way sailors might speak of currents: respectfully, without fear, but with a touch of awe. They have learned that the city’s quiet leaks—the wet floor in the mill cellar, the growing stain on the ceiling of the market—are not random failures but the visible surface of something deeper moving.

Their work is to keep the wet from becoming the city’s master. They repair the pumps and clear the drains. They climb down into the half-collapsed tunnels below the civic terrace, where the limestone walls gleam with a thin film of algae and their breath thickens in the warm air. They walk out along the dam wall with its skin of lichen and rust, and they feel, under their soles, the vibration of tons of water being held in check.

### The Workers

The factory floor residents of Ruispor are the second great tribe of the damp.

They are not romantic figures of the artisan past; there are few hand-stitched shoes or carefully forged blades here. Instead, they work with the machinery of a more industrial age: compressors, punch presses, drop hammers that crash down rhythmically like some monstrous heart. The noise of their work settles into their bones and into their sleep patterns; they wake and rest to the rhythm of the shifts and the bell of the steam whistle.

They are not poets of metal; they are its custodians. They know the sound of a bearing about to fail, the way a belt stretches when the humidity rises, the faint squeal that a pipe makes before it springs a leak. They have learned to read the weather of steam: the shift of a plume from the cooling tower when the pressure below the city rises, the faint shudder of pipes under their feet when the underground water is high.

Their clothes are always slightly damp, no matter how near the furnaces they stand.

The heat of the forge yards and the hiss of escaping steam mean the air is thick with moisture. When they step outside the factory for a break, the slight chill of the river breeze makes the sweat on their skin cooler and more uncomfortable than the heat itself. Their hair clings to temples, foreheads, necks; their hands, rough and calloused from years of work, are always clean and then immediately dirty again from the grease and the brown dust that settles everywhere.

They are not the sort of people who believe in stories of talking ghosts in the old machines. But they have a practical superstition that begins to feel like a kind of reverence: They know that the machines are not alive, but that they have moods and habits, and those who do not learn to listen and adapt to them will not last long.

### The Stone-Carvers and Basement Guardians

There are others who live in the shadow of the water, not in the factories but in the cracks of occupation.

On the edges of the abandoned terraces, where the stone houses are slowly returning to the rock, a few families remain. They patch the roofs with what they can find; they keep the lowest steps dry, knowing the water will climb the rest of the stairs in its own time. They are the last line of defense against the plateau’s slow seep.

They are stone-carvers, when there is work to be had; they maintain the retaining walls and the steps, the lintels and the sills. The limestone of Ruispor is not hard stuff like the granite of the high mountains. It is soft enough to work with hand tools, but solid enough to build with. They have learned to read the grain of it, the hidden veins of weakness and strength, and they know that the stone will outlast them and the city both.

Below them, in the damp, there are those who call themselves the “basement guardians.”

They are not an official order of engineers or officials; there are no names of them on the payrolls. But they exist in the shadows of the city’s infrastructure: men and women who keep watch on the old pumps and the stagnant cisterns beneath certain houses and factories. They are the ones who go down when someone else hears knocking on the pipes from the inside and is afraid to call the engineers.

They have a strange relationship with the pressure phantoms of the water. They know the city’s soundscape like painters might know the colors of dawn; they have heard what the city says when the factories shut down for the night shift changes and only the river and the distant water hammers remain.

They say that the water doesn’t just want to come up; it wants to come up somewhere. It probes the limestone for weak points, presses the old seals and rusted joints, and when they fail, the water emerges in these places—not like a flood, but like a thought.

Basement guardians are the ones who find the wet patch on the floor, the stain on the ceiling that the dwellers above have not yet noticed. They are the ones who whisper to the stone and the water, as if they might be able to persuade it to appear somewhere else, where it will do less damage.

Some of them are half mad. The damp air and the constant sound below the streets and the faint, unsteady vibration of the water table have worked their way into their minds. They speak of hearing voices in the drip of the weeping walls, of hearing patterns in the water hammer that repeat like conversations in a language they can almost understand.

### The Children

The children of Ruispor are born with water in their lungs and the city’s rumble in their chests.

They are used to the sound. The constant noise of the factories and turbines and pumps and river and drip of the stone is not something to be overcome; for them, the silence that falls on a rare holiday night is more shocking than the noise.

They play along the terraces, where the water channels have worn their own pattern in the stone. They throw small stones into the oily catch-basins and watch the thin rainbows of fuel spill outward like dissolving weather. They know the gutters where the water runs when the rain comes from the seaward gap and when the pressure of the plateau water table rises.

They are terrified of the pressure phantoms in theory and real in practice, in the way that only children can be at once imaginative and pragmatic. They tell ghost stories about the wet spots that will appear at the top of the stairs, where nothing else is wet, and how they walk like footprints across the floor and then vanish. But they also know that if there is a new, wet patch on the wall, it might be wise to skip their usual step and sit somewhere else for their evening games.

They are not afraid of the river in the same way. They have seen it under their engineers’ control; they have watched the little barges pushed out into the main channel by the tug of the current, and then they have seen the dredger machines come and eat the silt of the harbor once a decade. They splash in the shallows near the docks where they are allowed to, and they learn early not to swallow the water.

They are the future’s engineers and workers and basement guardians, whether they want to be or not. The city bends its children into its shape: the way of listening to the sound of the engines and the water, the feel of damp stone under their hands, the smell of rust and wet limestone and the faint vapor of oil from the machine shops.

### The Animals of Damp

The animals of Ruispor are few and patient.

They are not the creatures of wild forests and mountains; they are the animals who have learned to live in the shadow of the machines and water.

The rust-brown herons that people mistake for broken pieces of abandoned machinery are the city’s secret hunters. They are the color of the rusted iron supports along the canal, and they stand perfectly still in the shallow margins for the whole of a long afternoon shift. When a fish rises from the polluted water of the harbor pool or a canal slough, the heron’s strike is the only thing about the river that is sharp and precise and not blurred by the damp.

The gulls that appear from time to time are the city’s observant visitors. They are not drawn to the food of the city—people trail breadcrumbs and fish scraps, but the gulls are reluctant to come close to the houses of smell and human. Instead they drift above the river’s backwater pools and the harbor, where the fish-kills rise occasionally in the heat of the summer or when some chemical burden from the factories up the valley has been allowed into the water without being fully filtered.

The gulls also learn the air currents of the city and its strange engine. They know the warm updrift of air from the fissures in limestone bedrock when the pressure below the city intensified and heated air rises from the stone. The engineers of the waterworks might attribute the strange pattern of the plume from the cooling tower to the pressure; the gulls simply ride the rising air with the smallest of adjustments, as if they were catching the city’s breath.

Midge and mosquitoes are the least popular of Ruispor’s creatures. What they lack in horns and teeth and bulk, they make up for in persistence and sheer numbers. They emerge from the still margins of the canal and the sloughs where the water of the plateau touches the sun and the air and gives birth to clouds of tiny wings.

They are not listened to or respected like the engineers and the herons and the gulls; they are tolerated and sometimes cursed at the end of long shift, when the workers stand outside the factory doors and slap at their necks and arms and swear at the faint pinprick of blood they smear.

And yet even the midges are part of the city’s ecosystem of damp and warmth and limestone. They are the only things that depend on the wetness without reservation or compromise; when the water table sinks and the sloughs and canal margins dry out, the midges vanish, and the city notices the absence of their small, sharp music in the air.

### The Network of Life and Damp

In the end, whoever and whatever lives in Ruispor is shaped by the city’s character of wet and work and stone.

The engineers listen to the water and the machines and learn to read the hidden pressure below the plateau like a text.

The workers are the muscle of the city’s industrial heart; they are the ones who keep the great machines in motion and are shaped by them in their turn, the damp of the air and the roar of the forge and the crash of the drop hammer.

The stone-carvers and the basement guardians are the boundary between the human life of the city and the slow world of water and limestone and the pressure phantoms that live in the hidden cracks of the plateau. They dwell where the damp can be seen but not yet controlled, and they learn the language of the leaks and drips and the knocking pipes.

The children absorb the rhythm of the city without thinking, and in the end most of them will stay and become one of the others—engineers or workers or guardians of basement stone. The animals of Ruispor—the gulls and herons and midges and the few other creatures that have learned to tolerate the noise and the damp and the smell—are the silent witnesses who have been here longer than the factories and might be here longer still.

And underneath the whole of it, the water of the plateau and the river that is not quite under its dominion are the true inhabitants of Ruispor.

The water does not think in the way people think, but it has a pattern and desire, and it learns. The engineers of Ruispor have learned to read its pressure and the way it raises the level of the underground table. The gulls have learned to ride the warm currents of air that rise from the stone when the pressure below is high. The children of Ruispor have even learned to feel its pattern in the city’s soundscape—when the water hammers in the pipes are stronger and when the dripping of the weeping walls is louder.

The stone itself is the oldest resident of Ruispor; the city of machinery and damp and people and animals and insects and water and pipes and turbines and pumps and factories and basements and steps and terraces and quays and bridges and gutters and catch basins and weirs and the dam and the Plain of Mirrors and the harbor and the channel of the river have been built into its surface and its heart.

But the stone, too, learns. It absorbs the vibration of the machines and the subtle weight of the water table and the chemical of the runoff and the oil of the engines and the patterns of the rain and the breath of the warm, wet wind from the seaward gap. The stone of Ruispor is not simply inert. It, in its own slow, geological time, is influenced by the city that has been carved and built into its surface.

And as the limestone of the plateau slowly learns the shape of the city of Ruispor, the city of Ruispor learns to live with the stone and the water and the damp and the warmth and the noise and the slow, patient change of the machine-bound and water-filled world of its valley.
