At the center of the basin, where the ground should be deepest but isn’t, there is nothing.

You wouldn’t call it a pit, or a well, or even absence properly. It’s just that the ground stops pretending to be ground and becomes instead what the ground was when nothing else was looking: a thin cloth of water spread over whatever else it is, for which you have no name. It’s the same color as everywhere else—wet steel—but where the ring-gears and the water-pages and the suspended windows and the trees all circle around it, they never circle over it. This central circle is simply not where they happen.

It is the only place in the basin where nothing weeps.

Not because the water is forbidden, or because it has stopped wanting, but because, here, wanting has already arrived. The water has no need to move toward what it is already becoming; it is already becoming. It doesn’t weep, because it has no distance to cross.

You cannot step into it. You could try—but your foot finds no ground under it, and yet not nothing, either. It’s like stepping into a mirror that has decided to remember your shape instead of reflecting it. The water accepts your weight without protest, but it doesn’t support you. It simply doesn’t argue. It has better uses for its substance than argument.

Smaller things than you are not deterred by this.

At the edge of this center, where the ground still pretends to be ground, one of the stone ring-gears sits half-submerged, not fully sunk into the wet steel but not exactly separate from it either. It turns, not with the grinding that you expect from stone and metal and time, but with a silence so intense that your ears invent sound for it afterward, like a faint tuning tone heard when you think you’ve heard nothing.

Its teeth are not sharp enough to cut anything, but they are sharp enough to remind you that there are things in this world capable of repetition.

Around the ring-gear, the water is neither rising nor falling, but standing in place—standing up, as if water could remember when it had been asked to behave like pillars. Each of these tiny columns of water is about the width of your thumb and the height of your littlest finger, and they do not move.

The center of the ring-gear yawns open—not into darkness, but into surface.

A liquid surface.

Flat.

When you lean over it, you don’t see your reflection. You see something else—something that doesn’t know your face yet, but is willing to learn it.

Living in this circle—through this circle—are the ones who made it.

You should not think of them as worms or fish or turbines or angels, although all of those words are closer than any other you have. They are not primarily animals or machines or concepts; they are primarily water trying to act like stone trying to act like sound.

Each one is almost translucent, except that where there should be transparency there is instead the suggestion of a mechanism—lines of faint greyness that are not exactly organs, not exactly tubes, not exactly wires, but somewhere between all three. They’re not things you see clearly; they’re things you see in the shape of what you already forgot to remember.

Their bodies are long, thin, and continuous—streams that have not learned to stop running within their own skin. They rarely coil and never knot; they have no interest in tangling. They flow in straight lines within their outlines, following the paths of least resistance and not deviating from them, because resistance here is a matter of opinion and water’s opinion is always yes.

They are not much larger than your arm, but none of them have exactly the same mass as another because each one adjusts its size by how much of itself it chooses to occupy at any given moment. They compress the way water does when you cup it more tightly in your hands—which is to say, not by shrinking the molecules, but by refusing to occupy the space they are not currently thinking.

They move.

Not by swimming, not by crawling, not by gliding—those concepts imply too much friction against the world. They move the way instructions move through a machine, or the way intention moves through a prayer. You can’t say that they push against anything, because there’s nothing to push against. They simply are here, then are here more.

They pass through the stone ring-gear and it doesn’t resist them. It offers no surface, because it has not decided to be surface yet. To them, it’s just another sort of water—water that has forgotten to be wet. They pass through its teeth and they are not cut; the teeth are not hard enough to cut what they themselves might become if they thought about it.

From the metal disk at the center of the ring-gear, the creatures emerge—one by one, but also not quite: they appear at intervals that seem random but are not, because randomness here has not yet learned to be random. It’s still trying to be clock.

They appear slowly, not because birth is slow, but because time here is density—and this moment is dense.

Each creature’s arrival is like a word being formed in water-script and then deciding to fall out of the page and into the vertical: first a suggestion of shape, then a faint outline of spine, then ribs of almost-color that appear to be engraved inside the water. They don’t break the surface of the ring-gear’s center; they don’t break surface at all. They surface the same way a memory does—not by pushing up from below, but by occurring.

Once manifested, they hang in the water column just above the ring-gear, not quite swimming and not quite suspended. They align themselves to the vertical axis as if they were keys hanging from a ring that never revealed itself, but if you squint, you can imagine where the ring would be: somewhere beyond the edge of your attention, holding the whole of this basin together.

From their mouths—which are not exactly mouths but more like apertures where they’ve agreed to admit the world—they exude a thin stream of water.

This water doesn’t fall; it ascends.

It goes up—not far, but far enough—to meet the underside of one of the suspended water windows that hang in the air like panes of glass where someone once painted a picture and then decided to erase it.

When the exuded water touches the window pane, it doesn’t splash. It doesn’t break. It joins—as if it were being forgiven into the same substance.

The pane of water thickens once.

That’s the only word you have for it: it thickens. Its edges darken for a heartbeat, or whatever in this place is the equivalent of a heartbeat—which is to say, a duration of attention that ends when you begin to suspect it might never.

In the space of that thickening, the pane shows something else: not the basin as you see it from where you stand, but as it might be seen from somewhere outside the basin—from outside the basin’s idea of itself.

You see, for instance: the basin from above, as if it were a shallow bowl carved into something larger than a mountain. It’s one of many bowls, arranged in rows like teeth in a jaw that has only just begun to imagine chewing.

You see it from below, as if you were lying on the underside of the machine-world ceiling, looking up at this inverted bowl where water clings to the stone.

You see it from the side, where there is no “side” except that you see one anyway, and the rim of the basin becomes a thin ring of glass that has not yet been persuaded to be opaque.

You see it from inside one of the water-trees, where the pages of translucent text are not written in language, but in the history of what has tried to become stone and failed.

You see it from inside the stone ring-gear, where metal and water and time and teeth are braided into a single instruction that you cannot read but whose tone you recognize—it is the same as the tuning tone your ears invent after the silence is gone.

The creature that made that thickening does not react to this image. It does not look at the pane. It is not here to look. It is here to produce, and to be produced.
