The true inhabitants are the **Iron Eels**, though they’re not eels at all.

They are the long, slick shapes that move through the maze of pipes and gutters, bodies built for this clamorous underworld. From a distance they look like a thicker run of water, a dark line sliding along the inside of a copper channel or threading through the shadow under a walkway. Only when one pauses to listen at a joint—head cocked, ear pressed to vibrating metal—do you see the human in them.

Their skin is the color of old rust and tarnished brass, permanently damp and faintly iridescent with mineral sheen. Hair is either cropped brutally short or braided tight against the scalp and sealed with some black, waxy substance to keep it from molding. Eyes sit deep in their sockets, adapted to reading reflections in wet metal more than to any real light; their pupils widen until almost no iris shows, drinking in whatever dim yellow bleeds from the swaying work bulbs.

They dress in layers of oiled canvas and thin rubber, patched so many times that the repairs form their own topography. Every seam is sealed with tar or wax. Every pocket is lidded, every cuff cinched. They move with a practiced caution: never stepping where water runs freely unless they must, never trusting a handrail without testing it first, never putting weight on any piece of metal that does not hum back at them.

### How They Live in Endless Rain

The Iron Eels live *inside* the system.

Their homes are not separate buildings so much as excavations within the infrastructure: hollows carved into massive support columns where older machinery has been stripped out; narrow platforms wedged between tiers of gutters; chambers gouged into the rock behind walls of pipes. To call them rooms would be generous—they are more like burrows in steel and stone.

Each dwelling has three constants:

1. **Something dry to sleep on.**  
   Hammocks strung between conduit pipes, cots raised on stilts above perpetually sweating floors, bunks slung under overhanging collection channels that double as roofs. Everything is raised because moisture always finds its way in.

2. **Something loud to orient by.**  
   A particular pump pattern beneath the floor grating; a specific water column nearby thrashing into its basin; a constant metallic ping from an old pipe joint that has become their north star. Without these sonic landmarks they grow disoriented and anxious.

3. **Something alive but controlled.**  
   Tiny colonies of that ghost-white fungus coaxed into specific corners to serve as air scrubbers or early-warning rot sensors; shallow trays where pale insects are bred for quick protein; sometimes even a small overflow basin stocked with blind white fish—an emergency larder suspended over their sleeping heads.

Passage between these spaces is half climbing, half swimming. The Iron Eels swing along chains and handholds bolted into supports, drop onto narrow catwalks slick with condensation, squeeze through gaps between pipes hot enough to burn if you misjudge them. They know every route by the sound it makes: high ringing for thin copper; dull thud for iron; low hum for live electrical conduit they must not touch.

Water is both enemy and lover here. They curse it for invading every seam and causing rust tremors that can bring whole sections crashing down; yet they read its moods like sacred text. A sudden change in pitch as it hits gutters warns them of blockages upstream; a new vibration traveling along support cables signals leaks eating through metal somewhere unseen; bubbles or foam where there should be none hint at chemical intrusions from forgotten tanks buried deeper in the rock.

They collect what they need from what falls:  
- **Drinking water** drawn from certain protected channels where algae growth signals purification cycles completed by lightless photosynthesis.  
- **Bathing** is done in quick dashes under controlled overflows—rare, almost ritualistic acts completed before hypothermia sets in.  
- **Washing** is done by wiping bodies with bundles of moss kept semi-dry above steam vents.

### Daily Rhythm

Time here is marked less by clocks than by **maintenance cycles**—periods when certain pumps must be inspected or valves rotated or sediment flushed before it backs up and drowns lower levels.

An Iron Eel’s day begins when their particular pump shifts its rhythm: maybe a slight sharpening of tone as pressure climbs toward some invisible threshold. They wake already listening, roll out of hammocks onto feet trained not to slip even half-asleep, and check three things:

1. The fungus around their sleeping space (any blackening means air quality failing).  
2. The tension on key anchor lines (too loose means structural shift somewhere).  
3. The flow pattern visible through cracks or gratings nearby (abnormal turbulence warns of trouble brewing).

Work parties form organically around problems sensed days before they become catastrophes: rust blooms detected by subtle changes in sound as water strikes weakened panels; local bats abandoning sections where gas pockets might be building up unseen; fish dying off in certain basins suggesting contamination creeping back through forgotten lines.

They move like extensions of the infrastructure—hooking safety lines into sockets installed generations ago but maintained obsessively because each failure taught someone how easily bodies vanish here—dropped into churning overflow pools or dragged under by sudden pipe bursts whose pressure can cut flesh like wire.

Tools are few but specific: long-handled wrenches shaped like question marks to reach behind tangles of conduit; hooked rods for clearing blockages without entering full flow themselves; sonic probes hammered against metal to listen for voids inside where corrosion has eaten everything away but surface shine.

There are no bosses here except urgency itself—no uniforms beyond shared scars from old burns and pressure cuts—but there *is* hierarchy: those who can map whole sectors by ear alone stand higher than those who still need their eyes confirmed by touch.

### Relations With Other Life

The Iron Eels know every other living thing here as both competitor and collaborator:

- **Bats**: Partners in navigation more than pests. Bat movement patterns through sheets of falling rain mark safe passages (if bats avoid an area, there’s often reason). In turn Iron Eels maintain specific roosting ledges free from spraying water so colonies remain anchored close enough to matter.
  
- **Blind White Fish**: Half crop-half oracle. Fish schools suddenly clustering near overflow edges signal shifts in flow dynamics before instruments register anything wrong—and also provide quick meals when work strands teams far from larders.
