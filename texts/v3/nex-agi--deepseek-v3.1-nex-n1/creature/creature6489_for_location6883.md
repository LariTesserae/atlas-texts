Woven by root and water, the people of Spindrift Holt are more than inhabitants of this place—they are its fourth season.

### Builders in the Basin

You rarely see them as a single, defined body; they come into focus like the city itself, first as noise and motion, then as details that emerge from the green.

- **Trail‑tenders** are the city’s constant pulse.  
  They move with the tired, sure rhythm of people who know exactly which stones are slick and which roots will give under their weight. Armed with simple tools—short copper adzes, stone mallets, bone wedges—they trail along the edges of the terraces and channels, trimming reeds, patching lime with living root, and redirecting every storm‑sent rivulet that tries to misbehave. Their hands are permanently stained, moss‑green under the nails, salt‑white from the mortar of quick repairs.  
  When a sheet of slate‑moss slumps or a root bridge suddenly lifts, they are the first ones into the breach, not with heroics but with the weary efficiency of someone sewing a stitch that has come undone for the hundredth time.

- **Root‑sages** are quieter, but their eyes are everywhere.  
  They are the ones who can look at a braided beech‑arch and see not only one tree but many seasons—old scars, unseen rot under the bark, the stress of a new house built in its fork. They are the readers of the city’s bones: tracing the grain of bark with calloused fingertips, listening to the water moving through channels like the pulse of some vast creature.  
  When they decide to guide a new root or cut a support, the whole neighborhood listens—because if the root fails, the house built in it fails with it.

- **Water‑barrelers** are the city’s bloodstream.  
  They are haulers, dippers, navigators, and minor chemists. They know the difference between every spring: which basins are sweet and clear, which taste bitter with the old memory of mining, and which must be treated with lime or charcoal before they are safe to drink.  
  Day and night, their barrels and sloshing buckets move along the steps and ramps, traded from one level of the basin to another. They are almost always damp, from spray or sweat or both. Their sandals leave the faintest of prints in the moss‑tile pavement before it swells back to fill the shape of their footsteps.

- **Weavers of the Moss‑Wall** are artisans who blur the line between builder and gardener.  
  They are the ones who take hand‑worked wood, thin slate shards, and bark strips and combine them with panels of packed slate‑moss into the walls of homes and shops. Theirs is the art of controlled insulation: keeping out the worst of the wet and cold without letting the inside rot entirely from the trapped breath of a hundred people.  
  They are also menders of clothes, nets, and shade‑screens, using narrow looms and twine that run like the veins of the leaf. If something is torn in Spindrift Holt, it is more likely to be **woven back** than thrown away.

- **Ferntenders and Lantern‑Keepers** keep the city from disappearing into its own gloom.  
  They are the ones who string cords along the bamboo frames and train the shutter‑ferns into living curtains, opening and closing the city’s “windows” every day. They know the subtle moods of the lantern‑ferns—when the glow‑bulbs are sick, or too weak, or when they need more of the drip‑piper bat guano.  
  They are the ones who climb out to the hanging baskets and ships of the fisher‑gourds, pruning the vines and harvesting the fat, veined pods that the city’s poorer residents rely on for food and light.

### The Body as the City’s Weather

Life in the Bowl makes the people of Spindrift Holt look like extensions of the stone and water they cling to.

- Their skin is often a **permanent shade of damp‑weather pallor**, punctuated here and there by warm reds from the braziers they huddle around.  
- Many develop the habit of **always moving slightly**—shifting from foot to foot, leaning to one side or the other, as if they are quietly adjusting to the constant tilt of the terraces.
- You’ll see them with scarred shins and knees from climbing over the root‑steps, and with palms cross‑hatched by the rope and cords of haulers, traders, and builders.
- Some older folk move with a kind of **list**, like the beech‑arches whose trunks lean to find better light and footing—as if, over time, the body learns to grow the way the roots do.

### The Market‑Bowl People

The bowl of the Market is not just where goods flow—it is where the faces of Spindrift Holt become clear.

- **Stallkeepers and Lantern Strikers**  
  Every stall is built under the arch of a root or tree, and every merchant knows the precise weight of the roots that anchor their shop. If the root fails, nothing else matters.  
  The stallkeepers are talkers, hagglers, and performers of a small daily show that they hope convinces others to give them more coins than moss‑rations.  
  The “lantern strikers” are a specialized guild among them—not only do they maintain the braziers and iron lantern‑stands that the city’s few metalworkers are able to provide, but they also carry the small glass or translucent shells in which lantern‑fern cuttings are sold to poorer homes.

- **Water‑Weighers**  
  They are sometimes called “basin judges,” though they have no formal power but influence. They are old men and women who sit by the basins, counting the barrels of water taken from each spring and boiling the sample in their small copper pots over coals. They are the ones who decide which water is sweet enough for drinking, which must be sold at “health‑price,” and which must be labeled and used only for washing or stone‑work.  
  They are cursed by the water‑barrelers who dislike their decisions, and revered by the innkeepers and cook‑fires who need someone to blame when the broth is bitter.

- **Children of the Spindles**  
  Spindrift Holt’s young are practically amphibious.  
  Barefoot or wearing thin reed sandals, they dart through the narrow channels between buildings and under the root bridges, leaping from stone to stone with the confidence of spindle‑swifts. They are the ones chasing the drift‑squirrels from gourd‑gardens, collecting the root‑crucible beetles’ resin for a few coin‑bits, and learning the bowl’s dangers by near‑miss and near‑fall.  
  Some will be apprenticed to trail‑tenders, others to water‑weighers, or weavers, or to the few who deal with trade from the lower valleys and higher peaks.

### Faces of the Bowl

Spindrift Holt doesn’t have a uniform type. It is a graft of multiple people:

- **Descendants of Miners**  
  Their families are older than the city itself, or at least its older excuses. Theirs are the names that appear in the oldest carvings around the abandoned shafts—now mostly hidden by new root and moss.  
  Many of these miners’ descendants are now stoneworkers, tunnel‑clearers, and “seamers” who trace the old mining tunnels to ensure that no weaker, hollowed areas of the mountain will collapse and take part of the bowl with it.  
  They have a certain fondness for the dangers of rock, and they are often among the trail‑tenders who dare to climb out into the most exposed edges of the basin.

- **Highland Refuges and the Displaced**  
  Spindrift Holt is a city built into a cough—the place where the mountain spat out its ore and then, after, its people. As the old mining town failed, the highlanders and other displaced from beyond the Holt were drawn to its water, its trees, and its hidden sturdiness.  
  Theirs are the faces that bring the deeper mountain dialects, the wider mantles of the outer plateau, and the recipes for the thick eel and root‑stew you smell from many of the cook‑fires.

- **Root‑sages and Herbalists**  
  They are not necessarily born from the same families or lands, but they are united by the need to understand the plant‑body of the city itself. They are the ones who mix the resin from the root‑crucible beetles with mosses and the rare highland flowers to make healing pastes, the ones who know the names of every variety of the nodule‑orchids and the nodule‑roots they are named for.  
  In the quieter hours of the morning or before dark, they can be found at the edge of the basin, collecting rain from the weeping fissures, or observing the habits of the drip‑piper bats as they exit the crevices.

### The Unspoken Code of the Holt

In this place, where the ground can shift from one root’s movement to the next, and where every storm is amplified by the bowl, the people of Spindrift Holt live by some simple, unstated laws:

- You never stand alone on the **root‑steps** if you can avoid it; always leave one footprint of space between two people. A little root‑spring can change all the angles at once.
- You never **fully cap** a weeping fissure; you only redirect, cover, or filter it. The water must be allowed to speak, or it will find a worse way to be heard.
- You never **cut a root** unless you know what is above it and what is feeding from below. The tree holds the weight of many homes.
- The **lantern‑ferns** are not property to be taken; only the lantern‑keepers and ferntenders are allowed to harvest and move them. The city’s nightlight is not a game, but life and safety.

The people of Spindrift Holt are not heroes of some great story of the world beyond their basin; they are the wrestlers of moss and root, the readers of stone and water, and the perpetual adjusters of a city that refuses to stop moving. They are tied to the Holt by the simple fact that if they ever cease to learn the moods of its trees and springs, the whole bowl will either rot or break from under them.

And so they live with a constant, low‑level urgency—not quite panic, but never quite rest. The water doesn’t stop; neither do they.
