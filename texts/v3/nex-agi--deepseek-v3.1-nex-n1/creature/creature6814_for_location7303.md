You are moving through it.

That is all you can do. There is no rest, no pause, only slow, laborious transit between the ribs of the city. You are not a fish—you are not made for this. You are not a machine—you are not designed for this.

You are something else, something forced into shape by the pressure.

Your body has been bent into a compromise between spine and hull. Years of squeezing through the gaps between districts have left you with the wrong sort of flexibility: shoulders that can rotate in odd, shallow arcs, elbows that flare around the curve of metal, hips that hyperextend just enough to let you hook around a cross-brace without ripping your suit. You have been reduced to a series of useful angles, a set of moves that work in this hatch-thickness of a world.

You are not entirely bone and muscle anymore.

Your suit is not clothing. It’s a second skeleton, a layer of hardened seals and reinforced mesh that has conformed to the bends of your body over the years. Old patches overlap the original polymer like scar tissue. Each one is a different color: dull yellow from the maintenance bays, brown from hull-side, an oxidized green that almost matches the algae that cling to the seams near the light-clusters. You no longer remember where most of the colors came from; only that they kept the water out.

Some nights you dream you’re trying to peel off the suit. You get as far as your elbows before you wake up gasping into your regulator.

The regulator itself is a worn mouthful of metal and silicone. You can feel every tooth mark in it when you clamp down during the bad tilt-moments, when the whole joint clangs somewhere far above and you briefly lose your sense of which way is down. The mouthpiece is shaped by your jaw over time; the teeth are shaped by it too. You think you used to have a better smile once.

Your eyes have changed.

The lumen-screens of the city are inconsistent here. Sometimes you travel in the low glow of the cluster algae, their weird phosphorescence making the beams of the cross-bracing glow an uneven blue-green. Sometimes you’re in the halogen glare of a maintenance lamp, solar-grown by the algae, but so bright it whites out your whole world. You learned to squint and not-quite-focus through the gloss, to see motion instead of shape.

Your pupils rarely settle back to their normal size. They hang open, ready for the next dark stretch.

Sound has changed too.

Not silence, but a thick, constant pressure of sound: the low groan of the hull as the ocean above shifts, the high metallic ping of water coursing through a narrow pipe, the gurgle and tick of valves opening and closing somewhere else in the joint. Your own breath bubbles out of the regulator and into your helmet, a rhythm of popping and hissing that you know so well it can disappear, until suddenly you are keenly aware of it again and the panic edges in.

You are not alone in this hatch-thickness of a world.

You know the others by their lights.

There is the one who uses bioluminescent cuffs—little algae-filled loops around their ankles and wrists, warped from old pressure and glinting a faint green. You only see them when they are moving through the darker passages, and even then it is mostly just the suggestion of limbs sliding along metal. You’ve never spoken; they don’t seem to have a regulator, or any need for one. Their body has learned to live in this water without boundary.

Once, you caught them threading something small and wriggling into a gap between two plates of rust, fingers slightly longer than they should be. Maybe they were feeding the city. Maybe being eaten by it. You never followed.

There is the one with the chain of tools.

They rattle as they move. The sound is muffled by the water, but you recognize it anyway: a chain-loop of spanners and clamps and retractors, all hammered into uneven lengths by hand. They follow the maintenance schedules, or what they remember of them. You know them by the pattern of their breathing: a short inhale, long exhale, long pause, as if they can only let themselves take a breath when the work allows.

You’ve seen them patch leaks from the inside. They don’t stop for conversation. They’ll move you aside with a gloved hand if you happen to be in the way, their palm hard and unyielding through the suit.

There is the one who never goes anywhere.

You find them in the same cross-section every time: the place where three large support beams intersect at an odd, shallow angle, forming a kind of alcove that is not quite wide enough to be comfortable, but just enough to be occupied. They are not entirely a person; you can see the metal of the joint itself rising through their skin in places, where it’s been eaten by the salt and rust and then fused by time.

Their eyes are open, always open. The water does not bother them. They blink when you approach, which you take as welcome.

They are knitting.

You don’t know where the yarn comes from, or what it’s made of. It looks like filaments of cable insulation and frayed fiberglass bundle, twined into something soft and pale. It moves between their fingers, looped and pulled and slipped through, forming a fabric that seems never to grow longer, but never runs short, either. They work without pattern, sometimes unraveling everything they’ve done and starting over in the same motion.

You asked them once what they were making. They smiled, their teeth faintly stained with rust, and said nothing. You decided that was a better answer than any.

You are aware of yourself in pieces.

Teeth in regulator.  
Shoulders pressed against the city’s ribs.  
Fingertips sliding along the seam of a weld, checking for cracks.  
Lungs expanding in the insulated chest of your suit, flattening and expanding again.

You are aware of the water as another body.

It is always touching you: everywhere you move, it is already there. It flows into the gaps between your suit and your skin the moment you lift a joint, cold where you didn’t expect it, warm where you did not know it could be. Your vision is never clear; everything you see is refracted through the lens of this water-world, bent slightly out of shape.

Sometimes you think you can hear it purging: a low thrum along the inner hull that you feel in your teeth before you hear it in your ears. The city is taking some water in, putting some water out, maintaining the delicate balance of its inner sea. You are moved in it, whether you want to be or not.

You are not exactly a person.

Not exactly a worker.

Not exactly a creature of the depths.

You are the thing that has learned to fit between three districts and not be crushed.

You have chosen this.

You didn’t choose the ocean-being, who moves with weird grace, and whose eyes you think might be more than two. You didn’t choose the tool-ganger, whose breathing rhythm is now as familiar to you as your own, and whose name you will never ask. You didn’t choose the fixed one, knitting the same impossible garment into the fabric of the world.

But you chose to keep coming here.

You chose to know the sections of the hull that no longer appear on any map. You chose to learn how the pressure shifts when the far locks are cycling, how to brace yourself in the currents of them, how to move yourself along the inner edge of the joint where the water is less turbulent.

You chose to remember the names of the tunnels: the ones that are safe, the ones that are rusting through, the ones that are beginning to seal themselves with their own calcification.

In this brief thickness of lock and pressure, where the world has been shrunk to the space between hatches, you have chosen to be the one who knows what can be lived through.

And you will.

You will move until your body wears its shape into the city itself, until your suit fuses into the rust, and your regulator is finally pried loose by someone else’s fingers.

Or maybe you’ll simply be taken into the body of the world.

The water that you move through will become the water inside you; the hatch-thickness will become your ribs; the sound of the hull groaning will become your heartbeat.

Either way, you will keep describing.

To the rust, if the salt, if the sound.

They are listening.
