...light dims accordingly beneath them, and the rain that comes is not always rain.

The air beneath the Quillwood is never truly still. It hangs between the heat of the earth and the cool of the canopy in a slow, circular convection, like the breath held inside a conch shell. The cloud-forest high above exhales a fine, persistent mist that drifts downward through the gills: the droplets condense on the metallic ribs of the leaves and then fall in long, slow streaks that are not quite rain, not quite dew, but something else—half water, half sap.

Sometimes the gill-shields contract, like lungs taking a slow, unhurried breath, and the mesh beneath them—a net of woven aerial roots and mycorrhizal cables—sags, then tightens, then sighs again. The falling rain thickens for a moment into a heavier stream that slaps across the pathways and corrugates the surface of the canals, then thins to a shimmering haze that tastes like iron and jasmine.

### The things beneath the ceiling

The streets, if you can call them that, are the lower branches of the Quillwood brought into service of geometry.

They are ribs of some ancient lateral trunk, now hollowed, polished, and laid out in a pattern of broken circles that mimic the slough of a long-shed skin. The people walk on the living wood, but it never feels like walking in the forest—the floor here is planed flat by foot and time and ritual, worn smooth and uneven all at once like the floor of an old temple. The surface beneath your feet is not quite uniform: sometimes it gives way like very firm rubber under heel, then meets the next step with the gentle insistence of bone.

Along the ribs of the streets the canals run like green veins. They appear not so much carved as *remembered* into the wood—shallow grooves where the Quillwood, once asked to do something different, now complies without ever forgetting entirely. The water is not pure water. It moves slowly, carrying a fine silt of plant matter and fungal spores and microscopic things that once lit the city’s sky in other ways than the gills-shields now do.

Bridges are not stone but the living bodies of lesser trees, coaxed into forms and then frozen there by some forgotten negotiation of tree and keeper and market. They are arched ribs, smooth as polished horn, washed with a fine film of moisture that is never allowed to dry. The shops beneath them are carved into the lower ribs of the Quillwood itself: alcoves and nodules and chambers where the wood has been convinced to form hollows and then shelves, then stalls and counters and stairs.

Even the stairs are not quite stairs—they are ripples in the wood’s growth, like those in a fossil where the tide has paused and gone on again. The steps are not uniform but they are familiar enough to the locals who know the unspoken language of this place’s bones.

Lights here are not made of fire. They are not lamps in the way you mean lamps; they are not objects hung on walls but living things with the same relationship to walls as lungs have to torsos. The glow-sacs are pale nodules that grow out of the wood itself like cysts of captured light. They pulse slowly, sometimes barely at all, sometimes in sync with the breathing of the gills above—a faint, rhythmic brightening like the marrow of the world being slowly compressed.

Color of nearby light:  
- where the glow-sacs are younger, closer to the tips of roots still trying to remember what sunlight they once saw: the faintest green-blue, like sun through seawater  
- deeper into the market where the wood is older and darker and the exhalations of the Quillwood are thicker: the light becomes a warm amber, like oil over rusted metal.

In the deepest passages beneath the Quillwood’s heartwood base, where the ribs curve downward and disappear into the soil of the basin, the glow-sacs are almost black-purple, like cooling bruises. They illuminate nothing usefully. They exist simply to remind the world that they are still alive.

### The people in between the ribs

The people who remain here have learned to live with the city’s reluctance to be entirely a city.

They treat the Quillwood not as a shelter but as a body whose signals they have learned to read like a physician’s. They know the gill-shields breathe more deeply in the hours before a storm that never comes; they know the glow-sacs pulse faster when the Quillwood is thirsty, and slower when it is dreaming—and Quillwood dreams, they say, in the long memory of water.

You will see:

- **The Keepers of the Breath**  
  They are the closest thing to priests the Quillwood tolerates. They speak of their “service” to the tree but never “pray” to it—the Quillwood is not a god, they insist, but a living thing that has outlived gods. They wear the Quillwood’s own shed bark as armor and clothing, layered like scales: polished, dark, and strangely warm to the touch.

  Where they pass, the glow-sacs flare slightly with the rhythm of their steps. They place their hands flat against the wood of the walls, the branches of the streets, the ribs of the bridges, and listen for the slow pulse that runs through the tree like blood through a body turned to forest.

  They chant the water’s temperature; they regulate the opening of gills; they correct the tilt of branches that have begun to grow too heavy; they negotiate the loans of phloem and metal that keep the tree’s veins alive.

- **The Dreamers**  
  The Sanfish Market is not called “a bowl of dreams” for nothing. The Quillwood, in its long sleep of trees and cables and metals and clays, has learned to do something with its own breath that it was never intended to do.

  The air beneath its sleeping ribs thickens at night, not so much with fog as with something else—something less than matter but more than memory. The Dreamers lie on the wood of the streets, or in the sediment of the canals, or beneath the oldest bridges where the gills have never fully opened, and breathe the Quillwood’s breath back.

  The mist they exhale settles on the wood like dew on metal. It forms into shapes that are not symbols but lacings of light on the surface of the wood—images of places they have never seen but that Quillwood remembers somewhere in its lignin heart. The images are not flat but curved like insides they are already inside of.

  The Dreamers do not speak of *dreams* in the sense of desires but in the sense of things that the Quillwood has seen reflected in the water table beneath the basin: cities of glass and iron that sank into the sand before the two continents tried to kiss; skyline forests of concrete and wind that rotted and were covered over by the Quillwood’s own seedlings.

  They say the Quillwood dreams of *becoming* the cities it once replaced.

- **The Bakers of the Dust**  
  Everything here eventually becomes dust—not just wood but memory itself.

  In the oldest alcoves beneath the Quillwood’s thickest ribs you will find the Bakers of the Dust: small, quiet people who collect the fine pollen of the gills, the shed scales of the bark, the spores of the fungi that grow in the humid air, and grain they never grow themselves but simply trade for.

  They knead these things together and bake them in ovens built into the Quillwood’s side ribs, where the heat is not fire but the slow, steady warmth of the tree’s sap pumping through metal veins. The loaves that emerge are not golden but the color of old wood and old rain and old regret; they taste like something you remember having once eaten in another life.

  The people who eat the Dust bread say they can remember the Quillwood’s memories better than their own—can remember, for a time, being roots instead of feet, water instead of veins.

- **The Regretters**  
  They are not exactly a profession; they are more like a condition of the place.

  There are always those who come to the Sanfish Market seeking to forget. The Quillwood does not forget. It remembers instead as water remembers: by holding the shape for longer than you would expect, then gradually letting the shape soften.

  The Regretters pay the Keepers or the Dreamers or simply the Quillwood itself—no one quite agrees what—to sit beside the canals and tell their regrets into the water. The stories they tell are not stories but breaths of regret shaped into words.

  The water does not respond in any visible way, but the Quillwood does. The branches nearest the Regretters’ seats grow new, small ribs of bark that twist around nothing like fingers clutching at air; they harden over time into strange sculptures of their own making.

  The city’s most sacred places are not its altars but the quiet backwater canals where these ribs of wood have formed dense thickets of regret, frozen in mid‑gesture like hands reaching for something they have not yet decided to grasp.

### The market itself

All of this is the Sanfish Market: not a place where you come to buy but a place where you come to *leave*.

The transactions here are not made in coins but in quiet exchanges of breath, wood, water, and forgetting.

You will see:

- **The stalls of the air**  
  They are not stalls but open spaces beneath the gills-shields where the air is thickest and least willing to move. Here merchants sell not things but *coalitions* of air—various densities of mist carefully gathered into glass bottles that began as prolonged nodules on the Quillwood’s ribs. The air inside is not still air; it is the Quillwood’s breath forced to sample itself.

  Buyers bring their lungs to the stall and breathe from the bottles to see what the Quillwood has seen that day: whether the market is dreaming of rain, or of cities past, or of the future where the two continents, finally tired of their failed kiss, decide to slide apart once more and leave the basin to be swallowed by the sea.

- **The tides of the wood**  
  The shops themselves are not quite fixed.

  If you return to the Sanfish Market after years away, you will still find the same aisles between ribs, but the stalls will have moved slightly—like veins in an aging hand. The wood grows in uneven pulses; an alcove that once sold Dust-bread now holds the air of a new Dreamer’s sleep; an old Dreamer’s grove has become a Regretter’s canal.

  The Keepers say the Quillwood rearranges the market because it has learned to rearrange its own memory of what it is to be a city.

- **The Sanfish themselves**  
  The market’s name is not entirely a metaphor.

  On the few nights a year when the Quillwood’s breath and the basin’s water table and the sky’s withheld rain all agree to be something else together, the Sanfish appear.

  “Sanfish” is what the locals call the luminescent fungi that sometimes grow in the canals of the market, not in water but in the air just above it—like floating, translucent fish that solve the problem of gravity by forgetting they need to solve it.

  They have no visible fins, no fins you could call fins, but they move with slow, undulant motions as if swimming through something thicker than water. They emit light like the glow-sacs but colder, more like moonlight reflected in pieces of broken glass on the bottom of a shallow stream.

  The Sanfish do not belong to anyone but to Quillwood and the Regretters who still breathe near enough to the water to call their own regrets into the air above the canals.

  The local children—if there are children here; if “children” is the right name for the younger version of whatever lives in the Sanfish Market—have learned not to chase them. The Sanfish do not respond to being followed but to being breathed on.

  If you breathe your regret over them, they sometimes change color slightly, like the sky recalling the day before it remembers it is meant to rain.

---

The Sanfish Market is not a place you come to buy and leave.

The Quillwood remembers every visitor in its own way: as a fine line in the bark of a new branch growing inward toward the heartwood instead of outward toward the sky; as a subtle change in the density of the air; as a faint memory of a pair of lungs that once passed beneath its ribs and breathed its own breath back.

You come to leave.

You leave something behind, whether you want to or not.

And when you finally walk away, the gills above breathe a little deeper than before, the rain of water and sap falls a little heavier, and the Sanfish Market—the bowl of dreams and regrets that forgot the world but never forgot itself—holds another shape in its quiet water memory: a shape that is almost you but not quite anymore.

You leave, and the Quillwood remembers you in its way instead of yours.
