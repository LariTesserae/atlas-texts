They don’t have a name that anyone remembers now.  

If you pressed them—if you backed one of the maintenance crew into a corner after their shift, when the grease is still dark under their nails and the mist is still beading on their sleeves—they might say something like *“the shifts”* or *“the ones who come after.”*  

They don’t think of themselves as a people so much as a set of symptoms.

They are the ones who climb a ladder and realize that nothing in the world above them is the sky. It is all walkways and supports and cable runs and brackets that someone else bolted there years ago. They are the ones who feel the pressure of the structure above and below and to the sides and do not fall, because nothing else is falling either.

They are small enough to be mistaken, at a distance, for another tool hanging on a belt or a bundle of rags stuffed into a gap. The mist is their uniform: the water that soaks their sleeves and trousers and the hair at their necks and never quite evaporates. Their clothes are the color of wet brick and rusted iron and grey conduit; the dyes are cheap and industrial, and they fade beautifully into the patina around them.

They wear rain-slickers over work jackets, over padded undershirts because the damp somehow makes the cold seep in instead of out. Their boots are rubber-soled or steel-toed or both, the soles patterned by a thousand different gratings and steps. They move in a perpetual shuffle—half to avoid the worst of the wet, half to keep from slipping on the moss.

Their faces are narrow and sharp, not by nature but from habit. The mist slicks their hair down flat; they cut it short or tie it back tight with whatever wire or tape is to hand. Their eyes are the color of wet cement and tarnish and brass fittings—unremarkable until you realize that nothing else in the city has that color, except where they are.

They are not the ones who built the engine city. They are the ones who have to keep it from collapsing on itself.

### Work

Their day begins—has always begun and will always begin—with someone’s shift change.

In the mist-thick quiet before the whistle blows or the alarm sounds, you can see them moving through the corridors and levels: the unlucky night shift shedding their sopping jackets, handing over clipboards and logbooks; the day shift shrugging on their own slickers and checking their tool belts one last time. The vinyl and canvas creaks; the buckles clink. The sound of it is so ordinary that you might not hear it at all.

Their job is not to operate the machine, but to keep the machine capable of operation.

They are the ones who climb to the highest accessible gantries—not to the roof of the world, because the roof is a distant lattice of girders and overhead conveyors, but to the top of *this* level, where the rain-slicker yellow and work grey shrink to the scale of a thumbnail against the vast dark flank of a condenser bank.

From there they can see:

- The long drip-line of a leaky condenser, where water has been falling in exactly the same place for years and has worn a groove in the metal decking  
- The faint bloom of rust where a pipe flange weeps steam  
- The moss that has started to colonize the edge of a walkway that is supposed to be sterile

They are the ones who write this down with a pencil stub and waterproof paper that will smudge anyway. They are the ones who use the same language for the metal and the moss and the steam: *weeping*, *breathing*, *sweating*.  

If they write *“bearing-wall sweating at lower third”*, the engineers will understand. If they write *“condenser weeping at seam”*, another shift will bring out the welding rig and the caulk and the tarring brush.

They are the ones who crawl along the narrow service catwalks beneath the main spans, where the mist is thicker and the light is weaker and the sound of the water below them is a hollow roar under a grating. They move with their hips and elbows and shoulders, keeping their backs to the wall, because the center of the catwalk is where the rust is weakest.

When they stand at the edge of a culvert, watching industrial effluent ride the channel like a black river, they are the ones who decide if it is *“normal fouling”* or *“blockage coming.”*

They are the ones who know the difference between the mist that is always there and the mist that has begun to curl with urgency toward the intakes of a cooling tower; between the vibration that is the steady thrum of the generators and the vibration that feels like teeth being ground.

### Bodies

They are small by necessity and by design.

The world of Stokes Engine / Station Nine / whatever meaningless name is scratched into the enamel this season—this world is not built for the height of a man or woman but for the reach of their arm plus the length of their wrench plus the clearance of one pipe behind another.

The narrows between the machines are shoulder-width or narrower. The maintenance access shafts are designed around the span of a rib cage. The gap between two conveyor belts is just wide enough for a pair of maintenance hips. The world of the engine has been modified by generation of repairs and the people who crawl into it to effect those repairs have been subtly modified in return.

Their shoulders are a little narrower than the city intends to be generous. Their hips a little slighter, because the alternative is shredding their trousers on the sharp edges of every hatchway. They move with a compressed economy of motion: turns taken tight, elbows kept close to ribs, knees drawn up when passing under low beams.

The mist is always on them, so their skin never quite dries out. They don’t smell like soap; they smell like machine oil and sweat and hot metal and wet moss. The cut of their hair is not fashion but hygiene: short enough that it does not soak into their eyes every time they look up.

Their hands are the part of them that has been most clearly remade by the city. Their fingers are longer and more precise than you would expect for their size. Their nails are trimmed ruthlessly; dirt and grease collect under cuticles that are permanently stained dark. The joints of their fingers have thickened from years of twisting valves and sockets.

You will recognize them by their hands before you recognize them by their faces.

### Sign

They are not invisible to each other. In fact, they have developed a subtle language of appearance and gesture.

In a place where everything is made and remade and nothing made is ever truly removed, there is no room for markers or flags or badges. The walls themselves cannot be carved without damaging the integrity of the structure.

So they mark their presence in small ways:

- A knot of green wire tied around a valve handle  
- A stripe of paint, dorter than the surrounding wall, around the handle of a ladder  
- A chalk-scrawl of a sigil that looks like a wrench and a ladder crossed  
- A small ring of moss wiped clean from a section of rail

These signs are not decoration; they are information compressed to the point of illegibility to outsiders.

To *them*:

- A knot on a valve says *“this one’s been serviced within the last cycle.”*
- A stripe of paint on a ladder says *“safe to climb, but watch the third rung.”*
- A moss-bare ring on a rail means *“don’t lean on this section unless you want your jacket to cease to be waterproof.”*

They are the ones who know that the city’s “official” markings—the stenciled numbers and block letters that are yellow on some pipes and orange on others and white on a few—are not always reliable. The world of the engine is *layered*, and the paint is older in some places than in others. Whichever shift has most recently passed through this section might have painted over history or might have tried to preserve it, depending on their mood.

So they make their own marks over the city’s. Theirs are smaller. More transient.

They are the ones who understand the difference between a city that is *eroded* and a city that is *evolving*.

### Habitat

They don’t have homes in the way you might imagine homes.

Somewhere in the deeps of the structure there are rooms where bunks have been bolted to the walls and lockers slotted in between the pipes, and they call these *shifts’ quarters*. But they don’t live long there.

The city is not something you can step away from.

They sleep in the intervals between vibration spikes. They eat standing up in the service alcoves where the mist is a thin drizzle instead of a persistent rain. They drink their tea from metal cups that have *no one* scratched on them in a handwriting that no one remembers.

Their homes are not rooms but *routes*.

There is the woman whose path home from the day shift is six ladder climbs down and three catwalk crossings over and then a narrow service tunnel between a condenser bank and a steam pipe. The tunnel is warm and the metal is already dry by the time she gets there, and the vibration of the pipe is a deep steady thrum that she knows to interpret as *“still working.”* She doesn’t call this *her bedroom* but in practice she sleeps more nights there than on any bunk.

There is the man whose route takes him through the upper level where the moss is thickest and where the little translucent creatures scatter from underfoot. He knows the color of the moss in each season—not by the calendar but by the load on the system and the temperature of the intake air. The moss is his almanac.

They are the ones who have chosen to attach themselves to certain pieces of the machine not because they love them but because they understand the language of those particular parts.

One is attached to a particular boiler that no one else trusts because its readings are always erratic. He hears the fluctuation of its pressure as a voice that he can *almost* understand.

Another is attached to a condenser column whose old weep holes have been sealed and replaced half a dozen times. She sees the stains where water has found new ways out and says *“you stubborn bastard.”* The column, in turn, does not explode.

The homes of the shifts are not places where they are safe from the machine. They are places where they understand the machine.

### Relationship with the System

They are not the operators of the engine. The operators sit in the control rooms where the lights burn blue and the dials glow and the air is too dry, because no mist leaks in.

The operators *think* they understand the system.

The shifts *know* they don’t.

You can see the difference in the way they discuss the same event:

- Operator in a clean room, looking at a needle trembling on a gauge: *“Pressure dropping in quadrant three. We’re compensating on quadrant four.”*
- Shift on the catwalk above quadrant three, watching the mist curl around a new fissure in the insulation: *“Y’see that? There she goes again.”*

They are the ones who understand that the engine city is not broken and not fixed but forever in the process of *being mended.*

When they close a valve that has been stuck open for three years and the pressure drops in quadrant four unexpectedly, they are not surprised. They are the ones who have to go out into the cold and wet and re-open the valve by half and then three quarters and then *almost* but not quite all the way, because that is where the system has decided it wants to be.

They are the ones responsible for the *history* of the machine.

They are the ones who have seen:

- The same panel opened three times in a month, each time by a different shift  
- The same pipe replaced six years ago and now leaking from exactly the same kind of joint  
- The same section of walkway sagging under the same moss and the same weight of water and the same traffic pattern

They are the ones who have learned to read the scars of the city as a language of repetition.

They know the difference between:

- *rust* and *rust that’s been stabilized by moss*  
- *weeping* and *weeping that’s about to become a torrent*  
- *quiet* and *quiet with a tension in it*

They are the ones who have never been formally trained to do what they do but who have been trained by the city itself: the slope of the decking under their feet as the supports sag imperceptibly; the way that the mist smells when it is about to thicken; the way that a distant clunk echoes through the structure when *that* particular relay trips.

### The Others

They are not alone in the engine city, but they are the only ones who fully understand that they are not alone.

Around them move:

- The operators who think they are running the machine  
- The engineers who think they are improving the machine  
- The supervisors who think they are coordinating the work of the others

But the shifts are the ones who know that the real work of the city is not under the control of any of these.

The real work of the city is being done by:

- The mosses and algae and ivies that are slowly reshaping the surfaces  
- The rust and corrosion that are turning the metal into something else  
- The water that is finding its way through every layer of structure and slowly making it into something else again  
- The vibration of the engines that is shaking the bolts loose and the walls that are shaking back

They are the ones who understand that the city-engine is not a machine that has been built on a landscape but a landscape that has *learned to be machine*, and is now *learning to be something else again.*

When they say:

- *“The system’s breathing heavy today,”*
- or *“The moss’s taking hold over there,”*
- or *“The structure’s settling again,”*

what they mean is:

- *We are still in the middle of it.*  
- *We do not know where it is going.*  
- *We will be there when it gets there.*

They are the ones who have accepted that the future of this world is not a new design of engine but a deeper and more total *integration* of the engine with the moss and the water and the stone and the bones of the people who have been feeding into it for generations.

They are the ones who have begun to realize that even the humans who tend to the machine are not separate from this process.

### Relationship with Time

They have no memory of a time before the engine city and no expectation of a time after.

They are the ones who have noticed:

- That the layer of grit on their boots is thicker this year than last  
- That the moss on the walkway is three shades darker than it used to be  
- That the old steel has been patinated by the long application of salt and water and mist and exhaust

They are the ones who have also noticed:

- That their joints are beginning to ache like the bolts in the bearing walls  
- That their teeth are beginning to clench when the pressure rises in a quadrant they can’t see  
- That their skin is beginning to show the same lines as the drip lines down the walls of the condenser towers

They are the ones who have begun to think of themselves as *temporary* in the same way as a panel that has been replaced three times—but not *disposable*.  

They are the ones who have begun to suspect:

- That there will be a day when the engine is *entirely* moss and metal and stone  
- And that on that day there will be no one who needs to turn a valve or mend a leak  

They are the ones who have begun to wonder:

- If on that day they will be the ones who finally get to step outside and see what “sky” looks like  

and  

- If they will be afraid.

### Name

They call themselves *the shifts* because that is what they do.

They shift from one quadrant to another.

They shift the pressure from one set of boilers to another.

They shift the leakage from one channel to another.

They shift the moss from one section of wall to another, by virtue of where they allow or avoid cleaning.

You ask them what they are and they say, *“We’re the ones who come after.”*

You ask them what they think is happening to the world of the engine city and they say:

- *“It’s learning.”*
- *“It’s settling.”*
- *“It’s breathing.”*

And then they go back to work.

They are the ones who climb into the wet dark between the condenser banks and the steam mains.

They are the ones who tighten bolts and loosen valves and adjust the pressure until the system’s blood pressure drops.

They are the ones who wipe the condensation from their brows and take their break huddled against a warm pipe that has been sweating for a hundred years.

They are the ones who will never be remembered by name.

They are the ones who will never be removed from the structure entirely.

They are the ones who live here—where the engine becomes the mountain and the mountain learns to be engine—and when they die, their bones are in the concrete and the moss and the rust and the continued breathing of the city that never turns off.
