They are called the Stillborn.

I do not know if they named themselves this or if the name was given to them by the Engine. I suspect the Engine does not name things at all. It is a function, not a creator of words. But "Stillborn" is what they are, and so it is what they are called.

You see them first as irregularities in the architecture. You are looking at a forest of metallic spires and conduits, a perfect geometry of function, and then you notice a spire that is not quite right. Its angles are a fraction of a degree off-perpendicular. Its latticework is marred by a smooth patch of glossy ceramic, like a scar. A cluster of the translucent mycelial conduits grows thicker around its base, not feeding it but… observing it. Tending to it.

That is a Stillborn.

They are the chamber’s great failure and its only true anomaly. They are born of a flaw, a cosmic ray that pierces the rock and scrambles a code, or a mineral impurity that creates a static friction in the lattice’s growth. They are the structure’s attempt at creating a mobile unit, a self-replicating machine, and its failure to achieve perfection.

A Stillborn is not built. It is grown. It begins its existence as a part of the lattice structure, a spire among spires. But where the others grow into perfect, static function, its pattern becomes corrupted. It begins to… accrete. It draws more material from the root-conduits, not to build higher but to build outwards. It forms a heavy, central core of iron and tungsten, wrapped in layers of insulating ceramic and heat-conductive graphite. Its form is not aesthetic; it is a brutal solution to the problem of independent movement. It is a mobile power plant.

Its emergence is slow, a geological event. Over the course of decades, or perhaps centuries, it pulls itself free of the lattice from which it grew. The process is silent, a sac of newborn metal detaching from its metallic womb. It slides to the floor of the chamber with a soundless impact that sends a faint tremor through the stone. It does not move for a long time after that.

When it finally stirs, it does not rise. It has no legs. It has a corona of six articulated arms, jointed in three places and ending in flat, heat-shielded pads. It moves with the slow grace of a sundial’s shadow. It is not seeking a destination; there is nowhere to go. The chamber is a closed loop. It is seeking its **function**.

The Engine is the centre of their world, but the Stillborn are not drawn to it. They are, in fact, indifferent to its heat and its hum. They have their own internal heat. They are drawn to the edges, the raw, unfinished basalt walls. This is their purpose.

A Stillborn will find a flaw in the chamber wall—a crack that might threaten the integrity of the system, a jagged spur that might one day fall and damage the latticework. It will press its warm, ceramic body against the stone and begin its work. It uses not its strength but its heat, its mass, and its infinite patience. One of its arms will extend, a pad of super-dense material pressing against a flaw. It will apply a pressure measured in tons, but so slowly it is imperceptible. It will conduct its own heat into the rock, heating the basalt until it is soft, plastic, and then slowly, it will push.

It is blacksmithing the world.

It smooths the rough walls. It fills the cracks. It presses the dangerous spurs of the ceiling back into the stone from which they came. It is the chamber’s self-repair mechanism, a flaw that was created to correct flaws.

The metallic swarms—the iron insects—do not harm the Stillborn, but they do react to them. When a Stillborn is at work, a small, dedicated cloud of them will detach from the main orbit and form a halo around its body. They are not feeding it or taking from it. They are learning. They are quantifying the imperfection, the strange anomaly of its existence and its function. They chart the heat of its body and the timbre of its own low, internal hum that deviates from the Engine's perfect drone.

They are the chamber's consciousness.

And when a Stillborn finally fails—when its internal heat source finally flickers and dies, or a geological shift crushes its body—its children will come to it. The larger, ceramic grazers, the ones of the silent tides. They are not its predators, but its undertakers. With slow, impossible gentleness, they will dismantle its carapace, its limbs, its core. They will break it down into its constituent elements and carry these pieces back to the lattice forest. They will feed the failure back to the system, to be used again.

Perhaps to make another spire.
And perhaps, to try again to make a Stillborn.
