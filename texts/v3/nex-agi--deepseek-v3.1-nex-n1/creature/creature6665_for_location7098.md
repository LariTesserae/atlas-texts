On the bone balcony of the Rookbowl’s rim, the first thing you learn about its people is that they do not call themselves by a single name, because names are too much like cages. Instead they describe themselves by the bowl’s own terms: rim-walkers, bone-listeners, moss-binders, root-readers.  

You meet them in ones and twos as you stand there shaking in your chest from the vent roar below and the constant low thunder of wings above. They do not approach quickly or announce themselves loudly. They appear from behind the soft scalloped edges of moss-bark mats, from under archways of jade vine that form natural doorframes in the living wall, from staircases of fused root-treads that curl down into the green haze.

Their bodies are lean and corded, skin burnished by sun and wind until it has a faint metallic sheen where sweat slicks it. They are not tall by your standards, but they move with a compact economy that suggests every muscle has been trained for climbing and balance. Their hair is braided tight against the scalp or bound into coils threaded with small bone beads and bits of chitin that click softly as they turn their heads — not decoration so much as instruments; different arrangements rattle differently in the wind, telling others at a distance who is moving and in what direction.

Most striking are their feet.

After generations on bone platforms ridged and carved by Pelters, their feet have adapted to read surface as much as support weight. The soles are broad, toes long and prehensile enough to hook over irregularities in the bone. In some families you see a thickened ridge of callus along the outer edge of each foot where they test resonance by tapping gently — an unconscious habit now bred into habit-of-body. When they walk across a new section of platform they do it slowly at first, sensing hollowness or rot before it would bear their full weight.

They dress in layers that make sense only when you understand how capriciously water falls here: close-fitting undergarments of fine root fiber that wick sweat, loose over-robes woven from moss-bark strips treated until they are supple and almost silk-like. These robes can be unfurled quickly into wide funnels when standing under one of those narrow, personal showers that rain only along a single root canal; held just so, they become instant cisterns.

Around their waists hang small kits in pouches made from insect carapaces polished thin: curved plates lashed together with sinew, each one containing a specific tool or mixture. You see:

- Chalky nodules for marking temporary paths on bone.
- Tiny metal blades no larger than your thumbnail — their “great technology,” painstakingly traded for or mined from rare veins revealed when Pelters breach particular hollows.
- Bundles of dried moss soaked in resinous sap that can be lit for brief flame or smoldered for smoke-signals shaped by cupped hands.
- Soft mallets carved from rook-bone for sounding out hollows without damaging the structure.

### The Listeners

To live on the Rookbowl’rim is to be constantly listening.

The humans here have cultivated this to an art that borders on instinct. They can distinguish layers within what to you is one overwhelming roar:

- The deep fundamental from the central vent shaft — its pitch and timbre tell them whether hot air is rising strongly (good for gliders and calm winds) or if stratified layers below are clogged with moisture (bad; expect localized deluges or sudden downdrafts).
- The higher harmonics created by thousands of ventilator fronds fluttering in concert — these give clues about which “organs” of vegetation are active at any given moment.
- The chatter of Pelters somewhere along the platform network; each band has its own rhythm pattern etched into memory like music notation.
- The periodic cracks and groans as some plantation of stems far below collapses or is torn down by creatures too large to see clearly from here.

When something shifts — when a note drops out of place in this vast composition — they pause mid-step. Heads tilt; eyes narrow not in vision but in focus on hearing; fingers splay to feel vibrations through whatever surface they touch.

Their language has evolved around sound’s nuances. They don’t say “The structure is weakening” so much as “The bone has lost its middle tone.” A coming storm isn’t predicted by clouds alone but “The rook-cries cutting flat against an upwind breath.” Their conversations often include silences where both parties simply listen together before continuing.

Children learn young to map their surroundings acoustically rather than visually alone. A favorite game involves leading a blindfolded child along safe ledges while they identify locations solely by echo: how sound bounces off certain root walls; how particular openings whistle at specific pitches; how Pelters further down respond rhythmically when someone taps near an old feeding scar.

### Work Without Ownership

No one claims ownership over sections of living bowl — such a notion would be like claiming ownership over someone else’s liver. Instead there are responsibilities negotiated over time:

- **Moss-binders** maintain sections of moss-bark carpeting along walkways so walkers don’t slip into crevices worn by centuries.
- **Condensation-catchers** memorize which metallic veins sweat most reliably after certain temperature shifts; they rise before dawn with basins to guide droplets into shared cisterns.
- **Root-readers** know which bulges on inner walls indicate swollen passages where Root-Fish activity has altered flow patterns; they advise where new shelters should not be anchored lest a shifting nutrient channel undermine foundations.
- **Vent-watchers** stand at chosen vantage points during high heat pulses counting rook types aloft: if certain curve-wings change altitude or smoke-bills suddenly break their looping circuits there may be unseen turbulence brewing inside bowl weather.

These tasks cross family lines but remain local enough that everyone knows who understands what stretch best. Disputes arise rarely because there’s always more work than people: any group trying to hoard resources quickly learns how little control they truly have when vegetation decides to reroute some vital flow elsewhere on its own schedule.
