Life in this world answers not to light or seasons, but to pressure and pitch.

The organisms here are neither fully animal nor plant, nor machine. They are something birthed between the hammer of sound and the anvil of this thick, liquid air. If you stand still and let the roar soak into you, you start to notice them.

---

## The Ones Who Listen in the Ground

First, there are the **Resonators**.

They appear as low, humped shapes half-submerged in the glowing water, clustered around the edges of the stone platforms where the sound is loudest. They look like boulders at first—mottled, dark-surfaced, with a faint, iridescent sheen that mimics the rock beneath them. But if you watch, they breathe.

Not in air. They breathe in vibrations.

Each resonator is a hollow body, armored in segmented plates of fused stone and metal slag. Inside those shells is a complex chamber lined with membranes of transparent tissue: thin as a soap bubble but impossibly strong, held taut by internal pressure and tuned to precise frequencies. When the roar rises from beneath the water or rolls through the air, they flex.

You see them ripple as they hum back at the world.

The resonators don’t move much. They’re slow, deliberate creatures. Growing like a reef over time, they embed themselves on the stone platforms and gradually shape the local acoustics. They push out low, answering tones that reinforce the world’s roar—a deep undulating drone that feels like it’s tuning your bones to its key.

When something loud and sudden cuts through the noise (a cracking metal tower, a sharp shift of water in the channels), their surfaces shiver and then settle into a new resonance pattern. You can feel the change: the air pressure around you shifts slightly, like a door opening somewhere invisible.

They don’t have eyes, but they perceive detail through harmonic distortion. When you walk close to them, the water trembles differently around your knees; reflected sound from your step enters their chambers and comes back altered. They *know* you’re there—not as shape or color, but as a pattern of frequencies in the water and air.

## The Things That Grow in Glass

Then there are the **Vines**, but they are more than mere vegetation.

What you first called vines are actually organisms in three interlinked phases: root, conduit, and leaf.

**The Root-Phase**  
Anchored deep into the newly made stone and metal slag beneath the water, they resemble solid networks of glassy roots, thick as your arm and darker than the surrounding rock. Their surface is etched in fine, crystalline lines that look like fracture patterns but are actually growth patterns: precise branching angles, repeated over and over again. They draw not from soil but from the faint heat and trace elements in the molten glow beneath the world. They don’t drink water so much as filter it, extracting thermal gradients and dissolved salts, leaving behind faint streams of cooler matter that trickle away in slow currents.
