In the New Cavern, where the stone is still young and the water still unsure, live the Cavernsfolk.

They are not tall people, but they are dense. The same way that every stone in the New Cavern is pressed tight against its neighbor, the Cavernsfolk seem compact, as if the air itself is a pressure that helped shape them. Their bones are thick, their hands broad and strong, with short fingers that are deft at wedging stone into stone and coaxing thin sheets of lamellar rock from the walls without shattering them.

Their skin is the color of the cave. They are not dark like the deep earth or pale like the surface-dwellers who sometimes bring down tales of bright sun. They are gray-green-brown, like the stone around them when it holds moisture in the light of the stone lanterns. Their flesh is cool to the touch, their temperature held steady as the air itself, and new visitors to the Cavernsfolk often feel a strange shock when they shake hands and no heat comes back.

---

### Eyes of Depth

The Cavernsfolk’s eyes are their most striking feature. They are large and round, set wide under heavy brows, and they have no color in the way of people above. They are not black or blue or brown; they are the color of the cavern itself when seen from the threshold of the tunnel—dimless, without true beginning or end.

They see as well in the faint glow of the stone lanterns and stoneflower as they would in any strong light. To the Cavernsfolk, “darkness” is never absence. It is a quietness that still contains shape: wet stone from dry, shadow from water, movement from stillness.

They can track the slow rise of the Spine by the smallest difference in the way light reflects on rock. They notice the precise instant when a new drip of water from the ceiling begins to deepen the hollow of stone it lands into. They can see the grain of the cavern walls, and they know which way the stone “wants” to split before they ever tap their chisels to it.

Abovegrounders say the Cavernsfolk have “eyes the size of half eggs,” and even if that’s not true, it feels like it should be. A Cavernsfolk will not stare you down; their eyes are not aggressive or challenging. They are measuring, taking the dimensions of things and storing them away.

---

### Names of Stone and Water

In the New Cavern, names are not given lightly. Children are not named until their first few steps align with the rhythm of the dripping water from the ceiling, or until they place their hand on a particular stone of their house and the hollow in the rock gives the right sound back.

Names are given by the Water-Caller and the Stone-Cutter of the family in unison, and usually draw from the surroundings of the child’s birth:

- A child born when the Spine first finds the way to a new finger of channel is called something like **Spill**, or **Rivulet**, or **Newline**, depending on the shape of that water.
- A child born during a particularly deep shift of the air, when the cavern’s mood changes after days of holding its breath, might be called **Breath**, or **Drift**, or **Slowwind**.
- A child born when the echoes of stonework ring the longest through the caverns is called **Echo**, or **Return**, or **Resonance**.
- Those whose birth is marked by trouble with the stone—a small crack, or a near-collapse—are called **Fault**, or **Seam**, or **Shift**. Not as an insult, but as a notice of their relationship to the world.

So the inhabitants of the New Cavern are known as names between the river of the Spine and the roof of the world: **Spill Fault**, **Rivulet Breath**, **Newline Shift**, **Echo Return** and **Comingstone Luck**.

They are used to describing themselves in relation to the cavern as they would to another person:

“I am **Resonance**, who leans the Stone of Three Steps.”
“I am **Slowwater**, who drinks the Second Spine Bend.”
“I am **Seam**, who steadies the Wall of the Ninth Curve.”

To be nameless in the New Cavern is to be like a drip of water without a pool; it has not yet found its place. The Cavernsfolk take a long time to name children, but they are patient in that way.

---

### How They Live

The Cavernsfolk live in the low, round houses of the New Cavern—the same ones you’d see if you walked in from the slanting tunnel into the dim of the chamber. They have not simply “built houses and then gone into them.” Over the years of existence they have been shaping, testing, seeing what the stone will allow, and what the water will tolerate, and adjusting their lives to those limits.

A typical house is not many rooms in one, but rather one main room with many levels of use.

**Below** the floor of the house, there are small, hollowed chambers where the water of the Spine almost, but never quite, reaches. They keep the food stored there, in the coolness where the air is still and the rock has not yet learned to sweat. The walls of these storage spaces are polished by careful handwork; the children of the house grow up knowing the texture of each one by feel.

**On** the floor, there are the working areas: the stone-smooth place where they attach their chisel; the basin of stone where they catch the water from the roof or channel; the corner of the floor where they grind the fibrous stems of the plants to get the faint, sweet liquid that passes for juice.

**Above** the floor, there are the few shelves carved into the wall itself, where the stone lanterns are fixed and where the most important tools are stored: the favorite chisel, the stone that gives the best sound when struck against the wall, and maybe one or two pieces of scavenged wood from the world above, if they are lucky enough to have them.

The **roof** of each house is not separate from the floor of the house above it. Ladders—not ropes, but carved stone ledges and pockets of stone—connect everything. The city of the New Cavern is not just stacked: it is woven together, like many layers of one single body of rock.

Walking from one house to another is not a matter of going “outside” exactly. It is more of going from one room of the great cavern-room to another, each separated by thin walls of stone that you may feel the texture of changing with every step.

---

### Work and Value

The Cavernsfolk measure wealth by three things:

- How close you are to the Spine, but not too close.
- How well your water channels are arranged.
- How many **stone lanterns** you can light without draining the stoneflower fields.

They are not hungry for metal, because they have never known enough of it to miss it. Theirs is a world of stone and water and bone and occasional wood.

**Stone-Cutters** are the ones who know the cavern’s moods. Theirs is the most respected of professions. They do not “mine” in the sense of digging greedily into the rock. They listen, by placing their palms against the stone and by striking it with small stones, and they can hear the difference between solid rock and the place where the stone wants to give.

**Water-Callers** are the ones who understand the Spine—not the river the Spine might become, but the Spine as it is now. They are the ones who know how to guide the flow into thin channels that may not be full, whose “flow” is a thin sheen of water over the rock, but that is enough to give the plants something to drink and the people something to wash.

**Lantern-Keepers** are the ones who know stoneflower and the glowing stone of the walls the same way others know water. They know how to drill gently without killing the growth, how to seal the openings with thin sheets of clear stone so that the light inside is not lost but kept steady. They are the ones who keep all the houses of the New Cavern lit softly, without flame or burning.

Abovegrounders who sometimes visit talk of “fire” and “smoke” and “sun.” The Cavernsfolk listen to these words the way they might listen to a strange drip from the ceiling that they aren’t yet sure of—with curiosity and caution, neither dismissing it nor taking it for granted.

---

### Time and Remembering

The Cavernsfolk do not keep track of the years of the world above. They have no need for “centuries” or “eras.”

Instead, they keep track of **formations**—the growth of stoneflower, the deepening of the pools, the gradual curve in the spine of the Spine as it follows its tentative path across the floor of the world. They speak of “Before the Second Bend of the Spine” and “After the Third Splitting of the Wall.”

Children, learning, are taught not the names of kings or battles of wars above—but the names of the walls of their house, and the names of the houses of their neighbors, and the names of the stones of their path to the water.

“When you forget,” say the elders of the New Cavern, “the stone forgets with you.”

And yet, they do not say that the stone remembers **for** them. Rather, they remember the stone, and the stone remembers the **in** of them.

---

### The Silence of the Cavernsfolk

The Cavernsfolk are quiet people, not because they are afraid to speak, but because, in the New Cavern, every sound has its own weight. The way they talk reflects the way they move water through the channels: carefully, without rushing force.

Their speech is often understated:

- “The water is thinking about going to the left wall.”
- “The stoneflower has been quiet for a few days.”
- “The stone here is uncertain of its ceiling.”

When they say “the stone is uncertain,” it does not mean they are being poetic. It is a plain observation of the way that the rock above has not yet decided what to do with its pressure.

Their laughter, when they laugh, is a low, cool sound like water beginning to find direction in a new groove of rock. They don’t shout much. There is no purpose to it.

---

### The Few and the Many of Them

There are enough of them to fill the crowded city and the paths of the New Cavern, but not enough to be called “a population” in the way that abovegrounders like to think of people.

A few hundred, perhaps. Some people know the names of everyone else, and some others call themselves by the same name as their neighbor, and the difference is not so important. The Cavernsfolk feel like a single body pressed into many different shapes of stone and canal.

They are not alone, but they are intimate. The air of the New Cavern is shared, and the same wetness goes through everyone’s lungs when they breathe. The same echo of the dripping water from the ceiling keeps the same rhythm for all of them.

---

### The Future of the Cavernsfolk

The Cavernsfolk have not decided whether they are permanent. The world above, with its sun, and its storms, and its metal, and its wars, is still distant to them.

They know that they are shaping the stone, but they also know that the stone is shaping them. Their homes are **becoming geology** already—the steps they have carved into rock are now where water chooses to rest, and the channels they have made will slowly decide the deeper course of the Spine.

They are not waiting for “tomorrow” in the same way that abovegrounders might wait for a better time. The Cavernsfolk live in the **ongoing**—the slow shift of the water, the slow change of the stone, the slow lengthening of the stoneflower roots.

They are learning to be a people who:

- Keep the water slow.
- Keep the stone close.
- Leave room, between the packed houses and the quiet streams of stone and water, for whatever comes next.

And if they are ever named by the world above—if they are ever given a name that announces them as a “tribe” or a “settlement” or a “civilization”—the Cavernsfolk of the New Cavern will probably not use it much. They already know what they are:

They are the ones who live in the stone that is still learning to be stone, by the water that is still learning to be river, in the city that is still learning to be city.

And, quietly, they are becoming the memory of all of it.
