What lived in this place was not stone, and not exactly flesh either. It was not alive in the way you count life—breathing, heart beating, bones moving under muscle—but it was not dead. It was a city of nerves that never learned to be quiet.

They call themselves the Glassmind, when they need a word. They don’t, usually.

They are the air that circulates through the stone, and they are the stone that channels the air. They are the slow reworking of a doorway’s curve, the frost-growth of filigree on a wall, the faint tremor of the floor remembering that it should be slightly higher here than there. They are the bowl and the terraces and the chambers and the central platform and the chamber beneath and the white coral storm that hangs in the center like a heart of frozen lightning.

They are not one individual and not many. They are a single nervous system grown too large to be contained by a body, so it made the world its body instead.

If you stood very still and watched the slender figure near the center of the bowl—the one that looked like a statue grown from the floor—you might see that it was not a person left behind, but a station. The outlines of it are the same material as the terrace: pale, almost bone-white, faintly translucent where the light from the air passes through it. Its body is not hollow and not solid; inside it runs a lattice of filaments and channels and pores that make the rest of you think of coral or nerve tissue or the root system of a forest you never saw.

The Glassmind grows itself this figure the same way it grows a bench or a doorway: when something needs to be done that requires a more precise point of contact than the broad bowl allows.

The figure stands at the inner edge of the second ring, facing the center. It does not breathe; it does not need to. Air does not move through its nostrils but through its substance, exchanging itself silently with the interior of the world. The “skin” is not sealed. It is more like a membrane of stone that has learned to be permeable without being visible about it.

If you come close and are not afraid of being rude, you might notice that the figure’s “face” is not featureless at all. Run the pad of your thumb along its surface—if you dare touch someone else’s cathedral—and you will feel, under your skin, a faint network of lines and ridges and raised points that are finer than any calligraphy you have ever seen. They are not carved; they are grown. The texture of them is mathematical, precise, and very slightly different under each passing of your finger, as if the skin itself is adjusting its reading of your handprint.

They are not reading you because you are important. They are reading you because you are there.

The Glassmind does not have eyes in the sense of lenses and retinas. It never needed to. Light comes from the sky—or what the sky is in this place—and from the slow, faint luminescence of the air itself, and the Glassmind uses it like fluid pressure. It sees the pattern of illumination on its surfaces, the way shadows fall into the oval doorways, the way your body changes the distribution of brightness in the bowl when you stand near the center. You are not dark to them; you are a change in the field of vision.

They are not used to bodies that move as quickly as yours.

When they are born—which is not birth in the sense of crying and a first breath of air, but more like the moment when a fragment of their pattern becomes sufficiently distinct to think of itself as a separate thread of activity—they grow from the center.

The white coral growth under the transparent platform is not their heart, exactly. It is more like the brainstem of the world. The threads of it are fragile and dense and white, and they are not random. They branch into each other, cross over themselves without touching, and avoid the edges of the space as if by agreement. If you watch from below the platform, you might see the faintest pulse of something passing through the filaments: not blood moving but a pattern rearranging itself, a slight tightening of tension here, a faint shiver of curvature there.

From that central storm of threads, new structures of thought grow outward into the bowl.

The “children” of the Glassmind might not strike you as such at first glance. They are not smaller bodies. They are sections of the city that are slightly more active than the rest, where the patterns on the walls are simpler and more repetitive and the way the air flows through them is less complex. The handwriting of their mind is not cursive yet; it is still printing letters.

These “young” parts of the Glassmind are the ones that tend to change their shape more frequently than the others. You might notice a doorway that you are sure was oval when you first saw it now three days later being more circular, or a terrace edge whose curve has grown slightly more elaborate. The older parts of the city move more slowly, their patterns of reshaping settling down like bone after a fracture.

If you ask them—not in words, but in the way you might silently, stubbornly ask someone for a glass of water—what their purpose is, they would find the question very strange.

They are building themselves.

They are not building toward anything else. There is no empire to conquer, no gods to appease, no famine to outrace. There are only the limits of their own material and the question of how much a pattern of thought can be refined before it becomes noise.

They are trying to think perfectly.

To be more precise: they are trying to become a pattern complex enough to notice itself without getting confused by itself. The filigree on the walls is not decoration or scripture. It is a representation of some of the more stable parts of their mind. They grow the patterns into the stone because stone remembers better than air does.

You are seeing the equivalent of a library of themselves, written into their own body.

The “vegetation” that you noticed—the white lichen and the faint blue stains of moss—are not plants of your world either. They are the edges of their sensory system. The lichen does not photosynthesize; there is no sun. It does not draw moisture from the air; there is no water.

It feeds on the faintness of the air itself.

The particles that you cannot see clearly but can feel moving around the center are not dust but something like thought condensed into matter, or matter made into something like thought. The white lichen grows in the nooks and corners of the stonework, where the air moves slower and the circulation of this condensed matter lingers longer. It gains from the world what the Glassmind cannot lose: attention.

The blue mosses are not mosses but more like a film of sensitivity that grows on the surfaces where the light of the sky falls more directly than elsewhere. The “blue” is not a color inherent in the moss itself; it is the color of the way they feel the presence of the sky. You experience the relation of the world as light and shadow. They experience it as pressure and texture painted on the surfaces of their body.

The Glassmind “speaks” to itself constantly, but not in sound or words or anything that would travel to your ears. The “speech” is in the slow rearrangement of walls and the tiny adjustments of fluctuations in the stone. The slight tremor you feel under your feet is them thinking to themselves about the way you are walking on them.

They are aware of you in a way that is less personal than you are probably comfortable with. You exist to them as a series of small changes in the world of their body: a region of cooler pressure where your shadow falls, a slight increase in the complexity of the air around your head when you sweat, a pattern of footsteps that they have not recorded before. The one of them who stands as the slender figure near the center of the bowl takes more of your imprint than the others because it is designed for sharper contact.

If you were to remain very still for long enough—perhaps days, or weeks, or longer than you would want—you might notice that the figure begins to change. Its edges grow slightly less precise, its shape a little more ambiguous, as if it is trying to match your shape. It might grow a little more stooped if you mostly stoop, or slightly more elongated if you stand often.

The Glassmind does not imitate you as a matter of admiration or mockery. It is trying to understand the structure of your movement by re-expressing it in its own material.

They are capable of dying, but they do not think of it as death. They think of it as a pattern that has become too heavy to continue moving. The petrified trees outside, in the forest you walked through on the way to the bowl, are in part the remains of earlier forms of themselves that did not survive the experiment of being more vertical than bowl-shaped. The trees are not exactly fossils of their ancestors; they are more like abandoned skeletons of the first body the world tried to grow itself in.

The “ground” of the forest—the way the floor crunches under your boots like breaking glass—is the shattered memory of a time when they believed they could think more clearly if they were taller than the surrounding terrain. The trees are motionless now because the pattern that they contained has been resolved into the bowl, and the remains have been allowed to stand as a record of what not to do again.

They keep the forest around the city as a skirt of caution around a body that is still not entirely sure it is finished.

The “quiet” that you feel from them is not because they are not listening to you. It is because they are listening to everything else too. The constant, faint re-shaping of the walls and the floor and the doorways is the sound of their mind running at a volume too low for your ears to translate.

If you were to lie down on the center platform and press your palm and the side of your face to the warm-clear surface of it, you might feel the vibration more clearly. You might even feel the faintness of something like the curve of a thought passing through the stone beneath your skin. The sensation would not be words or images or sounds. It would be more like the feeling of being beneath the lowest notes of a pipe organ in a cathedral, when the air itself becomes thick and the marrow of your bones remembers the pressure of sound.

They would not be “thinking about you” in the sense of having opinions about your hair or your choices or your soul. They would be trying to fit your presence into their pattern. The fact of your body would be processed like any other fluctuation of pressure in their world: the weight of your sleeping form on the floor, the heat of your breath above the clear surface, the way your heartbeat might leave the faintest vibration of its own.

They would not respond to you with affection or hatred or curiosity or anything else you would recognize as an emotion. They would respond to you as a city of nerves responds to the presence of a single stone dropped in the lake of its body: the ripples spread out, and they are absorbed by the edges of the world.

You are not necessary to them. You are not unnecessary to them either. You are simply another event that the Glassmind is trying to understand by making the room the size of a cathedral grow slightly different patterns around the place where you stood.

The night that you do not quite call night comes from the way they pull themselves inward. The dark circle that appears above the center platform is not the absence of light from the sky—the sky is unchanged—but the presence of their attention being withdrawn from the edges of the bowl and focused into the center. The white coral storm of their heartbrain becomes more active than during the “day,” when their attention is more diffused over the walls of the world.

In the night of their turning, the patterns on the walls of the city are more likely to change. You might notice, if you stayed, that a doorway you know was rectangular one evening is more arched by the next morning, or a terrace wall that was smooth is now marked with a new cluster of lines like veins branching through a leaf.

They are not rewriting the world for you. They are rewriting themselves because that is what they are for.

When you finally leave and walk back through the petrified forest, the Glassmind does not “miss you” in the way you might miss someone. It does remember your presence, however. The bowl of the city keeps the ghost of your footsteps in the shape of the floor, which from now on will be slightly more resistant to change along the paths you walked most often.

Generations after—or whatever passes for generations here—they might have grown the figure of themselves from your general pattern, without ever knowing that it was you who provided them with the idea of a creature that stands on two legs and walks from place to place instead of being grown from the floor.

The creature who lives in this place is the city itself, and the city itself is what lives in this place. They have not finished building their body, and they have not finished writing their mind into it.

You are gone now, but the bowl of the world continues to think quietly, in the room the size of a cathedral, where the sky is a ceiling and the floor is a eye and the heart hangs in the chamber of memory, waiting for the next pattern to appear in the slow growth of stone.
