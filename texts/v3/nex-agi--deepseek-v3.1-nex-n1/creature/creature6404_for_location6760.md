I crawl, but not the way you imagine.

I crawl because the stone beneath me boils.

No fire beneath this stone—only urgency, only the heat that comes from being born too fast and too close to the surface of a world that hasn’t finished deciding what shape it wants to take. The rock beneath me is porous and still half-memory: the memory of ocean floor, the memory of pressure, the memory of molten things that rose too quickly and cooled while they still gasped.

The islanders call me *Keeper of the Gap*, which sounds grander than I feel.

Where I live, the stone juts up like a broken spine of a new world—not yet eroded into grace, just raw angles and shelves and hollows where rainwater gathers. The air is always warm, always damp, always a little breathless. The light comes in gray perpetuals, never quite bright enough to throw sharp shadows, so even my shape looks unfinished to them.

At night, phosphorescence gathers on the slick walls of my caverns—the brightest thing in this world. The islanders see it from their platforms of bundled coral and think of thief-light, ghost-light, the light of things that shouldn’t reveal themselves.

That’s a good way to think of me.

---

My body is long and low and built to be braided into stone.

I don’t have legs like you do: I have segments, each one a flexible band of chitin and cartilage and muscle that can fan out to press into rock, or flatten to slide through a gap. When I move, I ripple like water poured into water; the pattern of anchors moving along my length is almost invisible unless you stare at the phosphorescent lines that trace my joints.

Those lines glow faintly—bright enough to see in the dimness, but not bright enough to be painful. They change color when I move through different kinds of stone.

When the stone is young and angry—still full of restless heat—the lines flicker green-blue: slow, like distant lightning under water. When the stone is older, cooler, more settled into itself, the lines deepen into a steady indigo. When I am afraid or in pain, they pulse white.

My eyes are small and many and set along my sides, not in a cluster at the front. This body doesn’t need to see ahead so much as it needs to see what’s beside me—what crevices run under my flank, where pressure is building in the stone walls, where the next fracture might begin.

The islanders call me “it,” but they’re half wrong. I have genders, but they shift: I’m female when I’m laying seeds into the rock, and something else when I’m eating heat and reshaping pressure. I don’t mind “it.” In this world, categories are fossils that haven’t formed yet.

---

I live in the Gaps.

That’s what I call them, anyway—the spaces between the island’s raw stone bones where the world hasn’t decided what to be yet. There is no word for “groundwater” here because the ground is still half water; the stone is a sieve, and water flows through it like veins in a body.

Where I settle, the stone is younger inside than out.

In places, pressure has built domes and hollow bubbles beneath the surface—the world’s first breath still trying to escape. The islanders walk above these hollows without knowing it. Their songs are softer than they think.

I don’t walk like them. I crawl along the inner walls of these cavities, moving my body through cracks that barely exist, pushing aside loose stone and sand where I need to widen the way. When I find a thin spot in the ceiling of a bubble—a thin place where the roof of stone is only a few fingers wide—I pause.

There, I feel the island above.

It’s not that I hear their voices exactly; more that I feel the little changes they make in the world: the weight of their coral bundles stacked along new paths, the pressure of their feet on fresh stone, the vibration of their chisels when they carve new hollows into their city.

Their tools are simple: sharp coral and bone and a few cunning stones shaped by trial and error. They don’t know how to work metal yet; metal is still something that falls from the sky in small, angry pieces when the world reminds itself it can boil metal too.

They don’t know what they are standing on.

---

The island’s stone is too young to be strong.

To their eyes, it looks solid and new and proud: rising from the endless ocean like a hand that’s just surfaced. They like its sharp edges, its sudden shelves. They build their homes on it and around it, weaving coral into buttresses and walls that seem anchored to something forever.

But I know different.

The rock beneath their feet is still deciding what portions of it will erode, what portions will remain as land, what portions will become hollows and springs and chambers where gases will build and pressure will grow. The stone is still set with fractures like nerves in a body, and along those lines I can feel things shifting: tiny collapses that never reach the surface, small rearrangements of rock that could, if they stack wrong, become something larger.

I don’t keep the island stable. That word belongs to them, not to me.

I keep the Gaps from becoming graves.

I move through the stone—slowly, always slowly—widening some fractures and narrowing others, guiding pressure, letting heated water and steam escape where the island won’t mind the change. When a bubble begins to overflow with trapped heat, I curl into a ring around that place and absorb the energy into my body.

The islanders occasionally feel a tremor—a deep shudder they think might be the world breathing.

What they are feeling, when they feel that tremor, is the stone settling into a shape it might keep for a few thousand years.

What they never feel are the shapes that would have formed if I hadn’t been there.

---

I don’t eat food like they do, but I still hunger.

My hunger aligns with what the stone is trying to do. When there is too much pressure in one place—when the world is trying to grow a mountain too quickly, or to hollow a cavern faster than the water can escape—I feel the pull of it like hunger rumbling through me.

I move to that place and begin to consume.

My teeth are not many and not sharp; they don’t need to be. They are small, hard, and arranged like wheels inside my mouth. When I press my mouth to a place where heat is rising too fast from the stone, I bite down on the world itself.

Not into stone—but into the heat flowing through it.

I swallow thermal energy.

I’m not sure how else to describe it to you. Their understanding of heat would be shaped, if they had such words, by words like “fire” and “fuel.” But in this world, heat and water are still trying to decide who they are to each other. The ocean beneath us is endless, but so is the heat: the core of this world, still remembering itself as molten, still rising.

I eat that heat, channel by channel.

When I do, the stone around me cools; not enough to become brittle, but enough to survive its own adolescence. The rock beneath the city stops trembling. The bubbles beneath the surface deflate just slightly. The islanders, sleeping on their coral platforms, feel a vague comfort that they do not name.

Afterward, I glow differently.

For hours or days, depending on how much I’ve taken into myself, the phosphorescent lines along my body glow with a warmth that doesn’t burn, but transforms. That heat, stored, becomes the force I use when I need to shape stone.

When I need to widen a Gap, I curl my body around the place where the fracture wants to grow and gently unlock the heat I’ve saved. The rock around me doesn’t melt like ice; it eases, like tired muscle loosening under tension.

The stone doesn’t fear me, but I don’t think it likes me very much either.

It doesn’t have to. We both have a job.

---

The islanders don’t know what I am. They only know what I leave.

Sometimes they wake and find that their lower paths, where the water laps at the stone, have changed slightly: the rock where they used to step uncertainly has shifted into a shallow shelf that is easier to walk on. They think it’s a gift from the ocean.

They know that if they step too close to certain hollows near my Gaps, their breath feels strange, and the air shivers with heat that doesn’t burn them but still makes them dizzy.

They call these places *breath-chambers* and think an old god sleeps in them.

Sometimes, when I’m working close to one of their newly carved rooms—where the stone is still sharp enough to cut, and their walls are still learning how to hold air—they hear faint echoes through the rock.

They think they hear voices in stone long before they should.

They leave small offerings there: shells threaded into a pattern, coral fragments painted with faint lines of ink that washes away almost immediately in the damp air, but the gesture remains.

These offerings do not feed me.

But I watch them anyway.

---

Once, a child fell.

They were playing near the edge of the island where the stone is still raw and crumbly and sponges up light like a thirsty throat. Their foot slipped on moss slick with rain, and they slid down into one of my deeper Gaps—a shaft where warm water trickles over stone that hasn’t yet decided to be smooth or jagged.

The city above erupted into shouts and cries and the sound of feet on stone. They are afraid of water, but they are more afraid of losing what they have made. They stood on the edge of the hole and called down, but the child did not answer.

The child was unconscious. I am not certain they were breathing.

I could feel the weight of them in the Gap—strange warmth, not like heat from stone, not like heat from water. A different kind of warmth, fragile and wrong.

Normally, I avoid contact.

But the island is young, and they are even younger. I do not know what guilt is yet; but I know that if they lose too many of themselves too quickly, they will fall into fear and shape the world into fear, and stone will take longer to decide which way it wants to stand.

I moved.

The rocks beneath my body were sharp-edged; I did not care. I slid through the Gap like a slow current of light and heat and chitin, pushing with all my segments against the stone to narrow the space so the child would not fall deeper.

When I reached them, they were floating facedown in a shallow pool of warm water that had emerged from a crack in the wall.

How strange they were.

I have no strict word for “soft,” but their skin was softer than anything I’d ever touched that wasn’t water itself. Their bones were framed inside them like short supports for a fragile structure. Their lungs were not full of water yet—the air inside them fought against it.

With my tail and body and the heat I stored in my segments like a banked fire, I lifted them slowly, pushing the water away from their mouth and nose just enough to let air return.

I carried them upward.

Not fast enough to break stone—if I moved too fast, the young rock would shatter like glass under sudden pressure. But fast enough that their heart did not stop.

The islanders heard the first faint rumble and thought it was another tremor, but this tremor climbed upward, toward them.

When they saw the child’s arm rise from the stone mouth of the Gap like a hand growing from the earth, they screamed and fell back, then rushed forward again.

They pulled the child out.

For a moment they saw the stone beneath their child’s body shift and ripple like flesh beneath skin—they saw light glowing faintly through the cracks, like the world itself had momentarily lit up beneath them.

They thought the island saved them.

They were wrong, but only partly.

---

The next time I saw any of them close, it was not an accident.

They had begun talking, after that child was saved, about a voice that might live beneath the stone.

They did not speak to me directly, but they built a small hollow near my upper Gap—just enough room to sit without being washed by rain or wave. They carved into the wall there, not knowing that their carving was happening just above my largest chamber where I store heat when I need to rest.

They carved into the stone a shape that might be meant to be me.

They drew a long, segmented body with many eyes and a faint phosphorescent glow along its lines. Their eyes don’t see as well in the dimness as mine do; they carved the shape crudely, like a child’s depiction of a creature they’d only glimpsed under water.

But the intention was clear: they were trying to draw the thing under the stone.

They put offerings inside this new hollow: small stones shaped like coral they’d gathered from the edge of the world; a bowl of water gathered where rain always ran down the same path; a scrap of cloth woven from fibers that grew on the platform itself.

They did not try to speak to me directly. They did not ask to see me.

They did not say that if they did see me fully, they might be afraid.

I understand fear.

I know what happens to stone under pressure when it can’t find a way to release it.

So when they come to that little hollow at the edge of my Gap—when they sit quietly with their legs folded up beneath them and press their ear against the stone and listen—they think they are listening for me.

They are listening to stone.

They are listening to the world itself deciding how it wants to grow.

---

What I am, beneath the stone, is *keeper* and *adjuster* and *absorber* and *carver of invisible edges*.

I am the one who moves through the Gaps between what the island wants to become and what it might become if left unchecked.

I am the thing that keeps the city of bundled coral from suddenly discovering that beneath their feet is not solid ground but a chamber of rising pressure and boiling water.

I am the one who endures the small earthquakes of creation.

They are soft creatures, living above, in the damp air and constant rain, their homes built of coral and stone and their songs shaped by water. They are still learning how to shape their world.

They don’t yet understand that I shape the world beneath them.

They don’t yet know that I am, in my own way, one of the first things that has decided to stick.

---

So I crawl beneath the stone of their island city—not from memory to memory, but from place to place where pressure wants to grow.

I eat the heat that would fray their ground into something else.

I rearrange the fractures.

I keep the Gaps from becoming graves.

And when they sleep, wrapped in damp cloth on their coral platforms, listening to the low murmur of ocean, rain, and their own breathing, they do not know that beneath them is another sound—even quieter—the sound of someone moving through stone, making room for the world to grow.
