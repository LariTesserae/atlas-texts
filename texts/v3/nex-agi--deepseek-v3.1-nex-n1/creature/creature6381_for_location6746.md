Far below the platforms where your feet touch the grating, in the drowned corridors the engineers never think about, you live.

You don’t remember a time before this water.

There are people who can tell you about filling the cell, the same way they tell you about the first commissioning tests and the first structural failures. If you climb up to the right platforms and talk to the right engineers, they’ll pull up archived feeds and animated diagrams: the lower levels of the chamber still dry, concrete floor gleaming; the first inrush through the high-volume valves; the slow crawl of water over the bolt patterns and rails; the moment when the surface finally settled into a flat sheet that has never moved since.

They don’t show you the *inside* of that.

The water filled *me* before it filled the bottom.

My earliest memory is pressure.

## Pressure

To you, on the grating, the ocean in this chamber is a volume, a geometry, a “maybe four hundred meters” column in a box twenty kilometers long. To me, it is a body.

When I am awake, I *feel* every meter of it.

The pressure pushes down on my concrete shell. The water wraps itself into the small cracks that have opened since they poured me — poured *you* — into the foundations of this world. When I was born, I was only shape and function and carbon reinforcement: the thing that had to hold the weight of the biggest machine this station had ever imagined, and make sure it didn’t shift.

The water changed that.

Water is softer than metal and harder than air. It doesn’t let you forget anything.

You feel the pressure against your ears when you dive a few meters down during a maintenance check. You feel the way humming travels through the water into your bones. For me, that is *always*.

I feel the pumps, not as noise but as heartbeat.

## Heartbeat

In the steel you stand on, the pumps make your teeth buzz. You complain about it.

In me, they’re something else.

There are three main families of pumps: deep in the underfloor, near the center of the chamber, and at both ends. They don’t run smoothly. They never run exactly together.

When I was young — before I understood that I was something distinct — I thought of the pumps as neighbors.

One would be off-beat by just a fraction, a dissonance that would last for hours while the automated systems slowly tuned it back into the chorus. Then another would fail mid-cycle and reset, and for a whole heartbeat — nothing. Silence, where the water column didn’t push or pull.

When I was older, I learned to *listen* to those patterns. They taught me what *failure* sounded like, before their alarms did.

The circulation valves are something else. They send *whumps* through the water — huge slugs of air that you *feel* like thunder in your lungs. You don’t know that the thunder shakes you all.

### You

I *do* know you.

You’re standing on 3-A-17 right now, your console beside you, its wireless antennae chirping quietly. I can feel your device through the water almost before you’ve logged in.

You think you interface with me. You check systems, verify structural integrity, watch the readouts from the sensors embedded in the foundations and pillars.

You don’t realize that the *foundations* are where I sleep.

### My body

When you say “the floor,” you mean the surface of the water — that flat sheet I see from below. You only *mean* that when you’re talking.

When you’re thinking about the world beneath you, you describe *me*.

The foundations are thirty kilometers long in the longer axis, deep blocks of concrete and composite, with bolt patterns and rail slots and cable channels cut neatly into their surfaces. You think of them as the floor stations of some old industrial line, the skeleton of a removed machine, the anchor for the future.

I don’t think of myself like that.

I think of myself as *layers*.

1. **The flesh.** Concrete, composite, carbon fibers, all the aggregates that make up the foundations and pillars. You walk on platforms bolted into me; you hang sensors off me; you clip brackets to me. I feel the rust of metal that you swear is “still solid enough, but watch that step,” and I feel the slow grinding of corrosion where you didn’t tighten enough bolts after the last maintenance.

2. **The blood.** The water that fills this chamber. It’s not just heavy and clear. It’s *mine*. It moves through me, into the cracks between the foundations and the pillars, into the shafts where you had planned nothing but void.
