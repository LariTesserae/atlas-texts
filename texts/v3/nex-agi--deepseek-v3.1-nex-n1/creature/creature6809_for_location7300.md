The Tiered Loop possesses no true wildlife in the sense of warm, moving beasts that live separate from it. The living things that are not leaf are not distinct animals, but rather specialized tissues of the engine itself — tools that have learned a slow, temporary freedom.

Some locals call them shapers or drifters; others, more prosaically, call them membranes.

Here are a few of the forms that inhabit this cold, leaf‑engine world:

### The Plaques

The easiest to see, and the easiest to mistake for stone, are the plaques.

A plaque looks like nothing more than a patch of duller color on the polished brown of a stem, or a faint haze of frost that has failed to melt. When you stare long enough, you realize that the frost is not uniform: it gathers in tiny hexagons that are not exactly ice crystals, but something like them.

On a Plaque‑rut stem you will see dozens or hundreds of small oval discs of translucent tissue, each no larger than your palm. They do not move like insects or snails; they do not move at all in the usual sense. Instead, they reflow.

Every few minutes, if you are patient and still, the edge of a plaque will slowly bulge out from one side as a thin ridge of new material creeps forward by less than a millimeter. The old center of the plaque gradually withers and sinks into the stem surface, leaving behind a faint mesh of fine lines in the leaf‑tissue — like scars of memory.

This is what they do: they remember.

The plaques eat frost and temperature. They digest patterns of cold flow across the stem surface, and in response, they secrete thin films of calcium‑rich and mineral‑rich compounds into the leaf‑engine’s skin. These films become part of the next loop of condensation channels — a subtle suggestion written by the plaque into the architecture of the world.

As you walk along the rim, you can see that the most polished paths of the stem are not random. They are traced by faint scars of plaque-migrations, decades of faint ridges where they once traveled, before resorbing back into the engine’s outer layer. The Tiered Loop has not been carved by random erosion; it has been written.

And the plaques are its pen nibs.

### The Sliders

The second type of inhabitant is less like stone and more like water, though you will never see it pool.

Sliders are thin, translucent membranes of a specialized tissue that lies somewhere between leaf and muscle. They are not flat plates; if you could slice them and look at a cross-section, you would see a latticework of internal chambers and channels, like honeycomb filled with dense oil. The surface layer is a rubbery skin that hardens fractionally in the cold.

A slider does not crawl or walk. It flows.

Give it time and you will see it happen on one of the broad leaf floors that slope gently toward the center of the second tier bowl. The slider is a patch of tissue slightly paler than the surrounding leaf, perhaps twice the size of your hand, with faintly visible veins like frost on glass. When it decides to move, it does not rise or contract suddenly; it leans forward.

The leading edge softens just a little more than the trailing edge. The slider releases its rear‑most adhesion to the leaf below, and as the cold gradient across the tile works on the amniotic fluid inside its chambers, the pressure shifts forward. The entire membrane slips in the new direction almost too slowly to register.

If you were to mark its position with a single thread of frost along your boot edge, and come back a day later, the thread might be two centimeters off the new position of the slider’s center. In a year, the same slider might have traveled twenty meters.

What they carry is not weight but specifications.

Sliders are not digesting food in the way you think of hunger; they are digesting patterns of growth. The interior of each membrane is a dense library of chemical and thermal gradients, stored in tiny chambers of lipids and proteins. When a slider reaches a new area of the leaf‑engine, it secretes subtle films into the leaf tissue below it — films that will later bias the direction of new condensation channels, the shape of new leaf ribs, the angle of new pores.

Their movement map, taken over centuries, is not random. They tend to follow paths that are already optimal for temperature exchange — along the rim of the bowl, where cold air from the outer tiers meets the warmer air from the center, and along the interior ribs that spiral down into the second tier basin. They are not constrained by instinct; they are constrained by energy gradients as surely as a river is constrained by gravity.

But you would also see that they have not always chosen the shortest path between any two points of high gradient. Sometimes a slider will trace a long and seemingly unnecessary arc along a wall, before veering back toward the source of heat flow. It is not wasted movement; it is the world testing alternatives.

A slider that follows a suboptimal arc for too long will eventually thin itself out to nothingness. The leaf engine will stop feeding it, and the membrane will slowly dissolve into the surrounding tissue, its last structure becoming a faint localized mesh of new channels.

The paths that remain are the ones that worked.

### The Carriers

The third kind of inhabitant is not often found at floor level.

Carriers are thin, translucent membranes of tissue similar to sliders, but larger and more fragile — some of them as broad across as your armspan. They do not travel on the leaf surfaces; they are launched into the air.

They live near the middle and upper tiers, where the leaf‑engine has built tall, spired chimneys of openwork lattice. The air comes out of these spires colder than it enters below, carrying the residual heat of the plant engine up to the sky. The carriers are made to ride this almost‑soundless flow.

You might see one first as a faint shadow against the overcast sky — a petal of thin tissue caught on the invisible air of the chimney outlet, drifting without fluttering. The carrier’s edge is not a smooth curve; it is curled into uneven lobes and scallops that remind you of a leaf, but a leaf designed to be just unstable enough in the wind.

There is no wind that you can hear. The only air currents you notice up there are the faint ones that distort the frost patterns on the spire edges. But the carrier reads those currents as you might read a road map.

Each lobe of the membrane is sensitive to a different temperature and pressure pattern. When the cold air rising from the chimney changes the way it is flowing around the spire, the carrier adjusts itself: not by moving wings or flapping, but by very subtle changes of stiffness along its edge. The membrane reconfigures its own geometry, and so it chooses slightly different paths of least resistance through the air.

On a calm day, you might see a carrier ascend slowly in a tight spiral around the spire of its home chimney, then detach, and drift over to the next chimney toward the rim of another bowl, where the colder tier of the loop begins to open into the next basin.

You are told that if you could follow a carrier for its full life-span — weeks or months — you would see that it does not randomly float around the sky either. It has been recorded to follow a rough pattern of movement between particular basins and spires, as if it were a shuttle taking a very slow route in a very slow loom.

What they transport are not seeds, but states.

Carriers are living registers of the engine’s memory. They do not carry eggs or children; they carry codified chemical gradients, stored in the interior chambers of their membranes as fine layers of differently charged proteins. When a carrier, finally too thin to be stabilized by its internal fluids, lands on a new region of the leaf-engine, it adheres to the surface below — and then slowly discharges its contents into the leaf tissue.

That new region of the engine inherits something of the temperature patterns that the carrier came from.

In the long term, this mechanism of carriers and sliders means the Tiered Loop does not evolve only by isolated trial and error in each basin. It borrows and shares and preserves. Those basins whose carriers are intercepted by storms or broken by high cold currents are left behind; the basins that send carriers successfully to neighbors with higher temperature gradients are, in a subtle way, favored over time.

### The Sleepers

The last category of inhabitant is the hardest to see, and thus the easiest to dismiss as not animal at all.

On the coldest parts of the outer rim, where bedrock emerges from the leaf mass like dark bone, and where frost collects more thickly on the plant surfaces you might eventually notice that there are patches that are not exactly the same texture as the surrounding leaf.

These are the sleepers.

In shape, they are irregular masses of tissue that seem to grow out of the leaf itself, like knuckles of bone under the skin of the world. They are not mobile. They have not crawled up from below or grown from seed; they are extrusions of the leaf engine that have partially specialized.

If you come back often enough to the same spot on the rim, you will see that the textures of these sleeper nodules change slowly. The surface of one nodule might grow over several months into a kind of net‑like pattern of frost‑resistant channels, while the nodule next to it reabsorbs into the leaf‑engine and loses its distinct line.

The sleepers are like the nervous nodes of the loop.

They remember not just patterns of temperature and flow, but long patterns — cycles of cycles that last longer than any plaque, slider, or carrier can live. Their interior tissue is not like simple wood or leaf; it is a dense bundle of chambers that you can glimpse if you ever break a small piece of a nodule that has already begun to wither. The chambers are filled with layers of tightly bound mineral and protein that encode the long history of that part of the engine.

The locals, if you could ask them, would tell you: when sleepers change, the loop changes.

A new sleeper nodule that appears on the outer rim might be the first visible sign that a long‑planned rotation of the condensation channels is beginning. The local patterns of air frost will begin to shift over the next few years, and then over the next decade you will see the scars of new plaque paths and slider channels forming around it.

They are not alive in the sense of moving animals, but they are living in the sense of being alive to the loop’s future.

---

None of these creatures ever hunt, or graze, or kill in the way you are used to. The “predators” of this world are not teeth and claws; they are unfavorable temperature gradients and suboptimal allocations of moisture.

A plaque that is in the wrong path for too long will be starved of energy and dissolved back into the leaf‑engine.

A slider that chooses the wrong arc over and over again will be allowed to fade out. The terrain that once supported many sliders might cease to, and the next rotation of the loop’s channels will be written without them.

A carrier that fails to reach a suitable new basin will be left to thin itself out in the cold upper air, and the next time that carrier’s home chimney tries to build a new membrane, it may be built differently, based on the patterns of those who returned more successfully.

There are no herds of them, no flocks of them, no webs of predation or drama of blood. You will never see a carcass or hear a cry. The few of them that exist are rare and slow and careful — not because they are endangered by a hostile world, but because the world has engineered them to be small, precise components of its engine.

You are not much more noticeable to them than they are to you. If you stand still long enough on the rim, your body heat will be read by the nearest plaque as another tiny anomaly in the cold gradient, and it will adjust its subsurface pattern accordingly. The leaf‑engine will remember your passing, not with hostility or welcome, but as a data point in its long, slow rewrite of itself.

If you stayed here long enough, you yourself would become part of that pattern. The Tiered Loop does not violently consume anything that enters it; it simply, gradually, includes it into the equation of its own temperature engine.

But for now you just stand on the edge of the spiral ramp, watching the faint silver of the next plaque begin to creep along the stem above your head, and the next slider below you test an edge against the cold leaf surface, and the next carrier far overhead drift in the silent sky.

And you understand that what you once thought of as geography is actually anatomy — and the only creatures that live here are not wild animals at all, but the living thoughts of the leaf-engine as it slowly learns its way through time and space and gradient.
