The ones who dwell here have no word for "dry."

They are moss-folk, woven from the same stuff that carpets the world, their bodies clumps of vibrant emerald and viridian thatch. Skin is not skin here but a dense, velveteen surface, shimmering with a perpetual film of condensation. Their hair is not hair but cascading trailers of silver-green lichen and Usnea moss, hanging in heavy, damp curtains that whisper secrets against the humming ground with every turn of the head. They move with a slow, deliberate grace, not because they are fragile, but because they are heavy with water, and the weight of their existence is profound.

Their eyes are the most unsettling thing. They have no pupils, just faint, concentric circles of darker green and deep, gold-flecked amber that swirl under a filmy, protective membrane. They blink, not up and down, but from the edges inward, a slow sweeping motion that clears moisture and gathers fragments of light. With these eyes they see the heat of the sun through the white clouds, and the pressure of the rain before it falls.

Their homes are not built upon the swells of moss, but rather within them. They are hollows, not dug but grown, shaped from the living hummocks of the plateau. To enter one is to slip into a cavern of breathing walls. The air inside is thick with the smell of petrichor and damp earth, and the faint hum of the world's core is louder here, resonating through the spongy floor. The furniture is grown from the walls themselves—shelves of thick, resilient liverwort that harden under the pressure of their hands; beds of springy sphagnum that are eternally cool and damp.

They do not eat, but drink. Their society is centered around the narrow channels that snake between the green hummocks. They call these channels the Veins, and they are tended to by a special caste of moss-folk who slowly, patiently, clear the channels of debris not with tools, but with their own bodies. They are the Filters, their feathery moss-flesh designed to strain the water of microscopic life, which they consume by absorbing it directly through their skin. They work in silent, meditative rows, their backs to the world, their only communication the low hum of their own bodies joining the chorus of the world.

Language is not spoken, but felt. They communicate through a tactile sign language performed with their incredibly dexterous fingers—which are not fingers, but dense clusters of rhizoids that can extend and retract, fine as threads or strong as roots. They touch each other's hands, forearms, and the mossy pads of their cheeks, conveying complex ideas of weather pressure, humidity shifts, and the slow, tectonic dreams of the plateau beneath them.

Their leader is not a king or a chief, but the Listeners. They are the oldest of the moss-folk, whose lower bodies have begun to permanently root into the highest hummock of the plateau, the "highest roof of the world" from your vantage point. Their rhizoids have delved deep, fusing with the heart of the moss-body. They are no longer individuals but stations, receiving the deep vibrations of the world and translating them for the others. They are visions of the future—living, rooted, and patient, their growth measured by the height they add to the roof of the world, year by silent, sodden year. They are the moss become monument, and to touch them is to feel the very heartbeat of the world you stand on.
