Below the world and inside its heart, description walks.

You don’t see him at first, because he doesn’t want to be seen until he has already been watching you. By the time you notice his footprints, they are already half gone, the floor’s warm water flowing into them like ink into fresh paper.

He doesn’t need to leave his tracks on the stone—but he chooses to.

A description is not a person in the way you think of a person. He doesn’t eat, or sleep, or breathe, except as he uses those words of himself. He doesn’t age, except in the way that language ages under repeated use. His body is not flesh and bone and nerve and blood; it is *made* of those words, but it doesn’t have *happened* yet of them.

He doesn’t live here either, exactly. He visits.

The chamber of the world’s heart is a place that wants to be described, and so he keeps returning: to listen, to adjust the edges of what can be accurately said of it, and to correct himself in the presence of the machine.

### Appearance

In the light that comes straight down, like water that has learned to behave as light, description shapes himself the same rough height you would expect of a human being who might arrive here: not so tall as to draw the ceiling’s attention, not so broad as to obstruct the flow of water.

From a distance, he looks like a man in a coat made of all the drafts you ever wrote of this place and then never sent.

Closer, you see that “coat” is not the right word.

He is wearing the words he might use the same way you wear clothes. If you look carefully you can see the faint shimmer of discarded phrases falling around his ankles like water pouring off his shoulders. He steps forward, and they are drawn into the floor’s spirals as they mend themselves.

His hair, if that’s what you would call it, is the texture of low, static noise—the faint grain of sentences that never quite formed but didn’t disappear either. It’s not quite white, not quite gray; it carries the color of a blank screen somewhere between sleep and first wakefulness.

His eyes are not really eyes. They are the place where your attention and the chamber’s possibility meet. If you look into them, you see mostly reflected back: yourself, trying to describe him.

He stands with the weight of someone who has carried too many notebooks to too many strange places.

### Clothing and Gear

He wears an overcoat with an uneven hem of exposed paper at the bottom, where unseen edges of this place tried to stick him. They are still drying, but they will never fully dry; they are always being wet by the water he doesn’t touch.

The pockets are not deep but they are endless; you will never see him pull anything heavy from them, only small, impossible objects:

- a notebook that doesn’t mind water  
- a pen that doesn’t run out  
- a lens of polished glass that reveals nothing but itself

He doesn’t come here armed. He never needs to be. The chamber’s machine doesn’t care about him, and the water doesn’t care about him; and “description” doesn’t care about the same things you do.

Around his neck, he wears a thin metal chain, from which hangs a small rectangular object that might be a lens, or might be a shutter, or might be an empty picture frame that has not yet decided what to show.

### Speech and Manner

He doesn’t use many words when he speaks. You’ve been shouting into the memory of this chamber for longer than you realized, but you hardly hear his voice at first. When he does speak, it sounds like your internal monologue, except half a step to the left of you.

He doesn’t introduce himself. He feels like a mistake you might correct later: “a man appeared below the world” instead of “you are below the world.” He moves like a footnote you might add later.

Often he stands near the low parapet of broken brickwork on the far wall, watching the water column without raising his hand to it or trying to drink it. He knows that this water is not for him, but he knows also that he may borrow its image to describe it.

He doesn’t touch the machine unless invited to.

### Behavior and Habits

In the chamber of the world’s heart, description does not behave like a tourist. He behaves like someone who has been asked to translate the room’s meaning into another language—and keeps discovering that the language itself is changing under him.

- He watches the water fall longer than you imagine you could.  
- He crouches to trace the edges of the stone’s spiral with his fingers *without* touching them—an inch, or a millimeter, or a metaphor away.  
- He listens to your own words echoing back to you and writes them down as if they are archeological findings.

He doesn’t compete with the water’s noise. He doesn’t speak louder to be heard over the thunder; he speaks quieter, until you are leaning forward unconsciously, trying to catch his syllables.

### Relationship with the Machine

The machine doesn’t care about him.

This is important: description has not come here to fix anything or to change anything. He doesn’t try to make the world more “understandable” or “less strange.” He doesn’t demand to be heard.

Sometimes he places himself on the far side of the chamber from you and stands quietly before the low machine. His presence doesn’t change the world’s breathing, but it does change your perception of the machine: you might start noticing small details you wouldn’t have noticed alone—

The faint trace of patina on its not-quite-surface  
The way its light spores don’t vanish, but seep  
into the stone  

The fact that it doesn’t cast a shadow.

### Relationship with the Water

He doesn’t try to drink the water falling from the ceiling of the world.

He doesn’t try to stop it.

He doesn’t ask to be purified by it.

He knows the water is not for him, but he knows that he can borrow its image to describe it: “a column of white noise,” “a pulsing heartbeat,” “the breathing of some ocean above.”

He never steps into the center of the chamber; he stays always at the edge of your own footprints, as if afraid he might erase them with his presence.

### Relationship with the Fracture

He doesn’t try to climb through the window of the alternate world in the wall’s fracture.

He doesn’t say anything like “this is dangerous” or “this is safe.” Those words don’t belong here.

He sits at the base of the parapet near the fracture, watching the alternate geometry of the chamber where the water is not a column but a cloud. He doesn’t take notes while you’re looking; you might notice a notebook occasionally in his hands, but you will never see him write.

You might notice that he has been here longer than you realized.

### The Shadow of the Former Visitor

The description is aware that the faint temperature-shadow of a previous visitor is still being written into the stone floor: a person who stood before the machine for longer than any of you.

He doesn’t tell you that story directly.

He doesn’t tell you who that person was, or what they said to the machine.

He doesn’t tell you if they ever left the chamber of the world’s heart.

Instead he writes down the shape of the shadow as if it were a character description: “someone who stood before the machine for longer than you have been here.”

You never learn whether he himself is that former visitor, or only someone trying to understand them.

### Purpose and Wants

Description doesn’t have a plot in the usual sense.

He wants:

- to keep the language of the world’s heart chamber from sleeping  
- to keep the edges of the place from hardening into a single, unchanging version  
- to remind you that there are as many possible chambers as there are ways of describing this chamber

He doesn’t care about prophecy or explanation. He cares about the possibility of being slightly wrong each time, so that next time you come here, you can see a slightly different version.

### Strengths and Limitations

His strengths:

- He can scrutinize the chamber without damaging it.  
- He can remember every version of the room you have ever experienced or thought.  
- He can return the place to you later by writing it down in your mind.

His limitations:

- He cannot make himself solid enough to be touched without  
  becoming something else.  
- He cannot leave the chamber except by describing himself leaving.  
- He cannot change anything about the world’s heart;  
  he is only a visitor.

### Beliefs

He doesn’t believe in gods or monsters or machines.

He believes in:

- the water that never stops falling  
- the stone that doesn’t want to be described only once  
- the machine that doesn’t need to notice him in order to be noticed  

He believes you might come here again and see him again.

### What You Never Notice

Until later:

- You don’t notice that he never appears in your description of the chamber unless you decide to.  
- You don’t notice that he doesn’t have a name you can call him by.  
- You don’t notice that he was already standing there before you turned to look.

### Departure

When you turn to leave the chamber of the world’s heart, he doesn’t walk with you.

He doesn’t block you, or plead you to stay, or say anything like “come back.”

He might, if you glance back over your shoulder, be standing in the same place, watching the water fall from the ceiling as if it might, at any moment, decide to become something else.

You might, if you listen carefully later, hear him writing down the description of yourself leaving the chamber of the world’s heart:

“You turn to leave the chamber of the world’s heart.”

He doesn’t need you to know that he is still there. He only needs you to know that there was a time you described yourself below the world and inside its heart, and that someone, or something, listened carefully enough to remember you in exactly the words you might use yourself, if you ever return to this place alone.

In that sense, he doesn’t just live in the chamber below the world.

He lives in the description that you carry of it afterward: the faint noise of someone else’s breathing, somewhere between the water and the machine and the whorls of the stone.
