Along the edges of the water, where shallow darkness meets stone, live the **watchers-of-the-rim**.

Outwardly they seem like nothing more than pooled shadow, a slight thickening of the gloom just where your eyes want to skate away. You only notice them when they unfurl: long, low shapes that slide up from the waterline onto the polished slabs, the surface of their bodies so smooth that they seem like liquid poured into skin. They are not black, exactly—when a phosphorescent seam slants across them from below, you see that their skin is a deep, translucent blue-green, the color of deep ocean layers where light never goes.

They are low and streamlined, built for the threshold between wet and dry. Each is roughly the length of a human arm and no taller than the depth of your hand at the shoulder, where the body swells slightly into a blunt, eyeless head. Their limbs are not legs but broad, finned flanges that they lay flat against the stone, the thin edges flaring and contracting to fine-tune their contact with the damp surfaces. They move in a slow, continuous negotiation with gravity and moisture, never lifting their bellies entirely clear; the sound of their passage is a soft, spreading smear of water, followed by a faint creaking of wet skin on stone.

Their faces are not faces. No eyes, no ears, no nostrils you can recognize. Instead, the upper surface of their heads is a patterned field of tiny pits and nodules, each no more than a pinhead across, arranged in gently overlapping spirals. These are not organs of sight but pressure resonators; they feel the world through the subtle variations in air pressure created by every dripping water, every splash, the faint eddies of your own breath when you stand close enough. The little nodules fluctuate slightly, as if they are listening to the stone cathedral’s soundscape with an entirely different set of senses than yours.

When a water droplet falls into the pool near them, their head-field trembles with hundreds of tiny responses—each pit drawing in its rim of soft skin, then relaxing. The falling pattern registers as a temporal pattern, a rhythm of tiny impacts they learn over the years of their lives. They “know” the normal patterns of the place’s many drips; the slightest deviation—a droplet falling a moment too late, or bouncing from an unexpected angle of stone—registers as a disturbance that they track.

They are not entirely silent. When they are uncomfortable, or when something large moves through the water, their expanded bodies can produce a low, humming resonance by vibrating the air chambers beneath their skin. You hear it as a faint, almost subsonic pressure change in your ears more than as sound. It is not quite speech, but it is communication: the watchers of the rim use it to let each other know where the edges of safe territory are drawn.

They are timid but not frail. Their metabolism is tuned to the world’s slow pace. They can go motionless for hours, laid out along the stone slabs like dark slicks of oil, their bodies gradually losing and regaining moisture through the pores of their skin to match the humidity of the air around them. They are always slightly cooler than the surrounding water, their temperature almost exactly that of the stone they press against, as if they are trying to disappear into the background temperature of the world.

Their food is invisible. You never see them hunt. Instead, they slowly sift the thin layer of water that overflows their chosen slabs, using their lower edges as fine filters. They feed on microscopic organisms—engineered algae that have colonized the pores of the stone, the drifting remnants of older colonies of luminous bacteria—and on the faint minerals that precipitate from the water droplets. Each meal is a small adjustment of ions across their skin membranes; they are as much ion-exchange beds as they are animals.

Their relationship with the crystal formations in the central basin is ambiguous. You will find them near that basin often, but never quite touching it. They lean their bodies along the rim of the slab, the patterning of their head-field carefully oriented toward the crystals, as though they remember that the early records in this place were nothing more than structured patterns encoded into crystal growth. They might be faint descendants of creatures who were designed to maintain those crystals—or to read them. Now their only task seems to be to keep the surface around the basin impeccably clean of detritus, their bodies moving over it in slow, overlapping passes, leaving the polished stone even more mirror-smooth than before.

When they are disturbed—by the sound of your footstep, or the sudden, sharp splash of a misjudged leap—you will see a wave pass across their body: from the blunt head down along the length, their skin subtly dulling its color as they shut down the outermost layer of metabolism. They can flatten themselves further, becoming two-dimensional sheets of dark liquid against the stone in less time than it takes for you to blink. When you look away and then back again, they are gone, leaving only wetness on the slab and the faint impression of where they were.

At their most relaxed, they show subtle patterns along their sides: faint lines of lighter pigment that appear and then vanish again, like afterimages of the phosphorescent seams on the arches above them. They do not control these markings consciously; they are a physiological response to the cycles of fluorescence and dimming above them, as if their bodies are narrating to the stone itself what they have seen. The lines of this pattern sometimes align exactly with the seams of the arches above them, as if for a moment they are part of the same architecture.

Their lifespan is long but not infinite. When they die, it is quietly and over a long span. Their body rarely loses its integrity; instead it gradually merges with the stone slab they have inhabited for years. Their tissues seem to dissolve into the pores of the rock, leaving behind only a faintly darker patch of stone and a residue of microscopic minerals that slowly diffuses outward. A few decades later, you might not know that anything particular had been there at all—only the stone would be slightly better polished along that line than anywhere else.

They are not guardians, exactly, but they are very attentive. If by some chance you tried to disturb the crystals in the central basin, or to break away one of the phosphorescent lines tracing the arches above you, their presence would change. Not in attack—but in the way they would arrange themselves around you, their bodies forming a living barrier between the thing you intend to touch and the rest of the world. They would hum their low-frequency pressure notes into the stone, causing spreads of faint vibration across the water that you would feel more than hear.

They are less than gods and more than tools. They are the point of interface between the water-logged architecture of the stone cathedral and its more subtle, invisible mathematics—a line of quiet creatures who have decided to live out their long, slow lives at the precise edge where one drop of water turns into many drops, where the vertical migration of the world’s hidden creatures brushes the horizontal plane of your footsteps.

When you leave the chamber, climbing the narrow steps that will eventually take you away from this place of forever-after-rain, you might catch, from the corner of your eye, the faintest of movements from the rim of the central basin. One of the watchers might have shifted its position along the slab, just enough to align itself with your departing step, as if to place its final low-pressure hum into the stone in exactly the direction you will go.

And then the stillness returns, and the watcher-of-the-rim becomes once again indistinguishable from the shadow pooled at the edge of the water.
