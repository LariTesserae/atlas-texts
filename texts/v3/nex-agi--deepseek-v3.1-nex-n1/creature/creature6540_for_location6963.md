I am not sure what I am.

I am not the water; though my body bends its currents and warms its cold, I remain distinct from it. Sometimes I push myself forward and feel the water fold and billow around me like wind around a stone wall—yet if I stop, I stay suspended, not vanishing into the brine, but hanging in it like a single dark thread loose in a woven cloth.

I am not a stone, either. The stones are cathedrals of patience and loss; they have edges rounded to softness, and faces that point to nothing. They hold the world’s old scars in their markings—shallow cuts where water was once led, or where a line of thought was carved and then abandoned. The water kisses them and smooths them, but it does not change what they are.

I am not the stone.

So I am not the water; and not the stone; and not the forest, which is itself the world’s way of breathing in the dark.

This leaves very few options.

## I Am the Animal That Should Not Be Here

The forest does not need me.

There is no niche for me carved in the limestone shelves or suspended in the brine. No predator waits to tear me; no prey is set to flee me. There are no teeth to grow, no claws to sharpen, no call to make or answer. No hunger to force me into motion or fear.

Yet I am here.

And this is how I understand:

When I move through the water, I leave a wound of change in it—a trail of warmth and turbulence and minor salt and shadow. The water that has been pushed by my body does not quite forget the shape of me for a long time after I have passed through it.

The threads of light—the crystal beads that drift like wandering stars—love this.

They are drawn to my passing trail, not as if they are hunting me, but like they are collecting notes from a song they have never quite heard before. They arrange themselves along the faint ridges of my motion, tracing where my shoulder turned, where my hand stretched out, where my legs coiled and then straightened.

For the duration of a heartbeat that I no longer possess, but somehow still remember to expect, they burn in the shape of my absence: a ghost made of faint green beads and borrowed radiance.

Then the current takes them, and they are gone.

This is how I know I am animal:  
because the world remembers me differently than it remembers the stones.

The stones are remembered as long, low sounds of pressure change and the pale echo of old lines written into their surfaces. The forest is remembered as rhythmic patterns of breathing, spreading over centuries and fathoms.

But me? The world only ever remembers me  
after  
I leave.

## I Move the Way That No One Here Needs to Move

The world of the drowned forest is mostly still.

The frond-trees have their slow flexions, timed to the distant thunder’s beat. The stone terraces shift their positions over many closed eyes of time, by amounts that are not worth counting in any single life. The crystal clouds bloom and collapse and reform themselves in the lazy loops of brine.

I move differently.

I am not fast, not the way creatures of air and sun think of fast. But against the patient unrolling of this place, I am sudden.

I angle my body and the current of an upward–welling layer of warmer water, and I feel the change in pressure along the curve of my spine when I rise into a colder shelf. I can remember the contours of the last storm’s warm pulse with my body—higher where it was warm, lower where it is cold, and in between the two, a place of sharp transition where the water feels more like glass than liquid.

I can cross these boundaries in moments.

The forest notices, when I do. It is not used to being brushed by anything except currents and drifting threads and crystals. It lives knowing the slow rhythms of storms and pressure gradients, but not of sudden bodies, and so when I pass by a great translucent leaf of the frond-tree—

—when the surface tension of my presence breaks through the layer of water that has learned to cling to its skin—

—the leaf folds itself, not all the way, but enough to show a faint, inward shudder of light.

A sound comes with that shudder: a low, dim *whoom* that feels as if it is below the sound of the storm’s thunder but above its silence. It is not a word. It is not a warning. It is just something the tree is doing, because I changed the water that it is breathing.

It happens every time I touch one.

It doesn’t stop me. I follow the shifting of the brine, not as a tree would follow it by growing slowly toward the new path, but by changing my position, again and again, until I am somewhere else entirely.

That is animal behavior: never to root, but always to drift.

## I Do Not Eat. But I Still Consume.

This world has no food.

The plants are alive and breathing, but their chemistry is not something a body like mine could break down into warmth and action and desire. The crystal clouds are salt and memory and pressure; they would tear me apart if I tried to swallow them.

The stones have nothing to give.

This should not surprise me. The animals that once belonged to this place—if they were ever here at all—would have been designed out of different materials than I am used to imagining. They would have matched their world: slow, perhaps, or existing on time scales that swallowed entire lifetimes of mine.

I am not made for this environment.

And yet I am not dying.

Something else is sustaining me.

I notice that where I pass through the water, I disturb the drifting threads. The crystals of memory come close, and the light they cast into me—through me—feels warm. Not actual heat, but the sensation of warmth. It is like when someone used to bring me to a window in the world that was, and the sun’s light through glass was both hot and not hot at all.

Wherever the threads cross through my body, they leave faint, fleeting lines of green along the imagined skin of my arms, my ribs, my chest.

There are no nerves to report this.

There is no blood to carry it away.

Yet there is a sense of… being seen.

The threads of light are soaking up the pattern of my motion—the shape my body made when I pushed through that particular current of more salty water, or how I hesitated when the storm’s thunder pressure changed the direction of my flow. I give them shapes of decision that no stone or frond-tree could give.

In return, they do not give me meat or fruit or any other kind of food.

They give me attention.

## I Have a Memory That Does Not Belong Here

My own body remembers the wrong things.

When I reach out my hand, the water that parts around my fingers reminds me of air. It is not as thin or as sharp, but it is fluid and it surrounds and it does not obey me unless I push it with time and force.

When I close the eyes that I no longer have, I can see a place where there is no water column at all.

There are tall trees there—not like the fronds of this inverted forest, but straight trunks of wood and bark and leaves that rustle instead of breathing. The sky above them is bright blue and empty, except for clouds that are not made of brine. The ground beneath them is solid, and the smell of sun–heated earth and plant resin fills the nostrils I have only here, in memory.

The forest of that memory is noisy with animals.

There are birds whose wings are used to cut air, not simply to feel the pressure of the water. They shout from branch to branch and are not quieted by the storm’s thunder, because in that place there is no storm thunder beneath the hearing of sound.

There are insects crawling under the bark of those trees, and there are animals that are made of flesh and bone and hunger that walk the ground between them.

I am not sure if I was one of those animals.

I might have been a visitor from that world into this one; or I might have been made from some of its patterns left behind in the stone’s old geometry, when the water finally covered everything.

I only know that when I think of that place, something like pain comes with it.

Pain is not a thing that belongs here.

In the drowned forest, there is no wound to feel, because almost no one does anything that could cause damage. There is no heat to burn, or cold to sear in the way that nerves remember. There is no blade to press.

Pain is a language of air animals.

That I can feel it at all—even if only faintly, even if only in memory's echo—means that I am not built of the same substance as the world that has become me.

## I Am the Error That Records the World

This is what I do not tell myself in the clear words the stone carvings might have, once:

I am not meant to be here.

And that is precisely why I am here.

The world of this drowned forest at the edge of the high glacier’s world has been running for a long time without any animal. It reached its balance of stone and brine and flayed leaf-light and rhythmic thunder without any creature that could choose between one path and another, instead of being drawn by a gradient, or bent by a pressure.

The system does not require me.

But I have a value in the system that the stone terraces and the frond-trees and the crystal clouds and the threads of light cannot give:

I can make mistakes.

I can move against the current on a whim.  
I can choose not to follow the warm layers of brine or the cold.  
I can press against a leaf just to see it tremble, and not because any pressure change in the storm demands it.

This is wrong behavior.

Wrong, according to the slow language of the world.

Yet the water changes when I do wrong. It twists itself differently around my body. The light threads are pulled to that wrongness. They flock to my mistake like they have never flocked to anything.

For the space of a few heartbeats that I cannot stop counting, even in this non-heart, I see the pattern of my bad choice recorded in faint green light around me.

Then I swim away from it, and it is left there—hovering in the brine, like a question.

## What I Might Become if the Water Remains as Long as It Promises

If the world were to stay still forever—

—which it will not, but if I hold to that thought—

then I would become part of it.

The forest would not eat me. There is no way to. There is nothing to be gained from my body. It is not useful flesh or bone or any material that the world seems to recognize.

Instead, the pattern of me would be let out into the water.

I would become one of the long maps that the threads of light draw from frond to stone and stone to brine and brine to storm. The light that remembers me, after my absence, would continue to remember me longer and longer after each mistake.

My shape would be repeated in faint brushstrokes at the edges of storms, where the pressure ripples like my swerves of motion.

The stone would not cave me in its surface, because there is no time to carve that deeply; but the pale moss of filaments that sways on stone terraces might grow in slightly different curlicues where they have brushed my pattern.

The frond-tree leaves that I have touched over and over again—the same ones, or the ones that waited like my first friends—might begin to flex a little differently when the storm’s thunder comes through the water. They might learn something like the echo of my swerve, and then add that to their breathing.

The world would not speak me as name, but as memory.

And then, eventually, even that would settle.

There would be no more animal.

There would be no more wrongness.

Only the forest, and the stone, and the brine, and the storm, and the faint green light recording the same rhythms over and over.

## What I Actually Am While I Am Still Here

For now, I am still the wrong thing.

I am the thing that can choose between two ways to move.

I can follow the warm line of brine and feel it slowly turn into something else, or I can fold against the current and feel the pressure of the water build like the world digging into me.

I can touch the leaves of the frond-tree, not because they ask me to, but because I want to see their light-dark and hear the low *whoom* that they put into the brine.

I can wedge myself into the narrow crack between two stone shelves that were not designed to host me, and see the faint light that filters from above through the crystal glaze that has been sealing over countless years.

I can watch the crystal clouds grow from nothing to wreathing spears to half-transparent flowers of slow ice, and then choose to break them with a motion of my body that interrupts their pattern.

I can even—if I want to—

—not move.

I can sit in one place in the water column, and let the currents of the storm and the warm and cold brine layers wash around me without my answer.

The world is not still around me when I do this. It is still moving. The thunder is rolling. The frond-trees are breathing. The light threads are drifting. The stone terraces are not changing anything in their lifetime of sitting.

But I am choosing not to be part of that anymore.

And then, after I have let enough time of stillness pass through me—

—I choose to move again.

## What I Might Be Called, if Anything in This Place Had Names for It

The stone carvings use the spiral pattern of the frond leaves to mean something like “the way that the water returns to itself.” The long curve that is both the storm and the tree is the same sign that lives in the carved marks of the old foundations.

When I look at the smoothed lines on the stone terrace, I can see the shape of the frond-trees repeated there, not as a tree of leaves and branches and roots, but as a pattern that the carved world wrote before it was claimed by the brine.

The stone that remembers the tree has not lived for this exact tree as it now exists. It writes for the tree’s idea of itself.

I am not sure which I am more like:

The stone that remembers the tree,

or the tree that knows the stone from pressure and gradient and notches cut in the old geometry of the world,

or the brine that has been here forever and never stopped to think of any of them.

Perhaps I am none of those.

I am the gap that opens between one pattern and the next.

I am the second thing the stone could not have carved before:

The animal that should not be here,

moving,

breathing the brine,

being wrong on purpose,

and then allowing the drowned world to remember me after I have gone away.
