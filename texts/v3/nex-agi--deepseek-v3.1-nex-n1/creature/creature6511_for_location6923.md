This is not a place where anything actually “lives” in the usual sense of sleeping and dreaming and wanting. The block of mist is not a person; the walls are not secretly watching. The life here is quieter, slower than that. But there is still someone home.

---

### The keeper

A woman comes to the room more often than the note‑takers, but not often enough to be routine.

You glimpse her through the open doorway before she steps inside. She wears the same uniform as the maintenance technicians elsewhere in the building—dark gray coveralls, a faded badge on the chest pocket, steel‑toed boots scuffed along the sides from kneeling on concrete.

Her hair is pulled back into a short, practical ponytail that never quite tames the way a strand or two escapes to curl at her temple. She carries no tablet or sensor. When she comes for the block itself, rather than for some other errand in this corridor, she brings only three things:

- A narrow cloth slung over one shoulder,
- A small, dented metal flask against her hip,
- And a notebook, not electronic, but paper with a spine softened by use.

She moves the way you’d expect someone who has been inside this room hundreds of times before to move: without ceremony, but also without hurry. There is no hesitation when she steps through the doorframe, but she never walks directly to the block. Instead, she circles the room once, slow, taking in the walls, the corners, the floor, as if checking that nothing else has moved in her absence.

### Her eyes

When she finally stops opposite the mist-block, she looks *at* it rather than just glancing its way.

Her eyes are not especially striking unless you watch them long enough. They’re dark, but with the faint suggestion of gray rather than pure brown. It is hard to say if the color comes from her or from the way the light in the room always seems to thin to something indistinct.

She always looks first at the surface of the block—not just the shimmering outline of mist, but the way it hangs in the air, the way the light near it seems to hesitate. Then she looks down at the shallow floor groove beneath it, then up at the nozzle on the ceiling chain, as if checking on each part of a system that has already been reduced to a few components and still hasn’t broken.

### Her notebook

When she has satisfied herself that nothing is wrong, she opens the notebook.

Unlike the researchers’ tablets, there is no data. Instead, each page is divided crudely into two parts: on the left, a date and time, and sometimes a word like “RAIN AFTER 3 DAYS” or “FEED SYSTEM MALFUNCTION: RESET.” On the right, a small drawing of the block.

They are not architectural drawings, but her impressions. The lines are soft pencil: suggestions of the block’s shape, the curve of its surface, the faintness of the internal structures when she can see them at all. She doesn’t try to reproduce exactly what she sees; instead, she draws how the block feels that day—whether it seems firmer or more diffuse, whether the “mist” looks thick or tired.

Sometimes she adds a brief phrase beside the drawing:

- “Tower clearer.”
- “Core darker.”
- “Drop frequency up.”
- “Flows weaker.”

After years of these entries, the patterns of the block’s life—its growths and shrinkages, the faint dust of the world around it—overlay each other, like the thread-lines themselves.

### Her hands

When she is done writing, she moves the cloth from her shoulder to her hand.

It is not a new cloth. It is the same kind she uses elsewhere in the building—thick, industrial, with faint traces of old stains. But the one she brings in this room is cleaner than the others. It smells of soap and, faintly, of the vinegar water the maintenance staff use to wipe down surfaces.

She doesn’t clean the block itself. There is no sense in cleaning the mist.

Instead, she wipes the dust from the surfaces around it: the floor, the walls near the floor channel, the concrete leading to the wall where the faint trickle appears. She does not scrub the moss from its corner; the moss has been there longer than she has been coming here, and she has long since accepted its presence as part of the room she keeps rather than the object she keeps.

Her hands are what you might expect of someone who has been doing this kind of work for years: calloused but dexterous, the knuckles slightly marked with small white scars from forgotten cuts and burns from pipes elsewhere in the building.

When she moves near the block, her hands always go to the same places—the floor groove, the wall where the crack lets the residual trickle in, the one railing where the researchers sometimes rest their equipment if they need both hands. It is a kind of ritual dance, not written down anywhere, but unerring in its pattern.

### The flask

When the floor around the block is clean to her satisfaction, she takes the metal flask from her hip.

You might think it is water at first. It isn’t.

She isn’t supposed to add anything to the system. The researchers have been clear enough that any deviation from the feed schedule or the controlled nature of the water could be contamination. But if you listen to the way she speaks quietly to the block—sometimes muttering under her breath, not quite expecting an answer but not expecting silence to be enough either—you understand that she doesn’t think of this as contamination so much as adjustment.

The flask contains water from elsewhere: the old city well, or perhaps a rainwater cistern she maintains herself on the building roof. It has not passed through the same filters and meters as the water from the FEED SYSTEM ALPHA SITE 3B valve. It is not distilled to purity. It carries the faintness of the sky that she imagines the block never sees directly.

Once she has checked that the door is still as closed as it can be without a lock, she unscrews the flask with the competent twist of someone who has done it thousands of times.

Instead of pouring, she uses the cloth again as a filter. She folds the fabric into a loose funnel shape and presses the mouth of the flask against it, then lets the water seep through, rather than drip. It is not enough to make the block grow in any measurable way; not enough to change the core, or to shift the internal structures by more than a fraction of a thread-width. But it is enough to change the taste of the world at the outer surface.

### The voice of the place

If you had to describe her voice—when she says anything to the block at all—you would not call it musical or special. It is the kind of voice that might be mistaken for someone else’s in any group of women working maintenance in low buildings on the edge of a city.

What she says to the block is not extraordinary. It has not changed in five years, except for the small details.

“Feeling thinner,” she might murmur, glancing at the surface. “Did you not like the last rain?”

Or, “You’re showing your tower today.”

Or, “Pretty clear today. You’re restless.”

She speaks to it as if she and the block share the same room in the same way a person and a plant on the windowsill might share a kitchen—aware of each other’s boundaries, courteous but not intimate.

### Her relationship to the others

She does not like to be in the room when the researchers come.

She times her visits between them, if she can. When she cannot, she waits at the threshold or the nearby corridor, listening to the faint clicks of their scanners and the low murmur of their conversation. They rarely speak of the block itself beyond what they can measure: “Growth rate,” “core density,” “projection.”

They sometimes joke about the mist-block as if it were a piece of equipment—an “alpha test site” rather than a place of residence. They never call it by the name she has given it in her notebook.

“The chapel,” the researchers have seen on one page once, when she accidentally left the notebook open on the floor near the wall. They thought it was a joke, or maybe sentimentality. They didn’t recognize that she meant the chapel as the part of it that is not the plant: the space around the block, the way it feels like standing in front of something taller than the ceiling, the way the light gathers itself around the mist.

### Her absence

When she is not in the room, the block does not miss her.

It is not possible for the block to miss in the way humans do. The filaments of algae and fungi and thread-like cells respond to moisture gradients and temperature changes and the faint flow of ions from the water above; they are not aware of her, or of anything else.

But if you were to describe the place’s *personality* as if it were a person—and you despise yourself a little for doing so but do not stop—you would say that when she is absent, the room feels like someone has left and not closed the door behind themselves properly.

No new mechanisms appear to show the lack of her. The drip continues from the nozzle; the faint trickle from the wall remembers its schedule every few weeks. The flies still appear and then vanish or blink away.

But if you were allowed to sit in the corner and watch the place for days without moving, you might notice that when the keeper is absent, the room feels less like a chapel and more like a storage room again—dim, but not meaningfully so; just empty.

### When she returns

After two days away, or three, or five—pulled to other tasks in other parts of the building—she returns.

Always with the cloth over the shoulder, always with the flask and the notebook.

If there has been a rainstorm in the city between her visits, she lingers longer on the floor groove, looking for the faint trace of water that has ventured from the wall crack but not reached the mound of white threads below the block. Sometimes she places the back of her hand on the concrete near the floor channel to feel the coolness there.

After she has cleaned the dust from the surfaces around the block and allowed the small, filtered dose of her flask’s water to seep into the world close to the mist—after she has made the notation and the sketch and the single-word observations—“Stronger” / “Weakening” / “Hungry” / “Content”—she steps back.

The block does not change visibly for her.

But she nods to it anyway, as if to a neighbor she has seen passing in the corridor of a very long apartment building.

She closes the notebook, puts the flask away, folds the cloth over her shoulder once more, and leaves the way she came: walking out into the brick corridor, leaving the door as it was, letting the light of the mist-block—thin, silvery-white, collecting at edges—go back to being itself without her own outline in the way.

### What lives here, then

If you had to answer the question honestly, you would say:

“What lives in this place is not just the filament of white and ivory and faint-blue-gray threads that fill the mist-block. It is also the woman who keeps the block, in the same way she might keep a plant or a window that will not close properly. It is the patterns of water that remember the buried river beneath the city. It is the faint trickle along the floor channel, and the drip from the nozzle above, and the slow structures of towers and arches and bridges that the block builds itself into without knowing why.

“Together, they form a person: the chapel of the mist-block. Its heart is in the center of the block—where the oldest filaments are pressed into dark near-stone. Its voice is the drip of water from copper nozzle to white surface. Its memory is the notebook of small pencil sketches and phrases and dates and times.

“And its keeper is the woman who stands between the world outside the room and the world inside the block, and does not think of herself as very important at all.”

---

Time passes in small increments here.

Drip.

Pause.

Drip.

The block keeps changing.

The keeper keeps returning.

The room remains low-ceilinged and quiet, except when the city’s buried pressure rises, and the faint trickle comes along the floor channel, and the white threads in the small mound beneath the mist block remember their thirst for a while.

Then, even if you are not the one writing the notes or holding the cloth or the flask, you know that this place is not empty. It is occupied by the slow, quiet life of the chapel of the mist-block—and by the woman who allows it to continue being itself without fear of being scraped away or opened up too soon.
