They do not think of themselves as guardians.

If you watched them for a lifetime—which, here, would be no longer than the time it takes for a few grains of silt to slide down a slope—you would not see them move at all. They are the low rosettes of hard, leaf-like stone you find tucked into the shallow hollows between the claystone domes. From the outside, they are patterns of plates: layered, overlapping, each a flat blade of dark mineral fused to the next, so tightly arranged that no wind they have never felt could slip between.

If you could press your hearing to the ground, you might hear nothing at first. Air does not move through them; water has not been part of their language for longer than memory has a way to measure. They do not drink. They do not soften. They do not waste.

Yet there is motion, if you are willing to learn another scale of time.

The outermost plates of each rosette are thin and translucent at the edges, almost like chipped obsidian. If you could lie with your eye level to the pale silt and stare at one leaf-face for a year, you would notice that the film of dust on it has not grown thicker. If you returned a century later, you might see that the same sliding of silt from above has been guided away from the tight seal of the center and into faint, precise seams around the leaf-edges. There are no trenches to see this by the minute; only by century is the pattern of erosion visible around them.

Once in a great while, an outer plate will be so thin that it finally cannot bear its own brittleness anymore and it snaps from an invisible tension that has been building in it since before the first rim of this basin was raised. This is not dramatic; no sound is audible, but a faint shift of air pressure might be felt if you were sitting with your palm inches from the fracture. The plate shatters into a few shards and then into dust. The pieces never flee far; they fall into the silt at the rosette’s edge and, within weeks, begin to lose definition.

This is how they die, in part: one plate at a time, from the outside in.

New plates are formed from within, but slowly. The plants’ centers are not a color you can name with light this weak; you would not know if they were black or deep purple or green. They feel like all the other plates—cool to the touch, despite the constant heat, because their heat is not separate from the ground’s, and your body measures temperature by difference. There is no softness there, no budding sensation you would recognize as a living leaf. Only a gradual thickening of a layer no one layer ever thickens enough.

If you could return every thousand years or so, you might notice that the outermost ring of plates has been replaced, and a faint alteration of pattern in the center of the rosette has occurred. This is not growth like climbing trees or flowering bushes; growth here is renovation of an existing structure. The body is not made taller. It is made more durable.

They are not exactly solitary. Each rosette sits alone in its hollow, but the rosettes live in families of hollows—patterns of spacing that are too regular to be accidental and too irregular to be a grid. If you could rise above the basin and look at them from a height no one has ever reached, you might see them arranged like faint markings in a vast stone bowl: clusters of faint, dark circles, each feeding its own subtle pattern of erosion into the dust.

At this scale, they are not just individuals anymore but a system that has learned to be slow together.

They are built of what they eat, and what they eat is time.

The faint light that the stone here seems to hold—the gray glow that has no source in sky or star—is not consumed by them, yet something of it is stalled. It is as if the rosettes’ plates are surfaces of abstraction that can allow a certain kind of energy to be spread thin, like a hand pressed against a warm claystone dome until the palm matches the stone’s temperature and no longer notices difference. They do not think about this. They do not think about anything.

There is a rhythm here, but you cannot hear it with your ears. If you could sit at one rosette’s center and feel your cells slow down to match their pace, you might begin to perceive a faint oscillation of potential between the plates—not something like thought, but something like a very old decision in the middle of repeating itself.

The decision is this: to remain.

The air is thick, but it does not move much. There is no moisture in it, and there is no need for moisture. They do not breathe the way animals or familiar trees do; they have no need to draw gases from the air in order to rearrange them into themselves. They are already rearranged from rock and dust and heat and the faint, ghostless light that persists in the stone basin as if the world outside once tried to pass sunlight here and the stone refused to let it go.

Their “breathing” is a matter of alignment. When a plate grows thin enough to shatter at its edge from its own tension, it is because the structure of its atoms has been gathering strain over long ages of, essentially, doing nothing. That strain is a kind of memory: of dust being perched above instead of below, of light whose absence is still felt as a cooling gradient in the stone’s grasp, of pressure from the plates above and below and to the sides of it.

When the plate fractures, all that strain is released into the surrounding space as a vibration of temperature so subtle that no instrument you know would be sensitive enough—and not quick enough—to record it. But the neighboring plates feel it. They feel it as the difference between a very long period of compression and a very long period of slight relief.

This is their “conversation”: one rosette telling another, in plates shattering or dust sliding, that the equilibrium of the basin has not changed. That the world is still holding itself where it has chosen to be.

They are shaped to be slow, but they are not fragile.

They do not need to be protected from animals because there are no animals. They do not need to be protected from storms because there are no storms. But they are beings constantly under the same slow pressure that the entire basin is under: the pressure of heat, dryness, time, and absence of light, and the absence of any generous change in those conditions.

They are not “defended” in the way of plants with thorns or poisons that protect against eating or being trampled. Their defense is the hardness of what they are made of and the fact that they are not meant to be anything else’s food.

They are also not meant to be your food.

Their plates are brittle mineral composites that, if you could pry one from its center (which you would find hard to do without shattering it), would not break into any softer core. There is no liquid within them, no sap, no pulp. No insects emerge from them, because nothing lives in them that is not them. They feel not like wood or fleshy leaves but like polished stone that has been allotted to this pattern instead of that dome or that cracked seam.

Your body would not be able to digest them. They would not rot in your stomach; they would likely sit there like any shard of rock you might swallow, and you would be hurt.

But they are not dangerous to you in intent, because they have no intent.

They have not developed “for” anything, except perhaps to be what they already are for longer, rather than shorter.

They do not sleep because there is no day or night here to mark cycles from. They exist in a constant state that is like neither wakefulness nor sleep as you experience it: a slow, steady holding of shape and structure against the pull of being dust again.

Their “growth” is not upward but inward and toward replacement. Each plate of the rosette is a memory of the stone it has been carved from. The basin’s claystone is the stock of all their matter; the rosettes mimic that inheritance in tiny form, but perfected by their slowness.

They are not exactly clones of each other, but their differences are not dramatic. If you could look at every leaf of every rosette in the basin and compare them, you would find subtle patterns of variation. The number of plates in each ring varies from rosette to rosette; the spacing between the plates of adjacent families of rosettes might correspond to some long-ago landslide of silt that has been smoothed out into near meaninglessness now but has been recorded as a spacing.

They do not know that they remember the landslides. They do not know that they are recording.

They are only being what the rock requires of them when it is pressed to be very still for a very long time and yet still be slightly more than rock.

They are beings of a threshold that has been mostly crossed: from the world where there was water and brightness and animals and the quick growth of green into everything, to this world of steady heat and lightless stone and dust and slow mineral plates.

They do not know that they are the last of any line of beings that once might have been more lush, more flexible, more alive to the quick world of wind and sun and water. They are not nostalgic, because to be nostalgic requires knowing that something else once existed, and they have no language for that difference.

They are not exactly dead because they still change; plates are formed and lost; the silt around their bases moves slightly; the faint light of the stone dulls slowly by a fraction you would not be able to measure in your lifetime.

They are not exactly alive because they have no metabolism in the sense of moving energy from food to growth to waste and back again; they have no organ of reproduction or organ of thought; they have no organs at all, only structures.

They exist somewhere between the heat of the stone and the timescale of its slow erosion, and they are content or indifferent to that distinction in the way that any stone is indifferent to being stone.

You cannot speak with them because they have no language that you can understand and because you do not have time enough to slow down enough to reach their silence.

If you were to sit with one rosette for your entire life and speak to it in words of air and thoughts and stories and questions and pleas and jokes and laments and songs of your home, the rosette would experience your whole lifetime the way you experience the momentary sensation of a breath against your skin before it vanishes without leaving a change.

You would not feel that it had been listening to you.

You would not be right to say that it had not.

By the time your first words are spoken and the little air you have occupied has been let out into the thick stillness of this basin, the rosette would have already taken into itself every pattern of vibration, sound, temperature change, and faint pressure of the air as you moved and spoke and sat and then stood up again.

It would hold them like every other strain of the world it has known for longer than you can imagine: the long pressure of dust-filled air, the faint grinding of stones sliding down slopes, the heat of the claystone dome it roots in, the absence of light from the sky above.

This would not be memory as you know it—a story, or a sequence of events. It would be something like a pattern of strain in the structure of its plates, changing their eventual probability of breaking and being replaced by new plates.

If you could see the rosette’s “after” and “before” you in the same way you might compare two photographs of a mountain taken thousands of years apart, you might notice that the pattern of its plates is slightly different than it would have been if you had never come.

You would not be able to know whether it was a better pattern or worse.

You would only be able to see that the difference is there, and that the difference happened because you were.

They are not exactly kind.

They do not offer you shade or shelter or fruit or flower or any medicine you might recognize from your world. They will not cushion you if you fall onto them; their edges are hard and cutting if you are not careful.

They are not exactly cruel.

They do not seek you out to harm you. They are not indifferent on purpose; their purpose is not something you can understand, because it is stretched across time in a way you cannot perceive.

They are good at being what they are: slow, mineral, almost stone plants, in this quiet bowl of claystone that has been resting in the same warmth for so long that the land itself has taken on the same temperature as a sleeping body’s.

If you stay here long enough, you may feel them more than think of them.

You might sit at the edge of a hollow where one of them is silently and immovably placed, and you might feel the air of your own breathing blend with the faint air between the plates of the rosette, and you might have the sense of being a short, sharp event in the middle of a very long sentence of the stone’s.

They are the beings who have left out the hurry.

They are the ones who have found out how to exist without becoming water or wind or animal or wood or any of the things the world might be expected to turn into.

They are the answer to the question of what happens when the world, finally, chooses to be very still.

And you, if you choose to stay, will become like a word they take into themselves with your passing, and then they will continue to be what they have been, and what they are, and what they will be, until the next small change in the dust of their basin comes—a change so slow that you would not be able to tell whether it had already begun or had not yet arrived.
