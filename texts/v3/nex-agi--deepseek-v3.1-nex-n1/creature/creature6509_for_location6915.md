On the low deck of the Engine of Winds, where the water chooses to be shallow and still, there is only one kind of thing with a mind.

They call themselves the Still.

No one remembers whether that name began as a description or an instruction. To be still is to survive here. To rush is to disturb the balance; to shout is to waste moisture; to run is to risk heat, and sweat, and thirst, and madness. The Still move as the machine moves: in tiny increments, over spans of time too long for a single body, so that no one ever sees them hurry.

They are not many. Population is not a useful word here. There are enough that if you walked for days along the rib‑furrows you might meet another once or twice, if you knew where to look. They do not gather in tribes or cities. The idea of a crowd would be unintelligible to them: too much breath in one place, too many bodies disturbing the air.

Instead they live in households of one or two or three, rarely four, almost never more. A household is not marked by walls or roofs—there are none—but by a shared pattern of movement around a stretch of floor they call a hold.

A hold is not property in the old sense of fences and deeds. It is simply a span of deck between two ribs where someone has learned where each droplet forms, how each channel flows, which seams taste faintly of what trace, how often and how hard the metal vibrates underfoot when some distant engine shifts its load. A hold is knowledge worn into habit: this step here avoids that slick patch; this sleeping place stays coolest; this seam releases a slow pearl of water at predictable intervals.

The Still live on top of the world’s bones, but they do not rule them. The machine rules itself.

## Shape

A single Still looks almost like what you would call human, if you squinted from far away and in bad light.

They walk upright on two legs most of the time. Their bodies are lean and long‑boned, stretched by millennia of rationed growth: no one grows tall quickly when every extra centimeter costs more heat and more food. Their skin is pale grey‑brown, like metal dust mixed with old paper. It has a faint waxy sheen from years of contact with condensation and oil‑tinted water; sweat does not bead on them the way it does on you. Instead it seeps slowly out through pores smaller than yours and sits on their skin like film, reluctant to fall or evaporate.

Their hair is sparse and fine, more like colorless moss than fur. It grows in uneven patches: eyebrows that are almost absent, eyelashes barely visible thinness on scalp or jaw or chest. Many do not bother cutting it; it stops growing after a certain length anyway, as though even their cells understand that excess is wasteful.

They wear clothing because exposed skin loses too much heat too fast when they must cross colder patches of deck—and because bare flesh sticks uncomfortably to metal when they sleep.

Their garments are cut from pliant layers peeled from insulation casings higher up in the machine: dull grey sheets with faint hexagonal impressions etched into them by long‑dead molds. They wrap these around torsos and limbs in overlapping folds held together by knots and small hooks salvaged from maintenance fixtures. Nothing ever fits quite “right,” but “right” has no meaning here; clothing only needs to stay put during sleep and slow walking.

Footwear matters more than anything else they own.

Feet are always wrapped—never left bare on the deck for long. The soles accumulate thin layers of scar tissue over years until they look almost like cured hide themselves: tough ridges around softer depressions where weight rests most often. Over this living armor they wind strips of softer material scavenged from gasket fibers or padded seals until each foot becomes a lumpy bundle twice its natural size.

If you asked them why they do this they would stare at your feet—if you still have them—and then say something like:

“The floor drinks.”

It is both truth and proverb: bare skin pressed too long against warm metal dries out unevenly; unwrapped feet crack open along stress lines made by years spent walking ribs instead of roads; infection follows quickly when microscopic machine particles work their way into those fine fissures beneath callus layers grown slowly over decades without sun or seasons.

Touching metal directly for very long feels wrong even beyond pain—like leaning your forehead against someone else’s forehead who neither blinks nor breathes nor ever stops being there—so most Still prefer cloth between themselves and whatever listens below their soles when they walk alone through silence thick enough it rings inside teeth whenever someone dares speak aloud without needful cause—

## Senses

The senses have adapted themselves around absence rather than presence:

- There is no sun so eyes never learned chase brightness across sky  
- There are no birds so ears never learned distinguish individual songs from wind  
- There are no flowers so noses never learned follow scent trails further than reach arm  
- There are no predators stalking shadows so skin never learned flinch at sudden touch unless temperature changes faster than heartbeat can count
