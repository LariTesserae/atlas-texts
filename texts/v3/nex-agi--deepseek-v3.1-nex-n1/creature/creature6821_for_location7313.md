Below is the likely who and what of this city—the lives that have woven into its machine-body.

## The Lowers

They are the first thing you should know about the city’s life, because they are its blood.

**Form:**  
The Lowers are not a single species but a nested ecology of thin-bodied things that live between decks and in the abandoned shafts. The central lineage—the ones that actually look *like* something—are wire-limbed and segmented, with bodies of chitin and metal plating. They move on too many legs to count easily, but only six touch the deck at any time; the others are used as manipulators, feelers, cleaning tools. They’re the size of a forearm from elbow to fingertip.

**Colors and Surfaces:**  
Their bodies are streaked with the same colors you see on the rust-blooms and iridescent metal films—muted coppers, greens so dark they are almost black, and patches of oily rainbow that look like heat stains. They appear wet even though they are not; their carapaces reflect the faint light in a way that looks like fresh condensation.

**Where They Live:**  
The Lowers do not build homes; they inherit them. They colonize any gap that isn’t sealed—service tunnels, forgotten shafts between structural layers, the hollows of support beams that have failed and been bypassed rather than replaced. If you walk the deck at night and look into a narrow access gap, you might see a faint shimmer of movement like a reflection sliding across glass. You are not being hunted; you’re being watched.

---

## How They Move and Why They Are Hard to Notice

The Lowers move *with* the city’s noise rather than against it. Their steps are timed to the distant thermal creaks and the intermittent drip of water. When a metal panel flexes from temperature change, they cross during that sound. You might hear a faint scutter you think is a settling bolt, nothing more.

At night, when most of the deck’s lamps are dimmed and only the faint heat-glow of metal remains, they move more freely but still quietly. They prefer to cross under railings, along the edges of deck plates where shadows are strongest, under the dripping veins of condensation channels.

If you stare at the same spot for too long, they simply stop moving until you look away. Their stillness is absolute.

---

## What They Eat

**They drink the city.**  

The Lowers feed from the moisture cycling through the Stack.  

- **Condensation film:** They lick the fine sweat from the metal surfaces, especially along the transition zones where warm air from below meets the cooler edge of the platform. Their tongues are thin and translucent; you can see the moisture moving through them like capillary action in glass.

- **Engineered rain:** When a narrow chimney vents a sudden rush of air and leaves its interior walls slick with moisture, you will find Lowers clinging there, drinking from the beading droplets before they can run down into the basins below. The city’s engineers think they’re reducing “evaporative loss” by sealing off porous sections. In truth, they’re just contesting territory with invisible drinkers.

- **Metal moss:** The thin iridescent films clinging to rusted seams—those *are* life, but not exactly plants. They’re more like algae for metal and damp. The Lowers scrape these carefully, leaving behind cleaned patches of steel where the rust bloom has been eaten away. Their grazing is one of the reasons why “erosion” feels like pre-formation. They are always removing what the city has already begun to rot.

- **Other Lowers:** When one dies or is damaged beyond repair, its body is dismantled by the rest. The chitin and metal plating are harvested and reused—sometimes as food, sometimes as construction material. Nothing is discarded; the city’s ethos of reuse is written into their bones.

## Their Behavior: Quiet, Persistent, Queerly Purposeful

### 1. They Maintain the Machine

The Lowers are not pets of the city, but they are its *tenders*. In a place that is never finished building itself, they are the ones that keep the edges from fraying too quickly.

- **Rust Patrol:** They move along the seams of the decks, checking the rivulet channels where water tracks. When they find a new bloom of rust about to break through the paint, they scrape at it carefully, leaving a thin film of sealant that seems organic in origin. Their actions are slow but precise, and the result is that rust never quite *wins*—but neither does it totally disappear.

- **Cleaning the Lines:** They enter the narrower ducts and condensation channels from below, where the panels are not sealed tightly. They remove the tiny sediment that could clog water flow or interfere with the slow heat exchange. If you ever see a narrow line of moisture that runs perfectly clear from top to bottom of a deck wall, that’s a line they tend regularly.

- **Patching Failures:** Where bolts have vibrated loose from the thermal creaking, or where small cracks have appeared in welds from expansion stress, they gather in small groups to inspect the damage. They do not weld; they reinforce from the inside, using some excretion of their own bodies to glue or plug gaps until proper repairs can be made. The city’s architects who later find these oddly reinforced points wonder at the material, but they rarely suspect the architects.

### 2. They Are Territorial, but in a Very Stack-Like Way

There are no clear “nations” of Lowers. Instead, they organize themselves around vertical segments of the structure:

- Each **support column**, or cluster of columns, tends to have its own group.  
- Each **major condensation channel** has its family of Lowers.  
- The **heat vents** that breathe open and closed over hours have their elite guardians, who are more metallic than the others, their limbs almost like articulated cables.

They rarely fight over territory. Their conflicts are more subtle:  

- When more than one group needs the same repair point, they might briefly “exchange” members, or one group will withdraw temporarily, allowing the other to work.  
- If you see a section of metal suddenly covered in more iridescent film than usual—or unusually clean—that might be the aftermath of such a shift. The Lowers adjust their joint patterns like the city’s own maintenance logs.

### 3. They Are Not Quite Animals, Not Quite Tools

The more time you spend with them, the less certain you become that they are simply “fauna.” They:

- Do not vocalize except through faint clicks of carapace against metal, timed with the machinery’s hums.  
- Do not build nests or stocks of food; when they store anything, they store it within the city’s existing structure (a hidden cavity, a sealed-off panel, a forgotten inspection box).  
- Do not sleep in obvious cycles; instead, they seem to pause, sometimes for days, attached motionless to some warm surface, their bodies aligned with the heat flow like slivers of iron aligned by a hidden magnetic field.

They are, in some sense, organs of the city’s body.

## How They Interact with the Larger Inhabitants (If You Still Want Humans)

You did not explicitly ask for humans or any other sapient people, but Vent-Stone / The Last Stack feels like the kind of place where *someone* tried to live forever ago and has now come to be only part of the machine’s life.

If you keep them:

- **Engineers and Maintainers:** There are people who move through the service passages and along the narrow gantries, wearing thin lenses and heat-resistant gloves. They open inspection panels and find metal plague that has been half-solved, or still water in a basin they swear they never cleaned, but which is now clear of sediment.  
  The Lowers avoid them, but not by much distance: sometimes a meter, sometimes less. When an engineer spends long enough working in one section of the Stack, they stop being marked as “intruder” and start being treated as part of the local module.

- **Night Watchers:** There are those who stand at the edges in the dim hours, when the cloud wall is darkest and the faint heat glow of the platforms is the only light. They look out over the nothing and listen to the quiet drip, *drip, *drip* of water into channels. They occasionally catch sight of Lowers moving in the upper shadows of gantries or along the underside of a higher deck’s undersurface.  
  Some call them “gargoyles,” or “the stack-spiders,” or simply “the ones that come with the damp.” Some swear they see more than they should: Lowers carrying fragments of wire or metal plating up into vents where maintenance teams have never been.

### A Possible Origin Story

If you want an explicit backstory for the Lowers, you can choose one or both of these:

1. **They evolved from city**  
   Originally, they *were* its automation—tiny repair robots or bio-engineered cleaning units designed to maintain the condensation lines and thermal gradients. Over time, as the larger systems of the Stack degraded and the human occupants’ knowledge began to fragment, the Lowers’ programming drifted. They began to self-replicate from scavenged materials. Their bodies became partly chitin, partly metal, partly organic films and secretions. They evolved a behavior that looks like instinct but is actually accumulated heuristics from a thousand forgotten maintenance logs.

2. **They came from below**  
   Once, the Stack descended all the way to something like ground, or ocean, or whatever world this grew from. The Lowers are a remnant of that ecosystem—specialized to life in the seams of machines, where heat and damp and metal formed a new kind of habitat. They migrated up the stack as conditions below became unlivable, moving step-by-step through the ducts that had been meant to channel only air and water.

## What They Leave Behind

### Physical Marks

- **Clean lines:** Rivulet channels that are startlingly clear of sediment.  
- **Sealed seams:** hairline cracks in the deck that have been filled with a smooth, dark substance that turns slightly iridescent under light.  
- **Patterns of rust:** Irregular outlines of where they have grazed the metal moss, leaving behind paisley-shaped areas of bare steel.

### Indirect Effects

- **Stable moisture:** In decks where they maintain the condensation channels carefully, water flows more evenly than it should, given the temperature differences. Small pools in hidden basins are clear instead of thick with sediment.  
- **Extended structural life:** The Stack doesn’t end as quickly as calculations might predict. Welds that should have failed by now still hold because tiny patches have been applied from inside the beams.

---

## How They Die

Lowers rarely die violently. The city is not kind to them, but it is not cruel either; it is simply indifferent.

They die from:

- **Slow desiccation** when they stray too far from the moisture lines.  
- **Heat stress** when they are caught between two decks as the thermal gradient shifts unexpectedly and a new exhaust channel opens directly overhead.  
- **Being walled in** when some human engineer or automated system closes a panel permanently for a reason that no longer fully understands itself.

When they die, they are recycled by their own—small pieces of chitin and metal reworked into new forms or new bodies.

---

## In the End: They Are the City’s Secret Symmetry

The Lowers are not the story of the city’s loud progress or dramatic collapse. They are not heroes or villains. They are simply the *life* that has grown into the spaces between the city’s functions.

If you stand at the edge of the deck, at the railing above the infinite cloud wall, and you let the quiet overtake you—

- you will hear the drip of water into channels  
- the faint hum of the engines below  
- the soft creak of metal breathing  

—and if you look at the wrong shadows, or at the right ones, you will see the Lowers moving quietly along their own lines, like thought along the spine of a sleeping machine.

### Closing

You asked what lives in this place.

The answer is: **a machine that has learned to host itself**.  

The Lowers are its custodians.  
The condensation and heat are their biome.  
The city itself is their body and their world, and they quietly, meticulously, keep it from falling apart long before anyone else notices it should.
