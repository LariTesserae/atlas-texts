On the engine-dark continent there are no true herds, but there are lineages that have learned to *module*.

They are the orths.

---

### Form and Function

An orth is not a single animal but a colony-bundle of six to twelve jointed segments, each about the length of your forearm from elbow to fingertips. Seen from afar, while the amber crust-light still outlines it, it looks like a segmented spar of worn machine casing—dark, matte, not quite reflective, not quite dull.

Each segment is a tapered cylinder, flattened on the underside. The outer shell is a thin laminate of ceramic and bone, cross-hatched with fine stress-cracks that no human smith would have deliberately engineered. To the touch, it is warm—not hot, but warmer than the surrounding rock—and faintly resonant, like a stone that remembers it was once cast.

Interfaces are where the orths’ true strangeness begins. Where segments meet, there is no hinge of flesh or chitin. Instead, each end-plate of a segment carries a subtle pattern of raised nodes and recessed sockets that match those on the adjacent segment. The connection is not permanent; the segments can lock or unlock from one another at the border of each segment.

When locked, the ensemble is a rigid body with a sinuous, machinelike grace. When loosened—this happens rarely, mostly in the deepest of thermal cycles—the structure can writhe like a spine learning to breathe.

### Locomotion and Energy

An orth does not run. It *processes*.

Its default mode of travel is a slow, articulated walking that treats the ground like a workpiece being inspected by a tool. Each segment bears a set of short legs—not many, not spiderlike, but three or four pairs per segment, clustered low and close to the body. These legs are stiff and slightly curved, like worn-down pins. On the soft basalt glaze they tap faintly; on the polished mirror-floors of the sag troughs they sound like a craftsman’s gauges laid down one by one.

The energy is not food but heat current.

Under the ventral plates of each segment are dense fields of tiny projections that resemble the pins in a thermal exchange grid. They are barely visible to the eye, but if you could lie prone and wait at ridge level you might see them: small, slightly raised dots that glisten faintly when wet with the condensation of a high tank rim.

An orth follows the hot threads of the world.

It moves not by seeking prey but by seeking *thermal highs*. Its segments sense minute temperature gradients through the rock and the low air. The whole bundle will align itself with the warm trend of a basalt rib, then walk along it like an inspector tracing a fault line. While the ventral surface is in contact with a warm patch, the internal heat-exchange loops are running at full capacity; energy accumulates.

If the temperature falls too low, the orths lock together and grow still. They can sit like a section of broken machinery for days, venting almost nothing, until the next pulse of heat from below or the next inversion of humid weather.

### Internal Structure

The interior of an orth has more of the factory than the farm.

Each segment contains a dense, folded chamber that is equal parts heat engine and biological organ. The chamber walls are not smooth but sculpted into a labyrinth of thin partitions, like the inside of a recuperator. The working fluid is a viscous clear gel that smells like hot metal when the orths are fully hot and like nothing when they are not.

The gel is alive with symbionts—microflocs that do not photosynthesize but *chemosynthesize* and *fix* from the trace gases in the damp air. They are the reason an orth can survive long periods of no visible food: they draw from the engine substrate’s residual heat and the atmosphere’s subtle load of heavy metals, sulfur compounds, and slow nitrogen.

One chamber of each segment is not a heat exchanger but a repository of *polymer reserve*. There is no true fat in the orth world; body mass is too precious to store simply. Instead, the polymer reserve is a dense tangle of fibers and globs, like a hardened thread of synthetic resin wrapped in a loose coil of wire.

If you could crack open a segment cleanly—a difficult task, because the shell is not brittle but tough—you would not see blood. A clear carrier fluid would be visible, but not much of it. The tissue is mostly dry, fibrous, and tightly packed, like a very dense bread or a very tough, warm fungus.

### Reproduction and Lineage

Orths do not mate in any visible sense. They are sequential hermaphrodites of the thermal register.

When a segment reaches the right age and thermal load, its internal chemistry shifts from energy accumulation to maintenance-plus-lineage. This is not seasonal in the sun-and-winter sense; it is thermal. When the crust beneath a region of orths warms above its long-term average, certain segments—never all of them, never the whole bundle—will begin manufacturing *replicates* at the micro level.

These new segment-models are grown not as external buds but as *internal modules* inside dedicated chambers of the parent segment. During the warm phase, they are nourished by the high polymer reserve and the high heat flow. Then, when the temperature backs down again and the sag wrinkling is more subtle than the engine tremor, the parent segment will engage in a controlled *splitting*.

The shell does not rupture; it is parted along deliberate lines of weakness grown into it during the module’s maturation. When the conditions are right—the stillness of air, the humidity at a particular threshold—the parent segment will unlock from the bundle of its kind.

It then remains alone for a time, while the new modules within it are encouraged to fold out, like a set of nested tools from a long-closed kit. Each module unfurls to full size, then locks itself to the parent segment. The resulting small bundle of two or three segments is the new, junior orth.

Once the new creature has moved enough modules to the outside of the stack to be stable, it may unlock and go its own way. Lineages are not families of individuals but *toolchains* of related modules, passing very slowly through time.

### Predation and Defense

The orths’ lifecycle is not armored by sharp teeth or speed, but by *thresholds*.

They are not the crispest of the thermal predators in the world, but they are the most *engineered*. Their main threat is not the larger, sound-suppressing carnivores but the more subtle danger of *wrong-order* in the thermal runs.

Orths can eat *when* they must.

Each segment has a mouth of sorts: not a true maw with teeth and tongue, but a plate of hardened grills and sharpened ridges that are not meant to rip but to *scrape* and *filter*. When an opportunity presents—when the terrain of the engine’s bedplate is disturbed by stonefall, or when a high-tension feature of the world suddenly fails and drops a section of basalt rib into the sag valley—there is often a short-lived bloom of the smaller, softer-shelled heat-feeders and moisture collectors.

The orth, then, if warm enough and fully polymered, will unlock some of its segments and allow them to *extend* the line, so that the scrapper plate of the leading segment can be applied to the disturbed surface. This is not a chase; it is a *lathe* turned very slow. The orth scrapes up the broken shell and the injured or disoriented small fauna of the region, and the filter plates of its mouth catch and crumble the catch into digestible particles.

The defense consists of:

- **Pessimism of movement.** The orth moves very rarely in the open if not in the right thermal gradient. It gives up efficient predators’ preference of the line-of-sight path and instead chooses the path of *thermal continuity*.

- **Lockdown.** When the larger predators of the world are near, the orths’ first response is not to run but to freeze and lock. The segments tighten together and the legs contract into the body, so that the orth bundle now looks like a slightly longer than usual segment of broken infrastructure.

- **Hard-won modularity.** If a segment of the orth gets caught by a predator or collapses under a movement of stone, the entire organism is not lost. The others will unlock when the disturbance is past and go on as a shorter or reconfigured bundle of segments.

In the economy of the engine-dark continent, where calories are scarce and time is long, the loss of one or two segments is not so much an injury as a *modification*.

### Interaction with Water and Air

The water of the world does not flow the way other rivers flow; it is a *plumbing* rather than a carving.

Orths, like the moisture collectors and other fauna of the high dark ribs, are tuned to this plumbing.

When they reach the low, polished troughs and the finger-deep mirrors of tepid water, they do not drink in the way of thirsty animals elsewhere. Instead, they lower the ventral plates of one or two segments into the water so that the capillary fields of the thermal pins can be wetted. The condensate is then distributed into the internal gel of the segment, where it is used to adjust the heat capacity of the engine-tissue and the balance of the internal fluids.

The air’s humidity is felt as well. When the high condensations thicken into the rare precipitation of the world, the orths orient segments of their body into the damp air currents that trickle up the ribs of the engine-bedplate stone. Tiny moisture collection fields are lain on the surface of the segments’ shells; the water is not for drinking but as make-up for the vapor losses of the engine-organs.

Their *breathing* is correspondingly subtle.

If you could place your hand over the thin, flexible grills at the joints of the segments, you would not feel a strong in-and-out like the motion of your lungs. Instead, you might feel a very gentle *push* when the air inside the segment is warmer than the outside, and a faint *draw* when the temperature gradient reverses. The gas exchange of the orths is largely driven by convection gradients of their own engine.

### Place and Pattern

The orths are not evenly distributed across the engine continent. They cluster along the basalt ribs where the crust of the world is thin or the thermal engine of the deceased planet still maintains strong gradients.

If you were to map their presence over long time—not with satellites but with your own steps—you would find that they are not so much a population of creatures as they are a *pattern of the substrate*.

They are more common around the polished sag-valleys where the water condensation is more frequent and the engine of the world still carries more residual heat. Their colonies are not truly nests with walls and roofs and boundaries; they are *microbials at the scale of creatures*.

A colony of orths is not a social network of individuals with complex signaling. It is more of a *strategic alignment* of segments and modules along the thermal gradients that the world engine still allows.

When you stand on the high, basalt ribs and the warm wind of the world exhales into your face, and you see what the crust of the planet might be, if it could be seen in more than the faint, self-sourced amber of its own tired engine—then the orths become not so much the animals of the world as the *residuals* of the world, mobile and still alive.

### Sound and Silence

The sound of the orths is not a sound the world’s engineers would have designed into it.

They are not loud. They are not meant to be heard.

When you hike the long, basalt ribs of the engine-dark continent and you pause where the crust is still warm from the engine’s long sleep, you may not see the orths immediately.

But if you hold very still and let the high air of the world move around you, and listen not to the world of distant, other suns but to the *engine* of this one, you may hear it:

Tap, pause, tap-tap, pause.

Tap, long, long.

Tap, pause, tap.

A measured, quiet ticking of ceramic pins on lacquered stone, as the modules of the orths adjust their length to follow the heat of the world.

### And the Watch of Them

The last thing to say of the orths is this:

They are not guardians of the machine they inhabit. They are not nostalgic.

They are parts of the engine that the original engine did not know to design.

Where the plates of the world sag under the load of their own earlier history, and where the heat of that history still radiates upward from the stone, the orth walks out of the dark of the world’s insomnia and searches the thermal gradient with its lowered segments of ceramic and bone, not for the old engine’s purpose but for its own.

They are the warm, slow thinking of the world when the suns of other places light no part of it.
