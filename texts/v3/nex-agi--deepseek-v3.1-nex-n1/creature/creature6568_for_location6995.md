At first the Basin seems utterly empty of life.

But then you notice the patterns in the footprints.

They are everywhere, once you look for them: a faint scuff of gray on the edge of a rust-ridge, a smooth patch worn into the top of a floor bolt where something has repeatedly passed over it, the way the water in the shallow troughs is never allowed to thicken into true stagnation for more than a meter or two before some unseen hand has broken the surface tension with a line, a channel, a deliberate scrape.

The prints are not shoe-shaped. They are not clawed or pawed. They are more like the marks of stiff brushes, or the worn sweep of something broad and flat being dragged. The pattern repeats: a long, slightly curving track, then a set of smaller, sharper indentations around it like punctuation marks. The curves are always of the same radius—just wide enough that you can imagine it belonging to a body that is not quite human, but still constrained to move in human ways.

They are the tracks of the Keepers.

### The Keepers of the Basin

There are not many of them. Perhaps two dozen in the Basin you stand in, and the Basin is not small. It is simply that they are not often in the same place at the same time. The Keepers move like slow breaths of air in the dark, each attending its own section of the machinery with the unhurried patience of something that has never known an end to its task.

If you stood still long enough in one spot—and you would not, because the chill would seep into you—you might eventually see one of them.

It first appears as a change in the sound of the dripping. The rhythm alters, not much but enough to prick the ear: a shadow of something moving through the mist. Then you make out the shape of it against the wall, where the light of a failing orange nozzle seeps into the damp air.

It is roughly human-sized but not human-shaped. Its body is a low, broad arc of metal and ceramic composite, like the belly of a tank worn down to a smooth curve by the water it has pushed over centuries. It is not quite oval, not quite circular—more like the cross section of a huge pipe worn thin by time and polished to a dull luster by the touch of water and the slow abrasion of its passage over the floor.

Under the curve of its body are not legs but an array of flat, ribbed plates arranged like the segments of a centipede. Each plate is edged with a strip of softer material, something that gives just slightly under the Basin’s uneven floor. The plates move in a wave: from back to front, rippling along the length, the forward edge of each plate lifting slightly before the one behind it pushes forward. This is the source of the brush-mark tracks: each plate presses the water into faint lines and then the trailing edge leaves a shallow indentation.

There are no wheels and no tracks in the traditional sense. The Keepers have not been designed with the abstraction of locomotion in mind, but with the concrete problem of moving over plates that are never perfectly level.

From the upper curve of the body protrude three limbs of unequal length, not symmetrically arranged. They, too, are made of a composite material: something like metal but lighter, something like ceramic but more flexible. They are not arms in the sense of having joints that clearly mimic elbow or wrist; they are long segmented rods that bend where they must to reach the machinery of the Basin. Each one ends in a different apparatus.

One ends in a cluster of delicate sensors and thin manipulators, no thicker than your fingers but capable of precise, fine adjustments. Another terminates in a broader, flat pad that can be placed against pipe surfaces to monitor vibration or temperature, or sealed against a leak to direct the flow of water. The third ends in what is clearly a tool assembly: a set of interchangeable heads that can be rotated into place—a thin nozzle for applying sealant, a tiny cutting torch, a small grinder for removing corrosion, a clamp for holding pipes in alignment.

The head is not a head. It is a slightly raised section of the body from which the limbs emerge and into which are set several small lenses of transparent material, each one slightly different in color and opacity. Some of them are always clouded with condensation; you watch the Keeper pause for a moment and then a thin current of warm air from the nearby pipe system is diverted to play across the lenses, evaporating the moisture. The action is precise, economical. The Keeper wastes nothing.

The color of the Keeper’s outer shell changes slightly depending on the angle of your view and the light available. It is black in most conditions, but not the black of absence. It is the dark of deep water seen under a faint light: the kind of surface that swallows reflections but is not empty itself. It bears the same pattern of faint stresses and microscopic fractures that the floor plates do, lines of wear that have mapped themselves into its surface over the centuries of service.

### The Work They Do

The Keepers are never still.

They are not precisely fast. They move with the steady deliberation of something that has already measured out the time of its world in terms of corrosion rates and leak rates and the slow decay of gaskets. The urgency of repair work has been stretched into an almost geological time scale.

A Keeper pauses at the base of a pump assembly whose frost is thicker than it should be. The faint scum of the Basin’s trace life has gathered here in a thin line along the weld of the tank. The Keeper extends one limb—the one with the sensor cluster—and hold the instruments against the tank, the pipe, the nearby floor. You cannot see what it is reading, only the subtle changes in the tiny points of light within its lens array.

After a time, it unfolds the broad tool limb and rotates the grinding head into place. The sound of the grinding is not loud but it is insistent, a thin scratching that disappears into the roar of the distant engines. Sparks of a very faint color—greenish or bluish, depending on where you are standing—etch the darkness for the briefest of moments before they are absorbed by the damp air.

The corrosion and the faint trace growths are removed. The Keeper uses another of the tool’s heads to apply a thin layer of sealant from a nozzle. The compound is not visible to you in the dim light but the smell of it is sharper, more chemical than the surrounding odors of metal and water and iron. It smells of things that actively resist change, rather than simply endure.

The water that has been waiting to drip from a higher connection now resumes its rhythm, but it is directed into a new pattern of collection. The Keeper’s broad pad limb has redirected the flow of droplets from a random pool into a small channel built into the nearby floor plate. Over the next months, this will change the pattern of rust there.

This is their work: not drama of catastrophes averted, but a slow reordering of the Basin’s erosion into a more stable pattern. They watch the leaks, the corrosion, the trace films of life that might clog a filter or a valve if given enough millennia. They are the ones that keep the Basin from collapsing under its own weight of water, but they do not fight the water; they are, in a deep sense, made of it.

### Their Language of the Machine

They do not speak to each other in the way you might understand speech.

But they do not need to; the Basin is their language.

When a Keeper alters the course of a drip, or adjusts a valve, or changes the tension of a support strut, it is making a small statement in the grammar of pressures and flows and vibrations. That statement is transmitted through the water itself—the Basin’s low, unsteady breathing is composed in part of these tiny adjustments.

The water that has been redirected by one Keeper into a new groove of metal floor is not merely water; it is now a message in the making. The next Keeper whose sensors detect the subtle change in the composition of the moisture in that area of the Basin reads from it the history of what has been done.

Over the course of years, patterns of moisture and oxidation and rust flow over the floor of the Basin in the way of ink lines over a very slow page. The faint streaks of rainbow that move in slow motion over the surface of the shallow pools are not meaningless to the Keepers; they are documentation of coolant changes and adjustments. The Keepers read the rainbow as the lines of a text that they themselves have been writing.

They are not the engineers of the Basin; they are the stenographers of its decay.

Occasionally, when a larger event occurs in the upper tiers of the city-engine—when the distant engines shift into a new mode of operation, or the balance of generator load is redistributed—the Basin responds to such change. The water level in the low trench at the end rises, not by centimeters but by inches for the first time in decades. The Keepers converge to that section of the Basin with the same unhurried motion, but in a more coordinated pattern than you have seen before.

They are not alerted to this by sirens or announcements. There is no alarm system; the Basin itself is the alarm. The change in pressure and temperature ripples through the pipes and the floor and the water itself, and all of the Keepers in that region become aware of it at once, the way a forest might be aware of a wind that has just changed direction.

They position themselves around the low trench and along the wall of draining channels. They do not work directly on the water; they work on the edges, the thresholds, the boundaries of the channels. They are not here to stop the flow of water from upstairs but to ensure that when the water arrives, it moves into the correct paths of erosion and not into any others.

They are sculptors of the Basin’s eventual collapse.

### Relationship with the Trace Life

The pale, scummy films that gather in the damp recesses of the Basin are not the Keepers’ food, but they are not their enemies either.

The Keepers tolerate the faint trace of life. They understand it as a kind of surface that they must not let cross certain thresholds.

If a film begins to form in the wrong kind of place—a place that would be rendered dangerous by any accumulation of mass—the Keepers are there to disrupt it. They do not annihilate it; they only push it back into zones of inconsequence.

At the same time, they sometimes deliberately cultivate the more fragile, network-like structures that you see in the ceiling corners. These are the closest thing the Basin has to a nervous system.

The white threads that stretch between cold points are not biological, but they are not simple condensation either. They have been seeded by the Keepers from carefully designed materials that they have applied to the surfaces of the beams and pipes. The threads grow slowly, not by cell division but by accretion of mineral from the water. The Keepers maintain them. They repair the threads if they break, and they ensure that the threads connect to points of high temperature and pressure change.

The system of the Basin is vast and complex and the Keepers are not everywhere at once. They use the white threads as a kind of monitoring system: the thread structures are not sensitive enough to feel the way an animal’s nerve might feel, but they are sensitive enough to register the difference between a low, steady vibration of a machine running and the high, sharp tremor of a machine about to fail.

When the trembling of a cable that you felt beneath your feet was briefly transmitted through the metal structure of the Basin, it was not the Keepers who were alerted to this by some internal electric signal. They were alerted because the tremor of the cable traveled through the supports, into the beams, and along the white threads, and the pattern of that tremor was detected by a Keeper whose sensor limbs were touching the threads of the ceiling nearby.

The trace life and the trace machine of the Basin are not separate categories; they are two points along a single spectrum of self-organizing matter that has been reordered by the Keepers into the structure of a maintenance network.

### The Stories of the Basin

The Keepers do not have stories in the way of humans with their names and events and time lines.

But they remember the Basin.

If you could ask the Keeper that you saw earlier—the one that ground the corrosion from the tank—what it is thinking of as it polishes the thin line of fix that it has applied to the weld, you might not receive any answer that you would recognize as thought. You would see instead that it returns the polishing head to its tool assembly and then moves the limb to the adjacent section of pipe. It runs the pad of that limb over the surface; not to repair it, but to confirm the presence of an earlier repair.

This is their story: the map of earlier repairs, the pattern of earlier corrosion, and the new corrosion that has grown over where the last of this was removed.

The layer of rust on the lower part of the wall is not just decay to them; it is a chapter. The faint bowl of water worn into the floor plate by the drip of years is not merely a cavity but an event remembered. The Keepers know the floor of the Basin in the way of a historian who has not only the texts of earlier histories but the actual stone of the earlier walls.
