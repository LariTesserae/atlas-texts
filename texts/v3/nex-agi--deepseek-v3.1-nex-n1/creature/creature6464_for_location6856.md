In this drowned district of the city there are three kinds of people, and they are all more or less the same person.

---

### The Scuttlers

First are the Scuttlers—or so they call themselves when they talk to each other at all, which is not often.

They live in the mid-height buildings, where the third and fourth floors stay dry even if the stairs down to the street end abruptly in a sheet of lead-gray water. They are small people, usually, wiry and quick, with salt-chapped hands and shoulders that know how to duck under low pipes and broken window frames.

They move like seabirds that are slightly ashamed to be climbing on land.

When the tide is right—they never say high tide or low tide, they say "tight" or "loose"—the waterline drops a foot or two and the streets cough up their secrets: rusted bicycles fused to signposts, plastic bags twisted like pale sea anemones around the necks of parking meters, clusters of bottles that have rolled together and become a single lump of glass. 

The Scuttlers come down then, with nets and hooks and homemade prying tools, moving from roof to roof to tilted lamppost to the top of a submerged car like a city of their own making suspended above the water. They trade the salvage: wiring for water, metal for matches, plastic bottles refilled with brackish, boiled water for the bright blue plastic of tarps that never entirely seal the wind.

They know the buildings by their failures: this one will have good copper for another three years, that one is already too soft around the foundations to take your weight, that one over there is owned by a factory man who pays in batteries if you bring him anything that still remembers electricity.

They rarely sleep on the ground floor.

The water, they say, remembers. You never leave a full footprint of yourself on a wet floor. If you do, the next high tide may come find you when you’re sleeping.

Their children, if there are any, are born with the sound of the city breathing in their ears: the sigh of the ground under concrete, the drip of condensation from the broken ceiling of a captured room. They learn to tell the taste of the rain on the tongue—this much salt means the factory vents are open, this much metal means a pipeline somewhere is finally giving up.

They are not the birds of this city. They are the ones that live under the bird’s shadow.

---

### The Keepers

Second are the Keepers, though they seldom answer to the title.

They work the factory, on the far edge of the district, where the water still hasn’t risen to the doorframes but the bricks are sweating all the same. Most of the machines have been moved to the second floor or higher; the lower floors are for storage and for the slow pumps that nobody is willing to admit are fighting a losing war.

The Keepers are fewer than they used to be, but they have stubbornness in their bones. They come to work in the dim light of early morning, when the city is still hushing itself, and leave when the dim light returns and the world becomes a vague suggestion of itself.

Their job is to keep the city breathing.

The factory feeds power to the pumps that still strain the water from the tunnels; the same power that drives the warmers that keep the concrete basements from becoming too cold to walk; the same power that occasionally, on a good night, throws a soft orange glow over the low clouds and reminds the Scuttlers that there are still humans who can command light by name.

They know the city’s organs by heart: this line of pipes carries the heat of a deep generator that will never be turned off because nobody knows exactly how; that corridor of collapsed brickwork is where an old maintenance crew sealed off a leaking chemical tank decades ago and then walked away; this rusted ladder leads to a roof that gives you the best view of the channels, where you can see the lights from the inhabited buildings drifting like tired lanterns.

The factory has its own language.

The boilers speak in pressure and hisses; the pumps answer in steady thumps that the Keepers can feel through the soles of their shoes; the electrical humming comes from somewhere else entirely, a background choir that never rests. If a tone changes, they notice before the gauges have finished deciding.

They are the ones who still remember what the city looked like before the water rose, but they don’t talk much about it. They remember the shape of streets now under channels, the names of shops that are empty boxes behind the windows half full of brine and fish.

Sometimes, at night, one of them will stand on the roof of the factory and look at the scattered lights of the Scuttlers’ squat and think of it as their own constellation of tiny stars.

They are not the life of the city, but they are the ones that keep its heart beating.

---

### The Floaters

Third are the Floaters, though they never call themselves that. They don't call themselves anything. They are the ones who have decided to live closer to the water than to the roof.

Most of them trade with the Scuttlers. You will see them poling their flat-bottomed boats along the channels, their bodies silhouetted against the gray sky: thin shoulders, hunched backs, the steady drag of a pole through the water.

They know the submerged streets by the shape of the ruins under them: the third left turn after the building with the broken pediment is shallow enough to scrape hull; if the water drops the width of your palm, you can see the roof of the old bookstore where you slept that one winter; when the tide is loose, the submerged carousel of shopping carts in the square of the plaza makes a sound like someone whispering through metal teeth.

They grow food on their roofs and in the floating gardens of plastic and net and foam: tangles of green that have no pretty name, but that taste like salt and freshness and the city, boiled down into something you can chew.

They fish with the same birds that they watch from the corners of their eyes.

When a fish rises in the water, the birds know it, and they know the birds. There is a pact of hunger: the bird dives, and the Floater waits; the bird gets its share, or the Floater reaps what the bird has missed. The water is full enough of life that both of them can still be wrong and the fish will live.

They are the ones who feel the city's breath under their floorboards.

Their homes are boats tied to windowsills, scaffoldings turned into docks, the third floors of half-submerged buildings that have been gutted and turned into strange apartments with views of the water. The ground floor is always wet, but the second might be damp enough to be a storage room, and the third is where you can hang hammocks and see the factory smoke on the horizon.

They hear the sigh of the ground through the wood of their hulls and the cracked concrete of their foundations. When the pressure shifts deep below and the water levels change by the width of a finger, they know the city has taken a deeper breath.

They are the ones the city talks to, when it wants to remind somebody that it still has lungs.

---

What they all have in common is this:

They are few enough to be invisible to the city, but not enough to be rare to each other, when they need to be.

They know the same tides, the same cracking of rust underfoot, the same pattern of birds that has taken over the skyline. They trade the same things: warm for dry, metal for water, quiet for help.

When the rain comes—not sudden, but as a gradual thickening of the air until the droplets are indistinguishable from the humidity—they all pull in tighter. The Floaters tie their boats to staircases that lead nowhere; the Scuttlers climb one extra level and pull the tarps down; the Keepers check the seals on the factory and listen to the pumps, their shoulders tense beneath the drum of water on metal roof.

The city, under the rain, becomes one huge, breathing being.

The ground sighs.

The channels lip upward at their edges. The reflections of the broken buildings in the water shake and smear and then disappear under the rings of falling droplets. The birds sit on the ledges and become dark lumps that grow darker and heavier with the rain.

Below, in the layers of damp and collapsed tunnel and forgotten storage room, the pumps keep working, because someone is still paying attention to them.

Humans are not absent in this place. They're just rearranged, like everything else that has yielded to the water.

They have become the things that live on the edges of the world: between the wet and the dry, the sky and the brine, the machines that will one day shut down and the season that will never dry.

They are not the rulers of the city.

They are its librarians. Its secret keepers. Its last audience for the long sigh under the cracked pavement, where the air of the deep world bubbles up and the water level gently falls and rises again, as if something down there has finally learned to breathe.
