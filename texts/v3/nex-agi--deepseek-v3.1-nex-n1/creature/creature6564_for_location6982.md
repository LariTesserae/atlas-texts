Below the rust-streaked grates and the forgotten mesh, the air is thick and warm, and the first thing you notice are the **bellows-snails**.

You don’t see them at first. You see the tracks—silver lines that shiver and then go still on the damp stone, like threads drawn through wet cloth. Then you notice the snails themselves, pressed close to the cracks in the young stone, where the faint heat from the earth and the hum from the walls meet and turn into breath.

They are not like any snails you were taught to name. Their bodies are soft and colorless, translucent enough that you can see the slow pulse of their organs—thin white branching veins that quicken when you step near. The shell is almost absent—a nub of pale chalky matter at the tail, like the last bone of a memory that refuses to dissolve. They move in a concert of expansions and contractions: a small ripple of flesh, then a pause as the side of the body rises and settles with an audible, sighing sound. When dozens of them do it at once, the whole floor seems to exhale.

If you sit very still, you notice that when a factory shift changes somewhere above, the bellows-snails all shift their direction at once, as if pulled by a single change in pressure. They herd toward the warmer bricks, then back to the cooler stone when the temperature drops. Their “siphons” are not like the snorkels you see in garden snails; they are flattened like gills, and when they lie against the wet stone, they breathe the water out of the rock. Each time, a faint mist of spatter is released into the air—so minute you only feel it as a slight change in humidity, not a rain.

The locals—if anyone who comes here can be called a local—have called them *boilers* or *engine-snails* or simply *the lungs of the wall*. These are the names of people who have never seen a river or a forest; they are naming the snails after the only things they know. The names are wrong but not entirely wrong: the bellows-snails do not just filter water; they filter time.

When you pick one up—very gently, because the flesh is tender as a blister—the shell-nub will be worn to the shape of your particular fingerprint. If you hold it to the light, you see faint tracings in the chalk like circuits or old wiring diagrams. The locals whisper that if you leave your thumb on the snail’s nub for too long, it will begin to remember your heart-rate and attempt to match it, which is not safe for anyone, especially the snail.

Along the pool’s edge, where the biofilm throbs red and then fades to rust, you can find the **switchback beetles.**

They are the size of a grain of sand, and translucent as cheap glass. When they are still, you might mistake them for a ring of spilled condensation around the pool. But they are almost never still. They move in slow, dense waves across the algae, like a shifting stain of water itself. Each wave folds itself over and over, forming tiny patterns that you only recognize at scale: repeating triangles, branching lines, overlapping circles that could be accidental or could be deliberate.

If you look at a single beetle under a lens—some locals do carry tiny lenses, scavenged from broken inspection cameras—you’ll see that its body is not quite opaque enough to be called bug. It is more like a tiny container. There is a central thread, dark and coiled like a fuse, and on the top of the head, a cap of silvery film that ripples when you tap the surface of the water near them.

They move in apparent formation, but there is always one beetle that seems to be out of step, sitting on a slightly different line of cracks, moving in the wrong direction at the wrong time. That’s the cue-maker. The locals call it *the switchback* or the *wrong-way guide*. Once you spot it, you can’t unsee it; the others’ behavior suddenly makes sense, as though they are moving in perfect opposition to that single wrong point.

When you first step into the marshes, the switchback beetles will rearrange themselves into a pattern that you are not supposed to notice but cannot help noticing: the shape of your silhouette, drawn from below. They’ll trace it out once in the slime, then break apart and re-form into random noise again, as if embarrassed to have given themselves away.

At least once a day, when the light through the grates shifts from murky gray to a slightly sharper yellow, the beetles switch direction. This is one of the few regular events here. The locals will say: “The shift turned,” when they see the beetles’ old formation fall into the new, and it will sync with a shift change in some factory you’ll never see.

If you are very quiet and you sit on the overturned bucket rusting into the floor, you might catch a **night-veil.**

That’s what locals call the mat of thin, translucent insects that cling to the warmest wall, where condensation is always fertile and the faint hum of the machinery behind the brick is strongest. They’re the size of your thumbnail, but impossibly thin—almost like layers of skin shed by some unseen larger animal. Their bodies are translucent except where a faint network of pale blue lines maps out internal organs that you never quite understand.

When they are at rest, their overlapping wings make a single sheet of texture along the wall, as if someone has painted a thin membrane of darkness with too many brushstrokes. Then, when the temperature drops just enough with the evening shift change, they *liquefy*.

You don’t see the moment of change. You look at the wall and you see the dark sheet; you look away and back again, and now where the insects were there is a thin film of fluid, moving slowly down the brickwork in the pattern of a broken-down face. The locals swear that the face is always the same in each “event,” but none of them can agree on whether it’s old, young, smiling or screaming.

Then you look away for a second time and the fluid is gone again. In its place is a cloud of tiny moving shapes—the same insects, shattered back into their separate bodies, clinging once more to the moss.

They’re called night-veils because of the faint, membrane-like sensation they leave when the air stills: your skin feels suddenly covered by a weightless film of dampness, as if someone had draped a wet cloth over your face and then removed it in the same instant. You claw at your face and find nothing. There is no water on your fingers, but the smell that lingers—a faint, ozone-like scent mixed with mildew and powdered rust—goes away with that.

The most visible of the locals—if you can call them that—are the **overhang bats.**

They are the size of your clenched fist, but they seem smaller when they are in motion, because they are never still enough to fully measure. They are not bats, not birds, not insects. You see the outline of bat in them: the soft, furred body, the membrane of wings that shouldn’t be there but *are*, the faint hint of rodent teeth when you see one frozen at the wrong angle of motion. But then you notice the wrongness: the body is too matted with dampness, like a wet cloth thrown against a wall; the wings are not skin, but a thin, translucent film that clouds in the humidity like breath on a window.

They live in the upper corners of the shaft, where the brick walls meet the mesh ceiling and the faint crumbles of rust fall like snow. They hang there upside down from the remnants of old hooks and bolts, where alarms once used to scream, and it is widely believed that the sound that still sometimes comes from those speakers isn’t from machinery at all, but from their bodies.

The locals avoid the nights when the overhang bats begin to “talk” through the speakers. You can hear them then: not screeches, but clicks of short, sharp information that sound like “someone *should* check *that* filter soon *or* it *will* rust *through* again *and* again.” The words are garbled but the meaning is clear; they’re mimicking the constant warnings they’ve heard over their short lifetimes and out of context.

At dusk, when the light through the grates goes thin and blue, the bats abandon the ceiling at once and descend into the empty air between the walls, not individually but like a single net being cast from the corner. They fly *bracket-shaped patterns* around the center pool, never touching the water’s surface but always close enough to see their own reflection; they orient themselves by the tiny distortions made by their bodies passing over the water.

When they hunt, they’re not noisy. You’d expect a flurry of wings, but you hear only the soft *tump-tump* of their feet landing back on the wall when they’ve taken or failed to take a meal from the air. They’re pursuing the larger, translucent insects that drift from the mosses—followships of the night-veils, perhaps.

Stories say that if you manage to catch one—and this is dangerous, not because of their teeth but because of how fragile they are—you’ll find that its body temperature is exactly the same as your own breath when you first stepped into the shaft.

---

Last, there are inhabitants of the **pulse-crust:** the unnamed biofilm that creeps along the rock edge of the pool.

If you watch the red edge of the water where the biofilm thickens, you will notice that it does not just “pulse” randomly; it creates *waves* of color and activity that move around the circumference like the sweep of a scanner. The locals sometimes call these *the scans* or *the inspections*. When some factory process changes and the drip of water from above is suddenly faintly colored—blue, say, or yellow-green—the pulse-crust floods that direction with color. Then the color recedes and returns, altered by some incomprehensible metabolism.

The locals have never named a *species* for the pulse-crust, because they’re not convinced that there’s one species to name. It’s “like naming the windows of a factory as a kind of bird,” one old-timer might say, peering over the railing and shaking his head. “You’d name the wrong thing.”

But you’re from here, or you’re not; and you’ve been here before; or you’ll never come here again. The locals believe that the first time you come into the shaft, your breathing is all wrong, and the pulse-crust adjusts to you. Then, on later visits, the “inspections” sweep and you see yourself—your particular pattern of breath and heartrate—being reflected in the colors along the pool edge.

“Memory’s not in the walls,” they’ll say, pointing down. “It swims in the pool.”

### Some of the locals’ stories

- **The Boy with the Bad Engine**  
  Someone whose name no longer matters came down here as a child. He had been born with something wrong in his chest—a “bad engine,” the older kids called it. He’d sit on the broken ladder rungs and listen to the bellows-snails breathe until he could match his rhythm to theirs. Years later, when he left for factory work up top, people said that when he slept on his shift, the machines around him would slow to match his breathing, and the whole section would fall into his rhythm.

- **The Switchback Dream**  
  There’s a story that if you follow the switchback beetle *the wrong way*—tuning your direction to its deviation instead of ignoring it—you will emerge from the shaft into a city where the factories run backward. The buildings are still rusted but they are getting *cleaner*. The rust flakes are slowly retracting into the paint, and the warnings spelled out on the walls are being written for the first time instead of erased.

- **The Night the Veil Was Lifted**  
  On one particularly cold winter, the metal door at the top of the shaft was left ajar the whole night. The mist from the Boiler Marshes found the crack and climbed the stairs, like water seeking higher ground. When it reached the landing, someone sleeping on the floor of the warehouse above woke to the sensation of a thin film pressing over their face and then vanishing. They rushed to the window and saw, for just one moment, the faint blue-black lines of the night-veils drawn over the city like the relics of old wiring.

### Rules of living with them

The locals will tell you:

- Don’t touch the bellows-snails with bare skin for longer than a heartbeat. They’re trying to match your rhythm and you don’t want to be responsible for what happens when one engine thinks it’s another.
- Don’t try to correct the switchback beetle’s path. If you’re clever enough to see the wrong-way guide, you’re already participating in a pattern you shouldn’t. “Leave the wrong to be wrong,” they’ll say.
- Never stay when the overhang bats are relaying old messages from the speakers. The factory too far away to hear you might, and start *again*.
- If you see the pulse-crust forming your outline around the pool’s edge, don’t break eye contact. The locals say that’s the only time something “official” in this place ever notices you, and it’s better not to ruin the moment.

And there’s one unwritten rule that nobody speaks but everyone understands: if the mist from below climbs your stairs and you find yourself thinking of the Boiler Marshes as you stand at the factory overlooks or fall asleep at the edge of your own bed—then you’ve already become someone whose lungs are shared between the world above and the world in between the walls.

---

If you stand on the narrow metal catwalk halfway down the shaft and look at the Boiler Marshes below, you’ll see:

- A basin of young stone, constantly cracked and healing and cracking again.
- A thin skin of breathing that crawls over everything—snails, beetles, night-veils, bats.
- A faint blue-red pulse of inspection from the edge of the pool.
- The faint silhouette of yourself reflected in the motions of the swarm.
- The sense of being inside a secret organ whose purpose you could not possibly explain to anyone up above.

Even then, you are not seeing the place completely. You’re just seeing one version of its inhale. The exhale waits when your shift is over and you walk back up the crumbling stairs and into a world that doesn’t know how it feels to be inside someone else’s machine.
