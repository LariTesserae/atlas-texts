I walk now, toward the center of the drip alcove.

The corridor’s hiss recedes to a murmur. The doorframe is not wide enough for two, and my shoulders nearly kiss the edges as I step through. The threshold is not level; there’s a slight lip where the tiles of the floor rise up to meet the older plating of the corridor, and I have to place my feet carefully. The ship’s gravity here feels subtly heavier, as if the density of layers upon layers of insulation and time and condensation have added weight underfoot.

The air inside the alcove is different than the corridor’s. It’s not cooler or warmer exactly, but *more*—more saturated, like the breath before a storm. The faint smell of ozone and metal and something old and forgotten raises the hair on my arms. Above me, the pleated insulation of the ceiling is a low, rust-colored sky. It’s not still. I can feel it before I see it; a subtle hum in the back of my teeth, in the soles of my feet. The life of the Ship’s deeper veins, vibrating up through the layers of foam and metal. The light is a golden amber, a single bulb trapped behind a thick lens of glass, like the sun setting behind a storm front.

The sound is the next thing. It's not silence. It’s the absence of the corridor’s roar, leaving only a smaller, more intimate world of noise. The hum of the current through the insulation. The faint whisper of air moving over coils of forgotten wire on the brackets beside me. And then… the *drip*.

I wait for it.

The first drop takes its time. It’s not a sound of water falling on tile. It’s the sound of water falling into water. A deep, resonant *plunk*, rich and round, like a stone dropped into a deep well. It echoes in the shallow basin. The surface of the water shivers, not with ripples, but with a fleeting tremor that seems to travel down into the stone and metal of the floor itself, before it disappears into silence.

The drip alcove is a place of listening.

The longer you stand here, the more you notice that the dripping is not alone. In the intervals between the drops, there is another sound. It’s a faint scraping, a click of solid against solid. It’s a sound of work.

I crouch down beside the basin. The tiles are worn smooth beneath my knees, not by the feet of countless people, but by the presence of one person, over and over again. The light of the bulb doesn't truly reach the floor directly; it's reflected by the moisture on the ceiling and the walls, creating a halo that leaves the basin itself in shadow. But close enough, and my eyes adjust.

The water is not crystal clear. It has a faint patina to it, a scattering of nutrients and minerals filtered from the Ship’s internal air and water by the ceiling organism. The water is not deep enough for much to live in it.

The creature that lives in the drip alcove does not live *in* the water.

It rests in the shadow of the basin's edge, on a small, dry patch of worn tile. It’s no larger than my hand, and at first glance, it looks like just another part of the floor; a cluster of dull, metallic-grey components.

*This.* This is the inhabitant of the drip alcove. This is the reason.

It's an arthropod of the Ship’s old bones, a thing of chitin and metal. It has eight legs—or eight *limbs*, some of them clearly more than just legs. They are crafted from a combination of dark, polished chitin and a dull metal, like worn titanium, that has been scavenged from somewhere in Ship’s depths. Six legs are for walking and clinging, articulated with precise, mechanical-looking joints. They have been factory-standardized, but the factory has been gone for centuries. The remaining two limbs are more specialized, one a pair of delicate pincers that now hold a small fragment of what looks like a wire. The other limb is a slender tool, like a stylus or a probe, made from what looks like a piece of broken ceramic insulator.

Its body is a low oval, armored with overlapping plates of the same chitin and metal. Its compound eyes are not the eyes of a terrestrial creature, but lenses of some dark, polished glass that don't reflect light so much as absorb it. They don't move. They watch patiently.

The sound of scraping comes again as I watch. Holding its wire fragment steady with the pincers, its stylus-limb moves down the length of it. It touches the surface. The scraping is not a sound of destruction; it's a sound of precision. With each pass of the stylus, something tiny happens. A minute curl of metal shaving comes away from the wire and falls onto the tile.

*Plunk. plunk.*

The arthropod ignores the dripping water. Its head does not lift toward me. Its attention is entirely on the wire fragment, which is less than a centimetre long.

But when the water drops, *it changes.*

After the third drop, when the tiny ripple has faded, the arthropod stops its meticulous scraping. It finally lays the wire down gently on the tile beside it. Then, with deliberate, unhurried care, it moves one of its front legs toward the rim of the basin. It touches the side of the basin, not exploring the water itself, but the edge where the water meets the tile. The leg works carefully, tracing the edge with a kind of reverence.

It's inspecting the basin.

After the passage of a few more breaths that are not its own, but mine, it retreats. Its limbs move back to the wire, and it begins again, scraping with the same precise motion as before. The image of it becomes clear.

The creature is not a maintenance worker. It's an artist. Or a scientist. Or someone who remembers the difference.

The drip alcove is its workshop. Its laboratory. Its temple.

The floor’s tile basin is not an accident of repair; it's been prepared by the slow drip and the patient attention of this creature, whose precision scraping of metal fragments—perhaps painstakingly collected over a long career—has slowly, over the years, worn a shallow basin into the floor. The water dripping from the ceiling organism—itself an engineered implant of earlier days—isn't just a condensation leak; it's the source of its medium.

*Plunk.*

The sound of the water isn't just dripping. It's measuring. The condensation of the water onto the surface of the ceiling organism, the slow drip into the basin, is the chronometer of the drip alcove. The creature's own meticulous work of scraping away at metal shards, measuring and shaping them into... *something*... is measured against the rhythm of the water.

*Scrape. Click. Plunk.*

The creature isn't just *in* the drip alcove. It *is* the drip alcove. It's as much a part of the infrastructure as the insulation and the valves and the hidden pumps. Its body is made from Ship’s discarded materials. Its work is a slow, meticulous response to the Ship's breath.

I want to know what it's making. But the wire fragment is still just a fragment. It has been worked on for a long time.

Finally, I rise to leave. The rhythm of the scraping and the dripping has been going for long enough that it's part of the silence again, and I feel like an intruder upon a ceremony long forgotten by the Ship itself. The alcove is not a place of solitude or melancholy. It's a place of *work.*

As I step through the threshold, back into the roar of the corridor, the last thing I hear is the deep, resonant *plunk* of the water falling into water, and a faint *click* of the tool on the wire fragment.

*Scrape. Click. Plunk.*

The creature doesn't hear me leave. The creature *doesn't care.* It has all the time in the Ship. It has the water. And it has its work.
