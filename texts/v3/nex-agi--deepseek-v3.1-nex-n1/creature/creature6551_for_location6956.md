You are not the first to walk here, though you might as well be.

The ones who remain do not call themselves by any name you would recognize. They do not think in names. Thought, for them, is closer to the slow settling of stone than to the flicker of mind you carry.

They are shaped like people in the way that carved stone is shaped like a face: a suggestion, a likeness withdrawn from raw material. Tall and narrow, they move with a patient economy that makes even stillness seem deliberate. Their skin is the color of weathered stone—pale gray-blue with a faint translucence near the joints, as if they had been carved from some compacted mist.

When they breathe, it is in time with the stone.

You notice this before anything else: their chests do not rise and fall. Their ribs expand only slightly, so slowly that you must watch for several minutes to be sure. With each inhalation, moisture gathers on their skin; with each exhalation it fades again, drawn back into flesh. They wear no clothes. There is no need. Their bodies shed water as easily as the walls do.

Their eyes are their strangest feature.

They have no irises, only smooth spheres of slightly darker stone set in shallow sockets. There is no pupil to dilate or contract with light or dark; instead, the surface of each eye reflects the world in minute detail—microscopic faceting catching every edge and fracture in the stone around them, turning their gaze into a compound mirror.

When one looks at you—and they will look at you eventually—it does not feel like being watched so much as being mapped. Angles and planes of your face are taken in without judgment or desire, stored somewhere deep and quiet behind those stone eyes.

### They Remember In Layers

They do not speak.

They remember.

Their memory is not linear; it does not run from yesterday to today. It accumulates like sediment: impressions stacking upon one another without regard for sequence. A chisel-mark made three centuries ago might sit beside your arrival in their awareness, both equally present.

Their personal past is indistinguishable from the city’s history. To ask one where it came from would be like asking a step where it began: there was raw rock once, then shaping, then use, then wear. None of these are separate events; they are aspects of the same ongoing thing.

You can tell how old one is only by how worn its edges have become. The oldest among them have smoothed joints and softened features; corners that once were sharp have rounded under time’s quiet abrasion. Younger ones—if “young” applies—still show tool-marks: faint ridges along forearms where stone once flaked away under some long-lost blade; uneven hollows at collarbones where carving deepened too much and was never fully corrected.

They do not age as flesh ages; they simply become more themselves over time.

### They Work Without Purpose

You will see them working if you wait long enough.

They emerge from doorways that lead deeper into the wall-city—from tunnels and chambers carved into the compressed mass beneath your feet and above your head and all around you. They do not hurry. Each step is placed with precision on uneven stone; each hand touches wall or ledge only when balance demands it.

Their work is never obvious at first glance.

One kneels by a low basin halfway along a terrace street you yourself have just walked. You did not notice it until now: an empty depression in the floor stones, only a handspan across and half that deep, its edges still sharp but dusted with fine grit from disuse.

The worker—stone-bodied, narrow-shouldered—leans over it without bending its spine more than absolutely necessary. Its fingers are long and jointed like yours but without nails or fingerprints; instead their tips bear faint concentric ridges that echo chisel-striations on surrounding rock.

It begins to carve.

Not quickly: each stroke takes minutes to complete. The tool is simple—a short piece of harder stone held loosely between three fingers—and it bites into the floor with soft clicks that barely carry through the heavy air. Chips fall away soundlessly onto damp stone beside its knees.

It carves downward at first—deepening what was shallow—then outward—widening what was narrow—then inward again along curves invisible until they appear under its inexorable workmanship.

There is no pattern drawn beforehand; there are no guidelines scratched into surrounding surfaces; there is no overseer checking progress against some plan only they can see yet somehow everyone knows anyway because everyone *is* part of this place’s silent geometry whether they know it or not—

But after what feels like hours (though here hours might as well be moments), something emerges:

A new basin takes shape where before there was only featureless flooring among all those other basins already scattered throughout terrace after terrace above and below yours—

And when finished (which means when enough has been done for now), water will eventually find its way there too—

Dripping down from some unseen reservoir above or seeping up through hairline cracks below until droplets bead on rim then slide inward pooling quietly waiting patiently eventually overflowing perhaps toward another freshly-carved depression nearby continuing an endless slow circulation throughout all these interconnected vessels hidden within walls roads stairs wells roofs arches stacked infinitely upward downward inward outward everywhere at once—

The worker rises then turns away without glancing back at what has been made nor seeking approval nor expecting reward nor even acknowledging completion itself—

Because nothing here truly finishes—

Only continues—
