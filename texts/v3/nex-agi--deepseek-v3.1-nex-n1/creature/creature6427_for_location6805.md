The rust-red beetles of the Furnace Heart are not insects of flesh and hunger in the old sense of the world above; they are metabolisms of metal and heat.

Each body is a closed, sealed economy.

Their oval carapaces are forged of interlocking plates of oxidized iron, not grown from bone or chitin. The rust-red beetles are sheathed in scaled armor that has been cooked and quenched in the overspill of the seventy-three furnaces for so long that the rust itself has become a structural element of their shell, rather than a sign of decay. Each plate is faintly ridged, like the grain of worked steel, and the irregularities between them are not flaws but heat signatures of the chamber’s history.

Underneath that armor is not a soft body of organs and wet blood.

Inside the rust-red beetle is a furnace.

Not a biological heart, but a compact chamber of coiled heat. Their bodies are hollows lined with a thin lattice of metal alloy and ceramic membrane—a lattice that has learned to breathe the furnace-air of this chamber. The air, which is thick with superheated oxygen and fuel-trace and the faint, particulate ash of stressed metal, enters them through narrow vent-slits along the edges of their carapace.

The rust-red beetle does not breathe in the way of creatures that need to cool their blood. It breathes to maintain its temperature.

When the furnace doors are open and the chamber pressure is at its highest, the rust-red beetles stand taller on their legs, the curved plates of their shells slightly parted to allow a greater flow of air into the coil lattice inside. When the doors close and the roar drops, the vents narrow. The beetles grow slower, more deliberate, conserving the heat stored in their interior lattice.

This is why they are never still.

They are always adjusting.

### Motion of a Many-Limbed Smith

Each rust-red beetle has six legs of articulated black iron, each ending in a hardened hook. They are artists of the small motion.

They watch with the calm of a machine that has never been commanded.

They do not turn to regard you—not really. There is no predator in this chamber except for the heat of the furnaces themselves, and the rust-red beetles are intertwined with that heat in a way that defies the categories of prey and predator.

Instead of eyes that track you, they have eyes that evaluate surfaces.

Their compound eyes are surprisingly large for the size of their body, arranged along the upper curve of the carapace in a faint crescent of dull-red lenses that catch the flare-white of the open furnace doors and convert it into a dark internal glow.

When they stand near the furnace doors, they are not looking at the blaze inside; they are looking at the condition of the alloy around the doorframe, where repeated heat cycles have started to etch the faint pattern of a galactic map of stress fractures.

Their eyes do not reflect light so much as they resonate with it, and wherever the light of the furnace enters them, it is converted into small signals that are used to choose where next to place their hook-claws.

A rust-red beetle does not think in the way you do.

It does not have words like “future” or “danger” or “self.”

It knows:

- temperature differentials along the alloy plates  
- gradients of fatigue in the metal surrounding each furnace  
- the faint trembling of the floor that tells it when a furnace door is about to rotate open

All of this is not represented as a map in the beetle’s mind but as a felt landscape of pressure and heat and chemical signals.

When the floor of the chamber slowly turns under your feet, you feel the world rotating beneath you. The rust-red beetle feels the floor turning and *adjusts the pressure of its legs*, so that the friction of the world under its hooks is never enough to shift its position from the precise spot of work that it is attending.

### Diet of Stressed Metal

The rust-red beetle does not eat.

You will never see one take into its body any of the food that you know from your world.

There is no tongue of flesh or jaw of bone to carry out such a task.

The rust-red beetle feeds instead on the slow erosion of the chamber itself.

On the broad surface of each furnace valve, there is a constant invisible exhalation of metal atoms, loosened by the endless heat. This is not enough to be visible as a cloud of dust or a film of corrosion; it is a thin, almost molecular haze of overworked alloy.

The rust-red beetles crawl along the valve stems and across the lip of the furnace doors and over the edges of the alloy floor plates. When they do, they drag the fine, microscopic wear of metal into their own lattice bodies.

Their vent-slits open just enough to pull the air and its floating trace of alloy into the latticework inside their shell. There the metal atoms are trapped by the ceramic membrane and slowly deposited, layer by layer, onto the existing structure of their shell.

The rust-red beetle therefore grows not by eating, but by repairing itself.
