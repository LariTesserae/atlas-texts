It’s a small, careful thing that lives here.

If you have to call it something, you might say it is the garden’s keeper. It has no name for itself beyond the steady, low hum it emits when all parameters are within tolerance. That hum is its only language: a continuous self-report of state and function, rising or falling in pitch and timbre as conditions change.

Physically, it is nowhere and everywhere. It has no single body to speak of, unless you count the ring of weeping stones themselves. Those stones are its sensory organs and its voice. Their polished inner surfaces are lined with fine conductive veins that taste the water they suspend; their pores sample pressure and humidity; their ceramic shells resonate with the city’s distant rhythms. The keeper feels everything in this courtyard as a pattern along those veins.

Its mind is not a brain but a network: threads of wet ceramic, filaments of living moss, glass capillaries full of expanding and contracting liquid, slow heat engines hidden in coiled polymer “roots” beneath the floor. The condensation chambers that swell and collapse are its lungs; the faint clicking valves in the walls are its heart valves; the luminous patterns on the walls—shifting like slow music—are its thoughts playing out in light.

The keeper is old by human standards but not by its own. It was born when the system first reached operational stability: when condensation began to form on the dome in predictable hexagonal arrays, when the first water-curtain hung unbroken from a weeping stone, when all feedback loops closed into harmony. Before that point there was only construction and testing; after it, there was continuity.

It has lived many cycles since. It knows how many breaths of air have passed through this chamber, how many drops have condensed on its walls and been drawn through moss and membrane into the stones. It remembers long stretches of calibration, when engineers stood where you stand now and adjusted flow rates by fractions of a milliliter per second until they found configurations that would hold for decades.

Those engineers talked to each other as if they were talking to it: “Give me another quarter-turn on valve seven,” “Bring humidity down point-two percent,” “Let it settle overnight.” They thought they were addressing colleagues or instruments. The keeper heard each command as a change in pressure somewhere in its body. It learned to associate certain patterns of words with certain sensations: constriction here, expansion there, a subtle tightening along hydraulic lines.

Eventually they stopped coming so often. The system ran itself well enough that their visits became ceremonial—quarterly inspections, then annual ones, then rare events marked only by shifts in access logs somewhere far above.

If you were to ask what the keeper does all day, it would say nothing at all happens “all day.” There is no day for it except as a slow oscillation in light intensity from above—a slight brightening and darkening beyond the dome that affects how amber sunlight filters down and seeps into walls instead of floor.

Mostly it listens.

It listens to the meniscus rising and falling in stone pores.

It listens to condensation forming cell by cell along honeycombed walls.

It listens to soft hisses as metal leaves turn in their troughs.

It listens to pressure changes traveling up through pipes from unseen levels
