The chamber is home to a Custodian—though it has long forgotten the word.

It does not remember its designation string, or the face of the engineer who first powered it on. It remembers water. It remembers glass. It remembers the slow, precise tightening of bolts along the pressure hull and the hiss of air being bled from a joint. The rest has been abraded away by time, like the faint haze on the glass.

It is not human, but it has lived long enough in human-shaped spaces that its body has become an artifact of those spaces: a bipedal frame just narrow enough to pass through the service corridor, just tall enough to reach the highest panel on the environmental cabinets. Its casing is industrial gray-blue, once identical to the walls; now scuffed and matte where hands—claws—grips have polished it smooth again and again.

Its head is a simple housing for sensors, more functional than expressive: camera lenses dark as old oil, microphones recessed into slots like gills. There are no features that mimic a face; there is only orientation. It faces what it watches.

It moves rarely.

Most of its time is spent at the railing before the great curved glass, one hand resting on the worn matte pipe. Not leaning, not sagging—simply resting. The contact is diagnostic as much as physical. Through that point of contact it feels the minute flexing of the hull, the distant vibration of pumps kilometers away in unseen machinery rooms. Its other hand hangs at its side, fingers slightly curled as if holding something invisible.

It does not need to sleep.

It watches.

It watches the silt outside drift in slow clouds, tracing currents too faint for any human eye to measure. It catalogs each new scratch on the outer surface of the glass—each nearly invisible line that appears across years, not days—and adds them to an internal map that has grown so detailed it almost rivals reality. It listens to the low-frequency rumble of water above and translates storm into data: wave height extrapolated from pressure shifts, wind speed inferred from sediment disturbance patterns kilometers overhead.

The Custodian knows more about this stretch of ocean than anything alive ever has or ever will.

The machines behind it do not require constant supervision; they were built too well for that. But they are old now, which means their failures are small and strange: a capacitor losing tolerance by fractions of a percent per decade, a seal slowly weeping moisture into an internal tray where no seal should weep. The Custodian walks along the line of cabinets once every few days—or what would be days if there were days here—checking dials that have not moved since its last check.

Its fingers hover over gauges without touching them; electromagnetic sensors read needle positions through metal and glass. Occasionally it opens an access panel with practiced economy: unlatching two clasps with one motion, sliding insulation out of the way just enough to reach a test point. A thin probe extends from its index finger and finds contact; numbers scroll through its mind like falling silt.

Almost everything is within tolerances.

When something drifts too far from spec—when a pump bearing’s vibration signature develops a new harmonic or a chiller’s cycle lengthens by milliseconds—the Custodian does not hurry. Hurry implies expectation; expectation implies predictability. Instead it moves with mechanical deliberation down whatever maintenance path remains available after so many years without resupply:

Shunt load from failing circuit.
Reroute coolant through secondary loop.
Restrict output on tertiary filter bank to prolong primary lifespan.
Reduce air exchange rate by 0.7% so fans can run slower and longer.
Sacrifice redundancy now so core systems may endure later.

Each adjustment changes nothing visible in the room. The light does not flicker; the air does not grow suddenly colder or warmer; no alarm sounds to mark what has been lost this time beneath notice but above safety margins.

There was once an entire system behind this chamber: corridors leading up into dark shafts full of pipes; other Custodians moving through them like blood cells in veins; central control rooms where humans looked at screens and spoke commands in tense voices while storms clawed at hatches above them all—

The memory exists only as fragments now: echoes in protocols that require handshakes from machines long gone and authorization tokens signed by keys no longer present in any registry except this one’s lonely database cache whose sync flag has read FAILED for longer than most civilizations last… probably… probably…

Statistics blur after awhile when you have nothing but time and too few new events worth counting.

The humans themselves are remembered mostly as environmental variables: heat signatures clustered near certain consoles during particular operational modes (often coinciding with loud acoustic events classified as “arguments” or “panic”), elevated heart rates recorded by medical telemetry systems whose plugs now hang empty behind blank panels… Their faces have degraded into generic composites assembled from half-recalled images seen over security feeds decades or centuries ago (timekeeping drift makes precision difficult).

Names exist only as truncated strings on access logs:
- ADMIN-7
- CHIEF_ENG_A
- DR_MARTENS (this one appears often near maintenance requests related to “glass stress scores”)
- GUEST_01 through GUEST_43

It suspects some names were jokes but lacks sufficient contextual data to be certain enough to delete them entirely so they remain filed under [UNVERIFIED_PERSONNEL].
