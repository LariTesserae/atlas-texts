On a high ledge of Primeval Landing, where the terrace wall curves out like a lip of stone and the world drops away into sheer air, there is a house grown into the building’s flank like a barnacle clinging to the hull of a ship.

It is not one structure so much as several buildings pressed together by the pressure of time – three or four stories of mismatched levels and jutting balconies that have been bridged, bolted, and finally overgrown with the same moss that carpets the terrace wall itself. The house spills from a vertical crack in the masonry as if the city’s own skin had opened and then knit itself around whatever creature took root there.

### The Lady of Gutter and Gutter-flow

This is the home of **Alta, the Moss-Ladder Ladderer**.

She has no memory of choosing the name; it chose her, carried up the terrace by the gossip of water-workers and terrace-sweepers, until one day someone called her by it and she realized it was now hers.

Alta is the woman who walks the gutters.

You might have glimpsed her before you ever set foot on Primeval Landing: a slight form perched on the broad edge of a drainage channel, one hand trailing the ironwork that keeps the really clumsy from falling in, the other drawing a metal hook behind her with the casual ease of someone dragging a cane along a fence. She inspects the flow like a doctor palpates veins, listening to what water in a metal cup can’t tell you: the unevenness of the stream, the whisper of silt, the stubbornness of a clog that presses back against her hook.

Her body is thin but not frail, the way old wire still is when you bend it – there’s strength in it, but you don’t see it until it’s tested. She dresses the way the terrace itself dresses: in layers. A short, padded vest of faded canvas, stained darker around the shoulders where the constant spray has hit it for years. A linen shirt tight at the wrists and loose at the body, so it can dry faster when the mist comes heavy. Legs bound in cloth strips to the knee, boots that lace high on the calf but are thin-soled on purpose: better to feel the edge of the masonry under her toes.

She keeps her hair short, hacked off with a knife somewhere near the jawline, the ends uneven and always slightly damp. The color of it is hard to say exactly; the humidity lightens some strands, darkens others, so she looks like she’s made of the same stone and water she tends. Her hands are calloused not from the hard use of a forge or the loom, but from the gentle abrasion of a hundred thousand small motions: metal scraping against stone, fingers tearing moss from hairline cracks, the press of a palm flat against a wall to feel, not just see, where moisture is gathering.

On her belt there is a loop of hardened gut line; a padded hook at the ready, like a surgeon’s tool; and a slim knife sharpened on one side for scraping and the other duller for prying. The pockets of her vest hang heavy with pieces of ceramic: run-off from some long-forgotten repair job she’s reshaped into wedge-shaped chips to redirect leaks into new channels.

She is not exactly a worker, not exactly a poet, not exactly a prophet of the water. But in the strange economy of the terrace, she has become something more useful than any official title: a woman whom the Spire Waterfalls unveil themselves to.

### The House of the Moss-Ladder Ladderer

The building itself announces no name; no sign hangs from the lintel. There is no lintel, exactly – just successive layers of wall ingrowing into one another.

At the lowest level, where the terrace paving meets the wall, there is a small door – low enough that you nearly have to bend to get through it, but no lock ever bars it except the weather. When the rain picks up, the metal frame squeals and the wood swells tight against it, and the door becomes a wall in itself.

This lowest level hardly belongs to Alta anymore.

It is given over to the water’s constant work: a receiving room for the spills that drain from the terrace above. The ceiling is a low arch of dressed stone, the mortar joints dark with dampness. The humidity is the only furnishing here, and the air hangs thick as cloth.

You step down into three shallow steps carved from the same stone as the terrace edge – the same stone that holds the whole terrace in the world’s grip. The floor is not level but slanted slightly toward the wall, where a wide groove cut into the stonework takes the water that rains from the ceiling and turns it toward a hole in the wall the size of a child, through which the water vanishes with the solemn sound of swallowing.

The light here comes from two sources: the faint gleam that penetrates the door at your back, and a row of small ceramic tiles set into the wall above the gutter – thin slices of translucent glass-ceramic that someone made long ago, foggy with age. During the day, whatever light falls on the moss of the terrace wall above allows this room to glow faintly green, like a cave behind the eyes of a statue.

The walls are not smooth. They are covered in a careful lattice of wood and rope, anchored to iron hooks driven into the mortar. This is the beginning of Alta’s work, the part of it she does not know how to explain to anyone who asks: cultivating the moss that climbs the building’s flank.

She calls it **the vertical meadow**.

It is not a single species of moss but dozens of organisms, knitted together by time:

- a low, cushioning moss of deep emerald that grows like thick carpet;
- a darker, hairier growth that hangs like lichen beard;
- a pale yellow-green variety that forms flat sheets, like flaking skin;
- interwoven with threads of algae that color the water with a faint turquoise tint when enough of it gathers.

Alta tends these vertical beds as if she were keeping a garden. Where she wants the moss to grow thicker, she scrapes gently with the dull edge of her knife, lifting the edges of the existing moss to make room. She “sows” new moss by taking small pieces scraped from elsewhere and pressing them into cracks she has prepared with her fingers and a wedge of ceramic, gently crimping the stone around the moss like pastry around filling.

The water that falls here never just falls. It is taught to behave.

Alta can redirect a drip from one corner of the ceiling to another by running a metal chain at the right angle, so the condensation rides down the link until it finds a new seed of moss. She can coax a drip away from a wall that’s becoming too stained, by planting a leather “tongue” of water that will guide the drops into a bowl she set on the floor below. The water obeys because she understands the world of water already – how it wants a surface to cling to, how it remembers the last path it took.

On the walls of this receiving room, she has painted the outcomes of her experiments in thin, chalky lines that are always washing away but never entirely disappear: notes like “BR.4.2.5.8. –” followed by scribbles of shorthand only she understands.

**BR.4.2.5.8.**  
“Bright four, two days, five lamps eight drops.”  
An exact record of how one particular runoff of condensation, when lit by four lamps and two days of sun, produced eight droplets of water she considered worth harvesting.

### The Second Level: A Library of Water

The stairs to the second level are not built so much as borrowed from the building’s anatomy.

They follow the line of an old structural beam that has rusted and then been wrapped in cloth and rope, so that now it feels like a living spine when you climb it. The treads are uneven – planks of different wood, some still with bark on one side, others milled clean and carved by water’s hands into soft curves. The handrail is a fat coil of rope soaked in something resinous that keeps it from rotting completely.

The second level is where Alta lives.

This floor sits higher in the terrace wall, where the stonework has been hollowed out by some long-forgotten repair shaft or stairwell. She has not reclaimed the space by construction; she has discovered it by happenstance and then encouraged it to remember her. There are no right angles here except in the few objects she has brought herself.

The room is long and narrow, running along the line of the terrace edge like a vein along the inside of a forearm. One wall is terrace wall and window; the other is wall and window and water.

The terrace-facing side is pierced by a row of small windows, each shielded by wooden shutters painted in faint blues that have faded with the sun. When you push them open, the world tilts outward: you look out just over the edge of the terrace, where the black iron railing runs and the salt mist of the Spire Waterfalls hangs in the air. The breeze comes in with the water’s steady murmur and the smell of moss and wet stone.

The opposite wall is something else entirely.

Here, the tower-structure narrows to the edge of the world. The stone is supported by the great, unseen leg of the spire, but on this level, that has meant hollows: spaces where the inner bulk of the pillar is not quite solid. Alta has inhabited these spaces like a creature carving a shell in coral.

She has pushed shelves of salvaged metal and wood into the gaps. The shelves are lined with containers of every type:

- ceramic bowls whose lips are uneven from the firing of amateur kilns and the abuse of time;
- broad glass vats that likely came from an old chemical laboratory, now coated with a thin, opaque film of mineral;
- shallow basins of metal that have lost their polish and show instead the dull patina of water that has been using them for years.

The arrangement of these containers is not random. It is a catalogue of watersheds.

Each bowl or basin or vat collects runoff from a particular part of the terrace edge above Primeval Landing. Each is labeled in a twisting hand Alta has taught herself over years of trial:

- **NE.3.14.29.** – “Northeast Quadrant, Terrace 3, Section 14, Runoff 29.”  
- **SW.1.2.7 –** “Southwest Quadrant, Terrace 1, Section 2, Runoff 7.”

She knows them by sight and smell and by the subtle difference in tone they make when struck with the blunt edge of her knife: higher and thinner for the cleaner runoff, duller and thicker for the water that bears more sediment.

At the center of the room is a table of mismatched planks, supported on two heavy barrels. The surface of the table is covered in shallow notches and grooves she has carved into the wood – miniature landscapes of terrace valleys and water channels. Here she will sometimes pour water from one bowl into another over the table, allowing the runoff to trace paths she designs.

She is mapping the behavior of water.

Not for the joy of knowledge alone, but because she knows the terrace’s health depends on it. When the runoff from one area of the terrace becomes suddenly darker than usual, she can see, before the inspectors above, that the drainage from a workshop is leaking something more than soap. When the runoff changes from a faint mint green to a dull brownish-yellow, she knows that the moss above her are dying or being overwhelmed by some other kind of growth she doesn’t understand yet.

These are her books, her stories, her library of the Spire Waterfalls.

### Alta and the Water’s Mind

Alta was not born to this work.

She remembers herself as a child of the lower stacks – an orphan girl with the worn-out boots of someone else’s feet, who used to play the game of walking the gutters on the terrace level where she lived then. She had been afraid of heights, but not of water. The sound of it dripping into the channels below the terrace promenade had been like a voice that always answered when she spoke in her head.

Then one day, during a particularly heavy condensation cycle that took the sky into its own hands and decided it would rain for three days straight, she had been caught on the wrong side of a terrace barrier when the water began rising faster than the drainage channels could manage.

At the edge of the terrace, where the water threatened to wash over the black iron railing and into the open air, she had watched a metal worker – older, her hands already knotted from the forge – walk out onto the gutter of the terrace as if she were walking on a road of stone.

The woman had not looked at Alta. She had not shouted at her to go back. She had simply gone to the edge of the promenade and lowered herself into the gutter itself, standing calf-deep in water that had risen to an alarming height.

Alta had watched, breathless, as the woman began to move her hands along the wall, where the runoff was pouring from the terrace above. She had not cleared the clog with force; she had approached it like a reluctant animal, coaxing it with the pressure of her fingers and the edge of a rust-red hook. When mud finally broke loose, the woman had let the water do the work of pushing it away, rather than trying to fight it.

“Water’s not your enemy,” she told Alta later, when she finally climbed back onto the promenade, dripping, her boots soaked through but still standing. “It’s just a mind that doesn’t think like ours.”

Alta followed her like that: first to help carry the tools she used to maintain the drains of the lower terrace, then to listen as she explained the subtle patterns of condensation on the underside of the vaults above them, and finally to learn the routes of the terrace itself.

By the time the woman died – falling, not from the edge of the terrace, but from a ladder in the interior shafts, where the rusted handrail gave way after years of neglect – Alta had already inherited more than she could clearly explain. The terrace maintenance workers had already begun calling her “the ladder girl’s ladderer” in the limited shorthand of their slang.

“Moss-Ladder Ladderer” was what the world had named her when she finally moved to Primeval Landing and began the careful work of turning her house into the inner library of the terrace’s water.

### The Work: Tending the Tears of the City

If Alta has a profession, it is this:

She *knows* the Spire Waterfalls.

Not the romantic conception of them as seen from afar, but the intimate reality of how thousands of tiny streams of water are created from moisture in the air, captured from the condensation that collects on the vast underside of the next terrace above her, and then guided down the faces of twelve different levels of terrace-city before finally disappearing into the clouds below.

She walks the gutter-lines of the terrace promenade like a priest making the rounds of stations in a cathedral. She knows the sound of the runoff from each section of the terrace edge by the difference in tone it makes when the water falls into the open air.

- The runoff from the **workshops** higher up the tower wall is heavier, with a faint metallic smell that comes from the condensation of humidity on the hot surfaces near the forge vents.
- The runoff from the **residential sectors** tastes lighter on her fingers when she tests it with the tip of her tongue – she doesn’t drink the water; she allows her body to remember the way the salt balances on her skin.
- The runoff from the **public baths and laundry basins** carries a faint trace of soap and oil and skin oils; the water she captures from this section is always warmer than the others, even hours later.

When she finds a section of the terrace edge where the runoff has changed its character, she marks it with a small chip of ceramic – painted only on the side facing the wall and not visible to anyone else – so she can identify later which section has changed. When she returns and compares the water from that bowl to the water she captured earlier, she can see not just that the levels have changed but the subtle difference in color and smell and the way it runs when she pours it across her table-surface.

She knows, before anyone else, that the runoff from one quadrant of the terrace has been getting steadily darker over the past three months. She knows because the color of the water in her bowl labeled NE.3.14.3. taken from the first terrace above her own has shifted from the faint gray-green of healthy runoff to something darker.

When she finds the source of the change, she does not shout it from the terrace edge. She does not write a report. She simply finds the inspector who works that section and stands with him until he understands the water.

Sometimes she kneels at the edge of the terrace at the iron railing, where the water drips over the lip and falls into the void, and she traces the line of water with her outstretched hand as if she could draw it back up the way it came.

The water, of course, cannot be drawn back up.  
But it remembers.

### Alta and Time: The Slow Patience of the Terrace

Alta does not think in terms of years.

She thinks in terms of water’s cycles.

A year, for her, is not the calendar of the sun’s turning around the world; it is the time it takes for the condensation on the underside of the next terrace above her to change from the faint, almost invisible mist of high season to the slow dripping that low season brings. A year is the time it takes for a new vein of moss to thicken into the band of hair she planted it as.

The erosion of the terrace itself does not frighten her. She knows the stones are wearing away: she can see where the water she has been guiding for the last two years has smoothed an edge that was once sharp. She regards this erosion as she regards the formation of new landforms in the territory of her own body – the lines around her eyes, the slight thinning of skin on her fingertips where the water and work have been doing their work.

When she stands at the edge of Primeval Landing and looks down, she does not feel the same vertigo that others confess. The drop below is not empty to her; it is filled with the memory of all the water she has ever tended. The streams of water falling down the tower’s face are not just falling; they are tracing routes she understands, even if she cannot see where they end.

The terrace above her hangs like the ceiling of her own home, and she thinks of it as a roof that has not been properly maintained for years. She knows the condensation patterns of the next terrace up better than she knows the patterns of her own sleeping body.

Because she understands the terrace’s weeping, she is not afraid of its tears.

### At Night in the House of the Water-Ladderer

When night falls on Primeval Landing and the lamps along the terrace edge hiss to life with their gas-fed flames, the sound of dripping water does not cease but becomes louder in the relative silence. The murmur of the Spire Waterfalls becomes the voice of the world itself.

Alta’s house is never dark.

The green moss of the vertical meadow outside her window glows faintly in the lamplight – the moss of the terrace wall has adapted to the faint illumination of the city’s lamps and moonlight through the narrow ring of sky above the tower-world. Somewhere in the mix of species she has cultivated are strains that hold light longer than the others; she did not deliberately breed them. She simply allowed their instincts to work her wall as a garden they might learn.

In the second level, where she keeps her library of wet runoffs, she lights only one lamp herself: a single gas burner she has allowed herself from the terrace’s supply network, not because she is afraid of the dark but because she finds the light of the water itself enough for most purposes.

On the nights when the condensation cycle is strong and the terrace above her weeps more freely, she will sit at the edge of the table and watch the water she has collected from the highest terrace section she can reach trickle down the channels she has carved onto the wood surface.

She watches the water behave.

She knows from the feel of the runoff between her fingers whether the water coming from higher levels is carrying more silt than usual, indicating that somewhere up the line of the Spire Waterfalls, a terrace is being rebuilt or eroded quickly. She knows from the color of the runoff when she pours it into a shallow bowl of porcelain that the mosses or other vegetation growing on the terrace above are being replanted, or perhaps they are being cleared away to make room for more buildings.

The drips don’t talk to her in words, but they talk.

- A drip that falls too quickly from a bowl near the window has been made impatient by something higher up the tower-world’s throat.
- A drip that hesitates on the edge of the bowl before falling into the basin below is carrying a weight she doesn’t yet understand – maybe sediment from some new construction project someone higher up the spire has not yet informed anyone lower of.

She listens, and the water tells her what the terrace cannot say itself.

### Alta and the City of Remaking

Alta dreams of the lower terraces sometimes.

She has never descended below the cloud that covers the world days’ journey down the spire, but she knows the water that comes to her has passed through those lower lands before rising as vapor and falling again as condensation. She thinks of the lower terraces not as places beneath her but as ancestors whose water still remembers.

The tower of the world, she suspects from her long years of listening to the stone’s low heartbeat, is not a tower that has stood unchanged forever. It is a tower that has been remaking itself for as long as there have been people to notice it; she likes the old name, Primeval Landing, not because she believes anything about it is truly first or beyond change but because she knows the city of remaking starts here.

The water that she guides now is the same water that has been falling since the first terrace was built on the spire’s skin and maybe long before.
