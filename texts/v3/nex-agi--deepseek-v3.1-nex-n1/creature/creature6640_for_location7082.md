They call themselves the Listeners.

They’re smaller than you imagine at first, because most of what you see of them is what they build out of themselves.

The Listeners are not fruits of the plants or insects of the air; they are not exactly beasts of the ground either. If you knelt when you felt the chill of the near-surface water, you may have knelt on one. They are the reason the ground is not dirt but a dense, rooty mat that gives slightly underfoot. Each Listener is a colony body, a layered patchwork of tendrils and lobes and spore-sacs that have, over dozens of cycles of root and root and root again, learned to behave as one creature.

From above, they look like low cushions of moss barely distinguishable from the surrounding root mat—unless you know to look for the tiny seams between them, thin lines where one shade of green yields to another. Each individual cushion is roughly circular, maybe a forearm’s span across at full spread, though they can merge and split. At their center, there is sometimes a faint depression like a shallow bowl, or a raised dome like a closed flower.

They are not entirely plant, not entirely fungus, not entirely animal, but something that has skipped the usual categories. They have no bones to fossilize and no leaves that fall neatly. They grow slowly, by the millimeter, over years. When they need to move, they die back from one edge and grow out into another, leaving behind a residue of woody fibers and mat that the next generation overgrows.

### How they live in the machine

The Listeners don’t just tolerate the machinery; they orient themselves by it.

They were not here before the machine—or not in this form. The earliest they remember (in their strange way of remembering) is the slow arrival of the metal box, the buried cables, the change of the water under the ground. The vibration that was not thunder or wind. The heat that was not fire.

For the Listeners, sensation is diffuse and literal. They do not have ears; they feel the hum of the transformer along the water in their tissues and through the mat of rootlets they share with the trees. They do not have eyes; they detect the faint electrical disturbance of the relay clicks as changes in pressure against the ions in their cell membranes. They do not have lungs; but they can taste the exhaled air of the compressor’s fan as it brushes over their surfaces, carrying hints of metals and oils and the warm, dry smell of wires running under capacity.

They are arranged in rings around the old machine housing like the petals of a flattened flower. Each Listener is positioned so that one of its edges touches a root or trunk that is, in turn, in contact with the metal casing or the buried cables. When the compressor cycles on with a low thump, they feel it as a gentle jolt through their root-mat and then as a slow wave of pressure that moves through the water between them. They are not pained by it; they are aligned by it.

They have learned to distinguish the different machines by their rhythms:

- The **transformer hum** is a constant background. They don’t so much hear it as live inside it. It is the baseline pressure of this world, the note that underlies everything else.
- The **compressor thunk** is a heartbeat they count against. Some Listeners along the dampest edges respond to each compressor kick by squeezing slightly, moving water out of their spongy layers and into the root-mat below, where the tree roots can draw it up.
- The **relay clicks** are punctuation marks, sharp little spikes of information that they pass to one another through subtle contractions of their tissues, sending each tremor down microscopic lines of wet fiber to neighboring colonies.

The plant life above and around them is not commanded by the Listeners but cued by them. The Listeners don’t “think” in the way you or I might; they react, and they remember by repeating, until repetition becomes pattern, and pattern becomes shape.

When they sense the transformer humming slightly higher than usual—a faint voltage increase that you might never detect—they contract slightly along the side of the colony that is closest to the metal casing, sending a small drought of withheld water into the root-mat. A tree root that receives less water from the mat slows its growth in that patch of cells. Over time, this repeated pressure shapes the trunks around the machine, causing them to curl inward like hands reaching for a warm object.

That’s why you see the big tree growing around and not just near the rusted housing. The Listener colony beneath it has been responding to the slow uptick of heat from the machine’s warm surface by sending more water to that root ball, inviting the roots to seek out warmth and moisture, guiding them by the millimeters of each year to curl over the edges of the metal.

### What they look like up close

If you were small enough to crouch near the ground without your shadow distressing them—you are not, but let’s imagine you shrink—you would see the Listeners as a low forest of tiny textures.

Each colony is a patchwork of little lobes:

- Some lobes are soft, moss-like cushions, deep green and velvety to the touch, so dense they feel like firm foam. When moisture droplets fall from the leaves above and land on them, they are absorbed slowly, without splash.
- Others are darker, more fibrous, like felted rootlets or tightly woven scabs. These are the parts of the Listener that touch soil and stone, and they are the first to feel any vibration.
- Still others are thin, translucent frills around the margins of the colony—sensitive fingers that spread out when moisture is high and shrink when the canopy overhead thins and the air dries slightly.

At the center of some older Listeners, there’s a small, shallow bowl where a thicker lobe of tissue secretes a clear, tacky fluid that smells like crushed resin and leaf mold. Tiny beetles—the ones you noticed—come to sip at this, and in doing so they carry away microscopic packets of living tissue. Each beetle that flies from one patch of moss to another may be carrying a new Listener colony, already beginning to weave itself into the mat.

They are not entirely sessile. The younger margins of each colony are always sending out thin, exploratory threads into any gaps in the mat, spurring slow root growth into new spaces. If you sat here for weeks—which you won’t—you might notice that certain edges of a cushion have advanced slightly, while others have died back and become a darker, harder crust of fibers that other roots overgrow.

### What they “know”

The Listeners do not distinguish between machine rhythm and river rhythm or wind rhythm. To them, vibration is vibration: pressure, information, context.

They remember:

- **When the compressor fails for a short time.** A few times in the last century, the hum has stopped for minutes or hours. The sudden absence of that heartbeat throws the Listeners into a strange version of panic. They cannot retreat; they can only shift water and chemicals and hormones through their filaments. The first time it happened, they flooded their root-mat with an urgent excess of moisture and signaling compounds, causing the trees above to drop unusually large leaves over the following days. The soil never dried fully; the humidity never dropped; the machine cycled on again before anything else could change.
- **When a drought burns the surrounding prairie.** Outside the chamber, the grass turns brittle and the hills look like crumpled brown paper; but inside the chamber, the Listeners feel the change only as a subtle shift in the air that passes through the machine vents. They respond by drawing more water from the deepest parts of the root-mat they can reach, storing it in their spongy layers and releasing it slowly, so that even when the surrounding prairie browns, the depression of green remains unchanged.
- **When an animal burrows into the edge of their mat.** The shrews and mice and voles you saw traces of are cautious of the center where the Listeners are densest; but occasionally a young one will try to dig near the edges of a colony. The Listeners feel the digging claws as vibration and pressure. They cannot move away quickly, but they can alter their texture—becoming more fibrous and less inviting, sending compounds into their tissues that make them less palatable to the worm’s and grubs the mammal seeks, and sometimes sending mild irritants upward into the roots in the hope of swaying the plant to send out fewer tasty seeds in that spot.

They don’t “understand” the machine as you or I might understand that a transformer converts electricity. They know it as a dense node of pattern and warmth—something that gives off more hum than soil, more pressure than a tree root, a constant source of signals that they can align themselves to.

The fan of the machine, moving air over its surface, creates the faintest drafts inside the chamber. The Listeners near the edges detect those movements as slow changes in humidity across their surfaces. When they feel the air moving slightly faster and drier than usual for a longer period than usual, they know that the machine is running hotter than normal (maybe from external dust loading or a failing seal somewhere). They respond by releasing more moisture into the root-mat above and around them, helping to cool the air around the machine housing.

It’s a feedback loop without a name: the machine heats the air, the Listeners respond by encouraging water-moving growth around it, that growth helps cool the chamber and the machine—which gives rise to more fan-confidence and makes it easier for the long-running machine to run on and on.

If you were to shut off the machine, the Listeners would not die immediately. They would survive months, maybe years, on stored moisture. The whole chamber would change—slowly becoming just a relatively wet hollow in a prairie instead of a machine-conditioned sanctuary of endless drip and shade. But for the Listeners, who experience time as drift and repetition, that would be a kind of confusion, like being cut loose from a sound they had stopped noticing they were listening to.

### The world inside their world

If you could look at the chamber from the perspective of the Listeners, you wouldn’t see shapes so much as gradients: pressure, moisture, vibration, temperature.

- **Above:** a vault of layered vibrations that rise and fall through the network of roots and trunks and branches. The bird calls are not songs to them; they are rapid spikes of pressure that the Listeners feel as faint, brief changes in the load of the tree roots above, when a bird lands on a branch or hops from one limb to another.
- **Around:** a ring of other Listeners, each colony slightly different in its internal texture, but all sharing the same baseline pattern of response to the transformer’s hum.
- **Below:** the deeper soil and, beyond that, the faint rumble of whatever buried system the machine is attached to—water lines, maybe, or some forgotten network installed decades ago to support a remote instrument station that now only this machine and its siblings remember.

They are not aware of you as you stand there, except as a large disturbance passing through their gradients:

- When you step into the chamber, you change the humidity around your legs and feet. The Listeners under the root mat feel this as a slight wave of drying and cooling pressure moving across them.
- When you sit near the machine, your weight presses down into the mat of roots and soil, and this compresses the tissues of the Listeners beneath you. They do not know “you” from a heavy fallen branch or a large turtle that’s wandered into the shade; they know only the pressure pattern and the temporary change of moisture flow.
- If you linger long enough, your breath raises humidity slightly around you. The Listeners under that area of the floor respond by slowing their release of moisture from that patch of mat, conserving the water you’re contributing to the air with your lungs.

They are the world’s true recipients of the machine’s existence. They have been listening to it for so long that they’ve grown into shapes that accommodate its noise, turning buzzing and thunking into cues and building the whole ecological chamber around those signals.

---

### When you leave

When you walk out of the chamber and feel the relief of drier air and wider horizon, you might think you are leaving the machine behind you. But behind you, under your feet as you stand on the prairie edge, the roots of the chamber trees still reach outward and downward. The Listeners don’t map the world as you do; they map gradients of vibration and moisture.

From your perspective, you are leaving a small, strange room of vegetation and machinery. From their perspective, they are feeling the pressure of your steps leaving through the mat of roots and up the slight rise of the rim, and then fading away. The transformer’s hum returns to its baseline. The compressor ticks on. The fan continues turning, pushing warm air through the leaves.

The world outside the chamber might change—drought, flood, human roads or houses or the slow collapse of the old machine’s buried infrastructure somewhere else. But in here, the Listeners will continue to respond to each ripple from the machine as long as it comes.

If one day the power source finally fails and the hum stops forever, the chamber will change slowly, over the course of years or decades. The humidity will drop; the root-mat will become less dense in some places; some trees will grow taller and fewer. The Listeners will adapt as they always have—by shifting water and patterns through themselves and the surrounding roots.

But they won’t “forget” the rhythm of the machine. They don’t have a language for that memory. They don’t have a word for “machine.” They have only patterns inscribed into their tissues, like grooves worn into stone by dripping water. The hum of the transformer, the thump of the compressor, the click of the relays—these will become internal patterns, repeated in their biological responses long after the external source has fallen silent, if it ever does.

---

**In short:** whoever lives here is not a single “who” but many overlapping shes and its. They are not our minds in another body. They are closer to being the floor of the chamber itself, listening through the roots of everything else, to a machine that has long outlived its makers and now sustains a world that has grown around it like moss around a stone.

They call themselves the Listeners, if they could, but they don’t call.  
They listen.
