They do not give themselves a name that means “people” or “the living.” Their word for themselves is closer to “the patched ones.” It is a quiet word, spoken mostly to distinguish themselves from the stone, the river, and the moss. To be patched is to be mended; it is also to be a small piece fitted into a larger whole.

They are small and narrow of frame, built not for strength but for passage through tight spaces. Their shoulders are rounded but not broad, their torsos long; their knees and elbows have a sharpness that seems designed for folding themselves into passages where light barely reaches. Their skin is pale, almost translucent in the dim, shadowless brightness of the valley, with a faint bluish cast where veins come close to the surface. They tan only slightly under the diffuse sky, acquiring at most a soft grayish undertone like the underside of a river stone.

Hair grows thin and straight and cool-colored—greys, muted greens, dark browns that border on black. It falls in smooth sheets rather than curls or waves, as if moisture has trained it to lie close to the scalp. Many shave part of their heads where hair would catch on low lintels or hang into damp channels. Beards are rare among both sexes; what facial hair exists is kept short and neat, more like moss than fur. Eyebrows are sparse, often invisible from a distance. This gives their faces an unfinished look until you notice how clearly their eyes carry meaning.

Their eyes are large relative to their faces; they need to be. The pupils expand until little iris shows in the perpetual twilight under the mist. Most eyes are dark—deep browns and greens and grays that swallow light rather than reflect it. A few have eyes that seem almost colorless, like chips of quartzite held up toward the luminous ceiling of clouds. They blink slowly and deliberately; eyelids are thin membranes trained by centuries to sweep clear any fine spray from dripping eaves.

Hands and feet define them as much as anything else. Fingers are long and narrow, with many joints; they look fragile until you see how they move stones half their own weight by leverage alone. Nails are thick and hard, more like chitin than keratin, worn smooth at the tips from contact with stone and rope day after day. Their feet are broad for their size—short toes spread wide for balance on wet ledges and worn stair edges. Soles are tough enough to walk bare-skinned over gravel without bleeding; heels are thickened pads built by lifetimes on stone.

They dress in layers that blur them further into the city’s palette: soft leather cured to a dark grey-green; tightly woven plant-fibers stained with algae and moss-dyes; fleece-like under-layers from some small valley creature’s winter coat. Nothing hangs loose enough to catch in doors or channels; hoods have drawstrings so they can be pulled tight when passing beneath particularly low overhangs or through tunnels where water runs close overhead.

Colors never shout here; they murmur. Clothing tones match lichen-shadowed stone: muted olive greens, slate grays, deep teals that fade almost into black when wet. Patterns exist but they are functional first—subtle variations in weave density that indicate role or district or maintenance responsibility rather than decoration for its own sake.

They move like water finding its path: rarely in straight lines unless along gutters or marked channels on floors designed for straightness; otherwise they flow around obstacles with economy of motion rather than grace as such—there is no flourish left over after caution has taken its share of attention. On ladders worn smooth by centuries they ascend without looking at rungs beneath fingers hardened by friction with stone surfaces polished glass-smooth over generations.
