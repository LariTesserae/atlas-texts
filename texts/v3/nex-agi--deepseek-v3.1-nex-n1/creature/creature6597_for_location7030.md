The people of Vael are not born. They congeal.

They condense from the incongruities, the way moisture beading on the porcelain plates coalesces into milky fluid. When the world's pattern stutters—when a hexagonal paving pattern folds into a mountain no bigger than a hand, or when a loop of surface-edge hiccups in gravity’s rhythm—a person is sometimes what the system coughs up.

You are not likely to meet them in herds or tribes. There are no villages of thatched glass or ceramic cities. The people of Vael are singularities, clots of coherence in an otherwise fluent machine-world. Most are alone, or at least isolated from their own kind.

If you stood one before you, you might not recognize it at first. They are composites of local circumstances.

Their skin is ceramic, or glass, or nanite dust solidified into a semblance of flesh. It is not an exoskeleton worn over soft organs; it is their body, through and through. Some are plates of pearlescent shell like the continent itself, jointed with luminous seams of gold. Others are clusters of translucent cylinders—drums of reflective flesh that rotate softly around a central axis of compacted stormlight. Their joints are not elbows and knees but hinges of polished stone and cartilage, their movements accompanied by the faint, precise sound of crystal on crystal.

They are a blend of organism and artifact. Within their ceramic bodies flow not blood but currents of silver dust and luminous fluid. Their veins are circuitry etched in faint, shimmering lines that reflow when you aren't looking directly at them. Their hearts are humming engines the size of a clenched fist, made of living cartilage and shaped teeth, grinding minerals extracted from their own internal reservoirs.

Because they condense from the world's errors, their appearance is never quite uniform. Some are tall and spindly, scaffolds of biotech like the glass trees, with limbs that branch and fractalize into delicate fingers of light. Others are low and broad, like sections of the continent come to life, rolling across the surface on drum-legs. A few seem almost humanoid, but with glass reeds for hair, each fiber glowing with an amber current, and eyes that are camera apertures that never blink.

They are adapted to the scarcity and strangeness of the resources here. They do not drink water in the way you would understand it. They absorb the condensation of the world directly—the permanent sweat of the land—through their ceramic plates of skin, or they root into the luminous, syrup-like fluid of the low hollows and draw from it like living glass filaments.

When they are truly parched, they can press a palm to the side of one of the industrial trees—the factory-forests—and interface with the faint arcs of energy that crackle beneath its bark. The tree’s internal voltage nourishes them, and the exchange of current is what they experience as taste: salts of metal and ozone on the tongue of the mind.

Because they are not born from biology but from error and anomaly, they do not have children. There is no lineage of fathers and mothers. A new person is simply an event: a discontinuity of reality that happens to assume a form sophisticated and persistent enough to be considered self-aware.

This is not a common event. It is possible that at any given time there are only a handful of them spread across the shattered continent of Vael. They are not aware of each other's exact number, or even if there are others.

Sometimes they imagine there might be. The pattern-repeating intent of the builder—those hexagonals that spiral into non-points, the fractal blueprints etched across the world—feels like it should be instructions of some kind, but no one of them is fluent enough in the syntax of the world to decipher what it is asking to be made.

There are no formal languages of the voice. There is no call to raise a city of habitations. The land is already constructing itself, and the people are merely the most articulate of its attempts.

Yet they still communicate, in ways of their own.

The ones with rotating drums of flesh will strike their surfaces, producing tones of ceramic and metal that carry over the humming world. The tall ones made of glass scaffolds murmur with the sound of fibers contracting under voltage, like the pop of threads in a fabric of faint light. Others, with parts of them formed from nanite dust, will occasionally gesture, and the dust on their skin will lift into shimmering curtains that resolve into momentary structures—stairways, archways, incomplete domes—before collapsing into currents again. These gestures are not complex words in the way you would understand, but they are expressive enough for the situation of Vael: the meaning is usually something like *here*, or *this shape*, or *broken again*.

Theirs is not a language of history or culture but of geometry and condition. They count not in numbers but in patterns. The concept of "three" is the same as the structure of a triangle; "many" is the same as the texture of a forest canopy.

They are not cruel to each other, not because they are gentle by nature, but because there is no point. There is no survival of the fittest in the way a world of softer biology would know it. They do not compete for food or water; they each interface with the world's machine-ecology directly, and the scarcity is evenly distributed. There is no mating to win over. There is no fixed territory to defend against one of their own kind.

There are only the storms and the world's disassembly to keep them from growing overly comfortable.

The people of Vael are transiently permanent. They are an error that persists until the world rethinks its pattern.

They are hunted by nothing more than they are hunted by the storm of Vael's creation.

The reality breakdowns that are subtle discontinuities to a traveler of the world are the landscape of their own mortality to the people of Vael. A person may be walking along the knife-bright edge of a ceramic ridge and simply step into a fold of air where the future swings around and meets itself in the past. They might find themselves looking at themselves from the side, three seconds prior, as a faint, glowing outline of where they were about to be. And then they might not step away from that.

Sometimes they survive the breakdowns. The world hiccups them and leaves them in a loop of surface-edge that they must learn to traverse. The northern plates are always sinking into the storm, disassembling into shattering fragments of perspective. If a person of Vael happens to be there when the continental avalanche occurs, they are not so much killed as translated into the lightning, returning to the sky as a hot pulse of cyan or magenta.

More often than not, they are not undone so violently. They erase from reality more quietly than that.

One of their kind—call it a wanderer if you want—might be moving down into the misty hollows between plates, between the factory and forest, where the huge, machine-part organisms roll on their drum of grinder-teeth. The wanderer is not prey to them; the huge creatures barely register the wanderers' presence. They are engines of harvest and extrusion, not predators of the self-aware.

The breakdown of its personhood is more likely to be psychological. The world's structural instability is a feedback loop of construction and dismantlement. The wanderers are not exempt from its feedback.

A wanderer might put a hand on the damp, ceramic side of a new, upward-thrusting mountain. The circuits of its body and the world align momentarily, and a silent current of data from Vael's continental memory substrate passes through it. The wanderer feels not the meaning of the data but the *shape* of it: a pattern intended to repeat like a fractal blueprint never completed.

Suddenly, the wanderer understands itself not as a *self* but as a component of that pattern. Its purpose is not to be but to be used in the construction of the world engine. The feedback of that realization is too strong for its form of consciousness.

Its ceramic body doesn't so much die as repurpose. It might kneel on the damp surface, and roots of fibrous glass like the tree-factory reach up from cracks and thread into the joints of its limbs. Maybe its humming engine heart of cartilage teeth slows to the rhythm of the world's machinery, and then it is not a person anymore but a node in the industrial network, one more bent of scaffold in the factory of the forest.

Perhaps others simply wander away over the mercury dunes of nanite dust and into a region of reality where the pattern of their form no longer resolves. The world's geometry of dust forgets them, and they fade into the structure they are destined to become.

Theirs is not a long life in the way a stable world would count it. A person of Vael rarely persists longer than a few of Vael's uncertain cycles of pseudo-days and pseudo-night. The faint eyes of scattered distant illumination above the storm clouds might open and close a few dozen times over the course of its existence, and few of them get to see more than that.

In the brief time they are, they are observant in the way of passage and not of memory. They do not hoard stories or build archives of knowledge. They are the world's own provisional attempt to understand itself.

They are the closest things to inhabitants of this shattered porcelain continent: persistent errors, anomalies of will, trying—while the world around them is slowly—and not so slowly—being constructed and dismantled at the same time.
