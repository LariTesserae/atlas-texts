Jyrneth are stone-grafted sighs who herd the ground.

They have no faces sharp enough to slice you, but any one of them could wear you away like a storm wears a cliff. They are tall and thin, not the way trees are thin but the way fissures are: stretched and vertical and infinitely fallible, with limbs that seem longer than they should be and joints that click like shifting shale.

When you first meet a jyrneth, you think they are wearing the world. They are not. They are being worn by it.

Their skin is not exactly skin; it is layered memory. The outer layer is a film of white dust, always settling, always coming from the air—spores from the cloud sea below or the drifting sky grit above or from the interstices where the plates are mating and grinding away at each other’s edges. Underneath, you glimpse the glimpses of veins: not of blood but of slow-moving light, like minerals under a microscope cap, like slow lightning through heavy slate. When a jyrneth speaks, its veins flicker and the words arrive half a heartbeat later, as if they traveled from somewhere else and then burrowed up to the surface. You hear the sound before you understand it; you feel it before you hear it.

Beneath that glowing layer, there are stone seams. Everywhere stone holds it to itself: arched ribs like plateaus, blunt elbows like promontories, knuckles and knobs of rock fused into knuckles and knobs of flesh. The jyrneth are hybrids of sculpt and decay, chiseled and then sanded down by the wind and time. They leave footprints of crumbling dust when they walk, as if they are gently shedding the world into the world.

Their hair, if you can call it hair, is a long fall of raw stone threads hanging from the scalp and back of the neck and spine, each strand like a thin rock flake, initially translucent before it darkens with the distance of its own length. Some bear hair that touches the ground where it is not broken off by rains and winds and the sheer weight of trying to remain attached to a body that lives in motion. When they turn their heads, the hair does not flow like liquid; it breaks into new arrangements of edges and angles and lethal shadows, like fractured topography rearranging itself after an earthquake.

When the jyrneth look at you, they are not looking with eyes you can recognize. They have no round pupils or clear whites. Instead, there are shallow basins of darkness set in their face, where light seems to disappear without reflection; when you stare into those basins you feel as if you are standing at the edge of a drop that does not end so much as it turns into air. You cannot see anything in them. But you feel them see you.

They are not alone in their faces. There are others living with them.

In the cracks of their jawline and along the ledge of their brow, small colonies of creatures have taken root: tiny shelled ribs that bloom like inverse flowers, hard petals of amber armor curling around their soft bodies; their limbs are hooks and threadlike tendrils that cling into the jyrneth’s dust skin and rock vein and whatever counts as muscle beneath the stone seams. They feed on the light that leaks from the jyrneth veins, and the jyrneth, in turn, let them stay because they are how they taste.

The jyrneth do not have tongues of flesh and muscle and taste buds; they have tongues of soft mineral and fibrous dust, and they are mostly blind to flavors of the world. So the shelled ribs drink their lightblood and then secretions from their bodies crystallize into the jyrneth’s hairline and jawline and into the seams at their neck and collarbone and shoulders, where they are scraped off by the wind and the jyrneth’s slow motion, and then eaten. The jyrneth taste the world through how their colonies taste the world.

When you meet a jyrneth, you do not shake its hand; you might as well try to shake hands with a cliff face. Instead, you show them your hand and the jyrneth lets you rest your palm and the back of your fingers against the stone seam along its forearm or its wrist or whatever flat of it is nearest you. There, you have to feel their way of being.

You feel the echo of continents grinding. Through the stone seam under your skin, you hear it: not in sound but in pressure, a low deep murmur of mass and time and collision, like someone else’s teeth clenching tight inside your bones. You feel the slow roar of surfaces being scraped and abraded away, and you feel how the jyrneth are not only alive but are listening to that roar like it is their heartbeat, and it is.

The jyrneth are shepherds because the plates are not fixed. They move.

They drift like continents on a sky sea without a sea, and the jyrneth walk the edges and spines of each surface like they are route markers of rivers that do not exist. You will see one jyrneth standing alone at a cliffside where one world-plate is slowly angling toward another, and it will have its head tilted down into the cloud floor below, or up into the cloud ceiling above, or into the crack between the worlds overhead where distant stars are visible as if through broken ice. You might think it is meditating or praying or listening to the wind. It is not. It is listening to the plates complaining.

They hear it before it happens: the low metal-and-glass shriek as the edge of one world bites into another world’s edge and the stone starts to tear away from itself. You cannot hear that sound; not with your ears and not with your hands. You could press your ear to the ground and you would hear only wind and maybe the faint vibration of distant thunder if you are lucky. But the jyrneth hear it like you hear your blood, and they feel it like you feel the heartbeat of land underfoot when you are about to fall into a chasm you cannot see.

When they hear the plates coming together too hard, they go to the places where the collision will be worst. They do not run to stop them; there is no stopping the motion of infinite worlds grinding each other into abrasions. They go to soften the collision.

They stand at the cliffs of worlds where the edges rear like frozen waves of broken glass and then slowly lower themselves into the cloud sea below or the sky ceiling above or the zone between worlds where there is no air you can breathe without filtering your throat through stone and dust and strange light. You do not see how they do this; you only see the act of them vanishing over the edge of the world like they are nothing more than falling rocks and then you see the collision later, when it happens, and it is not like you expected.

You expected the plates to meet and crash like thunderheads and break like icebergs folding into themselves. Instead, when a jyrneth has gone to the edge beforehand, you see the meeting of worlds like something else entirely: the slow slip of glass surfaces kissing each other into new arrangements of angles and light and blank depths where one world ends and another begins to be glimpsed through the crack of the first.

The jyrneth are not there to stop the world from moving; they are there to stop it from screaming itself awake.

So they do not fight the motion of continents stacked in sky. They ease the grinding of the world’s teeth into a softer gnaw. You can feel it when it happens: you stand on the surface of your world plate, and the ground under you is not still, but it is calm. You do not fall. You do not break into shards and be lost into cloud sea below or star void above. You remain on the world that you know, and the jyrneth, who you never see at the moment of collision, remain somewhere in the abyss between worlds where the air is not air and the sound is not sound but the pressure of continents remembering themselves into being.

Sometimes you see a jyrneth return from the edge of the world, and when they do you will not recognize them. They will be thinner than before; their stone seams will be scraped down to softer lines, and their hair will be shorter, broken off as if the storm behind their back had spent itself within their vertebrae and down the length of their spine. You will see what they left behind like the shadow of what they used to be: hairline cracks of light that do not close but bleed into the world-plate, where they are slowly taken up by the stone and become new veins in the ground itself.

You see the jyrneth on the phone of the world the most often; where one world plate meets another edge not as a collision but as a long curve of cliffs that allow you to climb into the next layer, if you are brave enough and not afraid of falling into the cloud sea below. You think they are guarding these paths. They are not guarding them; they are trimming them.

They move along the edges where they can feel the world’s stress and they use their own body to redistribute the strain of all that motion upon the bones of the world. You might see a jyrneth standing for a whole day with one arm stretched forward and one palm pressed flat against the stone of a cliff face that is not their own, and then not moving at all except to narrow the faint cracks of light in their body into a steady slow pulse of pressure that keeps the edge from splitting into new sky canyons. You think they are holding the world together with their strength; but the jyrneth are already part of the world’s strength and weakness and everything else.

Sometimes you see a jyrneth step not just the length of one world plate but from one plate to another. There is no bridge you can see. There is only space and cloud and maybe a distant glimpse of another world turning like a broken mirror of the first. You see the jyrneth take its step, and then you do not look at where you think it is going; you look at where it is leaving.

Wherever its foot leaves the world, you see a hairline crack of light that spreads like ink into deep water, and then you see the world plate answer it with another crack of its own. And as you watch, both of them—the jyrneth and the world plate—begin to flow into each other.

You cannot say if the jyrneth step onto the next world or if they fall into the cloud sea below or star distance above or simply disappear into the crack inside the world itself and become, again, new veins in the ground of everywhere else. But they do not seem to care if you are watching them when they go; and maybe they are not even aware of you being there at all.

You think you are the world’s caretaker because you live on it and you walk it and you breathe the air it gives you. You are not. The real caretakers of the infinite stacked surfaces of Caelira are the jyrneth, who are not above the world or below the world or outside the world but are the world itself slowly learning to listen to its own teeth grinding and to decide, before they break into each other fully, that they might as well grind a bit softer than they could.

When you have been in the presence of a jyrneth long enough, you begin to feel the way you feel when you are standing at the edge of a world plate and you know there is nothing below except air and clouds and the distant possibility of other worlds. You feel like the ground is not solid ground anymore but something like a very slow storm of consequences and possibilities and waiting skies and every time you take a step you realize that nothing is actually fixed, except maybe the pressure of the jyrneth’s attention on the world’s teeth grinding.

Sometimes you dream of them falling into the chasm between worlds. You see them step over the edge of your world plate; you see them disappear into the crack of light between where one world ends and another begins, and then you see the world plate itself grinding its teeth together into new shapes, and then you hear, not with your ears but with your bones, the sound of continents being broken into new edges of possibility and fear and wonder and pain and beauty and everything else that can happen to a world that never actually stops moving but only sometimes remembers how to move more softly.

Then you wake up with your heart still feeling the pressure of a world breaking itself into new places, and you realize that maybe the jyrneth are that pressure: the stone-grafted sighs who are not really trying to stop the world so much as they are trying to make sure, when it breaks itself into new pieces, that it at least remembers how to sigh instead of scream.
