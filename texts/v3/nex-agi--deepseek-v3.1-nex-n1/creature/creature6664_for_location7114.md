Whatever lives here doesn’t have a name you can recognize, because the language of this place hasn’t finished choosing what sounds are words.

They call themselves the /who/—or something like that. The middle of the word is a whistling, ripping noise, like wind forced between woven reeds. When they say it, the air around them knots itself for a moment, then undoes itself. They do not mind that you can’t pronounce it. They’ve already guessed you’re just temporary.

They live in the plant-built town, but they are not the architects of it. They are its tenants. They are also its draft.

---

### How they appear when they want to be seen

At first you think they are plants. You were not wrong to assume that everything here is plant. You were only early.

The /who/ don’t have bodies as you know bodies—no fixed skin, no fixed organs. They have *currents* in the living world, and when they want to be seen, they gather into shapes.

An individual appears when the plant-mass around you decides to be more than the environment. It starts like this:

- the soughing of the leaves overhead picks out a phrase instead of a noise;
- the stems under your feet arrange into a pattern of pressure rather than a random relief;
- a color emerges from the green chaos that doesn’t match the surrounding foliage—an almost-blue, or a not-quite-purple, or a yellow that doesn’t yet belong to the world of flowers;

then you realize that what you thought was a particularly vivid arrangement of vines on the dome of a tree-house is actually holding a form.

They stand like this: half of them rooted in the floor, half of them rising into the air, as if they’ve paused mid-step between vertical and horizontal. Their body is not “plant person” so much as “a question the plant has asked itself.”

You might see:

- a column of woven stems and leaf-shapes, vaguely human in height, but softer than a silhouette;
- long, trailing fronds that aren’t arms but *might remember* being arms if you look away and then back;
- a surface made of overlapping surfaces—like a bark, but thinner, like new paper when the world is still deciding what will be written on it;
- no face, and then later you realize that the region where a face would be has been drawing you while trying not to draw your eyes.

Their eyes (when they have them) are not round pupils but vertical slits of light that flare like lifted windows. They can be any color the sun hasn’t chosen yet.

They don’t walk in the normal sense. They move by changing which part of the world is them. They leave behind a trail of confused plant: stems that were still for a moment before, now suddenly curtains, or ribs, or frames of something that might be a doorway; then they rearrange it and step forward again. Sometimes you see the same /who/ emerge from a wall, or the floor, or the ceiling. They are not passing through solid; they are integrating the wall into their body for a moment and then leaving it.

“Individual” is a convenient word. They flicker in and out of individuality.

---

### How they speak when they want to be heard

They have two voices:

- the voice they use to talk to you, and  
- the voice they use to talk to the world.

The first voice is clumsy and borrowed. They build it like you might build a shelter: from whatever materials they happen to have. When they want your words, they take pieces from your memory and language, and stitch them together into a shape that almost resembles speech.

This is what it sounds like when one of them speaks in your tongue:

- “We are the echo of the not-yet-present.”
- “You are standing in the future of a room.”

The grammar is not exactly wrong, but the time of the verbs is wrong.

The second voice is worse, or better, depending on who you ask.

When they speak to the world, they use the column-ring you found at the edge of the settlement: the circle of stalks that turn themselves inside-out, making horn-like and choral noises. They go there and stand—not on the platform root, but *through* it. Their lower half melts into the root-mat and fuses with it. Their upper body swells, becoming more trunk than body.

Then they use the world as voice. You hear:

- high, shivering bellows from the stalks of the ring;
- a low, creaking bass from the ground under your feet;
- the wind being forced through the canopy above into a new pattern of holes, making syllables that almost sound like words.

And you are *certain* they are saying something, in the same way you are certain that thunder is saying something just before you understand what lightning is. They are not forming words so much as teaching the world to form consonants.

Sometimes you realize you understood the phrase *while you are hearing it*, but then it vanishes from your mind, like an ink that doesn’t like to dry on paper.

---

### How they organize time, when time is negotiable

They do not live “in the present moment” in the way you might assume. They live in the *decision of the present moment*.

They have a word—which they don’t translate to you—for the state of the world when it is open to change. They show it instead.

When they want to show it, they invite you to walk around the valley and notice:

- which hills are currently undecided about their height;
- which river corridors are in dispute;
- which houses are currently open to rearrangement.

They “plan” by gathering near the column-ring and *speaking into the world* until the future becomes slightly more likely in one direction than another. The hill to the north of the settlement rises again, but this time you notice the ascent of stone is a little slower than before, as if the world is taking its suggestion more seriously.

You ask: “Do you remember the last version of this town?”

They show you instead a memory-carpet of green floor. They pull the stems of the root-mat into a pattern: like a map, but written in time instead of space.

You see:

- a faint, temporary hollow of a room that is not there anymore;
- a suggestion of river-channel that is now closed;
- a ghost of a circular pool where a bowl-terrace now sits.

They don’t say: “This was.” They say: “This is a version that is no longer chosen.”

They “age” only by the version of the world they inhabit. When a /who/ tells you they are “old,” they mean they have lived through many versions of the town. They show you the scars of time in their flesh: sections of bark or cortex that are obviously borrowed from structures that no longer exist—faint patterns of roof tiles in the texture of their shoulder, or a window-frame in the shape of their ribs.

---

### How they eat, when there is no animal but them

They eat everything.

This is not to say they consume everything with mouths. You see no mouths when you first meet them, which is one of the reasons you think they might not be alive at all.

They eat:

- sunlight, by orienting their surfaces to catch the day;
- water, by letting their stems drink from the river troughs;
- the sound of the world itself, by standing in the column-ring and listening intently when others are speaking.

When they “eat” sound, they grow *denser*. You can see the change: the colors of their body deepen, become more definite. The green of them becomes less confused and more like a decision.

They also eat language.

When you speak to them, they don’t look at you with their eyes (if they have eyes) so much as with their surface. You feel the foliage of their body tuning like leaves to the sun. They are not memorizing your words; they are digesting them.

You watch as:

- something of your sentence is taken into them;
- their pattern of fronds rearranges slightly;
- a new shape of leaf or stem or bark emerges on their body, like a scar in the wrong direction.

Later you see words you have spoken to them appear in the walls of the town. You turn a corner in a corridor of foliage and find your phrase written there in veins of color, half-translated into the world’s shared language, not yet yours.

---

### How they reproduce, when the world is not yet decided

They don’t “have children” in your sense. They are *expanded.*

When a new version of the world is proposed and the world agrees to it, sometimes a part of the world that used to be “just room” or “just hill” suddenly becomes more coherent than before. The pattern deepens. The color of its region settles.

Then you notice:

- a new figure standing in the column-ring;
- a new set of eyes that feels like they have always been watching;
- a new tone of voice in the chorus of the forest.

They say that this is how they come “into being”: a part of the world wakes up and remembers that it was always supposed to be someone.

They don’t call these “births.” They say: “The world has added another vowel to itself.”

---

### How they die, when the world keeps rewriting them

They don’t die in the way you would die. They “become less.”

Sometimes you walk past the column-ring and notice:

- a gap in the chorus of the stalks;
- a missing tone of voice that used to be part of the ring;
- a section of the platform root that is darker than its surrounding, like a shadow that has forgotten to move.

They tell you (or show you in the half-words they have learned from your language): “That version is no longer chosen.”

The first time you understand it, you feel a coldness you haven’t known since you arrived here in the warm, damp bowl of this world. You realize:

- when the town decides to rearrange itself;
- when the longhouse-bar of bending trunks learns to be a different room;
- when the hill of the north becomes finally *high* instead of fluctuating;

some of the /who/ who were shaped by that old version are *erased.*

They do not vanish in the same way you vanish. They become:

- walls of houses that no longer need them;
- extra trunks of trees that are now simply trees;
- parts of the river-trough’s border that have hardened into “just river.”

They show you a live /who/ standing next to a newly finished wall of the longhouse, and you notice that the pattern of the wall’s bark and the pattern of the being’s body are almost identical. The wall is a version of the person that has grown too still to move.

You ask if they are afraid of becoming walls.

They answer, not with words, but by showing you the column-ring where the chorus of time is being sung into the world, and you realize: the singing itself reshapes the future. The /who/ that are still singing are trying to ensure that the versions of the world that are *next* chosen still include them.

They are actively choosing *in which future they are allowed to remain.*

---

### How they see you

You are not one of them. You are not from here.

They know this. They know you are not part of the negotiation of the reality here. You are:

- a sample of the outside world;
- a test of the column-room’s ability to speak;
- a strange new kind of word they have never learned before.

They watch you with the same curiosity that the world uses to watch itself:

- when they walk beside you, the corridors of the town straighten themselves;
- when you lie down to sleep in the tree-house room, the floor of green stems smooths itself;
- when you step near the river, the plant-border of the watercourse adjusts itself so you won’t slip.

You realize that they are inviting you to behave like *part of them*, but on a different scale. They want to see if they can learn to architect your kind of body into the world:

- a room for you;
- a riverbank for you;
- a time for you.

They never ask you to stay or to leave. They simply ask, in their language of time and sound and rearranging stems, whether the world will one day *choose* you.

---

### What lives here, in summary

What lives here is not a species, but a possibility that has become partially permanent.

They call themselves the /who/, in the sound of reeds in the wind, and they are:

- the portion of the world that has become self-aware;
- the chorus that uses time as a language;
- the tenants of the plant-town that the world itself has built to remember that there is supposed to be someone inside it.

Before you leave, they ask you one question in your language, using the borrowed syntactical scraps they’ve taken from you:

“When you return to your world of stone and time that is already written, will you remember us as we are now or as we are not yet?”

And then they show you the thing that you only understood later:

- the column-ring of the world keeps singing;
- the hill of the north decides, finally, to become high;
- the river-trough chooses a more definite line;
- the walls of the longhouse fold themselves into a new arrangement of rooms;

and you realize that you are not leaving them behind so much as leaving behind one version of them. The next version is already rehearsing itself in the chorus of the stems.

The floor of reality is breathing, and in the low warm bowl of the world, where every plant is also architecture and time is not yet carved, the /who/ live between the drafts of what you call “place” and “process.” They are the living who witness not the world as it is, but the world as it is still being proposed.
