The people of the valley think of themselves as those who listen to the stone.

They are not tall, by the lowlander’s measure, but they are not slight either. Their bodies are compact, built for the steepness of the valley and the thin ache of the air. The children are sturdy, with round faces and quick eyes that miss nothing; the adults carry themselves with a careful economy of motion, as if they are always aware of the weight of the air and the cost of climbing. They are a few hundred families at most, but the valley makes them quieter than their numbers would suggest, and more careful.

They are not yet ‘stonecarvers’, in the sense the world will later understand the word. They are stone-shapers, edge-finders, pattern-makers. They have not yet learned how to carve deep or long or straight enough into the rock of the mountain itself to make tunnels that run through its heart. Instead, they carve the stone that has already come to them: fallen blocks at the foot of the cliffs, the raw slabs of pale slate they drag from shallow quarries in the valley floor, the smaller pieces of river stone and lakebed stone that litter their high bowl.

Their tools are simple and tactile. Stone hammers with handles of wood bound with leather. Chisels of bronze and sharp-edged stone. The thin pale axes of bone that they fashion from the ribs of the few mountain animals that dare to venture into the valley. They use the stone itself to learn: small hand-sized pieces of hard slate, brought into homes and kept by hearths until they are warm to the touch, then worked into shallow, abstract patterns—lines of chevrons, half-circles like sets of teeth, networks of interlocking shapes that never seem to end.

When they work the stone, they tap it rather than strike it. The sound is not a crash but a series of small, paused impacts: tap-tap… tap… tap-tap-tap. Each pause is a moment of listening to what the stone has to say, where it wants to break, where it wants to remain whole. No two rocks are quite alike, and the people have not yet learned the trick of forcing them into perfect uniformity. Instead, they learn the personality of each slab, and place the stone that prefers to be flat into floors, and the stone that prefers to be upright into walls, and the stone that wants to be cupped into basins for water.

They are not yet masters of metal. The bronze they have is bartered from rare traders who come from somewhere beyond the pass—when the snows allow and the mountain decides to open its throat. Those traders are themselves cautious of the high air, and they never linger long. The bronze they leave is worked into blades small enough to be carried on a person’s belt: knives for wood, not stone; awls to pierce leather; pins to hold hair or clothing in place. The people of the valley allow the metal to guide its own shape as much as possible, rather than forcing it into something that will not sit comfortably in the hand.

Theirs is not a language of loud declaration or elaborate argument, but one of repetition and inflection. Words are shorter than the lowlander’s, and they are repeated to carry meaning—once, twice, sometimes three times in an altered form—to show whether a thing is observed, believed, or still doubted. The valley’s people have more than one name for their home, depending on how they feel it at that moment:

When it is cold and clear and the stones are dry, they call it *ja-shai-shai*, “the-rim-that-is-still-learning.”  
When spring comes and the rocks weep, and the mist rises from the marsh, they call it *ja-reth-reth*, “the-rim-that-remembers-everything,”  
and when the first thunder of summer rolls over the mountain and no rain falls yet, they call it *ja-shal*, “the-rim-that-is-not-quite-closed,”  

They are not yet a city-minded people in spirit, though they are becoming one. The idea of ‘the city’ is still a new thing to them, a word they have borrowed from passing traders and reshaped. It sits in their mouths awkwardly, like a borrowed tool that does not quite fit their hand. They prefer to speak of home as *the-inner-line*, a phrase that means not so much the inside of a circle as the place where lines cross and choose to thicken.

---

The houses of the valley people are built uphill of the marsh, but never far from the water. The lake is not only their source of drink, but also their teacher. They watch its levels in the same way they might watch the breath of a sleeping child: with quiet attention, but not fear.

The lowest, outer ring of homes are the ones on stilts. The people who live here are called the *eth-thin*, the edge-walkers, though they no longer walk the edge of the same world that their ancestors crossed. They are intimately tied to the marsh and its moods. They are the ones who first feel the subtle shift in the water’s weight, who notice when the lake’s reflections are a little too clear, or a little too inverted.

The edge-walkers are more cautious than the others, and more prone to silence. As they move along their woven walkways above the marshy ground, they listen to the sound each step makes, and to the answering whisper of the reeds beneath. From long practice, they can tell from the sway of the platform under their feet whether the water below is rising or not. They are the first to know when the mountain has passed snowmelt to its valley floor.

Above the edge-walkers are the *eth-rei*, those who live on the firm stone of the valley floor. Their houses are built of layered stone and packed earth, with lower walls of heavier blocks and higher walls of mud and woven wattle. They are the ones who most often work with the tools of bronze and stone, because they are closest to the flat ground where they can shape rock without fear of it sliding away from them.

The *eth-rei* speak the loudest of all the people—when they speak at all. Their work is the most visible. The sound of their stone shaping travels the farthest in the clear air, and when they pause to rest from hammering, they feel the valley listening to their absence. The constant drip of water from their eaves into their cisterns is the *eth-rei’s* heartbeat, and when they say the city’s name, they say “the-place-where-we-count-the-water.”

Higher still, near the upper edges of the city, where the ground begins to slope into the mountain’s rough skin, are the *eth-saal*, the sky-holders. They are closest to the cliffs and the thin air that the world below the pass would call breathless. They are often chosen as the ones who interact with passing traders because they are less intimidated by the height and the wind and the strange light of the sky.

The *eth-saal* know the weight of water not as it laps at their floorboards, but as it runs from the mountain. It is their work to talk the water down: To build guide channels of small stone and packed earth, so that the mountain’s thin tears do not run randomly across the sloping city but are directed toward where they can be used. The *eth-saal* also know the weather of the sky best; they can tell from the color of twilight whether the night will be clear or cold enough to chip the stone of their roofs.

Although they all speak the same language, each group has its own subtle accent of using words. The edge-walkers speak in shorter, repeated phrases, as if expecting each sentence to be interrupted by the wind. The *eth-rei* tend to add longer descriptions of the work they have done, or have not yet done, to everything they say, as if they cannot separate their meaning from the stone they have touched. The *eth-saal* speak with a rising intonation at the end of many sentences, as if always asking, even when they are stating something obvious.

---

The children of the valley are particularly shaped by its contradictions.

They are bundled against the cold, but they are always near water. They grow up in the thin air that makes their breaths short, but they are used to climbing from early on. They are taught a posture of listening to the world before they even learn to listen carefully to each other’s words.

The children play with small stones—smooth, closed lumps of lake rock and pieces of pale slate they are not allowed to shape with metal tools. They learn the names of each pattern the stone can show them before they are allowed to hold the bright bronze knives of their adults. They create shallow games in the dust of the streets, drawing lines with sticks and placing small stones into the patterns they have been taught: the teeth of the mountain, the line of the lake’s edge, the shape of the valley rim.

They are allowed to run the suspended walkways of the edge-walkers’ homes before they are allowed to cross the lake itself. The sway of the walkway under their feet is the first rhythm of movement they internalize. Their legs are allowed to be strong, but their arms must be careful: they begin to carry water in small carved containers—not yet of stone, but of hardened wood and hollowed bone—from the earliest age they can safely lift.

The children sleep near the walls of their houses that are closest to the rock. Even if their house is not built of solid stone all the way down, they are placed near the heaviest parts of the structure, where the weight of the stone is greatest. They are told that the rock of the mountain knows their dreams better than the sky does, and that the stone walls remember them longer.

Strange lights of the world do not frighten them as they might a lowland child. They are not surprised to see shadows cast in colors they cannot name, or to see the lake reflecting people who are not standing where they should be. They are taught to treat these as slightly older versions of themselves—the valley’s equivalent of older siblings who have not yet chosen their words carefully.

---

The elders occupy the quiet center of the valley.

They are the ones who have lived longer than the memory of the city as a full city, long before any of the younger people have heard the word *jas-thain*, “the-color-that-is-not-the-right-color.” They are the ones who remember days when the houses were not as tightly clustered together, and when the valley felt more like a collection of families than a single organism.

When elders move, they are slower than the less-mended bodies of their lowland equivalents, but their eyes are sharper. The thin air has not dimmed their vision. Instead, it seems to have given them a slightly longer reach of sight. They can identify the faintest movements of water on the surface of the lake from the edge of the city—slight ruffles that the children would barely notice.

Their hands are marked with the scars of their earlier work with stone and bronze and bone. Some of them are missing fingers or parts of fingers from the times when they tried to shape the stones before learning the full of their language—the slow, methodical tap, rather than the quick strike. The missing parts are taken as a sign of respect, as if the stone has taken a small piece of their body as payment for teaching them.

Elders are the ones who determine the age of the city not by counting years but by listening to the way the water sounds from the eaves of each house. They can tell from the sound of the drip into the cistern whether the basin is nearly full or not, and whether the house is older or younger than others of its kind. They remember the days when there were almost no cisterns at all, when the people of the valley believed the water would always come from the lake, and the lake would always know where to be.

When they speak of the future, they speak of it as if they are already planning the unfinished parts of the city. The phrase *mala-thain*, “the-unfinished-work,” is common in their mouths. They are aware of their own death not as a boundary but as a point of adjustment in the pattern. The unfinished houses are not a problem for them in the same way a lowland might see it. They are not pieces of work left incomplete; they are places of possibility.

The elders are the ones who negotiate the ever-shifting boundary between the city and the lake. It is their work to decide when the marsh is allowed to grow outward and when the marsh needs to be pushed back slightly, by careful placement of stone and cut of reeds. The children of the edge-walkers are called to help them—carrying the small, shaped stones of their ancestors that were not used to build walls, but to guide edges of the water.

---

There are artists here among the stone-shapers, though they do not have a separate word for themselves.

The valley people have not yet learned to carve images into the stone of living beings, or even of plants, with much confidence. Instead, their art is abstract and precise. It revolves around the shapes of possibility: the incomplete circles that they carve into lintels and door frames, the grids of lines that spread across stone thresholds, like the patterns of water on the surface of the lake when the wind is just so.

The artist is not always the one who holds the bronze blade. The valley people have a practice, not yet fully named, of “the-one-who-marks” and “the-one-who-shapes.” The first is the person who traces the pattern with a piece of chalk or charcoal or the edge of a wet finger. The second is the one who follows the line with the stone edge or bronze tip.

They are particularly aware of the imperfections of their stone. Instead of trying to hide them, they incorporate them into the pattern of their design. If a stone’s surface is depressed by a small hollow that was not visible to the eye, they work around the hollow, or towards the hollow, as if the stone has already decided to have a bit of shade in that place.

They are especially aware of the way that the light interacts with the stone’s pale surface. The patterns they choose are designed to show shadow as well as shape. When the sun is low in the sky, their carvings show not just the lines they have made but also lines of darkness that they have not carved directly—but have allowed by cutting edges around them.

---

At the center of the city, not quite in the center of the lake and not quite on the shore, but near the first of the stilts of the edge-walkers’ homes, is a shallow structure that they call *ja-tha*, “the-center-that-is-not-a-center.” It is not a temple and not quite a council hall, though it is used for both kinds of gathering. It is built almost entirely of stone shaped by hand and not yet any tool of speed.

Its walls are low and thick. The roof of slate and thin wood rises over a shallow bowl of a floor: not quite flat, not quite round, but shaped like the inside of a cupped hand. When the rain falls on that roof, it runs into channels carved in the stone floor and then into the lake itself. The *ja-tha* is not built to hold water, but to pass it along. The people of the valley speak of this as being the city’s small model of the mountain itself.

The *ja-tha* is where they gather when they are uncertain of strange weather or sudden light. The elders of the *eth-saal* come to explain when a new kind of glow has appeared on the cliffs. The *eth-rei* come to argue if a new opening of stone or channel of water should be shaped in the city floor, or if the stone should be allowed to remain unbroken a little longer.

The children are not allowed to speak during such meetings, but they are allowed to sit in the circle of their elders and place their small stones on the floor in the way of the pattern that they see the city making. If they place the stones in a way that suggests an unexpected line of water or a new path of gravity, the elders will not dismiss them, but will ask them to explain the pattern of the stone’s placement.

Somewhere, in the not-quite-visible center of the *ja-tha*, is the city’s one collective name for itself, which the children cannot yet understand and the adults are only beginning to learn:

“Where the stones weep and the city sleeps.”

They are only beginning to understand that the weeping might be more than water, and the sleep more than rest.
