They are barely larger than your hand.

At first you mistake them for lichen, or for some kind of slow fungus clinging to the bone-ribbed walls. There is a faint, stippled texture over everything—doorways, thresholds, the worn grooves of the streets. Only when you stand still long enough do you realize that the texture is breathing.

The inhabitants are flat-bodied and soft-edged, their shapes irregular and adaptive. They do not have limbs in the sense of arms and legs; instead, their edges ripple with fine, muscular frills that can adhere to stone or wood or living flesh with equal ease. They are the color of old paper and dried blood and tarnished copper, their surfaces mottled with patches that shift slowly over days or weeks, like continents wandering across a tired map.

They have no internal skeletons. Their bodies are dense with cartilage and gelid tissue that can be pressed flat against a surface or gathered into a low hump when they need to move more quickly—though “quickly” here is measured in the crawl of shadows across phosphorescent moss. Their motion is patient, an endless negotiation between gravity, moisture-that-is-not-moisture, and the faint thermal gradients that rise from the stone.

There are many ways to count them, but all of them feel inadequate.

By area they cover most of what you think of as the city: every wall, every bone rib, every roof-curve is host to a film of them. By volume they are nothing—thin as skin stretched over an abyss. By number they are beyond counting; by individuality they are almost none at all.

Each one is a colony before it is a creature.

Under the faint glow-lamps you can see how their bodies merge and part without fuss. Two will press together along a seam until pigments and textures flow into alignment and their surfaces become continuous. Later—hours? days?—there will be a slow shiver along that same seam and they will peel apart again, each leaving a trace pattern on the other’s hide like a memory too stubborn to fade.

They have no centralized brains. Instead they think in layers: each individual carries a meshwork of simple ganglia distributed just beneath its skin; when they press together those nets interlace into something larger and more diffuse. Cognition here is a temporary consensus reached among neighbors—a decision made by patchwork committee and then executed at glacial speed.

Their senses are similarly distributed.

They taste-hear-smell through their entire undersurface: vibrations travel up from the stone through their bodies as slow waves; chemical traces in the air seep into them over long minutes and spread like ink in water; faint thermal shifts register as changes in pressure behind their eyespots. They do not have ears or noses or tongues as such; instead they possess an all-over sensitivity to gradients—of warmth, of vibration, of chemical drift—and out of those gradients they assemble something like perception.

Their eyes are small clusters of light-sensitive cells scattered around their rims rather than set in faces. There is no stereoscopic focus here; each eyespot sees its own narrow slice of world and contributes it to a shared mosaic image constructed slowly across overlapping bodies. When one moves past another there is always this brief period where images must be reconciled: two partial views of the same rib-column stitched together like torn pages being mended.

Because they see so dimly they rely heavily on memory-images passed by touch from body to body along chains that can span entire districts or even reach from one great creature-architecture to another through contact at shared doorframes or threshold-stones worn smooth by countless passages above them.

They reproduce rarely but steadily by budding: small knobs form along an edge where conditions have been favorable for many cycles (the definition of “favorable” known only locally). These buds swell over time then eventually pinch away with barely any ceremony at all beyond some quiet redistribution of pigments among parent and offspring so that both bear matching marks afterward as proof-of-lineage written directly onto flesh instead of paper or stone tablets elsewhere might use for records.
