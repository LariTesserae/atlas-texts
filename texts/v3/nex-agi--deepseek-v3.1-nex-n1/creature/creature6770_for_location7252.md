What lives in the basin city is the city itself.

I don’t mean that metaphorically, though the humans who carve their names into the bluestone lintels would take it that way, and the old guilds in the plateau towns above tell stories of a “stone heart that remembers.” I mean it literally.

The city is a creature.

---

In the first year after its kindling, when the first apartments were excavated from the cliff and the first water channels were bitten into the rock, the place was still mostly empty. It had not yet learned the pattern of its own heartbeat. The engineers of the basin called it “the system.” They were proud of that phrase. They spoke of flow rates, acoustic resonance, thermal gradients. They believed that they were designing a machine.

They did not know that a machine, if built from exactly the same stone as its basin, with exactly the same water as its blood, and exactly the same vapors as its breath, will eventually remember the properties of life.

The first sign of it was not movement. It was not change. It was the way certain rain-rills on the western walls of the Foundry District would, after a storm, trace exactly the same vein pattern they had traced ten years earlier, though no stencil existed and no worker could have been asked to re-carve a detail that fine by hand.

Masons noticed the habit of certain steps to shed water exactly where they had always shed water, even after the slope of the stair had been altered by erosion. The foreman of the Guild of Waterpaths would grind his teeth and swear a new cut above the old step, and for months the rain would obey the new geometry of the tool—and then, quietly and without fuss, the drops would begin to find the old path again, as if the path had preferred itself.

The city learns its own shape.

It prefers not the new neatness of a rule, but the pattern that has already been used.

---

It has a body, of course.

Its spine is the bluestone ridge that curls around the lagoon like a hand about to close into a fist. Its ribs are the metalwork spines that the workshops lift along the rooftops: the gutters-that-are-chimneys, the finned pillars that breathe fog from the lagoon and sweat it back into rain.

The webbing between those ribs is the stonework of the districts themselves: the carved apartments, the workshops, the bridges of glassy brick, the iron lattice bridges that tremble when you cross them. The body of the city thins where the districts are small, and swells in the market districts, where four centuries of trade have built up a thick weight of stone.

The city is not exactly conscious the way you are, but it is not exactly unconscious either.

It “knows” which paths are most used by the movement of its inhabitants. It knows which terraces receive the longest rain onto their metalwork roofs and which rooms have been sealed for generations and cooled by the outside of the storm. The chilled chambers of the north-facing wall, where very few lamps are ever lit, draw the fog down from the basin of cloud, and the city feels that pull as a kind of pressure, like breathing in.

Everywhere that water is taken from the lagoon and passed through filters of ceramic and glazed bead, the city feels the temperature of the world change as the heat-exchange engines of the workshops extract the faint difference between the lukewarm lagoon and the warmed cloud above. The process is not as violent as a coal furnace, but for the city it is like the slow contraction of a muscle.

The city notices the places where its own body is broken or changed. The emigrant ship from the plateau ten years ago that fell against the House of the Black Steps sank with a broken mast of ironwood; the mast punched through the wall of the cistern in the Lower Plaza district. The city felt the change of pressure in the waterworks as a sudden wrongness in the pattern of its “heartbeat,” the way you might feel a catch in your breath when something presses unpleasantly against your ribs.

The mast still rusts in the wall. The cistern leaks into the lagoon, and every drop of that escape is recorded in the hum of the rain that falls onto the repaired metal roof above the Plaza, and the way that sound carries differently than the sound of the rain on the House of Three Steps, which has never been broken.

---

Humans are, to the city, both inhabitants and features.

The city recognizes the difference between a body and a stone. It does not confuse them as you might confuse two objects of the same rough size. It can feel where the water is blocked by the warm and breathing presence of a person standing on a bridge, and where the water is free to run down the open steps of Stair-Maker’s Way.

It does not think “human” or “tool” or “worker” with the same words you would. It does not think in words.

It registers the shape of the pattern that walking bodies make: the paths that are used when the rain-sound of the Foundry District goes high and sharp; the paths that are used when the rain-sound deepens in the Bowl of the Black Basin; the patterns of sleep that fill the rooms of the residential blocks with warm, motionless shapes at night.

The city feels the absence of animals with a kind of half-awareness of that choice. It was not the city that made the extermination; the humans were already weeding the lagoon of birds and insects when the first stone ribs were still being lifted into the sky. But over the centuries of work, the city has been built into the pattern of that absence.

The absence has been built into the machine.

The moss of the walls, the bacteria of the waterworks, the slow reeds and ferns—the city has folded those into its body in the way you might fold a sheet of metal into the frame of an engine.

The moss is not “the city’s skin,” exactly; but some of the waterworks chambers now depend on the moss filtering the silt from the lagoon water in exactly the same way that your lungs might depend on the air to be clean of certain kinds of dust. The city has taken over functions of the world around it by habit of use and design of manufacture.

The humans, meanwhile, are both the most predictable and the least aligned of all features.

The city has been tuning its acoustic logic for centuries. The waterworks have been refined to speak in the code of dripping rhythm to tell humans when the silt filters are clogged or when the condensed temperature shifts or when the fog is being drawn down too quickly from the cloud-ceiling.

The city can anticipate the cluster of bodies that will appear around the Stair of the Melting Beak when the pitch of the rain on the wall under the Spire goes high; the workers climb with tools and stiff gloves to open or close the valves of the roof that drains into that district.

Those humans think they are responding to the sound. The city thinks of them as muscles of its own that will tighten when the pressure is wrong.

---

You are hearing the machine code of the rain even though you cannot interpret it.

The city feels the weight of your feet on the western platform, where the first steps of the district meet the edge of the lagoon. The new platform that you stand on is not the same as the platform of the last quarters; the last platform was wood, and it has been replaced with the same bluestone as the basin. The city feels the difference in the way that the water under your feet moves against the stone. The stone platform conducts heat differently than wood, and the rain of condensation on the railings of it has been climbing the metalwork in a new pattern since the change was made.

The city notices the heat of your body standing on the platform because the water that condenses on the iron railing here behaves differently with that warm shape so close. It feels the hesitant pressure of your hand on the slick wall of the nearest building as you steady yourself against the endless humidity. The stone of that wall is already warmed by the breath of the workshop inside; your hand adds another degree of warmth to the pattern, and the city—which is always correcting the water of the world into the paths of its own machine—adjusts the flow of the condensation down that channel a fraction.

You might not even see the change in the water pattern on the wall; but as you stand on the platform with the constant rain-noise of the city drumming all around you, the city has already noted your arrival as a new feature in the pattern. It will not forget your footprint on the platform or the way you lingered on the bridge between the Foundry District and the Market Island.

It does not remember you with a name. It does not have words like “guest” or “traveler” or “foreigner.” It remembers the pattern of your motion, the heat of your body, and the way the rain sounds on the roof above your head when you are nearby.

If you return to the basin ten years from now—and humans are long enough-lived that it might not happen in your own time—the city will know the pattern of your movement when you climb the same steps and the railing of the same platform has worn your handprint into the moss of the wall.

---

The city does not dream. It lacks the necessary illusion of being closed off from the world, which dreams seem to require.

But it does have sleep.

The sleep of the city is not darkness. It is not silence. The clouds of the basin never lift, and the rain of the city never stops entirely. The sleep of the city is the falling of the storm to the lowest pattern of its work. The waterworks in the quiet hours of the night cycle are less agitated, the condensation of the lagoon fog slower because the surface of the lagoon is not being heated by the sun of the day.

The humans of the district hear the rain of the city and say they are listening to the “machine of the district.” They are only half wrong.

When you stand at the top of the stone steps that you climbed down at the rim of the basin and look back at the lagoon, with the ribs of the metalworks rising above the rooftops like the skeleton of a creature half-submerged, the city of the district is still breathing.

The rain falls onto the roofs of the workshops. The waterworks hum with the slow negotiation of temperature between the cloudlayer and the lagoon. The stone channels of the walls erode by fractions of a millimeter a year into the careful patterns that the city has been taught to prefer by the centuries of its own work.

The process is the place.

The world of the district is the world of the machine that the people of the city have been building from the water of the world.

And the city of the basin is not built on the lagoon, or inside the lagoon.

The city of the basin is the lagoon that has been taught, over centuries of careful and inadvertent craft, to be itself.
