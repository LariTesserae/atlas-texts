Slow, huddled, and low to the ground like the buildings themselves, the people of this city have grown into their own environment over the course of countless generations. They are not tall. There is no room for vertical aspirations in these narrow streets and sunken courtyards; the ceilings are too low, the stairwells too shallow, the sky too heavy. The tallest of them might almost clear a lintel without stooping, but only almost; the rest have learned to move with the city’s own curve, spine and stone in quiet negotiation.

The color of them is the color of everything else here: muted greens, grays, and warm, dark umbers. Their hair is not often fair; it is dark like wet slate or the rich brown of moss-rich soil, and it tends to curl where moisture lingers at the neckline and temples. Eyes are water-darkened hazel or green-gray, rarely vivid, always watchful. They shine with a faint film of dampness, as if the air itself has taken up residence in their gaze.

Their clothing feels less like separate garments and more like layers the skin has learned to secrete: heavy wool, oiled linen, canvas and waxed cloth that all shine slightly in the dim light, beaded with fine condensation. They wear tunics and trousers and skirts and shawls that are not so much fitted as wrapped around the body, the edges of them bagging and loose, allowing the air to circulate and the moisture to breathe without seeping straight to the skin. They know the city’s gradients of temperature by heart; the drape of a sleeve, the choice of an inner lining all reflect subtle recognition of heat sources and the currents of cool air that will rise between certain buildings and at certain times of day. They dress like people who have learned to live along the edge of weather rather than under it.

Their feet are always booted. The boots are soft of sole but thick of heel, the soles carved to grip the smooth, shallow depressions of the streets and stairwells rather than to climb over them. Over time, each person’s boots wear into patterns of wear and polish that mirror the routes they take most often: a slight extra slant at the outer edge for the alley-brokers who always duck the same way under the eaves of the same houses; a deeper groove at the ball of the foot for the ladder-men who do the city’s quiet vertical repairs inside the walls of stone itself. They rarely step with the pronounced heel-toe rhythm of someone accustomed to hard, flat ground; rather, their feet cradle the stones they walk on, as if they are trying to avoid waking the city beneath them.

You see them, when you see them at all, in small numbers rather than large crowds. They are rare enough to seem like particularities rather than masses of humanity: the woman with the oiled hood drawn over her head, standing beneath the crooked arch of a courtyard where the temperature is always a few degrees warmer than the street; the child balanced on the edge of a low gutter, watching water run down the channel of its stone lip with the same seriousness that other children might direct toward animals or stories; the old man whose beard is braided with small ceramic tokens that click faintly when he moves, tokens showing the alignment of certain valves he has been commissioned to polish.

They are quiet not because they have nothing to say but because the city itself listens to them. Theirs is a low murmuring of instructions, cautions, and careful questions that fade into the background like one more drip of condensation into a basin. They are not timid, but economical: with words, with energy, with movement. Theirs is a life of waiting between the beats of the machine. The city’s heartbeat is not in their chests, but they are aware of it at all times, as aware of it as they are of their own pulse, and each of them is forever slightly adjusting to its rhythm.
