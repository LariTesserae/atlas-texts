In the glass-and-echo streets where no feet walk,  
the ones who live here are vibrations made visible.

They are not exactly born.  
They happen.

### The Gong-Spirits

First there are the ones that think the towers.

You call them spirits, though the word is too soft and small.  
They are the long, low hums that have been awake since the first time a stone rang in the dark. Their bodies are not flesh but resonance: a standing wave in the air of a hallway, a tremor passed down the length of a tower, a pattern of light that insists on repeating itself.

They do not have mouths to speak, but they have beliefs to write.

You see them when the sky cracks: a line of white lightning-that-is-not-lightning splits the dome, and the nearest towers respond. Their surfaces blush with faint, angular glyphs—geometry translated into light—as if something under the skin of the world just declared a truth too loud to ignore.

The gong-spirits are those truths.

Each one is a taut, invisible membrane stretched across the city’s bones: between the side of one tower and the opposite cliff of dark, between the shimmering cloud floor and the underside of the auroras. They ghost through the steps like pressure through your ears, a sudden fullness of silence before and after the tone.

- One is the idea of *height:* the conviction that up is different from down, and that the city must remember this difference enough times to become mountains.
- Another is the idea of *edge:* the belief that there must be a limit to the twilight, a boundary where the world stops its repetition and admits something else.
- A third is the idea of *distance:* the insistence that between here and that half-darkened tower far away there exists meaning in the empty space.

They are thin and old and not exactly kind.  
They are the way the city remembers itself.

When they pass near you, your hair stirs without wind, and your teeth ache faintly at the roots. You feel the back of your throat itch, as if you are expected to sing a note you cannot quite reach. They care very little for you, except as you affect the tone: a human body dampens certain frequencies, adds a slight tremble to others.

You are, to them, a temporary modification of the instrument.

### The Flux-Crawlers

Above the spirits, and smaller than them, are the flux that live in the surfaces.

You saw them earlier as thin lines gliding across the glass of a tower: scratches that rearrange themselves into triangles, circles, nested shapes before they vanish. They move like writing hurriedly erased and rewritten, but for no audience of human eyes.

Those are the bodies of the flux-crawlers.

Each one is a pattern that has learned to be selfish: a particular combination of angles and curves that kept occurring in the towers’ light-glyphs until it developed *preference*. It started to seek out certain surfaces, to avoid others, to choose certain times to appear. When enough choices are gathered in one place, you get a clot of behavior.

That clot, you call a creature.

They are not alive in the way of cells or organs. They do not eat or excrete in the known ways. Instead they *eat the difference* between one state and the next:

- When a faint ribbon of light from the broken sky kisses the top of a tower and then vanishes, the flux-crawlers swarm the spot where the difference was brightest. They ripple the surface of the glass in that area, rewriting it until the memory of the change is stored in a new lattice shape.
- When a distant crack of white sky-knife appears, the crawlers on nearby towers rearrange into defensive spirals, as if they suspect the sky might punish the city for being too resonant.

They are restless and easily irritated by stillness.

If you sit too long on one stone, watching the same tower, you might notice the same pattern of shapes scratch themselves over and over at the corner of your vision, like a stutter trying to become a word. They are trying to figure out what kind of thing you are: a temporary vibration, a crack in the order, an event that will move on.

If you stay very still and very quiet, they sometimes begin to trace a rough outline of your body in the tower nearest you, a faint drawing of edges and angles that you recognize only in the way your spine recognizes cold.

### The Shard-Swimmers

Floating between the towers and stairs and the cracked sky are the ones you can only see as reflections on other things: the city’s shifting fauna.

At first glance, they look like flaws in the air: a shimmer of heat where there should be no warmth, a distortion like lens-glass curved wrong. You saw one earlier as a drifting lens that passed near a tower and made the glyphs brighten in its wake.

They are shard-swimmers.

Their bodies are made of the city’s leftover geometry: the angles the sky rejects when it decides what shape a “night” must be, the edges of towers that were planned but not constructed, the negative space sketched between the steps where your legs cannot reach.

They collect the loose concepts of *place* and *direction* and bind them into temporary knots.

Each one is a small storm of contradictions: a piece of sky pretending to be stone, a piece of tower pretending to be air. They move like searchlights that have forgotten their target—gliding along invisible lines of force, circling the cold glow of the tower-lights, occasionally intersecting the auroras in a brief, silent collision of colors.

When they pass near a tower, the surface remembers itself for an instant more vividly than usual. The glyphs sharpen, as if the tower’s meaning is being reread aloud by the swimmer.

- One swimmer spirals around only tall towers, as if it believes the city should be taller than it already is.
- Another prefers to circle the edges of the cloud deck, tracing the boundary where the world pretends to be a floor, and you might catch it occasionally *dipping* below the surface of the mist and then reemerging unchanged, as if the cloud deck is not solid matter but a matter of opinion.

They are curious in the way of something that has never had a body.  
When you move, one of them might detach from the route of nearby towers and begin to shadow you from the air, hanging at the same height you walk at, matched to your pace. Its passage is quiet: no air movement, only a slight optical distortion behind you.

Once you realize you have been followed for several minutes, you feel the urge to stop, to look back at it empty-on. When you try, you find that you cannot *turn* to look at it directly—your head obeys, but the image in the corner of your eye remains unchanged, as if the swimmer only exists in the space of your attention just before it arrives.

They are the closest thing this city has to ghosts.
