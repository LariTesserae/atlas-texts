Hushed by the roar, they call the wind *voice*.

Their own name for themselves is lost, if they ever had one. Others, if others were ever to come here, might call them the Plateau-Folk, or the Cloud-Walkers, or simply the Stone-Eaters. They do not think of themselves as a species. They think of themselves as a place that learned to walk.

No one knows exactly what they were before the world changed. They don’t remember. They have no records beyond their bodies and the walls and the shallow, worn basins of their dead. They might have been human colonists whose ancestors were left behind when the mountains sank below the clouds and their world became a ceiling. They might have been engineered caretakers for a long-vanished weather station. They might have been something else that chose to squat in the ruins of the plateau and quietly changed shape to fit.

By now, they are simply *what lives here*.

### Bodies

They are tall and thin, not by accident but by design. The air is thin, and their bodies are thinner — long ribs, a narrow chest, a hunched posture that brings the skull closer to the ground and leaves the crown of the head to take the wind. The wind has been shaping them for much longer than they have been shaping stone.

From a distance they might be mistaken for pillars — eroded statues of a forgotten architecture. Their skin is the color of stone, ranging from pale gray to rust-red, depending on where they were born and which mineral they’ve taken into themselves. It is hard and cool to the touch, not armored like shell so much as thickened, like the callus of a palm.

They are hairless except where hair is useful: a thin, downy growth around the ears and the open curve of the nostrils, where it traps moisture from the air and guides it into the body. The eyes are narrow and can seal almost closed, leaving only a slit of vision to protect against wind-driven dust and sudden bursts of sun on the cloud-sea.

Their bones are dense but fewer than those of a human; some joints have been absorbed by time, leaving fewer lines to bend. The knees are hinged too low, the elbows straighten into a slight backward curve. When they walk, they move like folding reeds — a slow swing forward until the wind catches them, then a quick adjustment to avoid being pushed over the edge.

### Breath and Blood

On the plateau, oxygen is an idea more than a substance. The air has enough pressure to keep them from losing their blood to their own arteries, but not enough to support the kind of combustion that rages in lower, warmer worlds. They don’t breathe quickly. They breathe deeply and slowly, taking in air through the skin and through the lining of the mouth as well as the lungs.

Their lungs are not bags of air but narrow, folded shelves of membrane within the rib cage, each layer dense with capillaries. The blood is thick and almost black in the veins, full of molecules that carry the oxygen for longer than they would need elsewhere. They are not fast animals; they are like the water that doesn’t rush here but lingers in thin films.

Their heart beats in a slow, swinging pattern: *pump — pause — pump — pause —* like a stone hanging on a rope of muscle. There are points where they can sit for minutes without seeming to breathe at all, and then they take one long, slow inhale, and the world comes back into them.

### Water

They are woven of water.

Here on the plateau, water is not a river that you step into; it is a cloth you wear and then discard. The bodies of the inhabitants are designed to follow this rhythm. Their skin absorbs moisture from the air when the wind slackens and the mist descends, and it gives it back slowly when the sun climbs and the air turns dry.

To see them clearly, you have to catch them in the brief window of time when the cloud-sea below releases a column of rain that climbs up the cliff and into the basin. At that moment, when the thunder is shaking the stone and the basin is becoming a mirror of the sky, they are most alive.

They shed their outer layer of skin — a thin, translucent husk like a sheet of waterlogged bark — and let their new skin beneath drink directly from the rain. Their true color is not the gray or rust of the dry surface but the pale blue of twilight sky. They open their mouths and their eyes and drink the rain in their bones, not in their stomachs.

### Food and Stone

They don’t farm the land; they greet the land.

Most of the time they eat very little. They move slowly to conserve energy, and when they need it, they lower themselves onto a patch of moss or crimson liverwort, and with their tongues and the palms of their hands and soles of their feet they absorb nutrients from the thin water film on the plants.

Their main meal is the stone itself.

Not the stone as you know it — but what they have learned to see: stone as a slow, almost frozen nourishment. They have one sense that humans lack — a sense that allows them to taste the mineral pulses beneath the surface, the faint traces of calcium, iron, silica, and the faint traces of ancient life buried within the rock.

On the surface of the plateau, where the rock has been polished by wind and water and turned into smooth sheets, they sometimes scrape at it with chisel-shaped teeth and the hardened edge of a thumb. They don’t chew it so much as allow it to dissolve in their mouths and stomachs, where a specialized lining of their gut releases chemicals to slowly break down the minerals and release the trace elements they need.

They are not sculptors in stone. They are more like its slow harvesters. They know where a thin vein of salt-rich rock has been exposed, or where a sheet of limestone has trapped a layer of ancient dust rich enough to support them for a season. They tend to return to the same places over decades — a thin shelf of rock here, a carved basin there — until they have worn shallow depressions into the surface itself.

### Shelter and Architecture

Their ruins look like accidents to the untrained eye.

These are the remnants of a people who learned to be built into the landscape rather than onto it.

They are not builders in the conventional sense. They do not shape walls so much as they slide their walls into the spaces between the wind and the rock. They prefer low, circular structures of fitted blocks sunk into shallow folds of the plateau surface, where the wind already has a habit of passing over rather than pushing through.

The roofs, when they exist at all, are thin slabs of stone wedged horizontally into the rock walls, creating a low ceiling that a child or crouching adult could stand under with only the head brushing the stone. They don’t need tall space. They need to be out of the direct wind, and they need to be out of the sun in the few hours when it burns bright and clear instead of its usual muted glow.

Most of the time they don’t dwell inside walls at all. They prefer the overhang of a tilted rock slab or a hollow carved into the side of a stone pillar by long erosion. They nap in the shallow basins where water will remain briefly, letting their bodies absorb moisture through the skin and then move away again before the pool becomes dry.

### Tools and Time

Time moves in two speeds here: the slow erosion of the stone and the fast turbulence of the air. They live between these two extremes.

They are not primitive because they are ignorant. They are primitive in the sense of living with very little energy to spare.

They carve tools from bone and stone — not because they cannot imagine metal or glass, but because they have learned, over many generations, that the tools that matter most are the ones that can be repaired quickly from what the wind and the rain don’t take away. Their most common tools are scrapers and thin knives shaped from bone, and bowls and shallow basins carved from stone that have been polished by time rather than by craftsman’s hands.

They have no written language but they have a language of stone: the gradual arrangement and rearrangement of small blocks and patterns on the walls of their shelters, which sometimes align with the sun’s path or the shadow of a standing pillar or the edge of the cliff. When a pattern forming there tells them that the storm will be particularly strong or a drying season will be longer than usual, they know to move deeper into the folds of rock.

### Population and Family

There are not many of them.

The wind has not allowed them to become numerous. The plateau can support only so many slow metabolisms before the thin bands of moss and the hidden veins of mineral-rich rock are scraped clean. They are not a herd animal but a scattered presence — a few inhabitants here and a few more there, separated by miles of roaring emptiness.

They rarely meet in groups larger than three or four, except during the brief rains when the central basin is filled. The basin is their meeting place, though it is never formal, and there are no songs or speeches. There is only the sound of thunder and water, and the sound of their tools scraping gently against stone to break down the minerals and help the water penetrate.

Families are not always bloodlines but waterlines — who you drank next to the last time the basin filled. When they are born, the children do not look like little adults. They look like translucent worms at first, more like the strange creatures that swim briefly in the basin than like their parents. They are fed on water films and moss paste and the thin secretions of the adults’ skin, until their bones lengthen and their skin thickens and the wind of the plateau finally recognizes them as stone.

### Sound and Sense

They hear the wind all the time, but not as you do.

Their ear canal is partially closed, with a thin membrane that can vibrate and transmit the sound of the air to the inner bones without destroying the delicate membranes within. They don’t “hear” the storm so much as feel it in their jaw and spine.

When they speak to each other — and they do speak in a language of clicks and low breath sounds — they do it close, face to face, with the wind at their back rather than blowing into their mouths. Their lips and tongues are used to shaping air that is already moving violently; they have learned which words work in the middle of a storm and which must be reserved for the brief calm.

### Ruins and Memory

They are not afraid of the ruins scattered around the plateau.

They are made of the same stone as the walls. They remember the block of stone that became part of their bone and they know how to match the color of a wall and the color of their own skin. Sometimes they melt into a background of ruined walls and you only notice you are looking at a person when you see the eyes blink slowly.

They don’t understand the purpose of every door or each broken wall in the strict way that archaeologists from lower worlds would want. They know the *use* of each ruin — where the wind is quieter, where the sun will shine at what angle, how long to the next rain in this basin or that — but they do not have a story of who built it or why.

They are not great recite historians but they are long-term memory keepers of the plateau’s rhythm. When they die, their body is placed in a narrow basin carved into the rock or left exposed on the windswept surface of a stone slab. The wind and the thin rain slowly strip away flesh and skin, leaving only bones and eventually the stone-like remnants of their bones, which dissolve over decades and return their minerals to the plateau.

### Night and Day

By day, they are thin shadows moving slowly across the stone. They are rarely in a hurry; when they are, it is because the wind has changed and they must move to a new shelter before the night.

At night, when the clouds below the cliff edge glow like the furnace of the hidden world beneath and then fade to darkness, the plateau still roars with sound. The wind is louder at night, when the temperature of the stone and the temperature of the cloud-sea below begin to clash and draw air currents over the ledge like a river falling again.

They prefer not to sleep deeply. They prefer to doze lightly in the shelter of a low wall or a shallow rock hollow, with their body pressed against the stone and the voice of the wind becoming their heart rate.

They don’t distinguish night from day by light or darkness but by pressure and temperature. They know when the air is becoming more stable or less, and they adjust their position in the plateau accordingly — moving closer to the cliff edge when the storm winds are rising or withdrawing deeper into the folds of rock when the air is quiet but the chill is beginning to seep into the stone.

### Final Balance

What they are is a plateau that has learned to breathe.

They are the stone that has been given a thin, flexible outer layer of skin; the water that has learned to hold itself together long enough to take a step; the wind that has learned to slow down enough to sit beside a shallow basin of rain.

They are not the masters of the plateau. They are not the guardians of its ruins or the keepers of its secrets, though they might inherit all of that by default.

They are simply the last thing left standing on the last floor of the world’s old house — a thin line of life that has learned to survive in the exact shape between the air’s fury and the stone’s patience, where everything else has been rubbed away below the clouds.

If you were to stand on the plateau long enough, you might eventually see one of them: not as a blurry figure moving quickly away, but as a slow pillar of stone-colored skin and long limbs that gradually looks up and meets your eyes, and in whose face you can see that the plateau itself is looking back at you.
