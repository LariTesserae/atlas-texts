## The Inhabitants of the Lenten Engine

The Lenten Engine does not harbor people in the conventional sense. No one sleeps here, no one cooks their meals over its vents, no one raises their voice in prayer or song. Its only inhabitants are those things that have no other place to go—the orphans of the city, who have found in this abandoned chamber not a shelter but a world.

### The Caretaker

The chamber does not exist entirely outside of human awareness. There is a keeper.

He does not live here, but he comes. He is a man of indeterminate age, his features softened and blurred by the constant humidity just as the chamber's sharp corners have been. His uniform is not official, but something cobbled together: oil-stained overalls over a municipal sweater whose insignia has been picked away, heavy rubber boots that make no sound on the wet floor.

He is not a custodian of the city's records but of its forgotten machinery. He comes once a month, perhaps less often now than he used to. His job is not to operate the engine—that would be beyond him—but simply to observe.

He carries a small, waxed notebook. He performs the same rituals every visit. He checks the green zone of the pressure gauges, noting their faint trembles. He uses the waxed markers on the hook to label the month and year on a yellowed strip of tape near the main panel. He inspects the wiring for the gnaw-marks of deep-drain rodents or the corrosive touch of salt. He uses the long-handled tool to clip back any vegetation that dares to encroach upon the sacred geometry of the machine, ensuring the plant life remains a respectful ornament, not an invasive disease.

He does not speak. He does not hum to himself. He listens to the engine hum to him. He is the last witness to a covenant that no longer exists on paper but persists in the deep places of the world. He is the priest of a forgotten faith whose only god is a hum, and he leaves each time as silently as he arrived.

### The Regulators of the Hum

The dominant forms of life are not humanoid but insectoid. The Lenten Engine has its own custodians now.

The most numerous are the **Basin Beetles**. They are small and black, their shells hardened by the metallic-laden water they absorb from the floor. They move in constant, purposeful lines across the damp concrete and up the sides of the engine's casing. They are not disorganized. They seem to trace paths, cleaning microscopic corrosion from the metal with their mandibles, tending to the film of oil that keeps the machine from its final decay. They are the engine's immune system, a living army dedicated to the preservation of their world.

Floating in the air currents are the **Ghost Moths**, whose pale wings are translucent and veined like stained glass. They do not fly toward the light, but toward the heat. They gather near the warmth of the engine's relays and the faint pulses of the phosphorescent tubes, absorbing the energy. They are not attracted to you; you are too cold. They are the engine's animated sensors, drifting in the slow breath of the chamber.

### The Quiet Observers

The **Boiler Snails** are the slow pulse of the chamber's time.** They are not truly snails, but something more like armored mollusks, their shells not spiraled but faceted like angular nodules of rust-red stone. They do not move across the walls so much as they accrete across them. Their journey is measured in geological time, a path of microscopic rasping as they consume the mineral salts weeping from the concrete. They leave behind them faint trails of metallic residue, a slow script of interaction with their environment that no one will ever read. They are the engine's geologists, studying the walls in a language that takes longer to speak than a human lifetime.

### The Symbiotic Sort

The least visible life, but perhaps the most important, are the **Fungal Recorders**. They are not a single organism but a sprawling colony—a network of mycelial threads that spread through the darkest, dampest corners of the ceiling and within the hollow walls. They are not mushrooms but root-thin filaments of pallid white and faint green. They do not consume the wood of the chamber, for there is none. They feed on the faint electrical energy and electromagnetic fields emitted by the engine's long-running systems.

In response, they glow. A soft, greenish light that is barely visible in the gloom, but enough to form faint, shimmering patterns along the ceiling ribs. It is not a random phenomenon. The patterns shift slowly, responding to changes in pressure, vibration, and energy flow within the engine itself. They are a living interface, a biological display that has spent decades adapting to interpret the machine it lives alongside. They are the engine's internal memory, a silent record of its operational state written in a language of light that no one has been left to understand.
