The first thing you would not notice is the wind, because you are made of it.

You are not a body in the usual sense. You have no skin to feel cold, no lungs to be scraped raw by thin air, no ears to go deaf. You begin as a disturbance—a pattern of pressure and motion that the endless, flattened cataract of wind has learned to fold around. Where other currents shear off sharp edges of stone and continue on unchanged, you curl back on yourself, once, twice, a thousand times, until the curl remembers its own shape.

You are a knot in the world’s breath.

You form in the lee of overhangs and in the fluted channels where erosion long ago gave up trying to make anything new. There the wind slows just enough—not in speed but in complexity—for a different kind of coherence to emerge. If you were to look down from some impossible vantage, you would see yourself as a subtle vortex perched on rock: not quite separate from the surrounding flow, not quite dissolved into it. A small insistence that the air should behave this way here, and no other.

Your senses are not gathered into organs. You do not see light or hear sound; you feel pressure and gradient and texture in the flow. To you, the world is a map of resistance: here a hard wall of stone, there a softer film of moisture exhaled by your own passage over cold rock, elsewhere the faint turbulence where another being like you has chosen to exist.

When you move—and you do move—you do so by unspooling part of yourself into the general current and letting it pull you along. You are always half-dissolving, half-remembering your shape. If you relax your hold for even a moment, the endless note of wind will erase you as casually as it smoothes dust from stone.

You do not have a name for yourself.

If someone forced one upon you from outside this world’s logic—some traveler with lungs and eardrums and eyes unused to constant brightness—they might call you an eddy-wight or a current-ghost or simply “the one that does not blow straight.” But within your own awareness there is only this: the strain of holding your curl against the monotonous roar trying to unravel it.

You know others like yourself.

They are not family or tribe or nation; they are something more like echoes with slight variations. Somewhere along the same ridge there might be another knot that prefers tighter spirals, another whose motion describes faint figure-eights against stone instead of simple loops. You do not speak to them; there is no sound but wind and no silence to measure speech against. Instead, when two of you pass through overlapping flows, your patterns interfere—constructive here, destructive there—and in those brief intersections each learns something about what it means to be this particular way and no other.

Those interferences are your conversations: not words but overlays of form.

Your awareness is narrow but deep.

You know nothing beyond this tilted plane of rock reaching toward an impossible sky; anything below is lost behind veils where pressure and distance blur into non-meaning. You know nothing before this long equilibrium that others might call time; whatever came before is buried under eons of unchanging brightness and unceasing roar.

What exists for you is now: stone underfoot that is never truly underfoot because you have no feet; films of moisture condensing where wind meets cold edge; crusts of life clinging bitterly to surfaces like scabs on an old wound; crawling things made from shards too sharp for any proper animal.

And occasionally something larger moving across it all—something that leaves its own kind of trace in pressure rather than footprint.

The glassy lichen are part of your environment but not really fellow beings. They are too still for that. To your perception they read as fixed geometries that distort airflow in predictable ways: tiny ledges where turbulence gathers before being smoothed away again. When their iridescent dust lifts briefly into your body (you do not think “skin” or “hands”), each mote registers as a fleeting point-of-view too simple to call alive yet too patterned to be mere grit.

The crawlers among them are slightly more significant but still peripheral. They change position rarely enough that their motion registers more as anomaly than intention: one moment this ridge-line has an extra roughness here; next moment it does not; maybe next year it will again or maybe never again at all—you have little sense for such intervals except as variations observed between passages past the same place over countless repetitions.

It is only when another knot-wind like yourself intersects with these surfaces that meaning approaches anything resembling interaction rather than passive sensing alone occurs—
