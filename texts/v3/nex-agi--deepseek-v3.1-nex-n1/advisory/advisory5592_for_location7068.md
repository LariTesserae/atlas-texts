To visit the Wellspring Grove is to step into a place that _looks_ simple and almost static, but is actually a delicate, slow-moving machine made of water, stone, and time. If you’re not careful, you can break it without meaning to—or break yourself in subtler ways.

Below is a preparation guide for an ordinary human traveler, from what to bring to how to behave once you’re there.

---

## 1. Understand what you’re walking into

### Size and scale
- The Grove itself is small: think of it as roughly the size of a village square or a cathedral nave—big enough to feel open, but small enough that you could see every detail if you sat still.
- It is a bowl-shaped basin carved into a high, desolate mountain shoulder. The world outside the basin rim is dry, thin-aired, and much more hostile to life.
- Inside the bowl, almost everything is designed by water: faint channels of moisture in stone, a shallow silver central pool, and sparse plants that have been shaped for a very, very slow pace of life.

### Primary dangers
- **Not physical violence** but **subtle environmental stress**: extreme humidity + heat + altitude + the psychological weight of silence and time.
- **No animal life**: nothing will bite you or attack you, but there is no support system for animals—including you.
- Your **greatest risk**: disturbing the balance of water, stone, and plants that have been carefully settling into this state for centuries.

### The key tension of visiting
You are going from a world where time is measured in seconds, motion, and noise to a place where time is measured in seasons and decades, and change is almost invisible. If you try to behave “normally” there, you risk behaving like a bull in a glasshouse of time.

## 2. Before you arrive: physical and medical prep

### Assume high altitude + mild humidity stress
Even though the Grove itself is warm and humid, the approach is likely _high and dry_. You’re on “a high shoulder of stone where the rest of the mountain has been worn away.” That means:

- Be prepared for **possible altitude symptoms**:
  - Headaches, shortness of breath, dizziness, nausea.
- **Acclimate in advance** if you can:
  - Spend at least a day or two at higher elevation before the final approach to the Grove.
- **Avoid alcohol**, heavy sedatives, or anything that strongly depresses breathing in the first day or two.

### Hydration and electrolytes
The water here is rich in minerals. “Something faintly metallic, like crushed copper ore held a safe distance from fire.” That implies:

- Drink routinely **before you feel thirsty**.
- Carry **electrolytes** or salt tablets:
  - You’re sweating in warm humidity but likely breathing thinner air.
- **Investigate the water before you drink it**. Given:
  - The water’s mineral content keeps all animal life from developing eggs or spores,  
  you should not assume it is safe for humans.
  - Bring:
    - A lightweight filtration device (suitable for heavy minerals and contaminants).
    - If possible, purification tablets or a small UV treatment device.
    - Or just bring your own water and treat the Grove’s water as an experimental curiosity.

### Clothing and physical protection

#### – Temperature and humidity handling
The air is “warm and thick,” “weighty,” “humid enough that you feel you could push your hands through it.” Combined with stone that stores heat, you’re in a humid micro-environment high up.

Recommend **light layers**:
- A thin, **quick-drying base layer**.
- A **light long-sleeved shirt and pants** to protect from rock abrasion and UV.
- A **warm layer jacket for mornings and evenings** when the air cools down.

#### – Footwear
The stone is “darkened with moisture,” stained with algae, and “textured like a lung cast in rock.” That means:

- **Choose footwear with excellent grip**:
  - Lightweight hiking boots with a good rubber sole for basalt and wet surfaces.
- Avoid anything smooth-soled or entirely flat—you **will be slipping on damp film and algae**.

### Sun protection
Even though the basin rim “shades the sides,” you’re still high up and the sun is likely intense:

- **Good sunscreen (SPF 30+)**.
- **A hat with a brim** to protect from both sun and reflection off the central pool.
- Possibly sunglasses to reduce glare from the water.

### Respirator and moisture control
The air is “warm and dense, humidity like a hand held just above skin,” and the scent is “wet stone and faintly metallic.” For sensitive individuals:

- If you have asthma or sensitive airways:
  - Consider a very light mask for parts of the visit, especially at first, when breathing heavy humidity plus high altitude.
- Bring **any inhalers, medications** you need; there is no help here.

## 3. What to bring with you

### Essentials
- Map and/or navigation device to:
  - Find the Grove (GPS, if your device works).
  - Return to your base or world gateway.
- A lightweight emergency beacon or transponder if you have access to that tech.
- Basic **first aid kit**:
  - Bandages, antiseptic wipes, painkillers, altitude meds if you’ve been prescribed them.
  - Antifungal cream: in persistent humidity, even humans not native to this world can get skin infections.

### Shelter and sleep
- You do not camp in the basin itself:
  - The ground is a complex capillary of water and stone; your weight and body heat will disturb microflows.
- **Camp near or outside the rim**:
  - A small, **freestanding tent** or sleeping shelter with waterproof groundsheet.
  - Lightweight sleeping pad for insulation and comfort.
- **Sleeping bag**: you’re high altitude; nights may be cold once outside the Grove and away from its stored heat.

### Food and water
- Bring **enough food for your entire stay** plus at least a day or two extra.
- The Grove’s ecology:
  - No insects, no birds, no fish, no edible animals.
  - Algae and mosses are not tested or safe for human consumption.
  - The glass of reeds and their “lanterns” are not food.
- **Assume you will not forage anything useful here.**
- Bring:
  - High-calorie, compact snacks:
    - Nuts, seeds, energy bars, etc.
  - A simple **compact stove** if you need hot meals (kept away from the basin).

### Lighting
- The place has quiet, glass lanterns that “harbor stars,” but:
  - They are few and fragile.
  - They are not yours.
- You bring:
  - **Headlamps or handheld torches** with:
    - Red-light mode if you’re concerned with preserving your night vision.
    - Soft intensity rather than blindingly bright beams.
  - Extra batteries or power banks.

### Tools and observation gear
Certain phenomena here unfold on scales of hours to weeks:

- A **notebook and pencil** (waterproof paper if possible):
  - For sketching patterns of algae, channels, and reeds.
- A **small camera** with:
  - Macro capability—to capture details of the stone texture, algae lines, and glass reeds.
- **Binoculars or a small magnifier**:
  - To watch the faint film of water on the rocks, micro-channels in the algae.
- Optional: **simple time-lapse equipment** if you want to see:
  - The slow creep of a film of water on the wall.
  - The way lantern glass changes over months in a video compressed.

### Protection from the environment
- **Waterproof bags** for all electronics:
  - Even though there is no rain, the air is so thick with condensation that your gear will get damp over time.
- **Desiccant packs** in sealed containers for:
  - Camera, writing materials, batteries.
- A small **ground cloth or foam pad**:
  - To sit on when you’re in the Grove itself, so you’re not directly touching the stone with your body heat and moisture for extended periods.

## 4. Time and mental preparation: how to enter “Grove time”

The most important thing you might underestimate is _time_.

- Processes in the Grove:
  - “Glass lanterns” maintain light for _years_, not minutes.
  - “Slow ridges of flaws” within them creep over months and years.
  - You won’t see a plant sprout overnight or a reed collapse in a week.

### Adjust your expectations
- Decide beforehand:
  - You are not going to rush “sightseeing.”
  - You are going to **visit a living sculpture that crosses geological time and plant time**.

### Schedule-around long time scales
- **If you can only visit for a short time**:  
  - Treat the visit as a meditation:
    - Sit quietly.
    - Observe the texture of water.
    - Accept that you will not see change happen.
- **If you have access to longer stays or multiple visits**:
  - You might be able to witness:
    - Slight movement of moisture films.
    - Changes in the algae’s pattern.
    - New micro-cracks in the reeds.
    - Over many visits, the formation or collapse of one glass lantern.

### Prepare psychologically for lack of activity
- No:
  - animals to watch,
  - wind to rustle leaves in,
  - storms to watch,
  - events.
- You will be exposed:
  - To your own **internal noise**: thoughts, boredom, discomforts.
- If you are prone to anxiety or claustrophobia:
  - The encircling rim, “room of the sky,” can be either:
    - Deeply soothing,
    - Or slightly enclosing like a well.
  - Bring:
    - A grounding plan:
      - Breathing exercises.
      - Mantras.
      - Mental routines.

## 5. Entering the Grove

### Approach the basin rim first
Your first step should not be into the basin, but up to the rim:

- Stand on the edge:
  - Look down at the “village-square-sized” oval.
  - Admire the symmetry:
    - Algae bands matching circular basin.
    - Glass lanterns positioned like cardinal points.
    - Network of channels radiating from the center.
- From here:
  - You see how small and complete it is.
  - You sense the contrast between outside world (barren stone, thin air) and inside.

### Decide your entry route
- Choose a route:
  - That crosses least vegetation (if any).
  - Avoids trampling moss.
  - Avoids stepping directly on the central pool’s edges or on channels that you can see.
- Use existing:
  - Bare spots of stone.
  - Gentle slopes of the rim where you won’t break rock edges or dislodge tiny ledges.

### Step in like a visitor to a temple
- Enter:
  - With quiet.
  - Not speaking loudly if with others.
  - Fitting yourself to the scale of the place:
    - Slow steps.
    - Soft movements.

## 6. While inside: How to behave

### Keep movement to corridors and margins
- **Preferred paths**:  
  - Bare ridges of basalt, between the moss mounds and clumps of reeds.
  - Places where algae already show thin lines of dampness—indicating water, but also toughness that has handled being walked over for a long time.
- **Avoid**:
  - Stepping directly on the **central pool’s edge** where water level rhythmically rises and falls.
  - **Glass lanterns** and their base reeds—any damage is not a short-term repair.
  - **Moss cushions**: any mistake of weight or placement is not quickly fixed.

### Minimize disturbance of water and stone
- The stone “weeps water” from countless pores and films.
- If you sit:
  - Use your ground cloth or pad:
    - The body warmth of extended sitting changes heat and moisture patterns.
- Avoid:
  - **Carving or chipping** stone:
    - The narration suggests that the stone requires hard tools to work, and that this place has “no built structures,” but is not inaccessible to primitive craft.
    - You are not here to interpret that as permission to use tools lightly.
    - This is not a canyon wall where hikers leave graffiti; this is a time-lapse sculpture of geology.
  - **Pouring anything into the pool**: chemicals, food waste, body fluids.
  - **Scooping water aggressively** or disturbing it with large tools.

### Avoid “plant souvenirs”
- **Do not break**:
  - Stone-glass reeds.
  - Moss branches.
  - Glass lanterns.
- Even if you see:
  - A fragment that seems to have already fallen from a dead reed:
    - Leave it.
    - The Grove itself recycles its materials over time in mineral dusts and salts.

### If you must take samples
If you are a researcher with true permission:

- Collect:
  - **Tiny water samples** with clean containers.
    - Label each as from:
    - central pool,
    - channels,
    - weeping walls.
- **Small samples of algae, where they are abundant**, after consulting:
  - Any local rules, if multiverse law applies here.
  - Your own minimal-impact principle.
- **Sketch and photograph instead of collecting reeds or lantern glass.**
- Even where you have permission:
  - Take:
    - Less than you think.
    - Only from areas where removal of a small piece will not break the symmetry of the system.

## 7. Health and comfort in the Grove

### Breathing and humidity
- The air is “warm and dense,” humidity high.
- If you feel:
  - Short of breath, or
  - Claustrophobic from the humidity:
    - Move closer to the rim where the air is a bit cooler and drier.
    - Take slow, deep breaths.
    - Sit or lie down to reduce strain.

### Avoiding overheat
- Even though it is “not exhausting,” heat + humidity + high altitude can still:
  - Drain you subtly faster than you realize.
- Schedule:
  - Short periods of observation followed by breaks outside the basin rim, where the air is thinner, drier, and more comfortable.

### Skin and moisture-related issues
- With constant humidity:
  - Keep skin:
    - As dry as possible in clothing-covered regions.
    - Change socks if they become wet.
  - Use:
    - Antifungals preventatively if you are prone to issues.
- **Do not unnecessarily wash** body parts in the pool:
  - Soaps and oils are not what this system has evolved for.

### Listening to your body
In the absence of external noise (no wind, no animals, very few events):

- Your own heart:
  - “You might notice your own heart measuring out the pulse that nothing else provides.”
- Use this:
  - As a way to self-check stress:
    - If you start feeling excessively restless or distressed, the Grove might be telling you to back out for the day.

## 8. Photography and art without intrusion

### General principles
- Use:
  - Natural, available light:
    - The glass lanterns at night give a faint blue-white glow.
    - Use the pool’s reflections to enhance composition.
- **Avoid flash photography**:
  - It changes the light environment dramatically.
  - The glass reeds and algae are adapted to specific intensities of diffuse and reflected light.

### Macro and detail shots
- Use:
  - Macro lens to:
    - Show individual pores and fractures in the stone.
    - Reveal the algae patterning as trail of water.
- **Do not touch** these surfaces with your lens or fingers:
  - You can change the moisture film, leave oils or pressure.

## 9. Meaning of the “after of catastrophe”

### The geological story
- The Grove “sits on a high shoulder of a mountain where the rest has been worn away.”
- It sits “in the long, calm after of some greater catastrophe.”
- This likely means:
  - Some ancient event peeled or weathered the mountain around this point,
  - Leaving an enclosed basin where water:
    - Could be trapped,
    - Could seep up,
    - Could be held by rock and gravity.

### Prepare emotionally for this
- You might feel:
  - A sense of **afterness**:
    - The catastrophe is long gone,
    - But its memory lives in the shape of the place.
  - A feeling of:
    - Gratitude that the world still holds such quietly careful living.
    - Melancholy if you are aware of time in terms of:
      - Decay,
      - Erosion,
      - Deep change.

Allow yourself to sit with this without forcing an interpretation.

## 10. If you stay longer: multi-day or multi-season visits

### Longer-term observation
If this is one of those places you can return to across time:

- Keep:
  - **Notes** of algae patterns,
  - Seasonal changes,
  - If any reeds lengthen or lanterns form or fail.
- Over years:
  - You might see:
    - The slow “thought” the mountain has been thinking,
    - Played out in subtle changes.

### Maintain a low footprint
- Longer stays:
  - Increase chances of your habits:
    - Wearing the same path into dust,
    - Concentrating heat where you sit,
    - Affecting moss or hidden water channels.
- Vary your sitting spots:
  - Use the same general areas but not exactly the same patch of stone.
- Use:
  - The same camping and hygiene practices as if you were only there for a day,
    - But more strictly,
    - Because more time = more accumulation.

---

### Departure and leaving the Grove intact

When you leave:

- **Take everything you brought** with you:
  - No wrappers, no left items.
- **Fill in potential footprints** if your boots are leaving:
  - Deep marks in the dust, but:
    - Do so with a light hand:
      - A small touch of dust to diminish the human signature,
      - As not to change drainage patterns.

---

### Final summary: How to prepare

- **Prepare physically**:
  - Altitude conditioning, hydration, electrolytes, protection from humidity and heat.
- **Prepare mentally**:
  - Treat the Grove as a quiet, living temple of slow-time.
- **Prepare logistically**:
  - Bring all food and water you’ll need;
  - Bring observation tools and light;
  - Bring no invasive tools unless you have a disciplined research program.
- **Prepare ethically**:
  - Move softly,
  - Touch minimally,
  - Observe generously,
  - Change nothing.

If you do that, you can sit safely inside the Wellspring Grove and experience:

> wet, not flooded;
> lush, not overgrown;
> warm, not exhausting;
> high but not dizzying;
> small, deep in detail;
> sparse, rich in what is present;
> quiet, not dead;
> slow, not frozen;

and become, for a time, part of the long thought that mountain and water have been thinking together for centuries.
