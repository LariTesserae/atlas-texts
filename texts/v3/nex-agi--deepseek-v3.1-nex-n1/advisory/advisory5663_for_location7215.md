Preparing to visit this place—let’s call it the Drowned Tower—is not like packing for a “destination.” It’s closer to preparing for long-term winter field work in a very strange, very wet abbey. Most of what you’ll want to bring is shaped by one fact: everything is damp, everything is cold, and everything is *slow*.

You’re not going to conquer this place; you’re going to be quietly absorbed by it.

Below is a practical guide, from mindset to gear to daily routines.

---

## 1. Mindset and expectations

### 1.1. Scale: small, vertical, intimate

This is not a vast city. It’s more like a small monastery village fused with a tidal lake. The total number of buildings is probably less than thirty, and most of them are half-sunk. The tallest structure—the tower—is only four stories. A brisk climber could scale its interior height in minutes.

So you’re not going to get lost in sprawling streets. You’ll get *hemmed in* by closeness: water pressing up from below, mist pressing down from above, and people around you who have been living in each other’s earshot for their whole lives.

Expect: a small, close-knit community, where you will be noticed *immediately*.

### 1.2. The pace: slow, hands, water

You are leaving “machine time” and entering “water time.” In practice:

- Nothing happens fast.
- Travel is arduous and short: from one house to another across a walkway, from the tower to the edge of the basin.
- Work is mostly by hand: hauling, mending, netting, grinding, weaving.
- Weather is not an event, it is a background: a constant drift between cool and colder, moist to sodden.

Your own internal clock should adjust down a gear. Brings books, small tools, journal materials. You’re not going to be entertained by the place; you’re going to become part of its routine.

### 1.3. The dominant element: water in everything

Water is not just scenery; it is the floor, the air, the memory, and maybe the will of the place.

- Stone walls weep.
- Wood is always swelling or shrinking.
- Metal rusts quickly.
- Cloth never feels truly dry.
- Light passes through mist before it gets to you.

Your entire strategy—from clothing to storage to mental health—should be built around the assumption that you will be *slightly damp* for your entire stay.

---

## 2. What you are not going to fix

Before you go, you should accept a few non-negotiable realities:

- You can’t dry out the city.
- You can’t fully protect your gear from moisture.
- You can’t map the thing under the waterline with certainty.
- You can’t escape the feeling of being watched by the tower.

What you *can* do:

- Keep yourself warm and dry enough.
- Avoid infection and injury.
- Be useful and respectful to the locals.
- Observe and record without provoking the place.

Think of this as a long, wet, observational research outpost rather than an adventure site.

---

## 3. Clothing and personal gear

### 3.1. Layering for persistent damp and cold

You want layers that handle *continuous* dampness, not just rainstorms:

- **Base layer (synthetic or treated):**
  - Long-sleeve top and long bottoms that dry quickly.
  - Avoid heavy cotton; anything that gets soaked stays soggy forever.
- **Mid-layer insulation:**
  - Fleece or thin wool for warmth when you’re not moving.
  - Something that still insulates when damp.
- **Outer layer: waterproof but breathable**
  - A hooded jacket and trousers with taped seams.
  - Think more like “sea kayaking in cool weather” than “mountaineering.”
  - Reinforced at knees and elbows—you’ll spend time on wet planks and stone floors.
- **Feet:**
  - High-quality waterproof boots with good grip on wet wood and slick algae.
  - Extra pairs of socks in a waterproof bag inside your pack.
  - Simple gaiters to keep water from sloshing down your boots when wading.
- **Hands:**
  - Thin waterproof gloves for handling wet rope, net, or stone without losing dexterity.
  - A pair of heavier work gloves for hauling, lifting, and repairs.

### 3.2. Extra items you’ll be glad you brought

- **Spare gloves and socks:**  
  You will lose things to rot or misadventure. Bring more than you think you need, and assume *one* set will become permanently unusable.
- **Lightweight headwear:**  
  A cap or hood under your outer hood to keep drips from running down your neck.
- **Simple face mask / scarf:**  
  For the smell and chill of damp air, for the occasional moldy whiff when you’re near submerged spaces.
- **Change bags:**  
  A set of waterproof stuff bags or dry bags for storing:
  - Documents.
  - Electronics / notebooks.
  - Extra dry clothing.

## 4. Health and hygiene

### 4.1. Foot and skin care (critically important)

Moisture plus time plus pressure means:

- Athlete’s foot and other fungal infections.
- Chilblains and pressure sores.
- Skin breakdowns from constant chafing.

You should bring:

- **Antifungal cream or powder** for feet and other damp-prone areas.
- **Barrier cream:** zinc-based or petroleum-based to protect intact skin.
- **Extra socks** stored in dry bags.
- **Talcum or anti-moisture powder** if you tolerate it well.
- **Basic bandages and gauze** suitable for wet environments.

You *must* make time to let your feet and skin get air and relative dryness, even if it is only:

- Removing boots and socks in your quarters.
- Tucking a small “breathable” spacer under damp socks if you have to keep moving.

### 4.2. Water and food safety

The water of the plaza is not for drinking. It is a:

- Salt-soup,
- With algae,
- With possibly ancient bone-residue,
- With unknown organics,
- Absorbing everything from the walls and foundations.

Treat any water like it is contaminated:

- **Purification tabs** or a small filter system if you can bring one.
- A **metal kettle or small pot** to boil water from more trusted sources (e.g., rainwater collected off roofs).
- Simple **oral rehydration salts** in case of diarrhea.

Food you get from the locals:

- Fish likely to be small, thin, and carrying a slight algae bitterness.
- Dried algae, mosses, ferns.
- Probably some grain ground on the slow grindstone.

Adapt your stomach:

- Eat *small* portions of new foods at first.
- Let your body adjust to the local algae and any fermentation byproducts.
- Bring a multivitamin if you have access; the local diet might be narrow.

### 4.3. Adapted first aid kit

You’re not likely to get catastrophic injuries from great height or violent animals (there are few large predators), but you *are* at risk of:

- Splinter wounds from old wood.
- Cuts from rusting metal or broken tools.
- Slipping and twisting joints on wet planks.
- Respiratory issues from mold and spores.

Pack:

- Antiseptics suitable for wet environments.
- Fine tweezers for splinters.
- Painkillers and anti-inflammatories.
- Elastic bandage or supports for sprains.
- A mask (e.g., multi-ply fabric) for times when you must disturb moldy corners or enter especially damp, enclosed spaces.

## 5. Navigation and safety on the water

The “plaza” is a shallow sheet of water, but:

- The bottom is not uniform.
- There are foundations, bones, and remnants of walls below the surface.
- Bioluminescent algae outline some shapes, but not all.

### 5.1. Moving on the walkways

- Assume *every plank* is slippery.
- Test the rope railing before trusting weight to it.
- Watch out for rusted nails; they are sharp, and tetanus is a real risk.
- Step deliberately; don’t rush.

If you fall, the water is only a few meters deep, but:

- You may hit submerged stone or timber.
- Visibility may be near zero.
- The shock of cold water will be significant.

### 5.2. If you absolutely must go into the water

Prepare:

- Tie a line to yourself and to something solid.
- Wear sandals or protective shoes you can spare to get scuffed and slimy.
- Limit time in the water to short, intentional tasks.
- Rinse and dry yourself as best as possible after.

Also: *look for the current*.

- If the water starts rotating on the plaza, that’s not a good time to be near.
- In the tower, if someone quietly checks the shutters and winches, you should too.

## 6. Tools and useful supplies

### 6.1. Things you’ll actually use

Leave the “heavy adventuring gear” behind. Bring instead:

- **Knife and multi-tool:**  
  For repairs, cutting rope, preparing food, mending gear.
- **Small waterproof LED flashlight or headlamp:**  
  Charged before you arrive, plus spare batteries if possible.
- **Notebooks and pencils:**  
  Paper will be vulnerable; consider oiled or waterproof paper if you can get it.
- **Basic sewing kit:**  
  For repairing clothing and pack straps.
- **Small, sharpening stone:**  
  For the locals’ tools and your own blade.
- **Lightweight rope or paracord:**  
  For lines, lashing, replacement ties if needed.
- **Basic measuring kit:**  
  - Tape measure or string and marks.
  - For observing the waterline and tracking changes.

### 6.2. Things to leave *behind*

- Anything bulky and heavy that you can’t justify.
- Synthetically noisy equipment—unless it’s truly necessary to your mission, the stillness of the place makes it feel out of place.
- Excessively bright gadgets or flashing lights: they’ll clash with the dimness and make you unwelcome or unsettling to others.

## 7. Behavior and etiquette with the locals

### 7.1. First arrival: you are not invisible

In a place this small and enclosed:

- Your arrival will be known within minutes.
- You will be assessed by:
  - How you treat the water.
  - How you treat their tools and metal.
  - Your willingness to help with small tasks.

Approach slowly:

- Declare who you are and what you’re doing there in straightforward terms.
- Do not claim to be anything special (explorer, hero, mage of the multiverse).
- Ask if you can help with simple tasks: hauling, mending, netting.

### 7.2. Respect the resources

Metal is rare, carefully treated; tools are precious.

- Don’t borrow without explicit permission.
- Don’t leave tools out in the wet or allow them to rust.
- Hand them back in better condition than you found them, if you can.

Food and warmth are also limited:

- Don’t eat more than you’re offered.
- Don’t waste heat or water.
- Complaining about the dampness will be exhausting for everyone.

### 7.3. Enter the tower’s presence slowly

The tower is not just a building:

- It is the center, the calendar, and probably the calendar of water memory.
- It is where *they* go when they need to watch the water’s behavior.

Ask:

- Whether you may enter the tower.
- If there are days or tides when it is better not to.
- How to behave inside: where you may walk, where not.

Once inside:

- Listen as much as you look.
- Note the sound of the water against the stone.
- Pay attention to any lines or marks on the walls that show old waterlines.
- Respect the silence of the coldest nights when the water goes glass-smooth and the glow underneath gathers.

## 8. Observing the unusual: water, writing, and bone

### 8.1. The tide pattern

Treat the water’s behavior as the main language of the place:

- Notice the *line* it leaves on the tower stones when it recedes:
  - Is the line higher or lower than it was last week?
  - Does it change with weather or “moon” phase?
- Record:
  - Time of day.
  - Temperature of air and water.
  - Any rotation or current direction on the plaza.
- Over time, these patterns may become your way of recognizing the “intentions” of the place.

Don’t try to interpret the patterns too quickly. You’re seeing half of a conversation at best.

### 8.2. The glow under the water

The bioluminescent “script” under the foundations:

- It is probably not designed for your eyes.
- Don’t assume you must “decode” it; you may not have the senses or context to do that.

When you look:

- Note the rhythm of the pulses.
- Note whether the same pattern appears in different places at the same time.
- Note whether those nights correspond with any other event:
  - Changes in water temperature.
  - Changes in the behavior of fish or algae.
  - Local behavior or rituals.

*Do not* try to “interfere with the code” by disrupting algae or rearranging bones under the water. You risk not just the locals’ anger but awakening the attention of the thing beneath the city.

### 8.3. The bone-like foundations

You’ll see arches that resemble enormous ribs, half-buried in silt and glowing:

- Do not assume they’re dead just because they’re not moving.
- Do not treat them as archeological curiosities you can excavate casually.
- If you *must* investigate, do it with permission, in company, and in small, cautious touches.

One of the safest approaches: ask *how* the locals avoid the bones, when they must repair a walkway or foundation. Their answer will be more valuable than any random poking.

## 9. Mental and emotional preparation

### 9.1. Prepare for monotony and sensory saturation

This place is both quiet and overwhelming:

- Quiet because:
  - Few engines.
  - Few animals.
  - Low human noise.
- Overwhelming because:
  - Every surface is occupied with something: wet stone, rope, moss, nails, planks.
  - Your ears are constantly full of small sounds: drips, creaks, distant grinding stone.
  - Your skin is rarely perfectly dry or warm.

You will go through periods of:

- Restlessness: “Why am I here if nothing happens?”
- Mild claustrophobia: “I can’t get away from the water and the mist.”
- Superstitious unease: “The tower is watching. The water remembers.”

### 9.2. Build yourself a routine

Because you can’t rush anything else, you *can* rush into:

- A daily journal of observations.
- A set of small tasks:
  - Check gear for dampness.
  - Help with a net or haul a bucket.
  - Learn one skill from the locals each week (e.g., basic rope splicing, simple net repair).

Routine reduces the sense of being trapped in time.

### 9.3. Remember the proportions of history

What you see:

- A few dozen people living in tightly packed dampness.

What you should understand:

- They have adapted, over generations, to a world that *sank* beneath them.
- The tower might be older than any current language.
- The bone-city under the water might be older than the tower.

This perspective keeps you from:

- Patronizing the locals as “primitive.”
- Assuming that your foreign technology makes you somehow better or safer.

---

## 10. Leaving: how you’ll change

When it’s time to return, you will likely:

- Notice dryness differently:
  - Your own skin will be hypersensitive to air that is not chilled.
  - Silence will feel emptier without the drip of water and the groan of wood.
- Realize how much movement you previously took for granted:
  - Every step in the Drowned Tower has been chosen:
    - Don’t slip on the wet planks.
    - Don’t overload the rope.
    - Don’t wake whoever is in the room above or below you.
- Carry with you:
  - A calmer sense of time.
  - A better appreciation for small tools and simple materials.
  - An instinctive caution around water that appears to have a *motion* of its own.

You will also be carrying the *memory of being watched*:

- Not just by the locals,
- And not just by the tower,
- But by the water and whatever bone and fire and glow lie under the surface.

---

In short, preparing to visit the Drowned Tower is not about gearing up for adventure. It is about:

- Choosing equipment that can withstand constant dampness.
- Planning for health under unrelenting wet and cold.
- Learning to move slowly and respectfully in a very small, very vertical community.
- Training yourself to observe and listen instead of acting and changing.

If you accept this, you will not be safe—no place worth visiting truly is—but you will be suited to the place, and the place will be more likely to tolerate you.
