To step into that place is to step into the inside of a world’s circulatory system while the heart is still beating. It’s not a “ruin” you stroll through in sneakers and a camera; it’s a vast machine-organism whose wet, cold, conductive body can injure you in ways nature doesn’t. You’re not just visiting a scene; you’re entering into the body of something.

If you want to survive it and come out intact—rather than being dissolved, crushed, or shorted out—you need to prepare yourself, your tools, and your mind.

---

## 1. Mindset: What You Are Actually Entering

Before you pack a single item, it’s important to understand the kind of world this is.

- You are entering the **lower levels of a world-sized machine**.
- The machine is not quite dead; it’s asleep, or idling. Water is still flowing through it like blood through a body in shock.
- Leaks are everywhere. The air is damp, conductive, and chemically active.
- Height is not just “height” here; it is a **three-dimensional latticework of floors and voids**. You don’t just fall down; you can fall into the machine’s guts.
- The “fauna” and “flora” have evolved in relation to metal, water, and current. They interact with the machine. You are the fragile organism here.

This is not like hiking in a cold rainforest or exploring an abandoned factory. It’s closer to walking inside a submarine factory that is also a cave system and also a swamp, and is still occasionally powered.

**Key mental shifts:**

- You are not “safe” on any surface; you are only safe if you’re prepared and careful.
- Silence is not necessarily good; a sound might be the only warning you get before a floor section fails or a charge arcs.
- Water is not “clean rain”; it is more like blood or sweat from the machine body, contaminated with rust, oil residue and whatever chemicals were in the industrial system.

## 2. Dressing the Body: Clothing and Protection

### 2.1. Core Principle: Dry, Cold, and Insulated

Your priority order is:

1. **Keep dry**
2. **Keep warm**
3. **Stay insulated from electrical current**

If you’re wet and cold, you will lose dexterity, then strength, then clarity of mind. This place is designed to steal your body heat.

### 2.2. Undergarments and Base Layer

- **Base layer**: full synthetic or merino wool thermal underwear.
  - Avoid cotton if possible. Once cotton gets wet, it stays wet and chills you.
- Socks: thick synthetic or wool-blend socks in at least two layers.
- You’re going to move a lot; expect chafing. Use anti-chafing products.

### 2.3. Primary Layer: Waterproof and Insulated

- **Waterproof shell**:
  - A full waterproof suit or coverall is best, rather than just a jacket.
  - Choose something with sealed seams and strong waterproof material that you’ve tested before going.
- **Insulation layer**:
  - A lightweight synthetic insulated jacket or vest under the shell.
  - You can add layers underneath but avoid being too thick; you’ll cook if you work hard.
- **Pants**:
  - Waterproof overpants that are durable and abrasion-resistant, because:
    - You will be scraping past metal edges.
    - You might need to crawl on the floor grid.
    - Rust flakes are sharp.

### 2.4. Head, Face, and Hands

- **Head**:
  - Warm cap under a helmet.
- **Face**:
  - Full-face mask or even a full diving mask with integrated air supply if you can get it.
  - Consider a mask that can swap filters (for particulate matter and basic chemical vapor filtration).
- **Hands**:
  - Waterproof insulated gloves or 3-layer system (thin inner, waterproof shell over).
  - For fine work:
    - Lighter waterproof mechanics gloves so you can feel what you’re touching.
    - If you can’t get good waterproof gloves for work, accept that you may need to temporarily expose hands and then immediately warm, dry, and inspect them after.

### 2.5. Foot Protection

- **Waterproof boots**, preferably insulated:
  - Steel-toe or composite-toe boots are *not* optional here.
  - Look for thick, deep-tread soles that can:
    - Grip on painted wet metal.
    - Resist sharp punctures from bolt heads or broken floor edges.
- **Overboots or waterproof gaiters**:
  - If you can get them, they extend the waterproof coverage of your legs and stop water from pouring down into your boots from above.

### 2.6. Special Considerations: Electrical and Chemical Hazard

- **Avoid metal** in contact with your skin:
  - No metal bracelets, rings, or necklaces.
  - Eye and face shields with metal frames should be insulated from skin contact if possible.
- **Assume all surfaces can be charged**:
  - Don’t grab two conductive surfaces at different heights/positions with bare hands while wet—you’re creating a path for current.
- **Secondary “clean” layer**: have a backup inner layer of clothes and socks in a waterproof bag inside your pack, in case you get wet anyway.

## 3. Body Care: Combatting the Environment

### 3.1. Cold and Wet

- You will feel **cold creep**: from hands, feet, face. Your body temperature doesn’t need to drop dangerously to make you clumsy.
- You must:
  - **Move** to generate heat.
  - **Monitor your own extremities**:
    - Periodically ball your fingers into a tight fist and then release them to judge circulation.
    - Stop and warm your hands if you notice fine motor control deteriorating.
  - **Avoid prolonged sitting or contact with cold metal surfaces**; you will lose heat faster than you generate it.

### 3.2. Skin Exposure

- Moisture with oil residue and metallic dust will be in contact with your face:
  - Use **protective barrier creams** on exposed skin if your mask doesn’t cover all.
  - After exposure:
    - Cleanse thoroughly with gentle soap and water when you leave.
    - Watch for rashes or chemical burns from residues.

## 4. Tools and Gear: A “Pocket Workshop” for the Machine World

You’re not just visiting. You’re entering a body of machinery that has broken ends of metal, wire, and flowing water. You must be able to interact with it without dying.

### 4.1. Lighting and Navigation

- **Primary headlamp**:
  - Waterproof.
  - Wide and flood beam for general area.
  - Bright: minimum 500–1000 lumens.
  - Bring spare headlamp bulbs/housing.
- **Secondary handheld light**:
  - Narrow beam, high throw to spot distant machine shapes or floor gaps.
- **Extra batteries**:
  - More than you think you need.
  - Wrapped in waterproof containers.
- **Glow markers or chem lights**:
  - To mark path, dangerous floor sections, and safe handholds.
- **Notepad and pencil**:
  - For sketching maps of the floor grid, safe routes, and dangerous voids.
  - Pencil works when wet; paper needs waterproofing or protection inside a sealed pouch.

### 4.2. Environmental Tools

- **Compact umbrella**:
  - Not for rain; but to redirect water dripping from above in targeted areas.
- **Groundsheet / small tarp**:
  - To kneel or sit on when you must work on the floor grid.
- **Water bottle**:
  - Do not drink any water in this environment without treating it.
- **Mechanical watch / waterproof chronometer**:
  - To track time; you’ll lose time sense in the machine-body.

### 4.3. Safety Tools

- **Non-contact voltage detector**:
  - To sense if exposed metal parts or wires are energized before you touch them.
- **Compact multimeter** if you know how to use it:
  - To gauge if something is just live or highly dangerous.
- **Rubber insulating gloves**:
  - For cautious interaction with metal panels, wires, or handles.
- **Safety line and harness**:
  - If you can manage:
    - Climbing harness or secured full-body harness.
    - A strong **dynamic rope** (used in climbing).
- **Anchor points**:
  - Short lengths of rope and slings.
- **Carabiners and pulleys**:
  - To rig yourself when you cross over open voids or climb down near floor openings.

### 4.4. Mechanical Tools

- **Multi-tool** with:
  - Knife, screwdriver, pliers, wire cutters.
- **Compact wrench or socket set**:
  - To close, open, or secure access panels or pipes (if you need to interact with the machine).
- **Zip ties / hose clamps**:
  - To temporarily patch things you break or open.
- **Duct tape** and waterproof tape:
  - For temporary patches and marking surfaces.

### 4.5. Contact and Self-Rescue

- **Whistle**:
  - For constant sound over the machine noise.
- **Signal mirror** if light conditions ever allow it.
- **Basic first aid kit**:
  - Bandages, antiseptic wipes, splinting material.
  - Specific additions:
    - Eye wash solution for metal dust or splash.
    - Burn ointment for possible electrical arcs.
- **Space blanket / compact bivvy bag**:
  - To retain heat if you must stop longer than expected.

## 5. Survival and Self-Rescue Strategy

### 5.1. Before You Step Into the Machine

- Perform **final checks**:
  - Clothing waterproofed.
  - Headlamp and battery backup.
  - Harness and line correctly attached if you’re crossing any substantial voids.
- **Establish a clear rule:** if you become injured, wet, or exhausted to the point of shaking, you turn back immediately.

### 5.2. While You Are Inside

- **Always test footing before trusting weight**:
  - Floor plates are removed or unstable.
- **Avoid areas of intense water flow**:
  - Water can be heavy enough to physically sweep you into lower levels.
- **Be aware of overhead hazards**:
  - Cable “vines” of steel and copper can swing loose.
  - Dripping water can hide heavier drops of metal or debris.
- **Typical movement pattern**:
  - Move from **known-solid to known-solid**.
  - Stop at each machine “mountain” to rest:
    - Use the machine as a windbreak.
    - Lean on it only if it’s not obviously live or dangerously loose.
- **Never jump**:
  - You don’t know what is under the floor.
  - One impact might be enough to rupture your footing.

### 5.3. If You Fall Through a Floor Plate

- Your body **should already be tied to a line anchored to a solid structure**.
- If you slip:
  - Let yourself slide into the harness.
  - Do not panic flail; you might strike something else.
- **Rescue yourself** if you are conscious:
  - Use footholds on the machine structure below.
  - Ascend the rope using friction knots or a device if you have one.
- If you are not tied:
  - Avoid head impact.
  - Try to land on your feet and roll into an impact-dissipating posture.
  - If you end up in moving water:
    - Keep feet facing downstream if you’re swept into spaces.
    - Use anything for handholds to stop your motion.

### 5.4. Communication and Exit Plan

- **Tell someone** where you are going and when you expect to return.
- In this world, you can:
  - **Mark your path**:
    - Glow sticks or reflective tape on visible surfaces.
- Do not **discount your own need for rest and mental regulation** in the machine body:
  - Short stops to warm up.
  - Check hands and feet.
  - Check your own pulse to calm yourself if you start to panic.

## 6. Interacting with the Local Machine-Life

The “ecology” is not passive.

- **Plants**:
  - Dark stems and hard, leaf-teeth; likely hardened to resist corrosion.
- **Animals**:
  - Hard-shelled scuttlers from the water and gaps.
  - “Wings” that are membrane-like, possibly electrical or acoustic sensing membranes.

Given that you are traveling here rather than originating there:

### 6.1. Basic Rules of Interaction

- **Do not eat anything local** unless you have a way to test or are sure it is safe.
- **Avoid touching plants with bare skin**:
  - Their sap or coating may be irritant or worse.
- **Assume fauna are mostly defensive**:
  - Don’t corner them.
  - Don’t reach blindly into holes where you might be grabbing them instead of a grip.

### 6.2. When You Hear or See Things

- **No sudden aggressive movements**:
  - If you must defend yourself, use your tool handle rather than your bare hand.
- **Learn their sound signatures**:
  - The “machine bark” from the distance is likely some large part of the world-body interacting.
  - The local fauna scuttling is likely not targeting you; you are just noise to them.

## 7. Medical and Physiological Preparation

### 7.1. Conditioning Yourself Pre-Arrival

- **General aerobic endurance**: regular walks, jogging, or cycling.
- **Specific preparation**: practice with:
  - Full clothing system.
  - Harness and rope system.
- **Mental resilience**: work on breathing exercises under stress:
  - When startled, you want to go into controlled breathing, not panic.

### 7.2. Monitoring During the Visit

- If you feel:
  - **Numbness in your extremities** despite being covered:
    - Stop immediately and warm hands or feet.
  - **Confusion or light-headedness**:
    - Stop, secure yourself, and rest.
  - **Any wetness inside your clothing**:
    - Find a way to dry or replace the layer as soon as possible.

---

## 8. Leaving the Body Machine: Decontamination

When you exit the world-body:

- **Clothing**:
  - Wash separately, if possible.
  - Thoroughly clean and dry boots before reuse.
- **Body**:
  - Full wash in warm soapy water as soon as you can.
  - Pay attention to cuts or scrapes from rust edges.
- **Tools**:
  - Clean and inspect:
    - Blades for chips.
    - Rope for cuts or abrasions.
    - Illuminated gear for corrosion.

You will likely carry the smell of the machine-body with you for a time, no matter what you do. That is part of traveling.

---

## 9. Final Principles: Survival Mindset for This Environment

If you distill this down to a few rules a human traveler can remember:

1. **Assume every surface is wet and conductive until proven otherwise.**
2. **Never trust your full weight to a floor you haven’t tested.**
3. **Always have a way to be tied to something solid when crossing voids or climbing.**
4. **Stay dry inside your clothes; once wet inside, time becomes your enemy.**
5. **Do not touch “bare” metal with bare skin when you’re wet or when you hear strong humming.**
6. **Listen more than you look; the world’s body is speaking through its sounds.**

If you go into this world prepared with heavy layers, waterproofing, non-conductive tools, and a self-rescue mindset, then you can visit its inside. Otherwise you are just a fragile organism being pushed into the body of a sleeping machine. It may not mean you harm; but it will not mean you safe, either.
