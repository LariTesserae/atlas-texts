At the end of a disused spur tunnel, under the weight of the city, the Engine Room is less like a destination and more like a place you inherit. The air feels owned before you ever breathe it.

---

## 1. Before you go: understanding the basics of the place

### The kind of “room” you’re dealing with

You’re not visiting a ruin, or a museum, or even “infrastructure” in the usual sense of pipes and cables under a city.  
You’re visiting the inside of a body.

The Engine Room is not a space you happen to walk into; it is a container that does something. Its character is:

- **Deep:** It is below the city’s deep layer, not just “underneath” but genuinely submerged in the accumulated weight of everything above.
- **Hot:** The heat is not a sudden, furnace-like heat; it is a long, low boil that has been running for generations. Imagine somewhere already hot when you step in, and which stays that way.
- **Humid:** Moisture is recycled, not poured out or drained away. The air feels used.
- **Quiet:** Noise here is more pressure than sound—less rumble and more “you feel it before you hear it.”
- **Not dead:** Not quite alive or “haunted” in the ghost story sense, but more like a deeply settled routine. The Engine Room has been doing its work for so long it has become its own environment.

---

## 2. What you are going there as: “ordinary human multiverse traveler”

This matters because you are not entering:

- as a scheduled engineer,
- as a sanctioned inspector,
- as a local worker who knows the protocols.

You are going as:

- **Ordinary:** You don’t have specialized training in this world’s machinery or language.
- **Human:** You rely on your body’s limits, which are not adapted to this place.
- **Multiverse traveler:** You may have tools from elsewhere, but this does not make you invulnerable to heatstroke, metal burns, or bad air.

Your role is essentially **interloper + observer**. The machine was not made for you to stand near, or touch, or understand; its purpose is to serve the city it’s buried in.

Your key constraints are:

1. Your body cannot handle constant heat as well as metal can.
2. Your senses are not calibrated to read subtle signs of failure in this machine.
3. You don’t know how to behave around “things that have been running longer than you have been alive.”

---

## 3. Clothing and physical protection

### Basic layer: what to wear

You’ll want:

- **Long pants and long sleeves**, preferably in thin but sturdy fabric.  
  Avoid anything synthetic that melts easily against metal; you’re more likely to brush against hot or warm surfaces than to be engulfed in flames.
- **Closed boots** with thick soles, preferably with some insulation from heat, but also flexible enough to let you feel the ladder rungs and uneven floor.
- **Work gloves**, preferably leather or padded, but not too bulky (you’ll be climbing service ladders and feeling in the dark).
- A **lightweight layer you can strip off** if the heat becomes unbearable when you’re working or moving.

The room doesn’t radiate sudden flame-danger in the way you’d expect from a furnace, but simply **staying too long** is the bigger threat.

### Visibility and identity

- **Headlight** with a reliable battery, plus **backup batteries in a sealed container**.  
  The existing gas jets might exist or might be out of order; do not rely on them.
- **Reflective or visible tape** on your clothing or pack: so you can see yourself in dark spaces if you need to look back at yourself from a distance, or so someone else might spot you.
- **Something around your neck to ward off condensation** dripping directly from above: a thin scarf or hood that can be pulled up if you feel persistent dripping.

You don’t need to look like a professional; you need to be visible enough to yourself, and resilient enough to withstand damp heat and metallic surfaces.

---

## 4. Air and heat: what your body can tolerate

### The heat

This is a **persistent heat**, not “searing heat.” It’s like a long fever that has settled into the walls.

Expect:

- Sweat that doesn’t cool you much, because the air is already saturated.
- A feeling of pressure on your chest and head: you may feel like you’re being *slowly cooked* rather than violently overheated.
- Subtle dizziness and weariness if you stay too long.

Protect yourself by:

- Doing **short intervals**:
  - Go in with a plan: “I’m going to stay for 20–30 minutes at most, then retreat up the tunnel to cooler air.”
- **Hydration before you descend**: Have water in you before you enter the hot zone, so you don’t discover you’re thirsty while you’re already heat-loaded.
- **Bringing water** but not drinking it excessively in the Engine Room itself:
  - Sip small amounts to keep your mouth from feeling parched,
  - but save most drinking for when you retreat into the cooler tunnel.
- Monitoring **exhaustion**:
  - If you feel your thoughts becoming slightly thicker or harder to track, that’s time to leave.
  - If you feel nausea or headache coming on, don’t argue with it; leave.

Give yourself **“recovery halts”** outside the Engine Room itself, where the air moves a bit more and the heat is less profound.

### The air itself

The air has:

- Low airflow;  
- Collected moisture and **taste of metal, dust, and old heat**.

You don’t need to don a full mask, but:

- Bring **some sort of filter cloth or mask** you can wear over your nose and mouth:
  - It helps with dust,
  - and reduces the slightly metallic, oily tang you may inhale near brass and iron plating.
- Don’t assume the air is poisonous; but do **treat it as stale and deeply used**.  
  Your lungs are not accustomed to breathing air recycled through decades of machine cycles.

If you feel:

- Tightness or a sharp metallic sting,  
- or find you’re breathing harder than you should,

**retreat immediately**.

## 5. Light and visibility: what you’ll actually see

### Your lighting setup

The gas jets along the wall might be:

- Running,
- Lit but faint,
- Or out of service, leaving only dark, hollow sconces.

You cannot rely on them. Assume you’re on your own.

Suggested:

- **Head-mounted light:**  
  Allows you to have hands free while you move, grab, and climb.
- **Secondary handheld flashlight**:
  - To use if you need to brighten a specific surface without blinding your general view,
  - or to inspect bolts, gauges, or walls closely.
- **Optional: a small, cold-colored LED**:
  - Helps to see moisture and film details clearly.
  - But avoid very bright white; the contrast of surfaces here is already subtle.

### What the light will reveal first

The **engine**:

- It will not appear spectacularly huge in the sense of towering cathedral height;  
- but it will be **incompressibly dense**: a stack of modules and plates with valves and gauges protruding at strange angles.
- Many surfaces will be darkened from heat and age.
- Gauge glass will be faintly stained, ink turned to ghostly blot.

**Moisture and stains:**

- On walls and ceilings, you’ll notice:
  - faint, wet-looking shine where your headlight beam grazes the vault,
  - dark “finger-like” stains in the arches, where heat and cold tension have made the water remember.

**The floor and gutter:**

- A narrow gutter along the floor, carrying a thin pale stream of liquid:
  - It doesn’t look dramatic;  
  - it looks like the **veins of the machine**, rather than a river of the world.

## 6. Sound and vibration: what you’ll actually hear

The Engine Room does not roar.

The **sound profile** might be:

- A low, nearly subliminal hum:
  - felt more than heard,
  - especially in your skull bones and teeth, rather than your ears.
- Occasional, subtle **pressure changes**:
  - like a large valve shifting far off,
  - or something like a heartbeat you can’t quite confirm.

**Vibration:**

- The floor may not be visibly shaking,
  - but if you press your hands or your feet against the rail or the floor, you may feel a faint, steady thrum.
- This is not something dangerous in itself—  
  but it **helps you understand how the machine works**:
  - It is running,
  - but in its own way and on its own schedule.

---

## 7. Water and wetness

### The gutter stream

This is not water to drink.

- **Never drink from the gutter**, even if you see it clear and pale and moving:
  - It may be recycled process fluid,
  - distilled from steam condensation,
  - or even chemically treated to reduce corrosion.
- You don’t know what its purpose is;  
  assume you shouldn’t drink it.

The **purpose of the water** is likely either:

- Cooling some part of the machine we never see,  
- or carrying **energy or information** in the sense of flows the system understands.

If you see:

- Flakes of rust or a thin, oily sheen on the water,
- or hints of that strange weak-tea-colored algae on the walls nearby,

all that tells you is that **life isn’t afraid of this water**, but you should be.

---

## 8. Moving and positioning yourself around the engine

### Proximity rule

You don’t know how this machine fails.

If it has been running generations:

- It might never fail;
- Or it might fail in ways that are **slow and subtle and then suddenly irreversible**.

You, as traveler, should not:

- Adjust anything:  
  No turning of valves, no pressing of dials,  
  no twisting of bolts that might seem “stuck” but are actually: “sealed in place by design” or “holding back pressure.”

**Your principle:**  
You are not a repairman; you are a visitor inside a human body that doesn’t know you are visiting. Don’t start tugging on wires inside the rib cage.

### How to walk safely

- Watch the **ground**:
  - The floor might seem uneven;
  - expect hidden service steps, low lips in the rails, or shallow depressions defined by long polishing.
- Avoid stumbling directly into the machine:
  - Even brushing lightly against metal may **burn**, especially if you still think of metal surfaces as being cool at room temperature.
- Assume anything that is not **clearly floor** or **clearly untouched** is:
  - Possibly part of the engine system,
  - or a sensor,
  - or an unintended contact point between you and the machine.

## 9. Tools and small supplies to carry

You don’t need heavy kit, but some useful basics:

- **Small notebook and pencil:**
  - In the humidity, ink may smear,
  - but pencil is safer if you want to sketch diagrams or labels from the gauges and plates.
- **Cloth rags:**
  - To:
    - wipe sweat off your face,
    - dry your hands before touching metal,
    - check if a surface is wet using discoloration instead of skin contact.
- **Basic multitool or small knife**, but:
  - Only for use on your own equipment or minor safety problems,
  - not for “fixing” or “testing” the machine.
- **A thin insulated sheet or pad**:
  - To sit on if you decide to rest somewhere,
  - so you don’t draw heat from the stone into your body directly,
  - and you don’t sit on metal by accident.

## 10. Tending to your body and mind: how to leave safely

### Taking your time

The Engine Room cannot “decide” to hurt you;  
its processes are indifferent to you.

But you are still in **a place of load:**

- The heat,
- the humidity,
- the subtle pressure of sound,
- and the weight of being under the city

combine into a vague **emotional pressure** as well:

- You might feel:
  - like you are down inside some body cavity,
  - or intruding into a mind older than you are,
  - not hostile, but not aware that you exist.

**Countermeasures:**

- Pause **outside** the Engine Room itself:
  - Sit on the lowest steps of the motionless stairway,
  - or in the tunnel mouth where the air is slightly cooler.
- Take a few deep breaths,  
  sip some water,  
  and remind yourself that the machine does not care about you—  
  which is both terrifying and…neutral.

### Knowing when to leave

The biggest risk in such places is not something dramatic like explosion;  
it is **you losing sense of your own discomfort**.

Leave if you notice:

- Your sweat feels less like cooling and more like “skin leaking out”
- Your head feels “tight” or “thick”
- You notice you are staring longer than you mean to at the engine, without any clear consideration of time
- You feel like you have been there longer than you planned, but actually don’t know how long “that” is

Your **time limit** should be something like:

- First entry:
  - 10–20 minutes,
  - then physically retreat to the cooler tunnel.
- Second visit:
  - Maybe 30 minutes,
  - if you brought water and are reasonably sure about heat tolerance.
- Third visit:
  - Maybe longer again,
  - but only if you genuinely must.

“Must” might include:

- You need to sketch or record something,
- Or you are trying to understand the pattern of bolts or labels or rails that seem to suggest something **other than mere machine**—but still you don’t interfere.

## 11. Extraordinary precautions: if you feel “presence”

“Presence” here is not necessarily ghosts;  
it might be:

- The machine’s long duration of operation,
- The subtle hum of another city above,
- Your own mind reading too much into the dark, or just enough.

If you feel:

- Sensed and watched in a way that doesn’t feel like a human watcher,
  or
- The sense that “nothing should happen” but “something is happening anyway,”

then:

- Keep your **movements calm**,
- avoid startling noises,
- and **retreat without running**:
  - Running down in deep, heat-soaked tunnels leads to mistakes,  
    - falls,  
    - accidental burns,  
    - or getting lost.
- Back out slowly,  
  keeping your head towards the engine as you leave,
  so you can at least see what, if anything, might seem to change in your sightline,
  but don’t provoke it by yelling or testing.

“Respect” in this context doesn’t mean superstition;  
it means acknowledging **scale**:

- The machine has been running longer than you,
- It might be running long after you,
- Your interference is not necessarily better than its own steady decay.

---

## 12. Exit and return

When you decide to leave:

1. **Check yourself:**  
   Are you dripping sweat excessively?  
   Are your clothes soaked but you still feel hot?

2. **Make a deliberate last look:**  
   Take in one more glance to the highest arches of the vaulting,  
   to the dark stains where moisture collects,  
   to the engine where you cannot see where its machine ends and the world’s machine begins.

3. Walk slowly back along the tunnel,  
   keeping the gutter on your side as a landmark,  
   until the air starts to feel less like it is sitting on you and more like it is merely **around you**.

4. When you climb the ladder again:
   - Pause for a breath after each flight of rungs,
   - and let the heat uncurl from your body before you attempt to re-enter the world above.

From the surface, you will look like you’ve been **nowhere unusual**, except perhaps:

- A faint flush of heat,
- A smell of deep dust and metal,
- A faint dullness on your skin where you might have brushed what the machine has been touching for centuries.

But you have been inside something **older than you**, and smaller than the city it serves, and very nearly finished settling into its form.

---

In summary, as an ordinary human traveler:

- Prepare for **slow heat**, not sudden explosions;
- Bring **light**, **water**, and **restraint**, not tools and eagerness;
- See the Engine Room as **a body you’re visiting inside its ribs**,  
  not as a machine to fix or a puzzle to solve;  
- and leave when it feels like you’re becoming part of its environment,  
  rather than merely passing through.
