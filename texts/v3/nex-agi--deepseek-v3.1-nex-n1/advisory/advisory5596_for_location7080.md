To visit this place and walk out alive, you need to become **much more than a tourist** with a waterproof camera. You’re entering a sealed, underwater machine-room that has been left alone with heavy pressure and biology for centuries. The most dangerous parts are not dramatic monsters or rogue waves—they’re **pressure**, **microbes**, **heat**, and **time**.

---

## 1. Basics of the Place

Before you decide what to bring, you have to internalize what you’re walking into.

### Physical constraints

- **You’re under the ocean** but **not fully submerged**  
  - Chest- to neck-deep water.  
  - You are still breathing air, but the air is under a **ceiling of ocean** held above you like a solid lid.
- **No horizon, no wide sky**  
  - 100 m wide bowl-shaped chamber at most.
  - You can see its edges. The ceiling is low. You can feel the water above you.
- **No natural sun**  
  - Light comes from **bio‑films**, **filter-moss cushions**, and **microscopic floating glow**.  
  - It is always dim pre‑dawn, never day.

### Environmental rules

- **Pressure is everything**  
  It is constant, but heavy. The ocean ceiling above you is stable, but it will be *physically present* in your body, in your ears, your lungs.
- **Everything is warm**  
  The water is body‑temperature or slightly warmer. You will overheat easily if insulated.
- **Everything is alive**  
  Stone, metal, and water are coated with slow-growing organisms that do not show themselves dramatically but may interact with your skin.
- **Time here is slow but permanent**  
  Metal is softened by centuries of pressure. Nothing you do will change this place quickly—except *your* body.

## 2. Physical Health & Safety Limits

### A. Pressure and barotrauma

You are entering a **pressurized airspace** underneath a deep ocean ceiling. This is not just scuba diving; it’s like entering a **compression chamber**.

You need:

1. **Pressure equalization training**
   - Basic equalization for diving (Valsalva, etc.).  
   - Practice before arrival: airplane pressure changes, shallow pool session.
   - If you cannot clear pressure, your eardrums and sinuses are at risk.

2. **Medical check**
   - Lung function: asthma or chronic obstructive disease could make pressure changes dangerous.
   - ENT clearance: no active infections, barotrauma history.
   - For any world with serious pressure change, assume this trip is medically contraindicated for many people.

### B. Breathing and air quality

- **Air layer**: thin but breathable.
- **Humidity**: near 100%.  
- You will be exerting yourself in warm water, wearing protective gear, in nearly air‑saturated atmosphere.

Risks:

- **Heat exhaustion and dehydration**: You sweat heavily but you don’t notice it; you think you are just wet.
- **CO₂ buildup**: In a poorly ventilated gear mask or hood, you may rebreathe CO₂. Headaches, confusion, and panic creep in.

You need:

- Lightweight, **low-effort breathing gear**:
  - Full-face mask with:
    - **CO₂ scrubber** (if you can’t bring that tech, at least ensure high flow rate and fresh air source).
    - Short snorkel or just direct mask.  
  - Optional: portable air filter with small fan to slightly circulate the air layer you breathe in.
- **Hydration discipline**
  - Drink clear fluids before the trip and while you are exposed.  
  - Set timers to drink if you stay more than an hour.

### C. Infection and microbiology

This place is rich with **filter-moss**, **algae-grass**, **metal-moss**, and living films on metal and stone. You’re told there are “micro-lights” in the water. That’s a sign of **microbial life in monumental abundance**.

You are at risk of:

- Skin infections from open cuts or abrasions.
- Ear infections from water trapped in your ear canals.
- Eye infections if you get splashed and then rub your eyes.

You need:

- **Full-body skin barrier**
  - Thin but waterproof wetsuit or drysuit with integrated hood and boots.
  - No exposed gaps at cuffs, neck, or face.
- **Protective gloves**
  - Not heavy; thick enough to avoid cuts from hidden metal edges under moss.
- **Mouth, nose, and eye protection**
  - Mask or sealed goggles with anti-fog treatment.
  - Backup mask in case first one leaks or fogs.
- **Basic antiseptic**
  - Small spray bottle with disinfectant solution, safe for skin.
- **No contact if you are immunosuppressed**
  - If you’re on chemotherapy, strong steroids, or have advanced immunodeficiency, this place is a bad idea.

## 3. Gear and Equipment

### A. Core suit and protection

- **Suit**:
  - Shorty or thin full-length wetsuit/drysuit, depending on temperature comfort.
  - Synthetic material with no heavy drag. You will be walking chest-deep against water resistance.
- **Head protection**:
  - Lightweight helmet or cap:
    - Not heavy metal; but something to protect you from scraping the ceiling (water surface above) or hitting your head on metal ribs.
    - Absence of sharp rocks does not equal absence of collisions.
- **Buoyancy control**
  - Not a heavy dive BCD; a simple **float vest or pack** set to neutral or slightly positive buoyancy.
    - Helps you not stumble over unseen steps under the water.
    - Prevents you from accidentally submerging your face if you slip.

### B. Lighting and visibility

Natural light is **extremely** dim:

- You will be using **light** to see:
  - Soft headlamp (with red filter mode to avoid blasting the fragile bio‑films).
  - Handheld torch with adjustable focus.
- **Photography**
  - If you want to record:
    - Waterproof camera with macro and low-light modes.
    - No bright flash directly; use diffused or bounced light.
    - Think of this like visiting a deep reef: you want minimal disturbance.

### C. Tools

You will not hack open the metal blooms or rewire pipes. This is centuries-old industrial infrastructure under pressure. **Instead**:

- **Non-contact inspection**
  - Small infrared thermometer or simple temperature probe:
    - To check how warm the metal blooms are; know if they are actively hotter than the water.
    - Engage with them via touch only if you are thermally protected and the temperature is safe.
- **Mapping**
  - Simple slate and pencil or waterproof paper notebook.
    - You might want to sketch:
      - Shapes of metal ribs.
      - Spacing of the filter moss cushions.
      - Pattern of light from the films.
  - Maybe a simple depth sounder or range scanner if you have access to multiverse travel technology.
- **Sample-taking**
  - Optional:
    - Sterile vials for water samples.
    - Small forceps for lifting tiny fragments of metal-moss or film from **already** disturbed surfaces.
    - **But**: do not break clear, unbroken surfaces of mosses or films. This place is more stable than you imagine; do not introduce yourself as a hurricane of disruption.

## 4. Movement and Navigation in the Basin

### A. Walking

- You move in **water that is chest-high**.
- Each step you lift:
  - Your own weight.
  - Several kilograms of water.
- You will tire quickly, especially if you wear heavy gear.

Practices:

- **Move slowly**
  - Do not thrash. The water is smooth and your body creates turbulence.
- **Use deliberate steps**
  - Each step forward is also a step:
    - Over unknown **furrows of algae-grass**.
    - Around **filter moss cushions**.
    - Over metal service beds that may be slippery under film.
- **Keep one hand free**
  - For balance:
    - On walls.
    - On metal ribs.
    - On the central column if it is stable and not warm.

### B. Orientation and direction

There is **no obvious sky** direction. You will orient yourself by:

- The **open ocean side** versus the three **hydrocrete walls**.
- The central **metal column** with industrial efflorescence.
- The **entry ports**:
  - Four meters wide, six high.
  - They are squeezed by doors that have softened like wax.

If you feel disorientation:

- Stop moving.
- Note:
  - Where you last saw the black edge of deep ocean.
  - Where the faintest light source (from each filter-moss and film) seems to be brightest.
- Use your headlamp sparingly to re-check fixed features.

## 5. Environmental Sensations and Psychological Readiness

### A. What you will feel

- **Pressure** everywhere:
  - In your chest.
  - In your ears whenever you cross small depth changes.
  - On the top of your head knowing you’re under a literal ocean roof.
- **Temperature**:
  - Warm enough that you can be mostly comfortable.
  - Dangerously undifferentiated: you don’t “feel cold,” so you don’t notice how much you are exerting yourself.
- **Sound**:
  - Mostly absent:
    - You hear your own breathing.
    - Your heartbeat, when you are still.
    - Faint pops from water pressure settling on metal ribs.
    - Low percussion from machinery below in your bones, not ears.

### B. Psychological mindframe

You will experience **claustrophobia** as a density, not panic:

- Your body passes through multiple *layers*:
  - Air.
  - Water.
  - Moss.
  - Metal.
  - Stone.
  - Light.
- Your eyes are constantly *occupied*:
  - Nowhere is empty.
  - No clean restful darkness.

Preparation:

- **Expect to feel watched**
  - Not by monsters, but by the place:
    - Eyes of tiny pores in the filter moss.
    - Microscopic lights in the water.
- **Expect silence**
  - If you are used to city noise, you might be unsettled to hear your own heartbeat clearly.
- **Expect a slow pace**
  - You cannot move quickly.
  - You cannot “cover ground” like a tourist in a park.
  - You must allow time to absorb, instead of trying to rush through.

You might train:

- Meditation practice:
  - To sit quietly in shallow water.
  - To accept the feeling of gentle pressure.

## 6. Biological and Ecological Interactions

### A. Living organisms

This ecosystem is **settled**, not aggressive, but it is **not sterile**.

1. **Algae-grass**
   - Deep black-green fibers that stream like hair.
   - Ensure your suit has no loose loops that they can wrap around.
     - If you tangle:
       - Do not thrash.
       - Slowly free yourself with your hands.
   - Barefoot walking here is not recommended:
     - You will not see sharp edges under the algae; they may be present but mostly rounded.
     - But you *will* expose your skin to microbial living water.

2. **Filter moss**
   - Translucent cushions with pores opening and closing.
   - They are not predators:
     - They do not bite.
     - They are not poisonous to touch.
   - But:
     - Their surface may contain biofilm microorganisms.
     - Avoid touching if you have open cuts on your hands.
     - If you must touch, use gloves.
   - Do not step on them, if possible:
     - They are part of a long-established structure.
     - Stepping might break older colonies that have been in place for decades.

3. **Luminous films and organisms**
   - These provide minimal light.
   - Do not scrub or wipe them off surfaces:
     - You will not make things brighter by doing so.
     - You will just damage them and make yourself feel watched more heavily by your own guilt.

4. **Micro-lights in the water**
   - You are moving in a field of tiny particles that are part of the environment.
   - If you sweep your torch or headlight across:
     - They will brighten temporarily, then fade.
   - Avoid directing intense light into them:
     - They may be sensitive; or worse, you might blind yourself to subtle background light.

5. **Translucent eels and ghost shrimp**
   - They appear rarely:
     - Eel from a maintenance well.
     - Ghost shrimp on the metal ribs, with yellow stomachs.
   - They are **not** threatening.
   - You are:
     - Vast and heavy.
     - They are fragile and transparent.
   - Your interaction should be:
     - No contact.
     - No attempt to catch or poke.
     - Allow them to treat you as immobile structure.

### B. Microbes and long-term risks

Back home you might:

- Experience delayed skin or airway reactions if your suit leaked:
  - Rashes from exposure to “unknown algae.”
  - Minor sinus irritation from breathing ultra-humid air with high particulate count.
- **Medical protocol**
  - After the trip:
    - Rinse yourself thoroughly with clean water.
    - Shower immediately after removing your suit.
    - Monitor for skin reaction.
    - If symptoms show (redness, drainage, persistent cough), see medical care.

## 7. Interaction with the Built Environment

### A. Metal blooms and ribs

- These are **centuries-old** machine parts:
  - Softened by pressure and water.
  - Warm to the touch.
  - Faint thrumming from below.

Rules:

- **Do not attempt to break or open** anything.
  - They are part of a pressure-balance you cannot understand.
  - Collapse here would be catastrophic:
    - The ocean ceiling might give.
    - You might be trapped or crushed.
- **Touch lightly**
  - If you want to know what they feel like:
    - Use gloves.
    - Rest your hand; do not grip or push.
- **Observe color**
  - If you see the metal-moss around columns change color (blue to black) over hours:
    - Note that it is temperature dependent.
    - Do not use your body heat to try to change that color artificially.

### B. Entry ports and half-melted doors

- These doors have been deformed by pressure and time:
  - They look like wax tongues.
- **Do not wedge yourself** into tight spaces between:
  - They may behave like plastic under stress:
    - They can rebound slowly or move without warning.
- **Do not try to open or force them**:
  - You do not know what pressure system they are part of.
  - If you are not from this multiverse’s engineering tradition:
    - Everything you try to “fix” or “open” might misalign.

## 8. Time and Long-Term Exposure

This is not a place for **long stays**.

You may want to visit in **short sessions**.

### Suggested limit for first visit:

- **30 minutes** maximum.
  - Warm water.
  - Full humidity.
  - Pressure.
  - Slow movement.
  - All of these combine to fatigue you faster than you expect.

If you feel even:

- Mild dizziness.
- Slight headache.
- Thick-headedness.
- Tingling in your fingers.

### Treatment protocol:

- Move out of the water:
  - If you came from a surface access point.
- Sit down and breathe dry, less humid air.
- Hydrate.
- Rest.
- **Do not** force multiple repeated visits in the same day.

## 9. Clothing and What To Wear (Underneath)

Assuming you have access to waterproof gear:

**Layer 0 (underwear)**

- Synthetic thin underlayer:
  - Quick-drying.
  - Reduces chafing from the suit.

- **No thick thermal padding**:
  - Water is already body temperature.
  - Your main issue is **not cold**, it’s **heat and humidity**.

### 11. What It’s Like

- You are small in a room that was not built for humans but repurposed by time.
- Your body is surrounded by water and air and structures and lights that have been here longer than you, and will remain long after.
- Your main job is not to change this place, but to **pass through carefully**.

The drowned cathedral is not cruel or angry.

It is **patient**.

It will wait, whether you are slow or not.

So you must be slow enough to be worthy of the waiting.
