If you’re going to visit the Sink, understand one thing first:

It’s not a dangerous place in the usual sense.  
There’s no cliff edge to stumble off, no predators to bite you, no fire or ice to burn or freeze you.  
But it’s *very* dangerous to your attention. If you’re not intentional, you can lose yourself without even noticing.

Think of preparation as three layers:

1. **Physical** – what you bring with your body  
2. **Technical** – what you bring with your tools  
3. **Psychological** – what you bring with your mind

Below is a practical guide, from arrival to exit, as if you were planning an expedition.

---

## 1. Before you even decide to go

### 1.1. Who *not* to be

Some travelers shouldn’t go near the Sink at all:

- Anyone deeply claustrophobic (you’re in a tight warm chamber with no visible exit)
- Anyone prone to panic in absolute darkness
- Anyone currently fragile emotionally (this place will *not* reassure you)
- Anyone who relies on external change to feel time passing  
  (if days without sunsets, clocks, or routines make you dissociate, this can be unusually bad)

### 1.2. Why you might want to go anyway

Go to the Sink if you’re seeking:

- A place of **quiet** where you can actually hear your own thoughts
- A kind of **geological womb** where you can feel time being slow
- A place that’s **not indifferent in a hostile way**, but indifferent like a mountain that doesn’t care if you’re there or not
- A space that’s *almost* a room but not yet built — good if you’re trying to understand what it means “before” decisions are made

### 1.3. How long to stay

Your first visit should not be longer than **you can comfortably sit without needing a bathroom or food**.

- Good: a few minutes to an hour
- Risky but possible: one long night-cycle
- Don’t: camp here indefinitely like you would on a surface.

Why?  
Time *feels* unchanged here. Your body will still need water, food, sleep, movement — but you’ll lose track. That combination is dangerous.

## 2. Physical preparation

### 2.1. Clothing and protection

Assume the Sink is like a humid, warm body cavity:

**Temperature:**  
Constant warm, above your comfortable room temperature but not hot.  
You will not freeze. But you will sweat slowly and constantly.

**Recommendations:**

- Wear **light, breathable layers**  
  - Thin linen, cotton, or technical fabrics that breathe  
  - Sleeves and long pants to stop your skin feeling directly “read by the stone”
- A **thin outer layer** to protect from scrapes against the walls  
  - The walls are not sharp, but they are rough; you’ll be in close contact.
- **No heavy waterproof gear** unless you’re actually going to submerge.  
  - You’ll quickly poach in your own sweat.
- **Simple sandals or shoes with thin, flexible soles**  
  - The apron of stone is uneven;  
  - You need grip on wet rock, but you also want to feel the texture underfoot.
- **A headband or kerchief** to absorb sweat dripping into your eyes.

### 2.2. Light and vision

You *must not* rely on your eyes.

**Assume from the start:**  
You will see almost nothing. The water doesn’t reflect light properly. The walls are dark and close. Your visual brain will be deprived of work.

**Prepare by:**

- Bringing **multiple reliable light sources**:
  - A headlamp (hands-free)
  - A handheld lantern or torch
  - A backup light source smaller and sealed against water
- **Marking your light sources with tape or texture**  
  - So you can distinguish your own by touch alone.
- **Training yourself in advance** to:
  - Read your surroundings using touch, sound, and air pressure.
  - Practice moving around your room at home with eyes closed.

If you can, choose a light **color that you can easily identify as yours** —  
a blue-white glow, say, or something amber, so you don’t confuse the visual memory with your own mind later.

### 2.3. Heat and hydration

Your body will be deceived by the temperature:

- You may feel fine, but you are slowly losing water through evaporation and respiration.
- The humidity is so high you won’t *feel* dry sweat; you’ll just feel damp.

**Bring:**

- A water bottle that you can easily recognize by touch:
  - A distinct shape (not round like a stone)
  - Or texture on the surface
  - Or a strap attached so you never put it down *loose*
- If you plan to stay longer than half an hour,  
  **remind yourself out loud to drink at set intervals**  
  - Count breaths or heartbeats, or use a simple timer device in your pocket.
  - Don’t rely on thirst alone.

### 2.4. Basic medical supplies

- **Small, sealed first-aid kit**
  - Bandages for cuts (from stone tools or edges)
  - Disinfectant (if your world’s tech allows)
  - A cloth or tape for wrapping shallow wounds.
- **Asthma medication** if you have it (humid air can be heavy).
- Any medication you need regularly, **timed** clearly in your bag:
  - Label the container with a texture, so you can feel *which* pill you are taking in the dark.

## 3. Choosing tools and objects

### 3.1. What you need to bring *with* you

Assuming you’re traveling a multiverse and can carry a small kit:

1. **Light sources**
   - As above.
2. **A small container of water or hydrating drink**
   - One you will not misplace.
3. **A small dry towel**
   - For drying hands after touching the water or rock.
4. **A durable notebook and pencil**
   - Pencil because ink doesn’t like humidity;  
   - You will want a record that you existed *before* you lost the sense of your own history.
5. **One or two objects that clearly say *yourself* to you**
   - A ring, a token, a small stone from another world,
   - Something that, when you touch it, unmistakably belongs to *you*.

These are also called **anchor objects**.

### 3.2. What you should *not* bring

- No complex electronics or devices that can be damaged by humidity:
  - The water has *opinions*; you don’t want to rely on flickering screens.
- No small objects you can easily lose:
  - If it’s tiny enough to sit between folds of stone, assume you will lose it.
- No tools whose edge you can’t feel:
  - If you’re carrying sharp metal, make sure it has a very distinctive sheath.

### 3.3. Ropes and lines – marking your way

Because you might be moving in the dark with unreliable memory of turns:

- Bring a **thin cord or rope line**:
  - Tie one end securely at the last known “surface” point.
  - Use simple knots or markers you can feel with your fingers to distinguish:
    - “Away from the Sink” vs “toward the Sink”
    - Example: three knots in sequence = you are moving inwards.

This is not just about physical navigation; it’s about **psychology**.  
Knowing that a line behind you is *real* and leads to “where the world is still its usual self” can help when you feel dissociated.

## 4. How to behave once you’re there

Assume you arrive at the entrance of the Sink *from above* —  
following stone that you sense eventually leads to the sunlit world.

### 4.1. Entering

1. **At the threshold, stop and name your intention**
   - Say out loud:
     - “I will not stay long.”
     - “My purpose is to observe, not to disappear.”
   - This gives your mind a boundary.

2. **Secure your line**
   - Tie one end of your rope to something reliable:
     - A root, stone pillar, or anything clearly not part of the Sink itself.
   - Tie the other end around your waist or pack:
     - As long as you’re inside the Sink, you feel *tension* reminding you you can leave.

3. **Step down onto the apron of stone**
   - Be aware of:
     - The temperature difference  
     - The soundscape  
     - Your own breath.

### 4.2. Dealing with the stone floor

- The floor is not neatly flat. It is:
  - Uneven,
  - Curved,
  - “Auditioning” being a floor.

**Move like this:**

- Keep your feet low, sliding across rather than stepping high.
- Feel for texture:
  - The smoother, wetter surfaces may be closer to the water.
- Don’t rush:
  - The Sink rewards deliberation.

Sit at the edge of the water if you want, but:

- Keep your legs **crossed or pulled in**, so you can’t slide unconsciously.
- Place one hand on the floor to feel:
  - The warmth of the stone  
  - It runs like blood-warmth rather than sun-warmth.

### 4.3. Using the water without dissolving in it

The water is heavy, dark, and **not clear** in the usual sense:

- It doesn’t mirror you back.
- It doesn’t show you the bottom.

**If you touch it:**

- Test with fingertips:
  - Notice temperature,
  - Notice how your sensations spread.
- Avoid sudden plunges:
  - You don’t know where the bottom is,
  - You don’t know how your sense of self will behave in the presence of *very* opinionated water.

If you choose to let your hand stay longer in the water:

- Anchor your other hand on the stone,
- Keep reminding yourself:
  - “This is my hand, and this is water, and we are not the same thing.”

### 4.4. What not to do with the tools you find

You might find a **tool-like piece of stone** left by someone before:

- It’s not perfectly shaped.
- It might be a blade or something unfinished.

If you touch it:

- Feel the edge before testing:
  - The stone may be sharper than it looks.
- Avoid using it on the walls *unless* you’re ready:
  - You’ll be watched by the geology in the same way a patient watches your hand.
  - The stone yields not for you, but because it’s experimenting.

Better **not** to carve:

- Leave the Sink’s slow decisions to the Sink.
- Your carving will be gone in time, but it might mark you.

### 4.5. Dealing with the “fauna”

There might be almost-animals:

- So thin you can’t see them
- Maybe one or two individuals that don’t fear you because “change” hasn’t been decided yet.

If you sense movement:

- Don’t assume it’s your mind:
  - Pause.
  - Check if your objects are still where you placed them.
- If you’re not sure:
  - **Move your hands slowly and deliberately**
  - Feel whether your things are exactly where they should be.

Most likely, you *are* the fauna.

## 5. Psychological and timekeeping practices

This is as important as anything physical you bring.

### 5.1. Timekeeping in the absence of time

Because you cannot perceive changes in temperature or light:

- Bring **one simple, quiet timer device**:
  - Set to vibrate rather than ring.
- Decide *before* entering how long you will stay:
  - Example: 30 minutes or 1 hour
- When the timer vibrates:
  - You must leave, no matter what you feel.

If you don’t have a device:

- Try **counting breath**:
  - One cycle = 4 breaths.
  - Set a goal of 50 cycles.
- Or **pulse counts**:
  - Your heart rate is likely elevated.
  - Don’t panic if that rises slowly; you’re listening to *yourself*.

Remember:  
The Sink doesn’t change.  
*You* change.  
It’s easy to confuse “I feel unchanged” with “I should stay longer.”

### 5.2. Maintaining the sense of “self”

Because you will not matter very much here:

- You need **strong anchors** to remind you that you exist outside of this.

Useful practices:

1. **Speak your name out loud**
   - Only every once in a while.
   - Not to get the Sink’s attention; to get *your* attention.
   - If you’re superstitious about names in strange places, use a nickname or title instead.

2. **Touch your anchor objects**
   - Your ring, token, or stone from another world.
   - Remind yourself:
     > “I did not begin here.  
     > I have a home. I am going back to it.”

3. **Keep a physical journal**
   - Every time you feel a change in your thoughts:
     - Write down:
       - “I noticed [this].”
       - “Then [this] happened.”
   - This gives you a history of yourself inside a timeless space.

### 5.3. Handling the sense of “not being the center”

The Sink doesn’t hate you. It doesn’t love you.  
You’re a detail, not a plot.

To cope with this:

- **Practice humility before entering**
  - Accept that you are not going to be celebrated.
- **Notice what happens to your ego when no one and nothing cares**
  - Your mind might try to fill the silence by inventing stories:
    - That the water is listening.
    - That the stone is learning you.
  - These aren’t necessarily false; they are just not about *you*.

Stay **observational**:

- Say to yourself:
  > “This place is between being and not-being.  
  >   I am between staying and leaving.  
  >   That’s all.”

## 6. Staying safe in the “weird but not impossible”

There are two subtle weirdnesses to be aware of:

### 6.1. The air that doesn’t behave like “down”

In one corner of the sink:

- You might feel a faint **wet breeze**,
- As if that air is trying to be something else — maybe a doorway, maybe not.

You don’t need to investigate it.

- If you feel:
  - Your light flickering irregularly,
  - Or your hair or skin being pulled a little toward that corner,
- Then:
  - **Step back**.
  - Don’t lean closer.
  - Don’t ask it out loud.

You’re not there to become a doorway.

### 6.2. The layered feeling of the water

If you feel:

- That the water near your hand is not the same “water” as the water near your face,
- Or that you get *different* sensations of density or temperature,

Recognize:

- This might be the **Sink’s internal geology**,  
- Or it might be your mind without landmarks.

**Don’t decide:**

- You don’t need to know if it’s “physics” or “perception.”
- You need to know that you are safe and moving when you need to.

## 7. How to leave

The leaving is harder than you expect.

### 7.1. Why you might not want to leave

- The Sink doesn’t cling or beg.
- It’s **your own mind** that clings to the absence of pressure.

You might notice:

- “This is neutral. I’m not being judged.”
- “Outside, I’ll be pulled again by expectations.”
- “Here, I’m just…detail. That feels oddly peaceful.”

That’s part of the danger.

### 7.2. Practical steps for leaving

1. **Set your leaving before entering**
   - You decide:
     > “When the timer vibrates, or I reach [N] breaths,  
     >   I will leave, no matter what I feel.”

2. **When the time comes**
   - Stand up slowly.
   - Feel your body:
     - Notice stiffness.
     - Notice that *you* are the one who has been moving and time has been passing through *you*, not the Sink.

3. **Collect your tools**
   - If you brought:
     - Lights,
     - Water bottle,
     - Journal,
   - Check **one by one by touch**:
     - Don’t rely on memory.

4. **If you found a stone tool**
   - Return it to exactly where you found it:
     - As you remember, by feel.
   - Don’t leave it lying where you sat,
     - You might later accuse yourself of moving things.

5. **Follow your rope**
   - Hand over hand if you have to.
   - If you misremember the way:
     - The rope knows the path you deny.

6. **As you climb out**
   - Listen:
     - The dripping water doesn’t change its pattern behind you.
     - It doesn’t speed up or slow down to say goodbye.
   - That’s normal.

### 7.3. After you return

You will carry:

- A sense that **water has opinions**,
- A sense of **slow geology not caring about you**,
- And maybe a strange **peace** from knowing that you don’t matter to everything.

You might:

- Feel detached for a while.
- Find surface noise and human demands more irritating than usual.

**Ground yourself:**

- Touch sunlight, if your world has it.
- Eat something with strong flavor.
- Move your body in a way that clearly marks your presence:
  - Run,
  - Dance,
  - Climb something,
  - So that you cannot doubt you are still here.

Write in your journal:

- What you observed,
- How long you felt you were gone,
- How long you actually were gone.

You will almost always find:

- The time you felt inside was *not* the time measured on the outside.

---

## A simple checklist before you go

- [ ] Light sources (head + hand + backup)
- [ ] Water bottle you can recognize by touch
- [ ] Thin towel
- [ ] First-aid kit
- [ ] Timer / vibration alarm
- [ ] Notebook and pencil
- [ ] 1 or 2 anchor objects (recognize by touch)
- [ ] Rope or line for marking your way
- [ ] Clothing: thin, breathable, long sleeves and trousers
- [ ] Less shoes: flexible soles, good grip on wet stone
- [ ] A stated time limit and someone to report to when you return

And one **non-physical anchor** you can repeat to yourself:

> “I will go into the Sink,  
>   I will sit quietly,  
>   I will watch water being opinionated and stone being undecided,  
>   and then I will leave again  
>   because the world is still waiting for me to decide things.”

That’s enough.

---

If you do this carefully — if you prepare not just your body and tools but also your mind — the Sink can be a powerful place to visit.  
You will not leave unchanged, but you can leave safely, and with the strange gratitude that comes from having been welcomed by something that doesn’t particularly need you at all.
