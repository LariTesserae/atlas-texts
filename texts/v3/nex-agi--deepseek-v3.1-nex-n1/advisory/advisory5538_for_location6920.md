Welcome, traveler.

You’ve chosen — or been chosen by — a world-vessel of stone and light and absolute absence of water. Your body was not bred for this place, but multiverse travel tends to be bluntly democratic that way: you get to see vistas you were not designed for. This is not a “hostile” environment in the usual sense — there are no predators, no storms, no sharp edges — but it will kill you in ways that are subtle and almost gentle if you aren’t prepared.

Below is how you ready yourself for stepping inside a continent of hollow, glowing stone and staying alive long enough to understand what you’re seeing.

---

## 1. Understand the Core of the Problem

The key danger you’re dealing with is not monsters or fire. It’s:

- **Water scarcity to the point of nonexistence**
- **Everything that depends on water**
- **An environment that doesn’t understand your species’ biological needs at all**

Other dangers:
- **Accidental injury from falls or “gravity confusion”**
- **Time perception distortions**
- **Isolation, sensory overload / emptiness**
- **Potential interaction with unintelligent but very strange “fauna”**

Most of your preparation will be aimed at these.

## 2. Before the Jump: Equipment and Tech

### 2.1. Hydration and Water Recycling

This is your *top priority*.

- **Water supply**
  - Bring **enough water for the entire stay**, plus a margin. How long do you intend to stay? Double that.
  - Store it in:
    - **Flexible, collapsible containers** inside your backpack/rig
    - **Multiple independent containers**, so a leak in one doesn’t lose all of your water.
    - **Insulated** containers to minimize evaporation loss.
    - **Marked clearly** with volume and date of fill.

- **Recycling system**
  - A **compact atmospheric water condenser** is ideal if you have access to advanced tech:
    - It’s *not* going to pull water from the air of this world, because there is effectively none.
    - But it can:
      - Capture **all breath moisture**
      - Reclaim **sweat and urine vapor**
    - You may need to run it in a closed loop with your body, using a mask and suit vent.
  - If you can’t get that:
    - Bring **highest-end water filters and chemical treatment**
    - If you’re modestly advanced: a **micro-purifier** that can handle any urine reclamation you need to do in emergencies.
  - Consider **water rationing discipline**: set a strict daily consumption limit and track it.

- **Medical for dehydration**
  - **Oral rehydration salts** packets.
  - **Saline solution** for eyes and skin (if exposed).
  - **Basic electrolytes** in pill form.

### 2.2. Exposure and Heat Balance

Even though it feels cool “like autumn before dawn,” the lack of normal atmospheric convection and the weirdness of light that doesn’t heat like sunlight can make your body behave oddly:

- **Clothing**
  - **Insulated, full-coverage suit**:
    - Jacket and trousers or a full environmental suit.
    - Material:
      - Good wind resistance if there are faint breezes.
      - Reflective outer layer to help manage any light that has subtle heat effects.
    - Inner layers:
      - **Moisture-wicking base layers** to help regulate sweat (which you want to minimize anyway).
    - Redundancy:
      - A spare set of inner layers in case something gets soiled or damaged.
    - **No cotton**; it loses insulation value if you sweat unexpectedly.

- **Temperature regulation**
  - Zippers or vents to **dump heat quickly** if you feel warmer than expected.
  - Small **personal thermometer** to monitor your body temperature.
  - Lightweight **thermal blanket** in emergency kit.

- **Extremities**
  - **Gloves**, not thin but not bulky, something you can still move with easily.
  - **Thin socks** plus insulated **boots**:
    - Thick soles for smooth surfaces.
    - Excellent **grip** on glass-like stone.

### 2.3. Breathing and Pressure

The air is thin and “sparse” but supports breath surprisingly well. Still:

- **Basic respiratory gear**
  - **Compact mask** or hood:
    - Not a full pressure suit (unless your tech allows it).
    - Able to regulate:
      - Incoming air flow
      - Moisture recycling
  - **Filters**:
    - Particle filters if you’re worried about crystalline dust.
    - Even if not, a filter for your exhaled air can help capture moisture for your condenser.

- **Monitoring**
  - **Pulse oximeter**:
    - Measures blood oxygen saturation.
    - Lets you notice if your body isn’t handling the air composition well.
    - **CO₂ monitor** if you’re in a partially enclosed suit or shelter.

### 2.4. Light and Vision Protection

The light is cold, doesn’t heat like sunlight, but comes from everywhere in unfamiliar colors. That can be disorienting and physiologically unsettling:

- **Eye protection**
  - **Tinted** or adjustable lenses:
    - Not necessarily dark sunglasses — but ones that:
      - Cut glare
      - Reduce color fatigue from unfamiliar spectral bands
    - Consider amber / rose tints to avoid “too blue” everything.
    - **Wraparound** to reduce peripheral visual glare.

- **Headwear**
  - **Cap or hood** with a brim to shield your eyes from “overhead” light (even if there’s no sky).
  - **Helmet** if you’re prone to head injuries or falls.

### 2.5. Navigation and Time-Keeping

The world is huge, smooth, and repetitive in large-scale features. It’s easy to lose orientation:

- **Navigation tools**
  - **Compass** might behave oddly with magnetic fields if any exist; but bring it anyway.
  - **Handheld GPS** or equivalent position device, if your tech allows:
    - But it’s *not* geocentric; this is a hollow world-vessel. You might want to define “up” as “away from the dark and toward the core light” and track from that.
  - **Simple mapping tools**:
    - Small notebook, pencil (not ink that might dry out).
    - Markers or stickers to tag your path on visible features:
      - Subtle, not damaging, but recognizable to you.

- **Time-keeping**
  - The light “breathes” and changes; you don’t have days and nights.
    - Bring **multiple watches** (one on your wrist, one in your pack).
    - Set them to your home timezone or a “mission clock” and **don’t reset them** based on local cues.
    - A watch with an **alarm and countdown timer** is useful:
      - Remind yourself to drink water.
      - Schedule rest breaks.
    - **Logs**:
      - Write time and “local event” if you notice changes in the light’s breathing pattern.  
      This is not just for science; it’s for your sanity — you need to know that time is still passing in your world.

## 3. Physical and Medical Preparation

### 3.1. Conditioning

Before jumping, you should:

- Get **comfortable walking long distances on smooth surfaces**:
  - Marble floors, polished tiles, etc.
- **Balance and coordination drills**
  - Simple practice on slightly unstable surfaces to help with moving on glass-like stone.
- **Core strength**:
  - Helps with adjusting to unusual gravity pulls and sudden “edge” awareness.

No intense specialization needed, but your body should be used to a lot of walking.

### 3.2. First-Aid and Medical Supplies

- **Standard first-aid kit**
  - Sterile bandages, gauze, antiseptic wipes.
  - Medical tape; strong, adhesive.
  - Small scissors, tweezers.
- **Extras for this environment**
  - **Eye drops**: saline or lubricant.
    - The air’s dryness and the light could irritate your eyes.
  - **Skin moisturizer / barrier cream**
    - Not to “replace” water, but to protect from over-drying and cracking.
    - **Lip balm** with high protection factor.
  - **Basic painkillers** and anti-inflammatory.
    - For headaches from light glare or minor strains from walking.
  - **Any prescription medications** in clearly labeled containers:
    - Bring more than you need for the trip length.

- **Emergency signaling**
  - Mirrors or LED lights — if someone from your home multiversal hub needs to find you.
    - Even though you’re on a world-vessel, you might want to be retrieved by your team.

## 4. Psychological and Cognitive Preparation

This is not dramatically dangerous but cognitively *very* strange. You need to anchor yourself:

### 4.1. Expectation Management

- Understand before you go:
  - There are **no visible seasons, no weather, no sunsets or sunrises**.
  - “Day” and “night” are not concepts.
  - **Immensity of the space** can push some people into quiet, existential panic or awe that can be disorienting.

- Prepare **mental anchors**:
  - A phrase, mantra, or image from home.
  - Specific tasks you give yourself:
    - “Map this terrace.”
    - “Observe one leaf’s movement over three minutes.”
    - “Draw three structures of stone.”

### 4.2. Routine

You’ll help your brain a lot by maintaining **simple routines**:

- Regular sleep/wake cycles by your watch:
  - 8 hours of sleep, even if it doesn’t get dark.
    - Use a **sleep mask** and foam earplugs.
    - Even in the light, your body and brain need this.
- **Meal and water schedules**
  - Even if you’re not eating “meals” in the usual sense, set time to eat something, drink water, and briefly journal your state.

### 4.3. Communication with the Outside (if you can)

If you have access to multiverse communication tech:

- **Agree on check-in times** with home base:
  - Every X hours, you send a short status:
    - Coordinates or position descriptor,
    - Health status,
    - Short description of what you’re seeing.
- This will not just be safety; it will be a sanity anchor.

## 5. On the Ground: How to Move and Where Not to Step

### 5.1. Entering the World

When you emerge, you’ll likely appear on a “terrace” or bulge of smooth stone.

- **First steps**
  - Take a moment to adjust:
    - Check breathing.
    - Check your balance.
    - Check how your eyes handle the light.
    - **Do not immediately sprint to the edge**.
  - Before moving away from your insertion point:
    - Mark it clearly but not obtrusively:
      - Subtle chalk symbol (if the stone will take it) or a small reflective marker.

- **Edge management**
  - The world is full of terraces and voids.
    - **Do not assume “edges” look like sharp cliffs like you know from Earth**.
    - Here the edges might be gradual smooth curves where the slope becomes too steep to walk back up.
  - Stay:
    - At least a body length away from edges you *can’t see over clearly*.
    - If you need to look:
      - Go down on your knees or belly and slide forward carefully.

- **Falling risk**
  - “Gravity pulls inward toward the stone itself.”  
    This might:
    - Make you feel “held” to the terrace.
    - But if you lose contact with the stone, you could fall *into emptiness* rather than down onto a “floor.”
    - Treat any “out” direction from the stone with extreme respect:
      - Don’t jump. Don’t run carelessly near edges.

### 5.2. Walking the Glass-Smooth Stone

- **Footwear**
  - Boots or shoes with:
    - Excellent grip on smooth surfaces.
    - **Flat, non-textured soles might be slippery here**.
    - Prefer ones with a pattern that gives some bite.

- **Movement style**
  - **Walk deliberately**:
    - Short steps, not long strides, especially when you notice changes in slope.
    - Avoid sudden running until you know how the surface behaves under your feet.
    - As you feel “no friction” with your feet, reassure yourself that this is normal:
      - It doesn’t mean you’ll slide uncontrollably — but you *should* be cautious.

- **Path planning**
  - Always:
    - Choose routes **along the stone**, not routes where you might end up having to cross any empty airspace.
    - If you see **“leaf-forests”** or crystal trees, approach them:
      - For shelter and reference points.
    - But **don’t walk over or damage the glowing vegetation**:
      - Don’t break leaves.
      - Don’t try to harvest them.
      - They’re part of the world’s “breathing” balance.

### 5.3. Shelter and Resting Places

Given the endlessness, you’ll want to create “home bases” rather than wander aimlessly:

- **Choose shelters**
  - Under an overhang or at the base of a large tree-like pillar.
    - Not necessarily “from rain” but:
      - From mental glare and to create a sense of boundary.
    - Use **lightweight tarps** or fabric stretched as:
      - A visual barrier from the constant openness of the world.
    - Not to “wall” yourself in fully but:
      - To give your eyes and mind an occasional break from endlessness.

- **Sleep arrangements**
  - **Sleeping pad or thin mat**:
    - On the smooth stone your body will lose heat to the surface, even if the air doesn’t feel cold.
    - Insulation from below is important.
    - **Sleeping bag** appropriate to the temperature you feel with:
    - Adjust it for venting if you feel unexpectedly warm.

- **Lights and darkness**
  - The world never “darkens” fully:
    - Bring a small **personal light source**.
    - Even if you don’t “need” it to see:
      - It helps your brain feel “night.”
    - Use it to create:
      - A small circle of more familiar color around your sleeping area.

## 6. Interacting with the World’s “Life”

### 6.1. The Vegetation That Is Not Plants

The “leaves” and “tree columns” are not plants you can understand, but they are clearly sensitive:

- **Do not harvest**
  - Do not break off leaves.
  - Do not scrape the surfaces or carve on them.
    - The stone is brittle-looking but might be more durable than you think — still, don’t test.
- **Observation**
  - You can:
    - Sit nearby.
    - Learn their patterns.
    - Notice how they curve slowly.
    - Notice how their glow faintly shifts over time.

- **Don’t assume “food”**
  - Even if they glow like they might be energy:
    - **Do not eat or lick them**.
    - They’re not built for your body and your body is not built for them.

### 6.2. The “Fauna” That Are Not Fauna

The flat, thin shadows that glide across the stone surfaces:

- **Guidelines**
  - **Do not touch**
    - Maintain distance of at least a meter or more.
    - They don’t show interest in you, but that doesn’t mean they’re safe.
  - **Don’t block their path**
    - If they come toward you:
      - Step calmly to the side and let them pass.
  - **Do not try to interact**
    - No waving at them, no throwing things at them.
    - They’re not animals you can understand; treat them like dangerous phenomena you might be studying.

- **Observation**
  - Notice:
    - How their motion over the leaves changes the light pressure.
    - How their shape “wants” to be shadowless but still blocks some faint light.
  - These might be clues to how the world-vessel works.

## 7. Understanding the “Weather” and Time-Behavior

### 7.1. Light Patterns and “Breathing”

You might notice:

- Slow **dimming and brightening** of bands of light in the stone walls.
- Shifting quality of light:
  - Sometimes the silver-blue might dominate.
  - Other times the faint violet or yellow.

- **How to live with this**
  - Don’t treat it as “sun setting” or “sun rising.”
  - Treat it as background:
    - Note it in your journal as environmental changes.
    - But **don’t recalibrate your sleep schedule to match**.
    - Keep your own schedule based on your watch.

### 7.2. Sound of the World

The faint crystalline ringing of stone and occasional distant sighs:

- **Use ear protection for sleep**
  - Silence plus faint ringing can be hypnotically disturbing or soothing.
    - Some earplugs that reduce the very faint background noise but still allow you to hear if something else happens is ideal.
- **If you feel the “music” affecting your mood deeply**
  - Take a break:
    - Listen to music or recorded sounds from your own world.
    - Give yourself an auditory reminder you are not native to this place.

## 8. When You Decide to Leave — or Misstep

### 8.1. Return Plan

Before you step into this world:

- Know exactly:
  - **How you will return**
    - Is there a physical device you can carry?
    - Is there a preset time after which you’re pulled out?
    - Does someone else open the way from outside?
- **Always keep**
  - A device or token for this:
    - Secured firmly on your body (not just loose in your bag).
    - Protected from damage.
    - If it’s a remote device, ensure it:
      - Has backup power.
      - Is protected from shock and moisture.

### 8.2. If You Fall “Outward”

You’re described as not falling when you look over the edge, but gravity pulls toward the stone. If you somehow lose contact:

- **Don’t flail wildly**
  - The world’s “rules” are not like yours:
    - You may not be able to swim in air.
    - But you might still be bound to the stone’s gravity field in strange ways.
- **Carry a compact safety device if possible**
  - A small **personal breathing device** if there is any thin air at all.
  - A **paraglider or controlled-fabric** device:
    - Not to “fly” but:
      - To give yourself a chance of drifting back toward the stone surface instead of falling into emptiness.

- **Signal for help**
  - If you have emergency beacon technology:
    - Make sure it’s configured to transmit location.

## 10. Attitude and Final Thoughts

Lastly, how you approach this place decides a lot:

### 10.1. Respect the Balance

This world has managed without water for eons longer than you have been breathing.

- **You don’t need to change it.**
  - Don’t try to “fix” the dryness.
  - Don’t try to “terraform” or “improve” anything.
- **You are a guest in a body that doesn’t belong here.**
  - Act like an observer who:
    - Takes nothing,
    - Leaves nothing,
    - Touches only carefully and thoughtfully.

### 10.2. Stay Humble About the Silence

You might feel:
- Tiny
- Insignificant
- Overwhelmed by the scale and the “breathing light”

Let that feeling exist:
- Don’t panic into “action” to feel like you’re doing “something.”
- Sometimes the best thing to do:
  - Sit quietly,
  - Watch the stone breathe,
  - Notice the leaf movement,
  - And acknowledge that you are not at the center of the story here.

### 10.3. Know Your Limits

Before you step into the world-vessel, decide:

- How long you will stay:
  - One day? A week? A month?
- How you’ll know if you’re reaching your edge:
  - Signs:
    - Persistent dizziness,
    - Headaches from light,
    - Increasingly disturbing sense of time loss,
    - Difficulty remembering to eat or drink.
- If that happens:
  - **Leave sooner rather than later.**

### The Short Version of All This

If you want a very condensed version:

- Bring enough water to live entirely without local water.  
- Protect your body from dryness and light, with full-coverage clothing and eye protection.  
- Use a watch to hold time steady in your head; do not trust the world’s “breathing” to set your schedule.  
- Be careful near edges—the world’s “gravity” may not play like Earth’s.  
- Don’t touch, eat or walk on the glowing vegetation; it’s not for you.  
- Maintain a strict routine of sleep, food, and water.  
- Respect the silence and scale; don’t try to dominate the place.  
- Keep a clear exit plan and a device to return home.

This world-vessel does not need you.  
But it may tolerate your presence for a while if you arrive carefully, quietly, and prepared to watch more than act.
