You’re not going to be “comfortable” in Khe’len; you’re going to be alive *by arrangement* with the place and its people. If you treat it like a tourism destination with a theme (“fun glass desert garden”), you will either damage it or break yourself. This is not hyperbole. The city is designed around a single, fragile night‑work of climate and glass.

Assume you have access to a modest multiverse travel kit and common sense. You’re an ordinary human traveler, not a superhero.

---

## 0. Get your head straight before you go

### 0.1. This is not a desert of open space; it’s a desert of water.

Khe’len is not sand dunes and camel caravans. It’s a sealed bowl of stone, where people have built a relationship with *near-absence* of liquid water. The “glass jungle” below the city is their only reservoir, and their city‑mothers are meteorologists of a glass‑and‑air ecosystem.

Your instincts are wrong:
- You do not “drink” when you feel thirsty.
- You do not wash your hands.
- You do not splash your face.
- You do not open your skin to the sun.

If you can’t adjust to that psychologically before you even arrive, you will not last.

### 0.2. You are going to be a body that excretes.

Everywhere you come from, you’re used to being *wet*. You sweat, you urinate, you exhale vapor. In Khe’len, **your fluids are serious pollution**.

You will be taking moisture from their system and not giving it back in any useful way. That means:
- You must minimize *everything* that comes out of you.
- You must not expect anything like a “bathroom.”
- You must accept that you will be dirty, dry-skinned, and probably smell bad.

The city-mothers will tolerate this if you are careful and respectful. If you’re careless, you become a moving problem.

## 1. Before you go: physical and medical preparation

### 1.1. Hydration protocols

You *must not arrive dehydrated*. This sounds obvious but is critical:
- Hydrate well in the weeks before travel.
- Start the day of travel with balanced electrolytes: not just water, but salts and minerals.
- If you have a medical condition that affects hydration or salt balance (heart, kidney, diabetes, etc.), you should not go to Khe’len unless you have excellent support.

### 1.2. Fasting and diet

Consider:
- Traveling with a relatively clean gut: avoid foods that produce heavy gas, diarrhea, or constipation.
- A short pre‑fast so your body is less toxic and waste-producing.
- Bring your own food supplements that minimize need for water—e.g., nutrient-dense bars that don’t require cooking (if you can eat them).

You will probably eat very little while there. The locals don’t “eat” or drink in the way you’re used to; your food needs will be treated as a curiosity and an inconvenience.

### 1.3. Skin and membranes

Your skin is the primary interface with moisture. You want to *preserve* its moisture, not needlessly expose it.

- Use a barrier cream: a thick, unscented ointment that helps keep what moisture you have in your skin.
- Bring lip balm with a good sealant.
- Bring eye drops or saline gel if you wear contact lenses or have sensitive eyes.
- Avoid any lotions that contain mostly alcohol or evaporate quickly; you want to lock moisture in, not lose it.

### 1.4. Medications

If you take medications with:
- Diuretic effects (“water pills”)
- Anti‑histamines that cause dry mouth
- Any drugs that interfere with heat regulation or sweat

You need to talk to a medical expert about your trip to Khe’len. These medicines can be dangerous in a place where heat is at night and you are deliberately suppressing moisture loss.

Also:
- Oral rehydration salts (in packets) if your world has them, but you will be *very* cautious about how you use them. You cannot just “have a drink” whenever you feel like it.
- Any basic medicines you might need (for headaches, mild infections, etc.), in forms that don’t require much water.

## 2. Clothing and gear

### 2.1. Day: you want to be invisible and cool but *covered*

**Day in the bowl is cooler than night, but still hot.**  
You will be inside rock houses during the heat of the day while the stone soaks sun, and then you move at night when the stone radiates that heat out.

For clothing:
- Long, loose, light layers of breathable fabric in pale or matte colors (no shiny reflective surfaces that could confuse the city-mothers’ signals).
- Full sleeves.
- Long trousers or loose skirt.
- Light head covering that can be pulled over your face.
- Closed footwear with thin soles but good coverage—no open sandals that expose your soles to hot stone or glass surfaces.

**No bright colors or anything flashy.**  
You are entering a place where every movement is visible and consequential. You don’t want to be “the neon person on the terrace.”

### 2.2. Night: you expect heat *inverted*

The night is hotter, but more active. You will:
- Keep your skin covered to protect against moisture loss and possible contact with the present-glass.
- Still wear long sleeves and trous. The fabric should be such that if you accidentally brush against a column of the present, you do not get skin contact directly.

### 2.3. Gear you must bring

Minimal, but key:
- **Dark-lens goggles or glasses:** the city-mothers read gestures by night. You’ll need to protect your eyes from heat shimmer and avoid blinding others with any light you might carry.
- **No bright light sources:** no headlamps, flashlights, phone screens, etc., unless they are fully shielded and *only* used for an absolute emergency.
- If you must have light, use:
  - A single small, shaded lantern or an enclosed chemical light.
  - Only inside the guesthouse or rock chamber, never in the basin or near the city-mothers’ work.
- **A thin cloth mask or scarf:**
  - To filter dust if it gets kicked up.
  - To capture your exhaled moisture if the city-mothers have a custom around collecting such breath (do not assume this; you follow their lead).
- **A small, tightly closing vessel:**
  - For your own urine if local guidance requires you to collect it. You may be asked to store it in a sealed container in the guesthouse until you leave, rather than letting it evaporate into their basin.
- **Basic toilet kit (if any):**
  - Dry or low-water toothbrush, if you must.
  - No soap, unless it’s a no-rinse sanitizer in a form they don’t mind.
- **Notebooks in stone-safe or cloth cases:** if you write, use things that do not shred or crumble, and expect ink to behave strangely if night humidity briefly rises.

### 2.4. What *not* to bring

- Perfumes, scented oils, or anything heavily aromatic. You will be close to people who read the air with extreme precision.
- Any kind of electronic device that beeps, buzzes, or emits light.
- Anything that drips, leaks, or can be squirted.
- Metal objects that might clink or flash and be interpreted as signals from the rim.
- Any cutting tool: the city-mothers’ glass knives are their own craft and ritual. You bringing your own blades would be deeply inappropriate.

## 3. Getting into position: the rim and guesthouses

### 3.1. Approaching from the plateau

As you near the city:
- You will likely see the bowl first from the rim, where the stone falls away into a deep indigo basin.
- The city appears as faint terraces and doorways in the rock walls.

Respect this:
- Do not run toward the edge like any tourist would.
- Stay away from the rim edge at night if you are not invited to it.
- If you are feverish, clumsy, or in any way impaired—stop *before* the edge.

### 3.2. The outer guesthouse ring

The city offers you hospitality *outwards*, not inward:
- The guesthouses are at the top of the basin, looking outward, not inward.
- They likely have stone roofs and minimal openings.
- Expect small, cool chambers carved back into the rock.

Do not:
- Demand an “ocean view” of the basin.
- Try to “get closer” to the glass garden because you want to “see it better.”

### 3.3. Meeting with the city-mothers

Before you do *anything* else, you must be hosted by one of the city-mothers or someone they delegate to guest relations.

You will be:
- Told where you may sleep.
- Informed where you may walk.
- Explicitly instructed where you may *not* go (especially at night).
- Given their rules about your body fluids and noise.

Ask for:
- Clarity on what to do if you feel faint (where they want you to be, if movement is allowed).
- How they prefer you dispose of any waste you cannot help.
- Whether they have any protocols for off-worlders like you.

Speak clearly, but briefly:
- Their speech at night consists of short signals.
- They are not chatty.
- Never waste words.

## 4. Heat and moisture: your body’s new physics

### 4.1. The day

During the day:
- It is cooler than night, but still warm.
- The stone soaks heat from the sun.
- The city is quiet, most people inside.

For you:
- Stay inside your assigned rock chamber.
- Lie against the cool stone floor or wall if your host says it is permitted.
- Avoid any unnecessary movement or talking.
- Rest. This is when you recover your strength for the night.

### 4.2. The night

Night comes:
- The stone radiates the day’s absorbed heat.
- The basin grows hotter, but more active.
- The city-mothers go down to work among the present-glass.

You:
- Will be allowed only into specified viewing positions, if any.
- Might be permitted to stand on the terrace of your guesthouse or another house, but only if you are absolutely silent and do not distract.

Respect this:
- If you get too close to the city-mothers while they are cutting the glass columns, you could:
  - Ruin their air-pressure reading.
  - Confuse their signals.
  - Interfere with the decades of work they’ve inscribed into stone maps.

### 4.3. Thirst and heat management

You will feel:
- Thirsty often.
- Skin dry, tight.
- Eyelids heavy.
- Head possibly light if you’re not careful.

What you must not do:
- Drink during your visit if given water-like anything, unless you are explicitly instructed by them.
- Assume anything you’re given is “for swallowing.”
- Use your own water supply unless it’s a clear emergency.

What you can do:
- Rest frequently.
- Breathe slowly and shallowly to conserve moisture.
- Keep your mouth closed if possible, nasal breathing if you can.
- Use your lip balm or a small cloth to cover your mouth if you feel yourself gasping—but not in a way that looks like you’re signaling.

If you start to feel:
- Dizziness
- Confusion
- Very strong headache or nausea
- Racing heart

you must inform your city-mother host or whoever instructed you, *immediately*. They know the local body better than you do.

## 5. Movement and behavior: how to be in the city

### 5.1. Noise and silence

Khe’len’s “voice” is the present-glass breathing and the city-mothers’ signals:
- No unnecessary speech.
- No singing.
- No humming.
- No hasty footsteps or clanging.

You:
- Move slowly and deliberately.
- Keep your breathing as quiet as you can.
- If you must speak, do it in the shortest, clearest phrases possible, during allowed times.
- At night, especially during the cutting-work, you remain silent unless signaled to answer.

### 5.2. Space and boundaries

- You are a guest, *not* a participant in the night-work.
- Do not treat the present-glass as a theme park installation:
  - Do not touch.
  - Do not break bits.
  - Do not try to “collect a sample.”
  - Do not step on anything unless your path is explicitly allowed by your host city-mother.

- Respect the stone maps:
  - If you see any stone tablet with carved patterns, you do not:
    - Trace your fingers over it.
    - Write anything on top of it.
    - Move anything.
    - Take a picture with anything that might throw light (unless you explicitly design it to be zero-light and they agree).

The city-mothers’ relationship with erosion and stone maps is their whole library. You are not touching a library book.

### 5.3. Waste and contamination

You will most likely be:
- Using some form of sealed container for your personal urine and night soil.
- Using some cloth or absorbent material you can seal tightly.
- Either leaving these containers sealed in your guesthouse when you leave or handing them off to your city-mother host if they have a method of dealing with them.

You must not:
- Relieve yourself anywhere that might be visible to anyone or near anything the present-glass might use.
- Allow anything to evaporate into open air intentionally.
- Discard any cloth or tissue unsealed.

Every drop of moisture you let out into their air is *theirs*, by right of survival and craft. You are not entitled to squander it.

## 6. Seeing the present-glass without destroying it

### 6.1. What you may observe from afar

- The glass speakers:
  - Translucent pillars of dark stained glass-like tissue.
  - Subtle swelling of invisible air sacs along their sides.
- The sound:
  - A soft, continuous hum of thousands of glass sacs expanding and contracting.
  - Faint whistles and pressure shifts when the city-mothers work their cuts.

Treat the sound as you would treat a language you do not speak:
- You do not interrupt.
- You do not mock or imitate.
- You do not try to “duplicate” it (unless explicitly given permission to record and use *no* light while doing so).

### 6.2. Boundaries and the “don’t touch” rule

Absolute:
- Never touch the glass speakers, nodules, or threads.
- If a city-mother allows you to approach closer, you walk only where they indicate.
- If you are allowed to “lean” against something, you do not assume that means “press” or “test its strength.”

The present-glass is:
- Lush without water.
- Part of a climate engineering system.
- A living organism that *hollows stone*.

You don’t know:
- Whether its surface can be broken by your skin.
- Whether its internal structure can be disrupted by your body moisture.
- Whether its sap or anything it might excrete is harmful to you.

Strictly avoid:
- Trying to “slice” your own bit of glass.
- Thinking “it’s just glass” and breaking anything “because it’s cool.”
- Picking at nodules or “sleepers.”

If you damage something:
- You may not see the immediate result.
- You may have set in motion changes that the city-mothers will detect through years of observation.

## 7. Emotional and sensory expectations

### 7.1. The sensory experience

- **Visual:**  
  - Pale stone walls, terraces, indentations.
  - Indigo basin floor with glass pillars and tangled glass-veins.
  - At night, possibly a faint film of moisture on certain surfaces.
  - Stars sharp and “like broken glass” above.

- **Sound:**  
  - The hum of the present-glass.
  - City-mothers’ short calls.
  - Hand-signal clicks from stone above (maybe).
  - Your own breath and footsteps if you’re not careful.

- **Smell:**  
  - Chalk and baked stone, dust older than you.
  - Metallic aftertaste of lightning.
  - Possibly a faint sweetness if any present-glass exudes anything when cut.

### 7.2. Psychological discomforts you should accept

- You will feel dirty for most of your stay.
- You will feel *dry* in a way that is not trivial.
- You will be surrounded by a “water” that’s not water—the promise of moisture hanging everywhere but almost never landing where you can touch it.
- You will be a drain on their system, and you must accept that feeling of imbalance.

This is not a place for “centering yourself” or “finding healing in water.” The city-mothers may be centered; but they are centered by a *very* different relationship to moisture than you’re used to.

## 8. Leaving and aftercare

### 8.1. Departure from the basin

When it’s time to leave:
- Take any sealed containers you were using for waste with you if possible.
- If they have a method for collecting the moisture you’ve stored to you, they may tell you what to do with it.
- Thank your city-mother host clearly, briefly, and without any grand display.

Arrange:
- To return any borrowed items exactly.
- To ask if you may share any observations you made with others outside their world:
  - They may say no.
  - If they say yes, you must strictly follow any constraints about what you show, describe, or sketch.

### 8.2. Physical recovery after leaving

Once you’re back in a world with water:
- Rehydrate carefully: not a huge dump of water at once, but steady intake over hours.
- Watch your body:
  - Headaches
  - Skin rashes
  - Any lingering signs of heat stress or dehydration.

If you feel:
- Unusual fatigue or confusion after you return home  
- Long-term skin or breathing issues

you should consult a medical professional who understands your travel history.

### 8.3. Integration of the experience

You may have just:
- Visited a place where no rivers ever flow.
- Seen night hotter than day.
- Watched people who “drink thirst” instead of water.

This is a slow-burning experience:
- Don’t try to explain it to everyone you know.
- Don’t try to “replicate the garden” in your own reality.
- Let the contrast of moisture-dense worlds you now return to feel like *luxury* you should not waste.

## 9. Bottom line: how an ordinary traveler should prepare to visit Khe’len

1. You **prepare your body**:
   - Hydration and electrolytes before you go.
   - Minimal moisture output during.
   - Skin care, eye care, medications for your conditions if you have any.
   - Clothes that cover you, keep quiet, and don’t reflect.

2. You **leave your habits of comfort and cleanliness**:
   - No baths, no showers, no rinsing.
   - Possibly no drinking water at all.
   - Acceptance of being dusty and dry.

3. You **respect the work and the ecosystem**:
   - Accept that you will not understand the maps of erosion and speakers fully.
   - Do not attempt to “help” or “participate” unless you’re explicitly trained.
   - Do not touch, cut, collect, or break anything.

4. You **stay within the bounds they set**:
   - Guesthouse and allowed terrace, only.
   - No noise during night-work.
   - No light unless you’re permitted and shielded.
   - No casual wandering into the basin.

Khe’len is not about you. It is about the city learning to drink thirst, and about glass that breathes in the dark. If you go there prepared to be a *listener*, a *body that carefully controls its own moisture*, and a *quiet guest at the edge of their night-work*, you may be allowed to stand at the rim and hear the city of Khe’len go down on its knees and drink—from air that never gives you a river, but just enough.
