Traveling to the Column of Still is not about visiting a city;  
it’s about visiting a theorem. Everything there is complete, solved, and still.  
The danger is not so much chaos as it is being treated by the world like an extra decimal place that doesn’t belong.

### At a high level: What kind of place are you entering?

- You are going into a fully engineered reality: the hull and surrounding water are not separate; they are one system.
- Pressure is not a brute force crushing inward; it is *managed* by the structure so that gradients are spread out over huge volumes.
- Temperature is nearly uniform. Heat is not allowed to accumulate.
- Time is almost erased as an agent of change. The Column looks “young” because erosion and decay have been mathematically diluted out of existence.
- There is no visible life as you know it. No fish, no algae, no bioluminescence.
- The interior architecture is sparse, “urban” only by analogy: structured but mostly empty.
- Technology is beyond the physics of your world and operates on the edges of what makes matter matter.

Plan your visit around this: **you are extremely fragile, and the world is extremely indifferent**.

## 1. Know what you’re heading into

### Physical position & “point of entry”

From the description, you are not going to a seafloor or land surface. You will be:

- At midwater in a world-ocean;  
  “sky” above is ocean; “floor” below is unimaginably deep.
- Approaching a cylinder ~3 km long, 0.5 km in diameter, suspended in the abyss.
- The “door” is not a literal doorframe. There may be no visible hatches.  
  The hull is faintly porous everywhere: micro-openings you can’t quite see.

Think of your arrival less like docking at a port and more like approaching a single vertebra of a machine-god.

## 2. Character & threats (non-physical)

The main threats are not monsters but:

- **Hopelessness**: The place feels “solved”. There is no obvious invitation, only completion.
- **Disorientation**: No “streets”, no obvious human ergonomic logic.
- **Isolation**: Dead silence, eternal twilight, no visible society.
- **Cognitive strangeness**: The visible architecture is a “language” you can’t read, but your brain will want to.

You’ll want a strong pre-agreed “mission statement” for being there:
- Why are you going?
- What are you not allowed to do?
- At what point do you retreat?
- If you change, how do your companions know you’ve changed?

Write this down, physically, on something you carry with you.

## 3. Your body vs. the water

### Pressure & hull interaction

- Pressure outside is likely crushing *everywhere*.
- The hull spreads pressure gradients out over huge volumes so that they don’t rip the city apart.
- As you move near the hull, you may feel:  
  - A sense of being “smoothed” and compressed, but not pain.  
  - As if your body is being gently stretched or rearranged to match the water’s uniformity.

What you need to manage:

- **Always touch the hull or one of its devices only through your pressure suit / tool.**  
  Even if it feels benign, your cells are not engineered to the same standard.
- Assume **internally compensating pressure armor** as a baseline:
  - Maintains internal pressure to match the external load.
  - Includes distributed force fields or exoskeleton webbing to prevent your organs being sheared around by uneven pressure.
  - Has full-body redundancy: if one segment fails, others re-balance pressure so you do not implode.

### Temperature & uniformity

- Temperature is cool but uniform and “distributed” evenly throughout the volume.
- Your armored suit must:
  - **Spread heat around your body** to avoid hot spots or cold spots.  
  - Use internal heat exchangers, not external heaters, because you’re not allowed sudden “vents of warmth” into the water.
  - Be capable of running on extremely low power output so you do not introduce a noticeable thermal anomaly.

### Oxygen & waste handling

- Assume the water’s chemistry is not oxygen-rich in the way you expect.
- Your suit must be fully closed:
  - **No open water intake for breathing**, unless you know exactly how to process “alien water” into breathable gas.
  - Integrate a low-signature rebreather or closed-loop life support system.  
  - Waste handling needs to be chemically neutralized rather than expelled into the water.

## 4. Moving & navigation

### Outside approach

If you arrive via multiverse travel at some distance from the Column:

- Approach **slowly** along the axis, rather than straight at the cylinder wall.  
  This reduces the chance of crossing unseen “boundary” structures.
- Avoid sudden accelerations or changes in direction;  
  the water has been engineered to avoid motion—you don’t want to be an anomaly.
- Use **very low thrust** propulsion:  
  - Magnets interacting with a local field,  
  - or very low-force neutral buoyancy adjustment.

### Visibility & light

The “light” you will see:

- Outside: almost none.  
  “0.4/3 of visible light” means it is extremely dark, but not absolute void.
- From hull blisters: “absence of darkness” rather than light—areas of less-than-expected opacity.
- Interior “light”: it is described as a pressure of visibility on the mind rather than photons.  
  You’ll likely “see” the interior through your mind’s sense of space and form, not just retinal images.

Tools needed:

- **Multispectral imaging system**:
  - Works in near-zero visible light (passive imaging, sensitive lenses).
  - Includes thermal imager (though you might not detect obvious heat).
- **LIDAR / structural scanner**:
  - To map the hull’s microgeometry where you cannot see clearly.
  - To detect boundaries between “ordinary” water and engineered “machine–water zones”.

### Navigation inside the Column

Inside, you’ll face:

- No “streets” or corridors.
- Chamber-like ribs and voids “domestically-small” in scale (like a cathedral or neighborhood).
- Spatial geometry that tells you “this is for waiting,” “this is for receiving,” but in a non-human language.

Prepare:

- A **fine-grained inertial navigation system** tied to your suit:
  - Track your position relative to the centerline of the cylinder.
  - Maintains a map of “where you are allowed to be” vs. “where you should not go”.
- **Wall tethers**:
  - Thin, strong fiber lines that can attach to the hull surface or internal structures.
  - Let you move along surfaces without drifting unpredictably.
- **3D mapping software**:
  - Builds a real-time map of your own path, so you can reverse out exactly.
  - Marks “zones of subtle strangeness” where conditions change slightly (temperature/pressure/water-density).

## 5. Interfaces & tools

### How to interact with the world

The “machines” here are not machine-shops; they are regions where the hull behaves differently.

- **Minimal physical contact:**
  - Use handheld tools for any contact with surfaces.  
  - Prefer non-contact sensors: electromagnetic, field, and pressure/viscosity probes.
- **Instrumented gloves:**  
  - If you must touch, your gloves should be:
    - Insulated from direct temperature transfer.
    - Equipped with tiny sensors to measure changes in water properties, surface texture, and field effects.
- **Tagging system:**
  - If the multiverse travel tech allows, you can leave small “anchor tags” at safe points:
    - Not physical damage, but harmless markers that give you a way back to entry points.
    - If the Column doesn’t accept them, they simply disappear or are rejected; you do not force.

### Communicating with home / team

The environment will be hostile to traditional signals:

- No electromagnetic chatter to be expected.
- Water has engineered stability and likely damps waves.

Needs:

- **Tight-beam communication**:
  - Acoustic or field-based tight-beam, to pulsing stations or buoys you left in the surrounding ocean.
- **Physical data cartridges**:
  - If you can upload data to “anchors” you send out of the Column, this is safer than broadcasting signals.
- **Redundancy in communication protocols**:
  - Assume you can’t easily “yell for help”.  
  - Plan your mission so that if you miss check-ins at preset times, your team outside triggers extraction or rescue protocols.

## 6. Mental health & self-preservation

### The strangeness & dead completeness

You will feel:

- An “absence of invitation” rather than hostility.
- A sense that everything needing to be done has been done, and you are redundant.
- A heavy stillness of thought; time feels like a thin film of dust on a surface that doesn’t change.

Countermeasures:

- **Pre-trip psychological training**:
  - Familiarization with “completed worlds” simulations.
  - Practice in maintaining identity under conditions of extreme quiet and “solvedness”.
- **Structured routine inside**:
  - Maintain a schedule: “now I map this chamber, then I rest, then I return.”
  - Even in the nothingness of events, having *intentional* tasks is critical.
- **Emergency anchor phrases**
  - Examples:
    - “I am not from this ocean. I belong somewhere else.”
    - “This is complete for them, not for me. I am incomplete, and that is okay.”
  - You, or your team, can loop these as audio/text cues in your HUD.

### Monitoring for changes

The environment may rearrange your body or perception gently:

- You may feel “length and contracting” under pressure, but not pain.
- Physical sensations might feel “rearranged” to match uniform water.

Mitigation:

- **Continuous biometrics**:
  - Track body temperature, heart rhythm, neural patterns (if your tech allows).
  - Sudden flattening of emotion or bodily sensation might be an indicator the environment is treating you as part of its field.
- **Backup self-model**:
  - Keep a record of your normal “baseline” self:  
    - Heartbeat range, stress patterns, language patterns.  
  - If you significantly deviate, your team can treat it as a warning.

## 7. Logistics & consumables

Given you’re a human traveler in a world that doesn’t need food, air, or change:

### Assume:

- No food sources, no water you can safely drink, no air pockets.
- No friendly lifeforms to help you.
- No machinery “vents” you can exploit.

### Required supplies:

- **Life-support reserves**:
  - Enough air and water recycling for the full planned duration *plus* 50% margin.
  - Fully self-contained power sources for your armor, life support, navigation tools, and communications.
- **Repairs and self-maintenance**:
  - Sealant, patches, and components for your suit.
  - Tools to repair your own instruments if something fails.
  - No assumption of help from the Column or anything living there.

### Travel protocol & exit strategy

Plan before arrival:

1. Designate a **fixed extraction point**:
   - Nearby coordinate or volume away from the hull (where multiverse transfer can happen safely).
2. Set a **maximum time inside**:
   - Beyond which even if you are functioning “normally”, you must leave.
3. Define **triggers for immediate withdrawal**:
   - Loss of internal sensor feed from your body.
   - Blacking out of your map.
   - Loss of more than one redundancy layer in your pressure suit.
   - Any change in the environment you cannot interpret (tiny “current” appearing, new “blisters” forming).

## 8. Ethical and metaphysical safety

Because the Column is also law: geometry as physics, architecture as world:

- **Do not try to alter** the architecture:
  - No drilling, no cutting, no energetic assault.
  - The Column might not respond like you expect; you might break yourself rather than it.
- **Treat “void” areas as forbidden, not empty**:
  - The sparse chambers between structural ribs may be important fields of stored “possibility,” or machine regions.
- **Avoid the drifting “failed filaments”**:
  - They may be fragments of the self-repair system of the Column.
  - Disturbing them may be like tampering with a healing process.

Philosophically:

- Do not assume you deserve to be here.
- Do not assume you must be excluded.
- The place is complete; you are transient. Your job is observation, not “improvement”.

---

## In summary: how you should prepare

- Accept you are entering a world-state that has already solved most of the problems your world is still struggling with.
- Arm yourself:
  - **Physically**: with pressure-armored, self-heated, closed life-support, capable of moving in very low light and very still water.
  - **Cognitively and socially**: with clear missions, scheduled check-ins, mental anchors, and structured self-identity.
- **Move very gently**, touch the smallest thing possible, and **always preserve a way out**.

The Column of Still is not going to hurt you out of malice; it might simply not notice you.  
Your job is not to conquer or fix it, but to visit it without being dissolved by its completeness—and then to return, with your mind and body still intact enough to tell the story.
