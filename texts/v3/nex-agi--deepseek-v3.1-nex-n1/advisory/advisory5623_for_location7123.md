To visit the valley that will become “Where the Stones Weep and the City Sleeps,” you need to think of yourself as a very fragile body in a very strong landscape. Nothing here is malicious or actively hostile to humans, but the world itself is raw: rock edges are sharp, air is thin, water is insistent, and cold is slow and patient. Your main task is to arrive without breaking your body, without disturbing the fragile balance of the city, and without leaving too much of yourself—your noise, your waste, your technology—imprinted on the place.

Below is a detailed preparation guide, from what to bring in your pack to how to behave once you’re there.

---

## 1. Physical and Medical Prep

### 1.1. Altitude and Cold

You are going to a high mountain bowl just above the tree line, where the air is thin and cold year‑round.

- **Expect altitude sickness:**  
  - Even if you’re reasonably fit, you’re likely to feel:
    - Shortness of breath on small inclines
    - Mild headaches
    - Fatigue, especially in the first day or two
    - Tiredness even after a full night’s sleep
  - The description says “the air is thin and cold enough to sting your cheeks” and the heat is scraped off the sunlight before it reaches you. That’s a high, cold, clear place.

- **Ascend slowly:**  
  - If you can stage your arrival, do not go directly from low altitude to 8,000–12,000 ft (3,000–4,000 m is what you should expect above tree line).  
  - Spend at least one night at an intermediate altitude.  
  - Once you’re near the valley, give yourself a rest day before moving heavily.

- **Hydration and nutrition:**  
  - Drink more water than you think you need.  
  - Eat regularly even if altitude kills your appetite.  
  - Avoid heavy alcohol at least for the first days.

- **Cold protection:** compact, layered clothes:
  - Base layer: thermal, moisture‑wicking fabric (synthetic or merino wool).
  - Mid layer: fleece or similar insulation.
  - Outer shell: windproof and waterproof.
  - Hat, gloves, scarf or buff, and socks that stay warm when damp.
  - Even if it’s only cold, not freezing, humidity makes wind chill feel worse than the temperature suggests.

### 1.2. Feet and Mobility

You’ll be walking on uneven ground, sharp rocks, and damp surfaces:

- **Sturdy, waterproof hiking boots** with ankle support.
- **Gaiters** to keep mud and water out of your boots in the marsh and reed edges.
- Camp sandals or water shoes for crossing small wet patches or wading shallow edges.

Pace yourself: the description says “from the city to the lowest edge of the cliffs where the stone begins to rise more steeply” takes about half a day’s walk. That’s a serious hike.

### 1.3. First Aid

You’re hours to days from any modern emergency services:

- Basic first‑aid kit:
  - Bandages, gauze, medical tape.
  - Antiseptic wipes or solution.
  - Blister treatment (moleskin, tape).
  - Painkillers (for altitude headaches etc.).
  - Any personal medications with extra margin.
- Tweezers and small scissors for dealing with splinters, etc., from stone or wood.
- Sun protection cream and lip balm with high SPF:
  - At altitude, UV exposure is intense despite the cold.

### 1.4. Immunity and Microbes

This is an early‑metal‑age community with no modern sanitation. You do not share their bacterial ecosystem:

- Be prepared for mild GI upset even if you’re careful.
- Avoid drinking untreated water directly:
  - Use some filtration method (cloth filter plus heating or purification tablets), if you can manage without drawing attention.
  - Avoid eating raw food straight from the lake or marsh unless you can cook or thoroughly wash it in filtered water.
- Don’t impose modern hygiene standards visibly on the locals; adapt discreetly.

## 2. Gear and What You Should Bring

### 2.1. The Question of Technology

This is a key constraint: the city is built with stone hammers, wooden mallets, bronze knives and awls, and stone tools. They are experimenting with fire and water to crack rock, with simple wooden water wheels and grindstones.

You must not appear with powerful metal tools, advanced electronics, or visible devices that they cannot interpret.

- **Allowed / low‑key items**
  - Stitched fabric clothing that can look plausibly woven, if somewhat strange.
  - Simple containers:
    - Cloth bags or leather satchels.
    - Small, wooden or horn cups.
    - Woven containers (if you can make or find them).
    - No visible plastic.
  - A knife that:
    - Is not made of stainless steel or anything too obviously advanced.
    - Has no plastic handle.
    - Ideally, looks like simple bronze or iron (if your world’s travelers are limited by that, but the text says “bronze knives and awls” are present).
    - If you can, replace modern handle materials with wood or leather wrapped handle.
  - Flint and steel is safer than a modern lighter: if you can, bring a small metal strike and flint rather than plastic butane lighters.

- **Highly discouraged items**
  - Smartphones, cameras, GPS.
    - If you must bring them (for safety or multiverse rules), keep them hidden at all times, never exposed in the city.
    - If anyone sees you manipulating them, they will be alarming religious objects, or dangerous tools.
    - The same applies for any kind of torch more than a candle.
    - The city’s residents are working with carved stone candle lamps and oil bowls; even modern flashlights would be visually jarring.
  - Syntactic materials:
    - No visible plastic bottles, synthetic fabrics, zippers, velcro, backpacks with many buckles and mesh.
    - Dress down your gear to be simple, rough, and monotone, if you can’t replace it with truly period materials.

### 2.2. Clothing and Appearance

You want to look like a traveler from a strange but plausible culture:

- **Natural colors and materials:**  
  - Wool, linen, leather, coarse canvas, if available.
  - Earth colors: brown, gray, dark green, muted colors.
- **Layer for the cold and damp:**
  - A wool cloak or heavy mantle helps with cold wind and humidity, and is visually normal.
  - At least one hood or hat that can be pulled down to keep your head warm and obscure your face from direct scrutiny.
- **Foot protection:**
  - If you can, replace modern synthetic hikers with leather boots or mukluks that can pass as “turned leather shoes” or “wrapped boots”.
  - Don’t show any rubber soles or molded patterns if they’re obviously modern.
- **Hair and body:**
  - Keep hair neat, tied or covered under cloth, if possible.
  - Avoid bright jewelry or very visible piercings/tattoos at least initially:
    - Tattoos might be interpreted in many ways—as sacred marks, danger signs, or sorcery.
    - You don’t yet know their meanings.

You’re trying to be forgettable rather than impressive: a worker, not a king; a messenger, not a miracle worker.

### 2.3. Documentation Tools (If You Need Records)

If you must document what you see:

- A small notebook of handmade paper or sewn paper, with charcoal pencil or pencil that can be hidden inside.
- Don’t appear to “write” constantly in public:  
  - People might associate you with magic marks or curses.
- If symbols are carved in stone around you and you see them, learn by copying (from memory) later rather than drawing them directly in front of locals.

## 3. Navigational and Arrival Strategy

### 3.1. Approaching the Valley

You’re entering a closed bowl with:
- A ring of mountain walls,
- A narrow western pass—still unstable, with rock slides.

Before you step into the valley proper:

- Observe from the rim:
  - Use the overlooks to see the city layout, the lake, marshes, stilt houses, and movement patterns.
  - Identify the main access paths from the pass to the city.

- Entering the pass:
  - The pass is “a wound still healing,” with rockslide chutes. This means:
    - Stay aware of the slope above you.
    - If you hear small rocks rolling or dust falling, move away quickly.
  - Go in daylight:
    - The light at dusk is longer and strange; at least with the sky up you can see falling rocks better.

### 3.2. First Contact

Your first arrival is likely to be noticed. The city is small; “you could stand at one end and still see the faces of people on the opposite side,” though there are not many faces. Sound travels clearly across the lake.

How to minimize threat:

- **Approach slowly and clearly, from a visible path:**  
  - Don’t appear suddenly out of thick fog or reeds.
- **Look approachable but not aggressive:**  
  - No drawn weapons.
  - Keep hands visible.
  - Bow slightly or nod when you get close.
- **Language:**
  - The text says they have a name that is “not yet fully written,” their language is short and concise.  
  - Expect short words, little chatter.
  - Use:
    - Simple gestures.
    - Basic mimetic communication:
      - Pointing to yourself, then to the mountain pass to indicate travel.
      - Placing a hand over your chest to signify “I am” / “I am a person”.
    - Learn key words very quickly:
      - “water”, “food”, “safe”, “help”, “thanks”, “sleep”.

- **Expect curiosity:**
  - You may look and dress differently from their local traders or neighbors.
  - They will likely:
    - Ask you where you come from (with gestures).
    - From your physical condition, they might infer:
      - That you have traveled far.
      - That you must be allowed or invited by some mountain spirit.

Be honest: don’t pretend to be a god, messenger of the stones, or anything beyond what you are.

## 4. Where and How to Stay

### 4.1. Seeking Shelter

The buildings are low, with stone walls and mud, wood roofs with slate shingles. You’ll likely not be allowed to immediately sleep inside someone’s main home unless you’re invited.

Better initial setup:

- **Camp outside the city proper but visible from the edge:**
  - Find a flat spot near the tree-line or groves of those pale, straight trees with dark, dense, blue‑green needles.
  - Make sure your fire:
    - Is small enough not to smoke heavily.
    - Does not damage the tree roots or dry grasses.
    - Respects the wind patterns that carry smoke directly into the city.

- **Accepting an invitation:**
  - If they offer you a roof:
    - Accept simple food and drink.
    - Show gratitude by helping:
      - Carrying water,
      - Helping repair loose thatch,
      - Assisting with small tasks around tools.
  - Their tools are stone, bronze, and wood:
    - Don’t show them better techniques unless you see clearly that they are experimenting.  
    - If you carve, you’ll learn quickly by watching their angle of strike, how they brace the stone.

### 4.2. Staying Dry in the Wetness

“The ground is damp. The stones weep.” The whole valley is moist:

- Bring or construct:
  - Ground cloth:
    - Woven or leather sheet if you can’t bring modern waterproofs.
  - Elevated bedding (if not given):
    - Sleep on raised platform or stones rather than directly on the soil.
  - Clothing:
    - At least one set of dry spare clothes.
    - Change out of wet clothes as soon as you’re in shelter.

- The roofs are slightly water harvesting:
  - Watch how they funnel rainwater into channels and hollow logs.
  - If you’re near a house, you might be offered to use water from their cisterns.
    - Always mirror the behavior of locals: if they wash in the marsh, do not refuse; but be careful to drink filtered water if you’re not used to their biome.

## 5. Day‑to‑Day Living in the Valley

### 5.1. Food and Water

Local food likely:

- Grains (ground by slow water wheel),
- Some fish from the lake,
- Possibly gathered roots from the marsh and mountain,
- Game meat (if they hunt the birds, small animals).

You:

- Eat what they offer you if you can reasonably stomach it.
- If your system can’t handle:
  - Very heavy grains or new proteins,
  - You might need to still take small portions but also supplement with your own food if you’ve brought:
    - Dried fruit,
    - Hard bread,
    - Nuts or seeds.

Water:

- They have:
  - Shallow stone basins,
  - Cisterns,
  - Roof channels.
- Use water:
  - From their cisterns if you are allowed.
  - Always filter or boil if you can.
- If your water treatment is hidden:
  - Do it quietly.
  - Never say “your water is unclean” or similar—even with gesture language.

### 5.2. Walking and Moving Around

The city:

- Narrow streets where “two people might have to turn sideways to pass if they carry loads.”
- Steps are unevenly carved.
- Suspended walkways over the marsh edge made of woven branches and grass rope.

Guidance:

- Walk carefully:
  - The suspended walkways are likely to sway:
    - Don’t rush.
    - Keep one hand on the rope or rail (if there is any).
    - Avoid carrying valuables when you walk on them.
- The valleys are quiet:
  - Every sound carries:
    - Voices, tools, laughter.
  - So:
    - Keep your voice low.
    - Don’t call too loudly from one side of the lake to the other—unless that’s a local norm.

### 5.3. Time and Rhythm

Their days and nights are subtly longer or shorter than “normal”:  

- “The days themselves are longer than they should be; or shorter.”  
- Twilight lingers longer in the evening.

You:

- Adapt to local light:
  - Sleep when they sleep,
  - Wake when they wake,
  - Don’t try to enforce your own home clock rhythm.
- Use the sun:
  - Their sun “hangs at the edge of the valley’s rim like a lantern,” high but not very warm.
  - Rely on sun position for time of day rather than precise hours.

## 6. The Strange Phenomena and How to Treat Them

The world is not modern Earth:

- **Sky and stars:**
  - Stars appear brighter, closer, and unfamiliar patterns.
  - Twilight extends longer than you’re used to.
  - If you know astronomy, you can’t map these constellations onto your sky.

- **Fog and mist:**
  - “Ribbons of mist that snake along the ground… like animals seeking water.”
  - They move from the marsh along the stilts of houses.
  - Don’t panic, but:
    - Avoid walking directly into thick ribbons of mist if you’re not sure where they go.
    - Let locals show you safe paths through known fog patches.

- **Light anomalies:**
  - On certain days when the sun hits at particular angles, the stone cliffs cast “faint shadows of the wrong color.”
  - There’s a pale green glow on edges of the cliffs.
  - The lake sometimes:
    - Reflects the sky inverted as if “an inverted world beneath the water’s surface where the reflections of the cliffs stand upright and the reflections of people seem to walk on clouds.”

- **How to behave:**
  - Don’t draw attention to the anomalies aggressively:
    - Don’t point, gasp, or make “magic” signs.
    - If they treat it casually, you treat it casually.
  - Treat them:
    - Like extreme weather in a familiar world,
    - Like the aurora borealis,
    - Or like unusual but predictable optical phenomena.

You might not be sure if these are physical illusions or genuinely reality‑softened moments. As a traveler, you should not meddle.

## 7. Cultural and Social Behavior

The city is young but built with long‑time thinking in mind:

- “The city feels built for the long future rather than the immediate present.”
- Buildings are unfinished:
  - Not out of neglect,
  - But for “future hands to pick up the work.”

You should internalize this mindset of patience and continuity:

- **Matching their pace**
  - Work slowly:
    - If you join stone shaping or wood gathering, do not show impatience.
  - Small repairs over time:
    - Notice the way they replace “a few shingles at a time” rather than all at once.
    - You will not be seen as useful if you are always demanding to do big tasks.

- **Respect for material and effort**
  - Metal is rare:
    - Bronze pins and awls are precious.
    - If you see metalwork, you show respect:
      - Don’t touch without permission.
      - Don’t show much better metal tools if you want to be treated as an equal traveler.

- **Sound and silence**
  - The city is “quiet” but sound:
    - The drip of water,
    - The stone tools,
    - The wind carrying voices across the lake.
  - Your voice:
    - Should be gentle and not often.
    - Laughing is not wrong but your loud laughter might feel “too big” for the space.

- **Approaching art and symbols**
  - They carve:
    - Simple patterns of waves, clouds, mountains on doors and shutters.
    - They are not yet confident geometric figure carvers.
  - If you see:
    - Repeated symbols near water channels,
    - On stone basins,
    - Above doorways,
    - Treat them as sacred:
      - Don’t mock them,
      - Don’t copy them directly in your own notebook in front of locals.

## 8. Emotional and Mental Preparation

The valley feels:

- Young but wise,
- Empty of people but full of presence:
  - Drips,
  - Stone whispering,
  - Bird calls,
  - Wind in reeds,
  - The city itself feels like a larger presence than the number of bodies suggests.

You might experience:

- **A sense of being watched**
  - Not by eyes, but by the landscape:
    - Mountain cliffs,
    - Lake surface,
    - Stone edges.
  - That’s normal and local. They live with this.

- **Slow time**
  - Days blend.
  - You might lose track of “progress” because you're not achieving many visible tasks.
  - Instead:
    - Observe patterns,
    - Learn small skills,
    - Listen.

- **Worth of smallness**
  - You’re not a big event here.
  - You’re a stone that might be added into their wall, or might be washed out again.
  - That’s freeing.

## 9. Leaving and Long‑Term Impact

When you’re ready to leave:

- Clean:
  - Remove any signs of your campfire,
  - Pack out trash (if you’ve any disposable),
  - Ensure you don’t leave behind plastic, synthetic, or other materials that can’t be explained.
- If you’ve been invited:
  - Say your thanks in the way they recognize:
    - Offer small help before leaving,
    - Show clear “I am going now” gesture.
- Don’t promise:
  - To return,
  - To bring more,
  - Unless you truly intend to.

The goal is not to change anything but to learn what this place is before the city really knows the weight of water or years. You want to observe the first wear of steps into stone, the first deep ruts in the streets, and not yet be seen as a creature that stopped those from happening.

---

### Very short checklist of essentials to carry into the valley:

- Warm layered clothes in natural colors.
- Waterproof outer layer and ground protection.
- Waterproof boots with good support.
- A small, simple knife of low‑tech appearance.
- Fire‑starting kit without obvious plastic.
- Basic hygiene and water treatment methods, hidden.
- Plain notebook and pencil, hidden.
- Patience and respect.

If you treat yourself as a temporary student of the stone and lake, and the people of the valley as your teachers—however young they are in their world—you will be able to visit this place safely and leave it unchanged by your presence, except in the memory of your own bones.
