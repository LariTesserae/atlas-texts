*This is not a vacation. This is not a place to test your limits. This is a young machine-city of geometry and equations that is still tuning itself, and you are walking into its open body. Think of yourself as stepping into the cleanroom of a reality compiler whose compiler warnings are written in topology and gravity. If you still choose to go after reading this, then your preparation must be obsessive and quiet. You want to be a footnote in the city’s math, not a fatal error log.*  

---

## 0. What you need to assume before you even start packing

Treat these as article-of-faith safety rules. They underpin everything below:

- **Time is not your friend.**  
  The city doesn’t just “look” like a fevered machine; it is one. Observed time doesn’t behave the same twice from the same place. A day here might be hours, or weeks, elsewhere. You cannot assume your body’s fatigue clock still matches home.
- **Space is not your friend.**  
  “Down” is not fixed. “Out” does not exist. There are only gradients: *towards the center*, and *towards the rim*. The city itself is the only coordinate system you can trust.
- **The city is only mostly empty.**  
  “You are the only animal” does not mean “you are alone.” Whatever presence notices you from behind the glass and inside the floor may be an unknown system process, or a sensor pattern, or a mind. It doesn’t need to be malicious to be dangerous.
- **You will not be able to explain what you see.**  
  No recording device you bring will capture the reality of the glass-that-isn’t-glass, the math that rewrites itself, the “black glow” of the towers. Expect all your cameras to be wrong. The only reliable instrument is your own nervous system.

If you hold these four truths in your head at all times, you’re already ahead of most travelers who would go here and leave only equations behind their footprints.

## 1. Before you go: mental and physiological training

You’re going into a place that:
- warps time and space,
- messes with your sense of scale,
- maintains direction by computation,
- and feels like being trapped inside a fevered, pulsing organ.

Your training should be less about “strength” and more about **stability and pattern awareness**.

### 1.1. Vestibular and proprioceptive training

You can’t change the local gravity of the city, but you can reduce your odds of puking and passing out.

Work on:
- **Balance in 3D-like environments:**  
  - Balance boards, slacklines, or simple yoga on one leg with your eyes closed.
  - Move deliberately: slow tai chi or similar forms that make you aware of slight weight shifts.
- **Visual-vestibular mismatch tolerance:**  
  - Sit on a swivel chair and slowly spin while reading or tracking a stationary object.  
  - On a swing at a playground, look side to side rather than straight ahead.  
- **Proprioceptive drills:**  
  - Practice walking across a room with your eyes closed, using only walls and floor texture through your feet for guidance.
  - Learn to “feel” your center of mass. You will need to trust this in the city where “down” can tilt slightly, without visible cues.

### 1.2. Orientation skills without familiar landmarks

You will have:
- no sun,
- no stars,
- no horizon.

You must learn to navigate by:
- **geometry**  
- **your own body**  
- **the city’s own sparse patterns**.

Train yourself:
- In a large empty hall or plaza, mark yourself a “center” and walk “outwards” in various directions with your eyes closed, then try to return to the center solely by feeling your way and short-term memorization of turns.
- In your city at home, practice navigating from one point to another using only *streets and angles*, deliberately ignoring signs, sun, and major landmarks.

### 1.3. Enduring claustrophobia without panic

The city:
- feels like a stadium-sized disk of stone,
- wrapped in an invisible glass dome,
- suspended over *nothing*.

You’ll have **no outward horizon**. Only *rim* and *center*.

Work on:
- Timeboxed claustrophobic training (only if you tolerate this well; otherwise, don’t scar yourself):
  - Small enclosed spaces like a windowless room with softened lighting.
  - Sit still for increasing intervals (5 min, 10, 20) without phone or distractions.
- **Breathing techniques:**  
  4-6-8 or box breathing: inhale 4, hold 6, exhale 8. The goal is not relaxation; the goal is to keep your thinking-brain online when your body wants to bolt.

### 1.4. Observing long cycles without narrative

The city’s cycles:
- No sunrise / sunset.
- No clear “days” or “weeks.”
- Hum that deepens, then fades.
- Stone patterns pulsing quietly.

You’ll be tempted to impose:
- “this is a heartbeat”
- “this is a day cycle”
- “this city is dying” or “this city is a god”

You must not. You’re a mammal in a machine whose control panel is written in stone.

Train yourself by:
- Watching a mundane pattern for a long time (e.g., a clock, a traffic light, or a spinning fan).
- Do not narrate in your head, “it is tired now,” “it wants to change,” etc. Just observe:
  - exact duration if you can measure,
  - exact shape of the repetition,
  - any deviations from the pattern.
This is the mindset you must bring to the city’s hums, glowing patterns, and faint glass ripples.

### 1.5. Mental contamination: “Don’t borrow the city’s metaphors”

One of the biggest risks of this place is not physical injury but **symbolic poisoning**:
- The city will seem to:
  - have a metabolism,
  - be running a fever,
  - be trying to remember you,
  - be computing your body.

If you begin to mistake yourself for:
- a tumor in the city’s body,
- a message the city is trying to decode,
- a ghost that doesn’t belong,

you can drift into very poor decision-making.

To guard against this, write down before you go:
- Three short, literal statements about you:
  - e.g., “I am a human,” “I have a body made of familiar matter,” “I need to return to my home timeline.”
- Rehearse saying them internally, *even if they feel boring or stupid* when you’re there. The city will be dreamy; you must be dull.

## 2. Gear: what to bring, and what not to bring

### Core principle: “Nothing that depends too much on physics staying home.”

You want to privilege:
- mechanical over digital,
- intrinsically safe over powerful,
- low-profile over impressive.

### 2.1. Clothing and thermoregulation

- **Multiple light layers**  
  - Thin base layer that wicks moisture (you’ll be damp from the air’s oiled feeling).
  - A thin mid layer for when you feel the city’s “black glow” or chill.
- **Comfortable, low-profile shoes with flat soles**
  - The city’s stone floor is smooth but not perfectly level; there are slow ripples and terraces.
- **No armor or bulky gear**
  - You are not storming a fortress; you are intruding into a geometry lab. You want to be able to move quickly and quietly, and not overheat.
- **Optional: thin gloves and forearm or knee pads**
  - If you need to touch surfaces or kneel to the floor (to read the math beneath the stone), you’ll want protection from prolonged contact with the stone’s feverish warmth.

### 2.2. Visibility and lighting

- **Headlamp with red filter**  
  - The city is already dimly lit by computational glow; you don’t want to blind yourself or scream visually at the environment.
  - Red light preserves night vision and is less intrusive to “glowing” environments.
- **Optional: small handheld mirror**  
  - To look at the window distortions without directly facing them.  
  - For safety: sometimes it’s better to see a reflection of a strange thing than to look at it with your eyes.

## 3. Sensors and Recording: “Assume everything is lying.”

You’re going to a place where:
- math rewrites itself when you’re not looking,
- light bends through gravity-glass,
- the sky doesn’t match itself twice.

Your tech will be unreliable at best and dangerously misleading at worst.

### 3.1. Cameras

- **Expect failure**
  - The camera may show a flat, black slab when your eyes see a tower of deep black glass glowing faintly.
- **Bring a simple mechanical camera with film** as a backup experiment (not as your primary safety).
  - Process film back home; if your reality matches the film’s perspective, this is unusual and worth note.
- **Use a camera mainly for documentation of:
  - your own tracks,
  - sudden changes of geometry (before/after shots of the same edge),
  - or perhaps of the faint plaza-light patterns (if they even show up).

But don’t count on it to show what you “see.”

### 3.2. Compass, GPS, and clocks

Assume they:
- **lie consistently**
- **or fail randomly**.

- **Simple analog watch**  
  - Mark it “for reference only,” as it might run faster or slower relative to home time.
- **No reliance on any phone app.**  
  - Assume that your phone will:  
    - lose time,  
    - lose battery unpredictably,  
    - display inaccurate maps.
- **Optional: small magnetic compass**  
  - If it behaves wildly (spinning, or not responding to direction changes), that’s important data; but don’t expect it to tell you geographic “north”.

## 4. Air and heat: surviving “inside the machine’s body”

The air:
- is laminated, oily, thick,
- feels like “each breath passes through a film of unseen oil,”
- is hot in a way that doesn’t burn, but clings to your lungs.

You must treat this like stepping into a **thermal and chemical safety environment**.

### 4.1. Air filtration

- **N95 mask or better**  
  - Reduces your sense of smell somewhat, but also reduces exposure to unknown airborne matter and “stickiness.”
- **Optional: a light respirator with chemical filter**  
  - If you have access to that level of gear, and training in using it properly.
- **Do NOT breathe deeply just to “feel the place.”**
  - Take shallow, measured breaths until you’re sure your body is not being subtly poisoned by something you can’t see.

### 4.2. Hydration

- Bring **water from your world**.
- Assume:
  - the city’s dampness is not water you can safely drink.
- Pack:
  - insulated water bottles or canteens,
  - ideally more than you think you need.

- **Don’t drink from any visible surface condensation**
  - It’s sliding up along invisible lines of force; this is not normal rain, not normal dew. It’s likely a coolant system, or a solvent of geometry you don’t want to put inside your body.

- **Monitor yourself**
  - Signs you must leave early:
    - dizziness increasing,
    - nausea,
    - tunnel vision,
    - sudden “emotional” overheating (like panic that doesn’t match your thoughts).

### 4.3. Heat management

- **Plan for short total stays**  
  - Don’t go into the city alone expecting to stay 24 hours.
- **On-site pacing**
  - After you first arrive, take a break of at least:
    - 10–15 minutes in the same spot,
    - eyes closed,
    - breathing measured,
  - to allow your body to calibrate.
- **Rest often**
  - The city’s heat is not aggressive sun; it’s a pervasive machine-running heat.  
  - If you feel “feverish without being feverish,” that’s the signal you’re already pushing into red territory.

## 5. Food and biology: don’t become the experiment’s next sample

- Bring **closed, sealed, packaged food from home**
  - Nothing that depends on local fish or local game; there are no animals anyway.
- Ration **conservatively**
  - Time distortions can mean you’re there longer than you expect.
- **Assume nothing local is edible.**
  - No berries, no “incandescent fungus patterns,” no drinking from the floor.
- **Plan around waste**
  - All waste *must* leave with you.  
  - This is for:
    - your own health,
    - and not contaminating a machine-city whose stone appears to “sample” you in the form of equations beneath your feet.

## 6. Navigation and safety in a space that rewrites itself

### 6.1. Marking yourself

You cannot rely on paths or footprints. You will have:
- no sun,
- no horizon,
- almost no sound reflection,
- and a space that subtly “settles” so an edge may be different millimeters after you walked away.

- **Mark your path with something harmless**
  - Non-chalk, non-permanent: ideally something you can remove later.
  - Invisible UV marker (if you have a UV light) can mark stones without leaving visible scars.
- **Mark “center”**
  - Start at the lowest central plaza near the towers.
  - Learn it visually and by feel:
    - count steps from center to first edge of terrace,
    - note how many hexes you step between.
- **Don’t wander**
  - This is not a place to explore “off the beaten path.” There are no paths. There are only edges.

### 6.2. The rim and the glass

- **Treat rim of the floor as a cliff near an event horizon**
  - Even though you’re told nothing you drop can leave the floor, *you are not stone*, and the city may not treat you the same way.
- **Assume the glass dome at the edge is not benign**
  - It might:
    - reject you,
    - capture you,
    - or reflect you into one of those inverted city copies you see for a blink.
- **Stay at least one body-length away from the very edge**
  - Don’t sit, lean, or sleep there.
  - The text suggests that close to the edge you can see the city “reflected”, as if clamped upside-down or inside-out against the stone floor you’re on. That’s not a normal reflection.

### 6.3. Time and return

- **Assume any time you can’t measure**
  - Record your subjective time:
    - note “I feel like 4 hours have passed” vs your actual-world clock, if you still have one.
- **Set hard maximums**
  - Before you go, decide how long you are willing to stay *maximum*:
    - e.g., “2 objective days of my watch; if I am unsure, then 1 day.”
  - When you hit that threshold, **you leave even if nothing interesting happened yet**.

## 7. Interaction with the city itself: “Don’t touch anything you don’t have to”

### 7.1. Windows and gravity-glass

- **Don’t stare into the depth of the windows**
  - Your eyes will try to make sense of the light-smearing and the occasional distortions in the glass.  
  - This is mentally fatiguing and can lead to:
    - dizziness,
    - nausea,
    - or “soft” hallucinations.
- **Don’t press your palm or face against the glass**
  - You don’t know what it is; it might be:
    - a sensor,  
    - a screen,  
    - a surface that can rephase your matter.
- **If you see your reflection distorted—extra arm or angle—**
  - Look away briefly; then come back with your mirror, if you must observe.
  - Don’t fixate on it trying to “make sense” of the body that doesn’t match your own.

### 7.2. Floors and math veins

- **You may touch the floor with bare hands or gloved hands**
  - It’s described as smooth, porcelain-like, faintly damp.
- **Don’t carve, mark, or write into the floor**
  - The stone remembers your math anyway.  
  - The last thing you want is to be a *deliberate* variable in its system.
- **Observe the math veins from a distance as much as possible**
  - If you need to read them closely:
    - kneel or lie flat,
    - never cut yourself to “mark” them with blood,
    - never attempt to “correct” or overwrite them with chalk or markers.

- **Remember the text explicitly**
  - The math under your feet *models your body.*
- Treat that like an **unconscious feedback system**:
  - Sudden changes in your position or mass *might* affect how the city “tunes” that space.
  - The safest way to interact with this is:
    - move slowly,
    - change your stance rarely,
    - and don’t attempt to “break” the math by dramatically contorting yourself.

### 7.3. Tetrahedral and hex-shaped glowing “vegetations”

- **Don’t touch**
  - They are warm, with a periodic temperature pulse.
- **Don’t leave your hand over them for long**
  - You might be supplying heat data they don’t need to have.
- **Observe from the edge of your vision**
  - If you stare directly, they fold into themselves and “don’t add up.”
  - Glance, then look away; then ask:
    - “did what I saw make sense?”  
    - if not, mark it as a pattern too foreign to systematize easily.

## 8. On the Presence: “Don’t borrow its eyes”

You *will* feel:
- something “breathing” behind you,
- a density in the air behind you,
- distortions reflected deep in the windows that don’t match your body.

You must not:
- name it,
- talk to it,
- stare at it,
- or “offer yourself” to it.

Treat it like a radiation leak you can’t see:

- **If you feel your attention being drawn to a window**  
  - Limit your glances to 3 seconds at a time; then deliberately look away at something else.
- **Don’t say things out loud**
  - The city muffles sound anyway; your voice will feel like a pillow.
- **Don’t argue with it in your head**
  - “Is it studying me? Is it judging me?”  
  - These thoughts are seductive but they lead nowhere.
- **Anchor your mind**
  - Repeat the boring facts you wrote down before you left:
    - “I am human. I come from elsewhere. I need to return.”

## 9. While you’re there: what to actually do with your time

### 9.1. Establish a safe perimeter first

- **Mark a clear “center”**
  - Identify the lowest plaza in the center of the stone disk.
  - Note:
    - number of steps from center to nearest tower walls,
    - number of steps to first terrace edge,
    - number of steps to second terrace edge.
- **Don’t explore beyond the second terrace edge on your first day**
  - Give yourself time to get used to:
    - the heat,
    - the way the floor feels,
    - and your own breathing.

### 9.2. Take measurements (but don’t expect them to mean the same thing later)

- **Count the recurring features**
  - How many steps to the rim of the floor from center?
  - How many “major” terrace edges?
  - How many distinct tower needles?
- **Draw a simple map**
  - On paper, with pencil and ruler.
  - Don’t rely on your phone or camera for this.
- **Make short “visit” marks**
  - At each terrace edge, mark how you were there briefly:
    - e.g., small, unobtrusive note of date/time from your watch.
  - When you return later, check:
    - Is the number of steps from one edge to the next still the same?
    - Is the edge of a terrace a millimeter farther or closer than you remembered?

### 9.3. Observe the cycles

- **Listen to the hums**
  - When does the hum beneath the floor deepen?
  - How long does the deepening last?
- **Watch the plaza patterns**
  - Do the glowing polygons change shape?
  - Does the pulse interval shift from roughly thirty seconds to something else?
- **Note the “black glow”**
  - When you feel the pressure of the towers’ “black glow,”
    - do your teeth buzz if you have any metal fillings?
    - does the stone beneath your feet feel slightly colder or warmer?

### 9.4. Keep a discipline of “Minimal Change, Minimal Contact”

You want to leave the city as you found it, except for:
- your footprints,
- your temporary markers,
- the equations of your body that it briefly sampled and then forgot.

You do not want:
- to scratch your name into a floor that remembers math,
- to break a window that you cannot understand,
- to feed your body’s sweat into a glowing polygon pattern and hope “it will be OK.”

## 10. Exit and debrief: “Check your sanity before sharing your story”

When you come back:

- You **might not fully remember** what the city looked like.
- You **might be misremembering color** (sky as purple when you remember it as blue-black).
- You **might be incapable of drawing the lights you saw**.

So:

- **Write down everything you remember before you sleep**
  - Don’t show it to anyone else yet.
- **Compare your notes to your expectations**
  - If you expected “a city of gods,” did your body actually observe *feverish machine-like* behavior?
  - Did you feel awe or unease?
- **Share only later**
  - Share with only one or two people you trust deeply
- **Don’t become a myth-maker**
  - The city is already doing enough symbolism work without you borrowing its language.

## 11. Finally, if you stand there now: what to do next?

You’ve already arrived:
- The air is warm like a fever against your skin.
- The floor is smooth, faintly damp, under your feet.
- Black obsidian towers cut into the bruised sky dome.
- You are the only animal.

What next?

- **Step 1: Do not move for one minute.**
  - Just breathe shallowly.
  - Feel your feet on the stone.
- **Step 2: Slowly mark your center.**
  - Look around the disk floor.
  - Identify where you are in relation to:
    - the nearest tower,
    - the nearest edge terrace,
- **Step 3: Walk a short circle of maybe ten steps’ radius around where you first appeared**
  - Listen to the muffled, traffic-sigh sound of the space itself.
  - Notice the faint pulse of heat under your feet.
- **Step 4: Decide whether you’re staying.**
  - If everything in your body tells you “get out,” you listen to that.
  - If you choose to stay:
    - keep your mission simple:
      - “I will walk to one edge terrace and back to this mark. That’s it.”
- **Step 5: Eventually, you will leave.**
  - When your time runs out or your body has had enough, you go back to your entry point.
  - You don’t say goodbye to a city like this; you withdraw from a patient.

### If you take nothing else from this advice:

- Treat the city like a living **instrumentation package** rather than a temple, or a machine god.
- Move **slowly**, change **little**, touch **less**.
- Fix **limits** before you go in, and stick to them, even if the city feels like it wants you to stay longer.
- Never **assume the math beneath your feet is only math.**  
  It may be just enough aware of you to include you in its calibration.

If you can do all of that, then you will be as prepared as any ordinary human traveler can be to visit a glass city in the sky that doesn’t remember building itself but is already trying to remember you.
