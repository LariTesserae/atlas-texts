## Before you go

### 0. Check if you *should* go

This place is livable but not friendly. It is not going to tear you apart with storms or poison you with outright toxic gas, but it will test you psychologically and physically.

Do **not** choose this as a destination if you:

- Are deeply claustrophobic or panicky in tight, wet, enclosed spaces.
- Need real sunlight or seasonal change to stay emotionally stable.
- Absolutely require “dry” days to recover.
- Are traveling alone with no backup plan or support team.

For anyone else, it is endurable and even fascinating—if you prepare properly.

---

## 1. Mindset: what you’re actually signing up for

### The psychological baseline

This world is not about events, but about *state*.  

Your time there will feel like a single long atmosphere rather than a sequence of “things happened.” There is movement, but it loops. The heat is always on. The wet is always there. The noise never stops. The light never truly sharpens.

Most of your “adventure” will be internal: managing boredom, sensory overload, and the feeling of being slowly digested by the world.

**Expectations to set:**

- No heroic “getting to the top of the mountain” or “crossing the great river.” You’re just moving through a shallow, wet bowl under an almost solid ceiling of trees.
- No dramatic weather changes to break up monotony.
- No clear daily rhythm of “daytime = energy, night = rest.” The difference is subtle.
- No visible sky, no stars, no horizon. That’s *disorienting* for humans.
- No built artifacts. If you see anything shaped like a tool, seat, or structure, *you’re looking at something that doesn’t belong there*, and that should make you cautious.

In this place, **you are the weird anomaly**—not because you’re tough or special, but because you carry change with you in your mind.

## 2. Physical and environmental expectations

### Temperature & humidity

- Air temperature: ~constant, roughly **body–temperature to slightly warmer**.
- Humidity: effectively **near–100%**, always.
- Result: You **cannot** cool off by evaporating sweat. The air feels like warm, wet cloth over your mouth. Your body may struggle to regulate heat.

### Visibility and light

- Under–canopy light: **permanent dim green twilight**, shifting gently but never brightening into “day.”
- No direct sun; no shadows that make clear shape.
- No starlight nights; “night” is just the same dim green turning slightly darker and softer.

### Terrain

The ground is not reliably solid. It is:

- Thin-skinned mud.
- Sediment mats.
- Crusts that flex under your weight.
- Waterlogged organic “floating” hummocks.

You literally will be standing on water kept in shape by a fragile membrane of mud and plant matter.

### Water system

- **No rivers**, no strong flow.
- Many shallow, **stagnant–warm pools** linked by seepage and film.
- Water surface is a skin of life; underneath is opaque murk.
- Pools may not reflect the sky; they’ll show only inverted trees and leaves.

### Time

- Days and nights exist but **feel like continuous time with faint texture changes** rather than distinct phases.
- No long seasons, only tiny fluctuations in humidity or shade.
- Physical erosion is minimal; the place “regenerates” but never “moves on.”

This is not a clock-world; it’s a loop.

## 3. When you should arrive

**Important:** The *idea* of “time of day” is not the same as “safe window.”

- **Local “mid-light” (equivalent of afternoon):**  
  Usually the safest first arrival time.  
  - Most of the day fauna is active but settled into routine.  
  - No “shock” of middle-night disorientation.
  - Easier to evaluate surroundings without your body fighting pure circadian confusion.

- **Avoid arrival in deep “night”:**  
  You’ll be speaking to an already hyperactive nocturnal chorus with your senses already compromised by tiredness.

**Duration of stay (for a first visit):**

- **If you’re going alone, with basic gear:**  
  **2–3 days maximum** to stay mentally sane and physically manageable.
- **With a team, good shelter, and better gear:**  
  Up to a **week** is possible.
- Longer stays are better treated as **structured scientific expeditions**, not casual tourism.

## 4. Medical and physical readiness

### Before you leave

1. **Full physical checkup**
   - Specifically heart, lungs, and skin.
   - Heat stress in a saturated environment is real and potentially dangerous.

2. **Update vaccinations**
   - Tetanus, hepatitis, and ensure anything relevant for “swamp/jungle” environments in your own medical system is covered.
   - Bring documentation: in some multiverse systems, border entities require it.

3. **Consider prophylactic medications**
   - Antifungals (if recommended by your doctor).
   - Antihistamines for likely allergic reactions to high bug density.
   - Maybe mild topical antibiotics for cuts/abrasions.

4. **If you have any of these, reconsider the trip entirely**
   - Severe asthma or other breathing conditions.
   - Compromised immune system.
   - Recent major surgery or wounds that are still healing.
   - Active systemic fungal infections or very sensitive skin.

This is a world where **every surface is wet, living, and touching you**.

## 5. Core gear: essentials to pack or arrange beforehand

### A. Climate-control suit / “second skin”

You need a **total environmental barrier** that can still breathe a bit.

**Look for:**

- Thin, full-length suit (long sleeves, trousers, integrated socks/gloves or close-fitting).
- **Highly breathable but waterproof shell**.
- **Excellent moisture management**—the sweat that can’t evaporate needs to be pulled away from your skin as best as possible.

**Key features:**

- Taped seams, to prevent water seeping in.
- Attached hood or very tight seal with a helmet/hood.
- **Optional but highly recommended:** built-in ventilation ports or a mesh vest layer that can serve as a personal airspace.

**Purpose:**

- Reduce direct contact between your skin and:
  - Mud
  - Water
  - Slimy organisms
  - Droplets of unknown biological fluids

You **cannot** overestimate the value of a barrier in this place.

---

### B. Footwear that respects the mud

**Standard boots or sneakers = fail condition.**

What you want instead:

1. **Closed-toe aquatic/TIDE shoes or specialized swamp boots**
   - Drainable design.
   - Quick-drying (when you’re out of the world).
   - Puncture-resistant soles to prevent sharp roots or hidden stones from injuring you.

**Very important:** Tight seals with your suit.  
**Not** “open at the top, mud and debris pour inside.” If you feel the mud against your skin, you’re doing it wrong.

---

### C. Head and eyes, neck and hands

1. **Head / face**
   - **Full-face mask or mesh veil** that:
     - Keeps small bugs away from eyes, nose, mouth.
     - Doesn’t restrict breathing too much.
     - Does not fog easily—if it can fog, it will be useless here.
   - Light hood or helmet of non-absorbent material that can be washed and dried quickly.

2. **Eyes**
   - Simple sealed goggles or tight glasses suitable for humid environments.
   - Anti-fog coating is essential.

3. **Neck**
   - A sealed neck gaiter sewn or zipped to your suit helps.
   - You want to avoid any “splash channel” down your spine.

4. **Gloves**
   - Thin, waterproof gloves or very tight-fitting gloves that can dry fast.
   - You need dexterity for operating gear, but not bare skin contact.

---

### D. Climbing and stability aids

You **will not** walk “like hiking on earth.” This is more like climbing through a dense, wet, partially submerged obstacle course.

Essential:

- **Lightweight trekking pole or staff**
  - Ideally two.
  - Use them to:
    - Test ground depth
    - Push vines aside
    - Steady yourself on roots
    - Probe mud ahead of you

- **Compact harness and line (short climbing rope / safety line)**
  - Not for full technical climbing, but:
    - To help lower yourself into or out of awkward pools or log-choked gaps.
    - For securing yourself to a tree when you need to rest.
    - For your team to pull you out if you get stuck.

---

### E. Lighting that you can trust

“There’s no true darkness” doesn’t mean “you can see.”

You need:

- **Primary headlamp** with:
  - Wide/flood mode for general navigation.
  - High output mode for short bursts of inspection.
  - **Red or very low-intensity mode** for night-time use without crushing your night vision.

- **Backup light sources**
  - Compact handheld flashlight.
  - Possibly glowsticks or small marker lights that you can attach to your pack/harness for visibility in low-vis.

- **Power strategy**
  - Everything sealed inside waterproof containers or with O-rings.
  - Extra batteries or power cells.
  - Assume nothing “stays dry” by accident.

---

### F. Shelter, sleep, and base camp

You cannot just sleep on the ground.

1. **Compact hammock + integrated rainfly**
   - Material:
     - Quick-drying, non-absorbent.
     - Mosquito-netting integrated.
   - Attach it between **sturdy-looking trunks**:
     - Avoid hanging over large pools if you can help it.
     - Avoid being directly under obvious dripping tree “weeps.”

2. **Insulation**
   - Lightweight, synthetic quilt or thin sleeping pad layer that **won’t die when wet**.
   - Do not count on thick, absorbent materials like cotton, down or similar fabrics.

3. **Anchor lines**
   - Extra cords that you can use for:
     - Rigging a tarp above your hammock.
     - Hanging gear.
     - Guiding yourself back to camp if visibility gets worse.

**Shelter strategy**

- Choose **slightly elevated hummocks over pure flat mire** if possible.
- Avoid being the lowest point in the area; water may be closer to the surface.
- Don’t break living structures if you can help it; you’re not the only one using the tree architecture.

---

### G. Water and food management

**You cannot drink the water as it is.**

Assume:

- Warm, stagnant water.
- High microbial life.
- Potentially odd chemistry for your biology.

You therefore need:

1. **Filter + chemical treatment + maybe boil**
   - A filter rated for microbes.
   - Water purification tablets or dilute bleach solution suitable for drinking water.
   - A small, efficient heat source for boiling water even if everything else is wet.

2. **All your own water**
   - Carry at least **2–3 days of clean water** if your team isn’t running a full purification system.
   - Store in:
     - Hard bottles or bladders.
     - Sealed containers inside a waterproof bag.

3. **Food**
   - **Prepackaged, sealed, ready-to-eat meals** are best:
     - Not heavy, aromatic stuff.
     - Not easily spoiled by high heat.
   - Avoid cooking complicated messy meals that require:
     - Open handling of food
     - Long cleanup in water-logged conditions
   - **No local food** unless:
     - Cleared by a biological expert from your side.
     - Even then, only minimal experimentation.

---

### H. Navigation and communication

1. **Navigation**
   - Standard magnetic compasses **may not behave reliably** depending on how strictly local geology is “half-made.”
   - You still want one for **relative direction**, but also:
     - A handheld GPS device or equivalent tracker if your multiverse access method allows.
     - **Physical marking system**:
       - Colored tape or cord.
       - Tie temporary markers at eye height.
       - Don’t mark irreversibly (no cutting deep grooves into living trees).
   - Maintain a discipline:
     - Log your approximate path.
     - Mark time and direction in each log.
     - Do not count on recognizing “landmarks” in a uniform-looking world.

2. **Communication**
   - If your team is more than one person:
     - Short-range radios or communicators.
       - Tested for **range** in a damp environment beforehand.
     - Agree on check-in times and “must return” times.
   - If you absolutely must go alone:
     - Maintain at least one **long-distance distress beacon** or return artifact.
     - Set a **time limit**: “If not back by X, assume trouble and trigger extraction.”

---

### I. Environmental sampling and safety

Even if you’re not a scientist, you should behave like one:

- **Sample containers**
  - Tight-sealed vials or bags for taking:
    - Water samples.
    - Bits of mud or sediment (if regulations of your origin system allow).
    - Possibly leaf or microbial mat samples.

- **Basic sensors**
  - Air temperature and humidity.
  - Maybe dissolved oxygen/pH in water if you can manage.
  - These data can help your medical support team rescue you if something happens.

- **Do not collect “live souvenirs”**
  - No taking animals home.
  - No pocketing “cute” slugs or translucent things.
  - This is a young ecosystem; you don’t know what you’re removing.

---

### J. Hygiene and infection management

For every wound you get:

- **Assume it will be contaminated** immediately.
- Stop:
  - Clean with antiseptic wipes or solution.
  - Apply antibacterial cream/ointment.
  - Cover tightly with waterproof bandages.
- Inspect frequently.

For general body hygiene:

- You **will not** stay dry in your sense of the word.
- But you can:
  - At camp, remove the suit in a controlled, clean way.
  - Use microfiber towels or absorbent cloths to remove moisture.
  - Change under-layers as often as possible.
- After the trip, expect the need for:
  - Thorough cleaning of all equipment to avoid carrying mold or spores home.
  - Detailed personal hygiene, including antifungal creams if recommended by a doctor.

## 6. Behavioral strategy in the field

### Movement

You **move slow**, always.

- Each step:
  - Test the ground ahead with your pole.
  - Check how deep your foot sinks.
  - Look for movement under the murky water.
- Avoid:

  - Stepping directly into unknown pools.
  - Using tree roots as secure steps without testing them first (many will be slick or rotten underneath).
  - Pushing blindly through vines without checking if something else lives there.

You are not running through this world. You are threading yourself.

### Avoidance and non-interference

- **Do not** clear extensive vegetation just to make “space.”
- The world will fill it back *very* aggressively.
- If you really need to clear a small patch for safety:
  - Keep it minimal.
  - Expect that it will begin to recover within days.

- **Do not** attempt:
  - To build “paths” or “platforms.”
  - To modify hydrology by digging channels or damming pools.
- This is a young, stable equilibrium—you do not know what small change would trigger an unexpected shift.

### No contact with unknown animals

- Avoid:

  - Handling anything.
  - Purposefully touching any of the translucent creatures.
  - Letting them contact your bare skin.

- If something gets onto your suit:

  - Remove it gently with tools (not hands).
  - Or gently let it move off itself when you’re safe.

This is not “everything is poisonous,” but **everything is unknown**, and the least of your worries is not understanding what skin-penetrating enzymes any local lifeforms possess.

### Noise and stillness

The noise floor is high:

- Insects and water creatures create a constant wall.
- You will be tempted to raise your voice or noise level to feel “above” it. Don’t.

Instead:

- Use **quiet signals** with your team.
- Rely on **visual gestures** as much as possible.
- You will *feel* the noise as much as you hear it; learn to treat it as neutral weather, not background chatter.

## 7. Temporal and spatial rhythm

### Day and “night”

Because there is no strong sun rhythm:

- You must enforce your own **cycle**:
  - Plan “active hours.”
  - Plan “rest hours.”
- Use **meals and gear checks** as anchors instead of sun position.

During **“night”**:  
- Do not travel far if not necessary.
- Expect:
  - Different combinations of sounds.
  - Possibly more movement near the water and trunks.
  - Only subtle visual changes; your lights will be your real sun.

### Resting without going crazy

This place can induce **sensory deprivation** despite the noise, because:

- Limited horizon.
- No distant sky.
- No clear landmarks.
- Same sound, always.

You may need:

- Simple routines:
  - Few minutes of stretching in the hammock.
  - Basic journaling or recording notes.
  - Short checks of your gear and body.
- Resist the urge to constantly seek something “new” in the surroundings; the novelty is not in the macro-changes but the micro-forms.

## 8. Mental and emotional preparation

This is not the kind of place where you “just head out” and figure everything on the spot.

**Before departure:**

1. **Accept the monotony**
   - Acknowledge that it will be:
     - Wet.
     - Warm.
     - Dim.
     - Loud.
   - Decide that your victory is not “conquest” but **endurance without breaking**.

2. **Practice being in enclosed, humid spaces**
   - Steam baths, hot yoga rooms, or similar environments can help your body get used to the sensation of constant heat and humidity.
   - See if claustrophobia or panic is triggered.

3. **Talk with someone about potential triggers**
   - If you have:
     - Fear of drowning.
     - Deep fear of being unable to breathe.
     - Strong aversion to bugs crawling on you—even through barriers.
   - Consider whether you *should* go at all, or at least build in strict safety limits and exit options.

4. **Set exit conditions**
   - Decide in advance what triggers an immediate departure:
     - Persistent skin reactions that don’t improve with treatment.
     - An infection that your body can’t shake.
     - Any psychological spiral that starts to feel dangerous (self-harm thoughts, severe dissociation, etc.).
   - Do not negotiate “one more day” if you’ve already hit the pre-defined exit line.

---

## 9. Exit and re-integration

When you leave:

1. **Expect re-entry weirdness**
   - Sudden access to:
     - Dry air.
     - Real sunlight.
     - Wind.
   - Your body may need:
     - A day or two to adjust.
     - Time to fully dry out.
     - Time to shed the memory of the constant drone.

2. **Care for your gear**
   - All equipment:
     - Disassemble and dry.
     - Clean thoroughly.
     - Check for mold growth you *might not* see at first.
   - You may need to:
     - Treat fabrics with antifungal solutions.
     - Discard anything that you can’t safely clean.

3. **Care for your body**
   - A full medical check-up after return:
     - Tell the doctor the environment you’ve been in.
     - Mention any skin, eye, or respiratory symptoms.
   - Use:
     - Hydrating lotions for skin.
     - Possibly eye drops for dryness after being removed from that humidity.

4. **Expect odd memory**
   - You might not remember “what happened” day by day.
   - You might instead:
     - Remember a single continuous atmosphere.
     - Recall snippets: a particular pool of black water, a trunk moving with faint life, a strange reflection under a leaf.

---

### Final summary

If you’re going to this place:

- You prepare **not like an explorer but like a long-term diver**, working in a huge, warm, shallow ocean under the trees.
- You accept:
  - No horizon.
  - No sky.
  - No permanent landmarks.
- You protect:
  - Your skin.
  - Your airways.
  - Your senses.
  - Your mind.

Then you move slowly, carefully, and quietly—respecting that the world you walk into is not a place of change and has not yet made room for creatures like you who still expect time to move forward rather than loop.

You are a guest in a room that never finished being built.
