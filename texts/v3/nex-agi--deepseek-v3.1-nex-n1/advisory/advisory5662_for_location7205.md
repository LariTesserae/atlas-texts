Visiting this place is less like hiking a new continent and more like stepping into a running physics simulation that was not designed for meat.  

If you respect that, and prepare accordingly, you can walk there safely, perceive astonishing things, and go back in one piece—possibly even changed in useful ways.

Below is how I’d tell an ordinary traveler to prepare.

---

## 0. What you are actually dealing with

Before you pack anything, you need to internalize what kind of “world” this is:

- It is **not a natural environment**. It is an engineered reality system, “almost” stable but not entirely aligned with your sensory or physical laws.
- It is **not hostile**, but it is **not built for you**. It will tolerate you like a server tolerates an unexpected background process—unless you start consuming resources or destabilizing it.
- It is **not “time-less”** but time is handled differently. There is no conventional cycle of sun or rain; erosion doesn’t happen; but occasional *version jumps* still occur—where the world silently “rebuilds” a geometry or state.
- **There are no animals.** No fauna, no bacteria, no viruses…as far as we can tell. Dangers are not biological; they are structural and informational.
- **There is no water.** Perception is “dry.” The real risk is not dehydration from sweating, but from *protracted absence of water in your system*.
- The **glass underfoot is three centimeters thick and floating**. You won’t break it by walking, but if you violate certain rules (pressure, shock, vibration, etc.), you might fall.

Your main dangers are:
1. Falling off the edge.  
2. Your mind losing track of “where reality is.”  
3. Staying too long in the dry, dry substrate and quietly failing to return.  
4. Your equipment being “interpreted” by the world’s code and then re-rendered wrong.

Your goal is not to survive like Bear Grylls in the desert. It’s to be a *clean, low-impact, self-contained process* executing on top of a very picky OS.

## 1. Mental and psychological preparation

### 1.1 Accept that you will be constantly wrong

This is the first skill to practice:

- You *will* see geometric edges that exist in multiple positions at once.
- Reality *will* slide slightly (“interface drift”) where the scene seems to reframe itself.
- The “fauna” will be perceptual glitches rather than animals.

Prepare by rehearsing, **before you go**, the idea: “I will almost always misread what’s happening here. That doesn’t mean I’m in danger.”

Train yourself to treat strange perceptions as **data**, not as threats. Curiosity is your best defense.

### 1.2 Anchor your self-narrative

Before departure, write down in clear, simple language:

1. Who you are.
2. Where you came from.
3. When you left.
4. What you personally look like.
5. That you **must return** by (time limit).

Read this at least once before you step onto the plate and again after you return. This text is your “save point” narrative if your memory of home-world timelines feels strange.

### 1.3 Use time limits

Set a hard cap on your visit. I’d suggest:

- First time: **no longer than 1 to 2 hours of subjective time**.
- Only later, with experience and proven equipment, consider longer visits.

Because the world is not designed with day-night cycles, your body will not reliably “feel” time passing. You’ll need *external* metering (see below) to enforce your limit.

### 1.4 Embrace reality checks

Equip yourself with very simple, everyday-world “rules” that you can test regularly:

- “Are my shadows consistent with *one* light source?”  
  → (Here the answer is “no,” because the light is uniform. This will *always* fail the ordinary-world test. That’s fine.)
- “Can I see the same feature from two different angles?”  
  → If you see *different* architectures from slightly different positions, that’s normal here.
- “Does glass feel like glass to my soles and ears?”  
  → Here, glass underfoot might be almost perfectly silent when you expect *clink* or *groan*.

Use reality checks not as “I am insane if this fails,” but as “I confirm: I am still in the strange-world mode.”

## 2. Clothing and protective gear

### 2.1 Full-cover, non-slip, low-contrast suit

You want:

- **Full body coverage**:  
  - Long sleeves, trousers, closed gloves, socks fully covering the ankles.
  - Avoid bright colors. Choose *matte* neutral grays/soft greens/blues rather than glimmering colors or pure black.  
  Why:  
  - You don’t want to be confused with the geometry around you.
  - No reflective or “jewelry-style” surfaces that could be “read” by the world as part of its own structure.

- **Material**:  
  - Synthetic but non-floating fibers.  
  - Avoid fabric that sheds fibers or dusts.
  - Fully breathable but tightly woven; no big mesh or “transparent” parts.

- **Footwear**:  
  - **Flat-bottom boots**, not heels or anything that focuses pressure.
  - **Exceptionally good grip**: deep rubber tread pattern.
  - The glass surface looks strikingly smooth; but for traction it’s better to be over-prepared than to slide at the boundary edge.

### 2.2 Safety harness and tether (for edge work)

If you plan to go near the edge of the plate:

- **Climbing harness** plus a **sturdy, stretchable tether** (dynamic rope as a simplified model).
- One end firmly attached to you; the other end to:
  - A “fixed anchor” you brought from home (hard metal carabiner and “ice screw” type device or similar).
  - You *do not* anchor to anything that the world itself might decide to reinterpret (especially crystal trees).

Keep the tether reasonably short (e.g., 2 meters), so you physically *cannot* casually step into the transitional edge.

### 2.3 Eye protection

- **Polarized sunglasses with clear optics**  
  - The world is full of facets, surfaces, and crystalline light redirection.  
  - You want to preserve the contrast and color but not be blinded or “taxed.”

### 2.4 Mask and respirator (optional but smart)

Bring a light **mask or filter**:

- Not for air toxicity (the air is not “poisonous”).  
- But to protect your lungs from accidentally:
  - Inhaling small particles if you disturb *anything*.
  - “Over-tasting” the mineral-tickle air.

Use a simple particulate filter, not chemical filters.

## 3. Hydration and “wetness” support

### 3.1 Drink before; hydrate *during* minimal time

Even though the world feels “breathable,” the absence of water is a serious issue:

- Drink **a full liter of water** within the hour before entering.
- Take care **not** to drink so much you need to pee within the time window (unless you’re prepared to handle that waste; see 3.2).
- Bring:
  - **Small sealed container of water** you can sip from through a hose or straw without removing mask.  
    - Sip only *enough* to keep your mouth from feeling dry and to maintain mental clarity.
    - You don’t actually want to drink large quantities here; water is for “shortening” your visit, not extending it indefinitely.

### 3.2 Waste handling

If you might need to pee during the visit:

- Use a **simple, externally sealed urine container/bag** that you carry out.
  - Be absolutely strict: **no spillage**.  
  - You don’t know how the world’s substrate that “never knew water” might react to it.

## 4. Equipment and tools

### 4.1 Timekeeping device (critical)

- **Wristwatch with good illumination**  
  - Optional: second timer counting down from (e.g.) 90 minutes.
- **Backup**: a small second device or “timer token” that vibrates or beeps at your preset limit.
- Why:  
  - Without time-of-day or color changes of the world, your internal clock is unreliable.  
  - You need *an external anchor to your own universe’s time stream*.

### 4.2 Light source

Even though there is “light everywhere,” bring:

- A **headlamp or small torch** with:
  - **Warm white light**, not harsh blue.
  - Multiple modes including low (to avoid “shouting” into the world’s visual system).

Use it to see:
- The edges of your body and gear.
- The ground texture clearly to avoid tripping.
- Your *return* gate or anchor point.

### 4.3 Mapping device (not electronic)

Bring:

- A **small notebook** and **pencil**.
  - Make schematic drawings of your path:
    - “From here, I see three crystal forks; then the clear lane curves.”
  - Don’t try to map everything.
  - Just mark:
    - Your entry point.
    - The main line you followed to get here.
    - One or two clear landmarks.

You can’t rely on GPS or anything that depends on a world–network. This map is for *human memory*, not “satellite navigation.”

### 4.4 Probes and contact tools

- **Short, non-metallic walking stick or probe**:
  - Use to test the ground in front of you, especially near the edge of the plate.
  - Do not prod crystals aggressively or “tap” them repeatedly as if debugging code. You are not the debugger.

- **Thin polycarbonate sheet or plate**:
  - If you want to test the “solidness” of the edge visually:
    - Lay it flat on the surface near the edge.
    - Place a small weight on it.
    - Observe *what* the edge *doesn’t do*.
  - This is more of a “scientific curiosity” posture than survival advice.

### 4.5 Recording and “grounding” tokens

- **Simple camera**:
  - Avoid excessive video recordings; a few **still photos** are preferable:
    - You don’t want to be “always rendering” the world into another device; it might be perceived as noise.
- **One tiny object from home**:
  - A coin, key, or small token you always wear.
  - Touch it periodically and remind yourself: “This is from *there*; I will go back *there*.”

## 5. Navigation and route planning

### 5.1 Have a clear protocol

- Define **three modes**:  
  1. **Safe mode**:  
     - Remain within (e.g.) 30 meters of the **center** of the plate.
     - Do not cross near any edges.
     - Do not touch anything crystalline.
  2. **Exploration mode**:  
     - Wander up to 100 meters away, guided by straight lines and clear paths.
     - No approaching edges intentionally.
  3. **Edge mode (advanced only, with harness and backup)**:  
     - Short visits to the edge with tethers and safety protocol.

As first-time visitor, **strictly safe mode**.

### 5.2 Pick “landmarks” but don’t trust them completely

Because the world subtly rearranges itself:

- Use landmarks as:
  - Reminders of the *type of pattern you just passed through*, and not exact coordinates.
- If you notice “interface drift,” where the scene reframes itself:
  - Check your map and notebook:
    - “Did I cross three forks of crystal trees and then a clear lane?”  
    - Even if the tree-configurations are different now, *you keep it simple*.

### 5.3 The “return” loop

Before you move:

- Look behind you frequently.
- Keep track:
  - “If I turn 180°, does what I see roughly match the ‘pattern’ of my incoming path?”  
  - If not, don’t panic—just backtrack *slowly*, using general direction rather than exact geometry.

## 6. Interaction with the world’s structures and “life”

### 6.1 Look, but don’t touch without planning

Basic heuristic:

- **You may look closely.**
- **Do not touch unless you have a reason and a method.**
- **Never break, peel, or “test” durability of any crystalline structure.**

Why?

- The “plants” are not plants; they are **data structures made manifest**.
- “Growth” you see is like debugging, code rewriting, or configuration rebalancing.
- If you interfere:

  - At worst, you could be interpreted as a “corrupting signal” and trigger:
    - A sudden state-change of a nearby region.
    - Unintended re-render or boundary shift.

### 6.2 “Feeling” textures at a distance

If you experience “velvet moss” from a distance:

- Notice that sensation is *independent of contact*.
- **Do not panic**:  
  - This is the world trying to render you into its own interface system.
  - Your job is to acknowledge it and then:
    - Move gently away.
    - Re-ground yourself in your body and harness.

### 6.3 “Creature” motes

If you notice “malfunctions in rendering” that feel like faunal glints:

- Don’t chase them.
- Don’t try to “catch” them.
- Don’t interpret them as entities “watching” you—unless your pattern-recognition system clearly trends to paranoia, then ignore entirely.

## 7. Mental and spiritual safety

### 7.1 Periodic grounding checks

Every (e.g.) 10 minutes:

- Stop.
- Breathe slowly in and out three times.
- Name:
  - Who you are.
  - Where home is.
  - What time you arrived here.
  - What time your device says you must leave.

### 7.2 Beware of “being part of it”

You will feel:

- That lines orient to you.
- That the world seems to *measure* you.
- That your presence might be “recorded” as a new node of geometry.

This is not wrong. But:

- **You are borrowing from the world’s attention, not merging with it.**
- If you start thinking:
  - “This place is more real than home.”
  - “Maybe I should stay longer, just a bit.”
  → That’s the time to **leave immediately**.

### 7.3 “This is not a better world; it is a different type”

Add to your pre-written narrative:

- “This is a beautiful world but not my *home* world.”
- “My home world is where I have friends, long-term projects, water, and change.”
- “This place is a library or archive; I am not its data.”

Read that before departure and, if needed, during the visit.

## 8. Physical edge safety protocol

### 8.1 Approach technique

If you must approach the edge of the plate (for exploration or photography):

- **Lie down or crouch** low to the glass surface as you get close.
- This lowers:
  - Your center of mass.
  - The chance of tipping over accidentally.
  - The “vertical signal” you present to the world.

### 8.2 Use your tether correctly

- Attach yourself **before** you reach the last 10 meters.
- Have someone else from your group hold the anchor end *on your side of the boundary line* if you travel with a team.
- **Never test** “what happens if I lean over”:
  - Treat the edge as you would treat a solid, uncapped industrial pipe into vacuum.

### 8.3 “Into the non-space”

What you see “below” is not a drop:

- It’s a boundary where “into” ceases to be defined.
- If something falls in:
  - It is not falling “down” but “out of rendering.”
- You should not attempt to throw or drop anything into the void:
  - You don’t know if the world’s logic can interpret that as:
    - A signal.
    - A request.
    - A kind of “pollution.”

## 9. The “living” soundscape: code and noise

### 9.1 Listen, but do not engage deeply

The hum you hear is:

- The world’s **configuration file being recited aloud**, in a sense.
- Not meant for you—possibly not meant for any listener at all.

It’s fine to:

- Record short snippets.
- Notice that it’s structured like blocks of code.
- **Not** fine to:
  - Play it back at high volume while you are still *there*.
  - “Answer” it with your own sounds or attempts to algorithmically match it.

### 9.2 Use your own voice to anchor reality

If you feel disorientation:

- Speak aloud (softly):
  - “I am [name]. I am wearing [color] suit. The time is [time] and my exit is in [minutes].”
  - Stark, simple statements of fact.

## 10. Power and technology management

The world’s “hum” of hidden machines:

- Does not mean there are “visible” machines.
- Does imply there are **processing systems**.

So:

- **Switch off  unnecessary electronics.**
- **If you must use electronic devices:**
  - Keep them shielded:
    - In simple metal containers (like foil-lined pockets).
  - Expose them only for short, necessary uses:
    - Taking a photo.
    - Checking time.
  - Watch out for:
    - Screens flickering in patterns not aligned with your hardware.
    - Battery or time readings behaving unreasonably.

If you see “wrong rendering” of your own screen contents:

- **Put the device away and relocate a few meters away without interacting further.**

## 11. Physical health and body monitoring

No fauna means no bites or standard infections, but:

- Fatigue can still be:
  - **Dehydration-related**
  - **Sensory overload**
  - **Time disorientation**

Keep track of:

1. **Dry mouth:**  
   - If you feel noticeably dry, *it’s time to start planning your exit*, not just time to sip more.
2. **Headache or vertigo:**  
   - Could be from:
     - Overlooking complex geometries.
     - Stress from seeing “multiple versions” of objects.
   - Shorten your visit.

3. **Breathing changes:**  
   - Slow and shallow is fine, but hyperventilation or panic means:
     - Stop, sit down, *ground* yourself.
     - Use your mental anchor script.

## 12. Ethical and contamination considerations

Because you are visiting an engineered reality:

- **Do not leave anything behind.**  
  - No trash.
  - No tools.
  - No water droplets.
  - No data-storage devices.
- **Do not take anything away.**  
  - No crystal shards.
  - No “samples” of the lattice.
  - No matter how tempting they seem.

Even if it seems inert:

- You don’t know how its codebase might react to being moved into a world with *sun*, *water*, and *time*.

### 13. Final check-list

Before you step onto the glass plate:

1. **Have:**
   - Written identity/time reminder.
   - Neutral, full-coverage clothing.
   - Gripped boots.
   - Safety harness & tether if going near edge.
   - Time-keeping device(s).
   - Small water container with sealed sip system.
   - Pencil and notebook.
   - Grounded, simple token from home.
   - Headlamp.
   - (Option) Mask and filter.
2. **Set:**
   - Hard time limit.
   - A clear rule of how close to edges you allow yourself to go.
   - A simple protocol for what you do if you feel disorientation (return immediately).
3. **Promise yourself:**
   - You will not extend the stay spontaneously.
   - You will not “test” the world’s rules as if you are immune.

### 14. What it might do to you afterward

This place is a “fixed instant of being.” That doesn’t mean it’s *trivial* to walk out of unchanged.

- You might notice:
  - Your world feels “too watery” for a time.
  - You feel strangely comforted by rain and wind.
  - Geometric patterns in your world (like architecture or tiles) suddenly seem more alive than before.

You might also:

- Remember more details than you expected—like suddenly recalling the exact code-blocks of the world’s “humming,” as if your brain were storing a file it wasn’t meant to.
- If that happens, write it down *after* return and then:
  - **Treat it as a travel report**, not as a new program you should run.

---

### 15. In short, what you must remember

- Think of this place as **a highly curated database of reality**, not a country.
- You are **not meant to live there**, but you can **visit as a clean, low-impact, observer process**.
- The main dangers are:
  - Edges and “out of rendering” drops.
  - Time and sense-of-self drift.
  - The absence of water and entropy.
- Your safety plan:
  - **Time limit**, **external timer**, **simple gear**, **no contamination**, **mental anchors**, and a **clear exit intent**.

If you prepare this way, your visit can be:

- A short, shining exposure to a world built of code and crystal and desire,  
  where the rain never falls and where “now” is not aging but only being readjusted—  
  and you can emerge from it again, into your own world of erosion and water and change, *with all of your “self” file intact*
