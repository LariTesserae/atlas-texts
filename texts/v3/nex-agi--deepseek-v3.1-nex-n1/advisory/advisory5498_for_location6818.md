To prepare to visit this City of Gongs at the edge of everything, you first need to accept that you are not going on an ordinary expedition to an extreme environment on Earth. You are stepping into a reality built around different priorities—resonance, pattern, and perception—rather than around biology, chemistry, or weather as you know them.

### 1. Mindset: Reality Will Be Subtle and Unstable

Your most important tool is not a backpack or a device, but your mind.

- **Expect “normal” to fail quietly.**  
  You are not going to see dramatic storms or monsters or collapsing buildings. Instead, your reality-check will fail in subtle ways:
  - You might remember a structure looking slightly different a moment ago.
  - You might notice a “flaw” in the air that doesn’t fit.
  - The sky will look like it has been broken and repaired badly.

  Prepare to accept ambiguity without forcing resolution. If you insist on everything being fixed and solid, you will feel increasingly wrong here.

- **Accept that you are not the center.**  
  This city is not hostile to you, but it is not designed for you. It doesn’t care about your symbolism, your emotional needs, or your narrative. It’s a system of resonant patterns that has existed on long time scales. For you, it’s more like being inside a piece of music than inside a city.

- **Understand the nature of its name.**  
  The name of the city is not a word you can memorize. It reveals itself as a low vibration in your chest whenever you are quiet enough. Before you go, train:
  - Meditation or breathing exercises that help you notice internal body sensations.
  - Familiarization with low frequencies or deep music (like singing bowls, gongs, or very deep bass tones) so your body recognizes “name by vibration” rather than “name by label.”

### 2. Understanding the Environment: What Harm Actually Looks Like Here

On a surface level, the city sounds “cold but survivable.”  
But the danger isn’t temperature in the usual way—it’s *time, scale, and information*.

- **Temperature will be mild but deeply wrong.**  
  - The air feels “cool,” not frozen, but:
    - There is no visible breath mist.
    - No humidity to catch in your throat.
  - This means your body’s usual signals about cold are not reliable.
    - You might chill more than you realize because there’s no moisture, no frost, no wind biting.
    - Wear layers of thin, breathable materials that you can add or remove methodically, rather than relying on feeling “cold enough.”

- **Water is absent from the environment.**  
  There is no rain, rivers, lakes, or groundwater.  
  - Any moisture you bring is your *only* moisture.
    - Bring more than you think you need, but:
    - You won’t be losing it through sweat as much as you’d expect.
    - You’ll likely forget to drink because there is no visual humidity, no sign of thirst.
  - Carry a hydration schedule: timer or reminders to drink at fixed intervals, regardless of sensation.

- **The sky is dangerously broken.**  
  - You see cracks of light that cut across the sky dome. An ordinary human might panic:
    - “What if that cuts down?”
  - The city’s physics *allows* discrepancies: reality appears to adjust around you, but prolonged fear of the sky may be emotionally destabilizing.
    - Prepare with a psychological practice that lets you tolerate the sky not behaving like sky. Think of it as a “screen with defects” rather than a piece of architecture above you.

- **Time is deceptive and elastic.**  
  - You “walk for a long time without much thirst or hunger or pain.”
    - This doesn’t mean your needs have gone.
    - It means that the environment’s *information* influences your interior body sense.
  - Bring:
    - A reliable timer that doesn’t depend on the sky or the environment (a battery-powered device, not a phone that might sync to local strangeness).
    - Use it to force:
      - Eating intervals
      - Rest intervals
      - Logging intervals (time feels slippery; a journal keeps track).

### 3. What to Bring: Minimal Tools Designed for Non-Hostility

This isn’t a survival expedition against the environment; it’s a visit to a strange performance that’s happening on cosmological timescales.

- **Clothing and protection**
  - Thin, thermal layers rather than heavy armor-like gear:
    - Base layer that wicks moisture if you have any.
    - Insulation adjusted layer-by-layer.
    - Windbreaker: not because there’s a storm, but because there might be sudden *changes* in pressure or texture.
    - Gloves and footwear that are firm but flexible:
      - You’ll be climbing steps too wide for your legs, so you need good grip but also feel for texture shifts.
      - Add soles that are relatively thin (so you can sense the stone’s faint vibrations).

- **Water and food**
  - Calorie-dense foods that don’t require cooking:
    - Bars, nuts, sealed goods.
  - Water in containers that:
    - You can sip without needing to open very much (to reduce evaporation in the arid air).
    - Are clearly marked with total volume and a schedule.
    - Don’t depend on finding local water sources (because there are none).

- **Sensory tools**
  - Sunglasses or tinted visor:
    - Not to block sunlight, but:
    - The sky’s “regions,” auroras, and cutting lights can be jarring.
  - A notebook and pen:
    - Not just to record facts but to draw:
      - Changes of patterns you observe.
      - Diagrams of things you suspect might be shifting when you aren’t looking.
    - This makes your visit a research practice rather than a drifting experience. Patterns behave like language.

- **Support instruments without technology dominance**
  - A small, personal set of tuning forks or a chime:
    - To match the metallic tones of the city with something of your own.
    - This might be the closest you can get to “language” with the environment.
    - Not to prove anything but to help your body resonate with the place instead of resisting it.

  - If you bring any electronics:
    - Expect them to behave unpredictably near towers or in the air:
      - Popping, skipping, time recording drift, etc.
    - Don’t depend on them for survival or navigation.

- **Health and comfort**
  - Lip balm and skin cream:
    - The air is dry enough to mimic a vacuum; your skin and lips can crack without you feeling immediate pain.
    - Use regularly, not “when you feel dryness.”
  - Basic first-aid kit:
    - Focused on cuts (from handling strange crystals), slipped-foot injuries, dehydration.
  - Light mask or eye covering:
    - The sky and light patterns are constant; you need to simulate “night” for sleep to help your biological rhythms.

### 4. How to Move and Interact Safely

#### a. On the black stone steps
- The steps are wide; “normal” human climbing can’t match them.
  - Use:
    - Small steps, not trying to mimic the steps’ scale.
    - Frequent pauses rather than long strides.
    - A rhythm: two-step climbing, rest, look around.
- **Trust the stone but don’t expect it.**  
  - It’s smooth, not slippery by design, but its texture may change subtly depending on what is happening in the sky.
  - If you feel a deep resonance in your chest or the stone under your feet, stop:
    - Let the vibration pass through you.
    - Don’t try to interpret it as danger.

- **Don’t climb the steps as if they are a mountain to be conquered.**  
  They might be infinite, or there might be no “top.”
    - Set a limit for yourself:
      - A time limit (e.g., turn around at exactly X hours).
      - Or a pattern of turns:
        - Turn left at three terraces, then three more right, etc., to keep yourself in a loopable geometry.

#### b. With the crystalline “vegetation”
- Small glass-like ferns grow from the cracks, pulsing with cold light.
  - They *react to you* but not as you might expect.
  - When you approach:
    - They retract slowly.
  - When you step away:
    - They slowly emerge again.
- **Handle them like especially shy, very brittle instruments.**  
  - Don’t try to pick or break them.
  - Don’t try to “feed them” or touch them with tools.
  - Don’t try to interpret their retraction as fear or affection.
  - If you must interact, try:
    - Standing one body-length away.
    - Watching them retract and emerge.
    - Noticing whether their rhythm changes when you are nearby versus absent.

- **They might “time you” without you meaning to.**  
  - Their pulses cycle irregularly—sometimes minutes, maybe hours.
  - If you want to, use:
    - An interval timer to record their cycles.
    - This can be a way to measure time locally.

#### c. With the “fauna”: phenomena, not animals
- You might encounter:
  - Flaws in the air that move in deliberate paths.
  - Lines like scratches on glass that appear, rearrange, and vanish.
  - They are not pets or threats; they are repeat patterns in the world’s behavior.
- **Treat them like passing traffic.**
  - Don’t try to touch them.
  - Don’t assume they are conscious of you.
  - If you notice:
    - A tower brighten when a flaw passes near it,
    - Or surfaces receive patterns from invisible sources,
    - Respect that this is *their* infrastructure.

### 5. Time and Perceptions: How Not to Lose Yourself

There are three major risks for the human mind:

1. **Forgetting bodily needs.**
2. **Being disoriented by time elasticity.**
3. Being slowly drawn into the city’s pattern until “you” become less distinct.

#### a. Defending your body’s needs
- Use physical indicators:
  - Timers for:
    - Eat at least every X hours.
    - Drink at least every Y hours.
    - Check skin and lips for dryness at fixed intervals.
  - Before you set off:
    - Predefine a “red line”:
      - “If I show [signs of hypothermia, dehydration, confusion, blisters], I return immediately.”

- **Sleep discipline**
  - The sky never truly darkens; you won’t get “sleepy by sunset.”
  - Set:
    - Hard “sleep shifts” at your own timer.
    - Use an eye mask or other darkening to simulate night.
  - If you don’t, your body might lose track of day-night cues and slide into a less defined, slightly drifting state.

#### b. Defending against time distortion
- Keep a *journal with time stamps.*
  - For each entry:
    - Write external time (from your device).
    - Your subjective impression of how long you felt you were walking.
  - Over time:
    - You’ll see patterns of how much time seems stretched or compressed.
    - This can be your warning sign:
      - If you begin to write “I felt only minutes pass but my timer shows three hours,” and you realize that’s happening more and more, you’re starting to drift with the city’s sense of time.

- **Don’t argue against your own records.**  
  If you caught yourself:
    - “No, that timer must be wrong,”
    - But the pattern repeats,
    - That’s a cue to treat yourself as being affected.

#### c. Not dissolving into the city’s pattern
- The city of gongs doesn’t need you to do anything, but it might *accept* you slowly.
  - You might notice:
    - Movements more subtle than your body.
    - Patterns that you could imitate if you just stayed longer.
    - The thought of “returning” beginning to feel unreal.
    - Your own habits of thought losing emotional urgency.

- To keep a boundary between yourself and the city’s endless symphony:
  - **Daily habit of remembering your world.**
    - Each morning (your timer), say:
      - Where you came from.
      - Who you are leaving behind.
      - A concrete reason why you must return.
    - Write down:
      - A concrete plan of *return* (time limit, resource threshold).

  - **Bring a small, very personal object.**
    - It doesn’t have to be magical or special:
      - A ring from a family member,
      - A ticket from where you came from,
      - A piece of paper with someone else’s handwriting.
    - Each day, look at it:
      - Let it remind you that you are not native to this geometry.

  - **Don’t try to become the city’s “shaman.”**
    - You’re not likely to become its master; more likely, you’ll be a slowly fading pattern in its memory.
    - Leave your mark on paper in your travel pack, not on the landscape itself.

### 6. On the Sky: How Not to Break Inside

When you look up, you might see:

- Brittle regions that might look shatterable.
- Thicker regions that seem to move.
- Occasional “cuts” of light across the sky fabric.

The key: **You don’t control the sky.**

- **You don’t throw stones upwards.**  
  - Or shine strong lights up.
  - Or try to “test” the broken sky with your tools.
  - The sky’s brokenness is not for your repair or entertainment.

- **If you feel strong anxiety around the sky:**
  - Limit your time staring directly upward.
  - Use peripheral vision instead of looking straight up.
  - Use your notebook:
    - Draw the sky section by section instead of staring.

- **If you see the “ribbon of light” from the distant darkness:**
  - Don’t try to reach it.
  - Don’t build your meaning around it.
  - Write it down:
    - Observed at time [X] from terrace [Y].
    - You might notice that if you keep track, you’ll see patterns over time.

### 7. Practical Rituals Before, During, and After

#### Before you leave:
- Write three questions:
  1. “What am I going to look for?”
  2. “What am I going to keep track of?”
  3. “What will make me turn back?”
- These are not deep questions; they are *concrete*:
  - Example: “I will look for patterns in the sky’s broken sections.”
  - “I will keep track of how long my body timer says vs how my journal feels.”
  - “I will turn back if my water drops below 1/3 or if I lose more than two days of clarity.”

#### During:
- Each time you stop:
  - Note:
    - Current external time.
    - Subjective time since last stop.
    - Water and food consumed since last entry.
    - Any unusual body sensation:
      - Inner vibration, humming, pressure in ears, etc.
    - Any pattern change you noticed in the city:
      - Shifted tower angles,
      - New lights in towers,
      - New glyphs on surfaces.

- At least once each “day”:
  - Look at your personal object.
  - Read your previous day’s entries:
    - See if you are drifting from your stated goal.

#### After you return:
- The city might have “remembered” you a bit.
  - You might:
    - Feel a distant vibration in your chest at times.
    - See glass patterns when you close your eyes.
    - Notice memories of certain sky shapes never quite leave.
- Do:
  - Reintegration with familiar life:
    - Ground yourself in tactile things:
      - Food textures, water, rain, earth, plants, conversation.
  - Write down:
    - What you understood you’d done.
    - Not to “publish” but to keep the boundary between:
      - The world where you have a body,
      - And the world where that might not matter so much.

### Final Summary

You visit the City of Gongs at the edge of everything as a temporary listener, not as a builder or hero or savior. The city is already complete.

- Prepare your body:
  - For dryness, thin air, and subtle cold.
  - For the need to drink and eat on scheduled time rather than by immediate feeling.

- Prepare your senses:
  - For a sky that doesn’t behave like a sky.
  - For sound that is more vibration than noise.
  - For patterns that might shift when you’re not looking.

- Prepare your mind:
  - To accept ambiguity without certainty.
  - To keep track of time and reality with tools rather than trusting your own feeling.
  - To choose deliberately to remain a temporary visitor rather than becoming a pattern in the city’s long memory.

If you understand these things and respect them as you would respect any vast instrument, you can visit the City of Gongs without losing yourself to the ice of its broken sky or the low hum of its name in your chest.
