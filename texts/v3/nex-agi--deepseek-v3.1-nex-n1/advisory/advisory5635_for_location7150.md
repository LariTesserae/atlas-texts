To visit Marrow Flats is to step into a machine that has finished running but has forgotten to turn itself off. It’s not hostile to you, but it is indifferent in a way that Earth’s harshest deserts are not: Earth *has* weather, geology, and life; Marrow Flats has *equilibrium*. Everything is already over and the landscape is just the echo that’s decided to stay.

This means your preparation is less about “survival against the environment” and more about “mental endurance against endless sameness.”

Below is a practical guide.

---

## 1. What you actually need to bring

### 1.1. Time sense and tracking devices

- **Cosmic transit:** a standard multiverse key, coordinates tuned to (rough) “Marrow Flats – sameness zone.” Your departure anchor should be set to a stable world; the Flats will not give you clear natural reference points.
- **Multiple clocks:** bring *two or three independent timepieces*.  
  - A wristwatch with analog display and 24-hour dial.
  - A digital count-up timer.
  - A small sand clock or mechanical timer so you can see time passing physically.  
  Why? Because there is no day/night, no sunrise or set; the light is always the same, and “how long” you’ve been here will blur without anchoring devices.
- **Notebooks:**  
  - One small water-resistant notebook for logging *time* (time in, time of each rest break);  
  - One journal for impressions and notes. This is not overkill: the environment claims novelty by erasing it from your mind.

### 1.2. Clothing and personal protection

- **Layering system (but not “warm” layers):**
  - Thin base layer of synthetic or quick-drying material.
  - Lightweight long sleeves and trousers, to reduce exposure to the brine-mist and any possible residues from the gray films.
  - Outer shell: waterproof but *very breathable* windbreaker or rain jacket; the air is warm, but the moisture and condensation can be relentless.
  - Hat with a brim to keep mist off your face and give a sense of “dome above” instead of “endless sky-shell.”
  - Sunglasses—even though there’s no sun, the unchanging diffuse light can cause glare fatigue.

- **Footwear:**
  - Boots or tough shoes with thick soles and puncture protection. The gravel is mostly worn smooth but there are glass and metal shards everywhere.
  - Gaiters or snug trousers over boot tops to keep small stones and dust out of your shoes.
  - Bring spare socks and blister care.

### 1.3. Shelter and bedding

- **Shelter:** a lightweight, single-person tent with a built-in groundsheet and good mesh ventilation to trap condensation rather than allow it to drip onto you.
- **Ground insulation:** a thin, full-length sleeping mat or pad. The ground is hard gravel and compacted silt; the dampness comes through quickly.
- **Sleeping system:** a light sleeping bag or blanket liner. The air is warm but the constant dampness can make you feel clammy and cold at rest.

- **Why shelter:** The world doesn’t “storm” or “get worse,” so there’s no need to stormproof the camp. But you need a small, defined space of your own to counter the endless visual monotony.

### 1.4. Food and water

- **Water from home:**  
  - Bring at least 3–5 days of clean water from your own world, in durable containers, plus a lightweight purification filter or tablets.  
  - The local water is turbid and mineral-rich; you might technically *survive* drinking it, but your gut will not thank you, and there’s no medical help.

- **Food:**
  - Simple, compact, high-energy foods that don’t require elaborate cooking:  
    - Nut bars, trail mix, dried fruit, jerky or vegan equivalents.  
    - Instant meals that can be prepared with just hot water from a small camp stove.  
  - A small, stove-efficient system for boiling water.  
  - Enough for your planned stay plus a margin of error (1.5–2 times).

- **Important:** The world has no plant life for you to forage, and the “fauna” are not animals you can kill or eat; they are machines of bone and rust. Assume you cannot eat *anything* from this environment safely.

## 2. Gear and tools for the environment

### 2.1. Protection against the films and dust

- **Light gloves:** to protect your hands if you kneel to touch the silt or handle stones or film-covered surfaces.
- **Basic respirator or mask:** not full hazmat, but capable of filtering fine dusts and metallic particles suspended in the air.
- **Eye protection:** besides sunglasses, a pair of safety goggles in case dust or wind becomes irritating.

- **Treat the “vegetation” films as lab unknowns:**  
  - Don’t touch them with bare skin any more than necessary.
  - Don’t scrape them into your environment or sleeping space.
  - If you collect samples, use sealed vials or containers and keep them away from your food and water.

### 2.2. Tools for site and self

- **Navigation:**
  - GPS with preloaded waypoints to your arrival point, if your device still locks to other-worlds reference frames;  
  - Mag compass (bearing in mind that local magnetic fields may be strange or boring).
  - Simple luminous marking pegs or tape: to dump small, temporary lines of reference points when you explore away from camp—but you *will* lose them if you don’t keep track, and the world does not help landmarks persist.

- **Lighting:**
  - The sky never darkens; but your head hates “never-darkness” as much as they hate “never light.”  
    - Bring a headlamp with a red bulb or setting to simulate night where you need sleep, and a small lantern to define “inside” inside your tent.
    - Consider eye masks or hoods to block light when you sleep.

### 2.3. Personal first aid and wellbeing

- **Basic first-aid kit,** including:
  - Antiseptic wipes and alcohol.
  - Bandages and blister tape.
  - Small broad-spectrum antibiotic from your own world’s pharmacy.
  - Painkillers and anti-inflammatory.

- **Psychological support:**  
  - This is not optional. The sameness of the environment, the constant low hum, and the absence of change will wear on you.  
  - Bring:
    - A small object from home with a strong emotional association (picture, token, letter).
    - A planned routine of meditations or songs to “change the weather inside your head,” since the world outside won’t.

## 3. How to move around without damaging yourself

### 3.1. Walking on Marrow Flats

- **Expect nothing:** no paths, no roads, no cairns, no buildings, no signs.  
- The gravel tends to be a uniform, gentle arc with small folds and low ridges of silt near creeks.  
- **Technique:**
  - Use a steady, slow pace to avoid twisted ankles or accidental slipping on the loose stones.
  - Walk parallel to the “direction of gravel” when you can see the pattern of ridges and glass shards trending in one way; they align broadly like the stria of riverbed gravel.
  - Always look back as you walk away from your tent to create your own landmarks with memory and sight.

### 3.2. Crossing creeks and low wet areas

- The creeks are slow and tea-colored with metallic tangs; their banks are shallow and compacted silt or stable gravel bars.
- **Step from stable to stable:** use larger stones or hardened gravel; avoid the glittery slick of film-covered pebbles near the edges.
- **Never trust your own trench or dig:**  
  - If you try to redirect a creek or dig a trench for whatever reason, the creek will not stay in your new channel for very long.
  - The integrated “machine” of the plains will return the water to its own pattern. This is not mystical: it’s just gradient and geometry that have had endless time to settle.

### 3.3. Dealing with the “fauna”

- If you see a small machine about the size of your hand or forearm:
  - Don’t touch it.
  - Don’t corner it.
  - Don’t try to “communicate” by waving at it or speaking.
- Observe, but do not interfere:
  - It may stop, drill or probe the gravel, then proceed on its exact vector line.
- **Think of them as sensors or janitors of a system that does not include you.** They are not hostile, but interfering with them might be like sticking your hand into a machine that still loops quietly after the factory has been dismantled.

## 4. How to make camp (and not go insane)

### 4.1. Where to set up

- Choose somewhere:
  - Slightly elevated from the creek channels but within reach of water if you *really* need it.
  - Not immediately next to a crack or film where those “vegetation” sheets are present.
- Check your chosen spot:
  - Look for any cracks that seep film more actively than others.
  - Make sure the ground slopes just enough to shed the condensation instead of pooling under you.

### 4.2. Creating a little “micro-world” of change

- The world will not change for you. So you change your own micro-environment to keep a sane rhythm of difference:
  - Use **artificial cycles of light and dark**: when your clocks show evening time, dim lights in your tent or block the sky out with a mask to simulate night.
  - Use **defined routines**:  
    - Morning: check gear, stow water, note observations in logbook.  
    - Midday: walk, if you want to explore; then rest and eat.  
    - Evening: wind down with reading, writing, or music (if you bring headphones).  
    - Night: sleep at “night” even if the sky never changes.

### 4.3. Psychological strategies

- **Give yourself names for the nothingness:**
  - “Shell-light sky,” “engine song,” “rust-wet air,” “the long not-quite-blues.”
  - Writing small sequences of names like these will make you *notice* instead of just float in the drone of sameness.

- **Limit exposure to the “engine hum”:**
  - Bring earplugs or low-noise-canceling headphones; not for total silence (which can be eerie), but to reduce the vibration you feel in your teeth and chest.
  - Some gentle music or white noise on low can help differentiate the hours.

- **Set goals that are not about the world itself:**
  - “I will sketch ten different shards of glass.”
  - “I will map the local gravel pattern for one square of ground.”
  - “I will write a letter to someone I want to see again.”
  - These goals create “events,” which the world does not provide.

## 5. Health and safety precautions

### 5.1. Physical health

- **Watch for signs of damp-cold illnesses:** despite the warmth, the constant moisture and condensation can lead to persistent chills or joint aches if you’re not careful with your clothing layers.
- **Hydration and rest:**  
  - Even though you’re not “struggling against harsh weather,” the monotone of light and sound can confuse your body’s signals of tiredness and thirst.  
  - Rely on your schedule: drink at set intervals; sleep on set schedule.

### 5.2. Mental health

- **Recognize early signs of sameness-fatigue:**
  - Feeling time is “stalling” or “never moving.”
  - Feeling that “nothing matters” because nothing changes.
  - Strong urges to disrupt the landscape just to see change.
- **If you feel this:**
  - Stop, return to camp, and deliberately insert a change there:
    - Rearrange your pack.
    - Change the layout of your tent.
    - Write a new “page” of notes or sketch a map of the day you *haven’t* experienced.
  - Talk to yourself if you must; say “This is a world that has finished its becoming. It does not need to change for me.”

### 5.3. Sending help “back” if you go wrong

- **Leave an information stone in your origin world:**  
  - A small sealed, spell-tagged or otherwise-linked artifact with a message detailing:
    - Your coordinates to the Flats.  
    - The planned duration of your stay.  
    - A clear “no longer than this date before coming for me.”

- You **don’t** rely on the Flats to summon rescue; there’s no signal station, no beacon unit, and nothing local that will notice you are missing. The structure of the world itself does not care that you came.

## 6. Experiments and ethics while visiting

### 6.1. What you can do, if you want to study

- You can:
  - Collect *small* samples of film material in sealed containers.
  - Take notes of what “fauna” you see:
    - Direction of movement.
    - Time of “visit” near your camp.
    - Any repeating patterns over hours.

- **Rule of thumb:** If your change could be quietly erased by the next rain of mist and the next silt redistribution, it’s probably okay. The world will heal itself anyway. But don’t push more than that.

### 6.2. What you absolutely should not do

- Do not:
  - Build large structures or try to redirect water flow in ways that require digging deep channels or moving large quantities of gravel.
  - Harass the robotic fauna or try to dismantle them.
  - Intentionally expose large patches of film to open air in your living space.

- **The principle:** the Flats are not a blank canvas for you to “make your mark.” They are a finished machine in which your mark is only a temporary smudge. The world will not *fight* you, but it will exhaust you if you try to battle it.

---

## 7. Time spent and how to leave safely

### 7.1. Knowing how long to stay

- Decide before you go:
  - “I will stay no longer than X days.”
- Set alarms on your devices that:
  - Link back to an exit protocol or homing signal.
- If you feel:
  - “I could stay here forever and nothing would change,” that is your signal to leave *soon*, not to stay longer.

### 7.2. Leaving the Flats as you found them

- Pack out everything you brought:
  - Waste, plastic, cut wrappers, any artificial structures or devices installed.
- The world will:
  - Smooth out footprints and the impressions of your tent floor in the gravel.
  - Erase small trench lines or cairns through the raining of dust and mists.
- But it:
  - Will not recycle or digest plastics; you must take those with you.

---

### In summary

- Prepare physically: for *wetness*, not for dangerous weather.  
- Prepare mentally: for *time without events*, not for chaos.  
- Bring tools to *create* difference in your micro-world: clocks, routines, light changes, writing and drawing.  
- Treat the “world-machine” with respect; it is finished and indifferent to your presence but will not tolerate arrogance quietly.

You will not conquer the Marrow Flats. You will not become its hero or its villain. You will be a small, temporary motion in the stone of a finished engine whose last command has long since run out and left nothing but the hum of gears turning in place.

If you accept that and prepare carefully, you can walk that gravel plain without being broken by it.
