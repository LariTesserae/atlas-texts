Preparing to visit this place is less about “survival gear” and more about *recalibration*—of your body, your habits, your expectations of time and space.

---

## 1. Core Mindset

You are entering a world that has achieved a very stable, low-energy equilibrium. Think of it as a long, slow dance in a cold room: if you try to sprint, you will collide with everything.

Adopt a few mental principles:

- **Move like you belong.**  
  This city does not welcome “tourists.” It ignores the loud and the impatient. It rewards those who match its quietness and deliberation.

- **Expect sameness, not variety.**  
  There is no day/night drama. No weather surprises. No “changing seasons.” Expect a monotony of temperature, light, and sound that can be disorienting if you’re used to constant stimulation.

- **Accept integration over mastery.**  
  You don’t conquer this place. You are inserted into it like a new pebble in the riverbed. You will be shaped, not the other way around.

- **Do not fight the cold.**  
  You will not *warm* this place. You can only *insulate* yourself and *slow* your heat loss.

## 2. Clothing & Personal Protection

### 2.1. Layers (cold + damp)

Layering is non-negotiable. The key is *warmth + breathability*:

- **Base layer:**  
  - Synthetic or moisture-wicking merino wool.  
  - Avoid cotton at all costs—cotton kills in damp cold.
  - Long sleeves and long legs. Full body coverage.

- **Mid layers:**  
  - Thin fleece or wool pullover that can be added/removed easily.  
  - You won’t be overheating through exercise, but your body may generate more heat than you expect if you climb stairs.

- **Outer layer:**  
  - A windproof shell with some water resistance.  
  - Doesn’t need to be alpine-grade, but must be reliable against condensation and dripping eaves.
  - Hood that can snug around your face without ruining your peripheral vision (you will need all of it).

### 2.2. Extremities & Protection

- **Gloves:**  
  - Thin but warm gloves; liners if needed.  
  - You’ll be touching stone walls and moss. You do not want to lose sensitivity to moisture or temperature through your hands.

- **Head:**  
  - A warm hat or cap that can fit under a hood.  
  - Much of your heat loss will be from your head and neck.

- **Feet:**  
  - Waterproof or highly water-resistant boots with good grip.  
  - The soles must be able to grip on smooth, wet stone and moss-covered steps.
  - Insulated socks, but not too thick—you need circulation to keep feet warm.

- **Eyes & Face:**  
  - No need for sunglasses—the light is diffuse white-gray, not glaring sun.  
  - If your eyes are sensitive to monotone light, a very light tint might be used to reduce strain, but you want visibility in the dimness.
  - Consider a thin scarf or mask to help warm the air you breathe. Not vital at these temperatures, but it can make long walks less harsh.

### 2.3. Backpack & Containers

- **Small, comfortable daypack:**
  - Adjustable straps to stay snug as you climb stairs and narrow ways.
  - Keep your center of gravity close to your body; you will be squeezing through spaces.

- **Dry bags inside:**
  - Anything that must stay dry—maps, electronics, medicine, matches—goes in sealed dry bags.
  - Condensation and drips are constant.

- **Extra layers:**  
  - At least one spare base layer and socks.
  - If something gets damp, you must change it. Dampness = cold = danger.

## 3. Food & Water

### 3.1. Water

- **Bring your own filter and bottle or hydration bladder.**  
  - The water sources *might* be safe, but you are not adapted to this world’s microbiota.
  - Even if the locals drink directly from a channel or basin, you should not assume your body will tolerate it.

- **Use clear, enclosed containers:**  
  - Avoid open cups or unsealed bowls.  
  - You don’t want moss-related or insect life accidentally colonizing your water supply.

- **Practice sip discipline:**  
  - In constant cold and damp, you may feel less thirsty even though you need fluid.
  - Drink small, frequent sips rather than large gulps.

### 3.2. Food

- **Bring high-calorie, low-volume rations:**  
  - Nuts, trail mix, nutrition bars, jerky, dried fruit, chocolate.
  - You need quick energy for climbing and warmth, but not so much bulk that you feel slow.

- **Plan for self-sufficiency for at least a few days to a week.**  
  - The locals will likely share *some* food, but not in large quantities—everything is measured here.
  - Don’t count on food “generosity” as you would in some places. Theirs is a rationed world.

- **Pack in resealable, hard containers:**  
  - Avoid paper packaging; it will become sodden and useless.
  - Keep some food in your innermost layers to keep it from becoming cold and brittle.

## 4. Health & Medicine

### 4.1. Metabolic & Thermal Regulation

- **Monitor your body temperature:**  
  - Mild hypothermia can creep in if you sit still too long without adding insulation.
  - Signs: shivering, mental confusion, apathy, clumsiness.
  - Prevention: keep a layer on you if you stop to rest; don’t “push through” a cold stupor.

- **Keep core warm, not extremities only:**  
  - If your torso is warm, your body will send blood to your hands and feet.
  - Extra gloves + thick socks are useless if your core is cold.

### 4.2. Physical Strain & Injuries

- **Watch your knees and ankles:**  
  - All the walking is on stone, and often stairs.  
  - If you have bad knees, consider lightweight trekking poles or a cane for stability.

- **Basic first aid kit:**  
  - Bandages and gauze.
  - Antibiotic ointment or cream.
  - Anti-inflammatory pain meds.
  - Adhesive tape that can stick to slightly damp skin.
  - Any personal medications in more than the quantity you think you need.

- **Slips and falls:**  
  - Moss + stone + water = slippery surfaces everywhere.
  - Move deliberately, test each step. Expect to slip occasionally; try to fall *controlled*.

### 4.3. Infections & Skin

- **Cuts and scrapes:**  
  - Treat every small cut on stone or moss as infection risk.
  - Clean, cover, and monitor closely.

- **Fungal issues:**  
  - Keep your feet dry as much as possible.
  - Change socks frequently.
  - Use foot powder or antifungal cream if you’re prone to issues.

### 4.4. Mental & Circadian Health

- **Time disorientation:**  
  - Without a sun track or stars, you may lose track of how many days have passed.
  - Keep a simple wristwatch or small device to track time *by yourself*, if technology is available to you.

- **Sleep discipline:**  
  - Establish a “night” routine by your own timekeeping device or internal schedule.
  - Even dim light and constant noise/drip can be mildly sleep-disrupting.
  - Eyeshades or a cloth over your eyes may help, but they’re less important than noise and damp.

- **Monotony & depression:**  
  - The lack of sensory change can be subtly oppressive.
  - Plan mental activities: journaling, sketching, memorization, counting games, etc.
  - Keep in mind this is *their* normal, not a broken place.

## 5. Navigation & Safety

### 5.1. Spatial Sense

- **Expect to get lost.**  
  - The city is a vertical labyrinth of shared walls, roofs-turned-floors, and internal passages.
  - “Streets” are not gridlike; they are channels of air and water, often doubling back or ending.

- **Maps & tools:**  
  - If you can, sketch a map as you go.
  - Keep mental landmarks: particular carvings, a moss pattern, a unique basin in a wall, a specific roof garden.
  - Consider chalk or a water-safe marker to make tiny, *temporary* signs, but be respectful of the stone. If you mark anything, imagine it must remain for centuries.

- **River as orientation:**  
  - The river is your main axis of navigation.
  - Learn early to distinguish upstream from downstream.
  - “Toward the river” and “away from the river” are easier orientations than north/south in here.

### 5.2. Light & Visibility

- **Bring your own light source:**  
  - Even if the city lamps burn seed oil and moss, you may not be given continuous access.
  - Pack:
    - A headlamp (with red lens if possible, to preserve your night vision).
    - A backup light source.
    - Extra batteries or power.

- **Reflective markers:**  
  - Tiny reflective stickers (easily removed later) might help if you mark your own door or path along a corridor.
  - But: *ask* before you mark anything. Many stone surfaces are living documents to them.

### 5.3. Structural Safety

- **Don’t assume load-bearing capacity of roofs or terraces.**  
  - Some will be designed for gardens and people; others are not.
  - Watch for locals’ behavior: where they walk freely indicates where weight can be supported.

- **Mind the edges:**  
  - Terraces and roof-gardens often have no railings.
  - Vertigo can be intense—especially with mist hiding depth below.

## 6. Social & Cultural Preparation

### 6.1. Noise & Silence

- **Train yourself to quietness.**  
  - Speak softly, almost below conversational level, if you want to be heard at *their* frequency.
  - Loud footsteps, scraping packs, metal clinking, are disruptive in this muffled environment.

- **Footsteps matter:**  
  - Soft soles or leather are better than heavy boots for *sound*, but balance safety with noise.
  - If your shoes are loud, try to move more slowly and deliberately.

### 6.2. Language & Communication

- **Prepare for minimal speech:**  
  - Their language is tonal, low, and focused on necessity.
  - Learn basic concepts:
    - Water / need water / no water.
    - Safe / not safe.
    - Help / injured.
    - Permission / thanks.
    - Up / down / river / upstream / downstream.

- **Use gestures and repetition:**  
  - Show numbers with your fingers.
  - Show direction with hand movements.
  - Repeat what you think you’ve understood to confirm.

- **Listen more than talk:**  
  - You will learn the city by listening to its structure and flow: who brings where, when; channels and schedules; who repairs what.

### 6.3. Power & Hierarchy

- **Never assume you are entitled to:**
  - Water from a particular channel or basin.
  - Access to a rooftop garden.
  - “Guides” or explanations.

- **Do not seek “authorities” on the assumption of a centralized government.**  
  - This city likely operates by *agreements* and procedures, not by “mayors” or “kings.”
  - Authority is probably distributed over maintenance of channels, water levels, and schedules.

- **Avoid giving unsolicited advice:**  
  - This city has existed longer than your entire civilization, probably.  
  - Suggesting “improvements” to their technology or social structure will come across as arrogant noise, unless invited explicitly.

## 7. Tools & Utensils

Bring minimal tools that:

- **Don’t break their stone.**
- **Don’t contradict their low-tech world.**  
  If they see metal as a precious resource, you walking around with large shining gadgets might be uncomfortable.

- **Suggested kit:**
  - Small, multitool knife with pliers and screwdriver.
  - Small, durable folding knife or fixed blade for daily tasks.
  - Compact notebook and pencil (or waterproof paper/pen).
  - Lightweight mug or bowl with a lid.
  - Small metal container that can double as a pot or canteen, if open fires are allowed at all.
    - Note: open fires might be rare or controlled due to lack of ventilation and the risk of altering humidity conditions.

## 8. Interaction With the Environment

### 8.1. Water & Humidity

- **Don’t waste a single drop.**  
  - Every basin you see is placed deliberately.
  - Don’t wash your hands or gear directly *in* their basins unless you are sure it’s allowed.
  - Use water sparingly. If you’re offered a basin, use it the way they do.

- **Roofs and gardens:**  
  - Treat every garden as someone else’s livelihood.
  - Don’t walk on plants unless a path is clearly worn.
  - Don’t pick samples “just to look at them.” This isn’t a display case; it’s their food and insulation.

### 8.2. Stone & Structures

- **Don’t alter or “test” stone.**  
  - No scratching graffiti to see if it stays.
  - No “breaking off a piece” to take home.
  - Every stone surface is part of a carefully balanced system.

- **Walls are structure:**  
  - Don’t lean on anything you can’t see others leaning on.
  - Some stones are load-bearing in ways that are not obvious.

---

## 9. Leaving & Long-Term Presence

### 9.1. Before You Leave

You will likely not be permitted to just *settle*.

- **Ask permission:**  
  - If you want to stay longer than a brief visit, expect to negotiate:
    - What you can contribute (work, maintenance, sharing).
    - Where you can sleep.
    - What you will eat and drink.

- **Expect discipline to be required:**  
  - Staying in this city is like joining a monastery or a long-term research station: 
    - You are assigned to a role;
    - You follow a schedule;
    - You live beneath the technology of noise and speed.

---

### 9.2. Psychological Preparation for Departure

When you return from here:

- Your world will likely feel *terribly loud and bright*.  
- You may have trouble adjusting to sudden temperature changes, strong sunlight, and fast movement.
- Consider allowing yourself a transition interval:
  - Quiet spaces;
  - Reduced noise;
  - Gentle increase of light and social complexity.

---

## Final Thoughts: The Checklist

- **Mindset:** Quietness, patience, acceptance of constant cold and damp.
- **Body:**  
  - Proper layers for damp cold;  
  - waterproof boots with grip;  
  - gloves, hat, scarf.
- **Health:**  
  - First aid kit;  
  - foot care;  
  - meds for infections, allergies, and pain.
- **Food & water:**  
  - Own compact rations;  
  - sealed, filter-safe water bottles;  
  - sip discipline.
- **Navigation & safety:**  
  - Own light source;  
  - sketch map;  
  - river orientation;  
  - cautious movement on slippery stone.
- **Social & cultural tools:**  
  - Minimalist language kit;  
  - quiet demeanor;  
  - respect for channels and gardens.
- **Tools:**  
  - Minimal and durable;  
  - nothing loud or shiny unless needed.
- **Time:**  
  - Own timekeeping;  
  - sleep and mental routine.

If you prepare along these lines, you can be inserted into this valley-city like a new pebble: held quietly in place by the slow, steady pressure of its balance, rather than crushed by it.
