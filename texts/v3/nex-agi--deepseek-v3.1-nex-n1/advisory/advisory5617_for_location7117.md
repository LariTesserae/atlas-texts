Of all the places you might jump to on purpose, this one is close to the worst suited for a human being: a sealed, cold, wet machine-case at the top of a factory-city that was not designed for visitors, or even really for its own few human workers. It’s the kind of environment that wants you to stay out, and will grind you down gently but relentlessly if you don’t.

But you’re going anyway. So you’d better prepare *very* well.

Below is a practical survival guide for an ordinary human traveler stepping into that condensate‑drenched chamber at the roof of the world.

---

## 0. Mindset before gear

Before you even choose your kit, understand the kind of place this is:

- **It’s a total machine environment.** There is no ground to plant a seed, no outside to escape into, and no help coming from “below” unless you personally call it.
- **The cold is slow and persistent.** It’s not instantly flash‑freezing, but it’ll leach your warmth over time.
- **Everything is wet, but in a controlled way.** You will not drown. You will just be continually damp.
- **The creatures are everywhere but not obviously aggressive.** They’re like the room’s biological wiring. Do not antagonize them.
- **Time is weird.** There’s no day/night, and your senses will subtly distort under the unchanging light and sound.

This is not a place to be “brave” in. It’s a place to be methodical, cautious, and intellectually ready to be wrong about how things work.

## 1. Jump strategy and arrival point

### 1.1. Choose your arrival point carefully

You’ll materialize somewhere on the main floor of the chamber. That’s a hard floor: welded steel plating, maybe a quarter‑inch thick, over the girders of the city below.

Try to manifest:

- on **spare floor space**, not straddling a gutter or basin, and not under a low girder.
- **not directly inside a housing module** — you might fuse with the metal wall or the occupant.
- away from ladders you might be draped on if you pop in mid‑air.

The best target is the center of the “room” or the thick of the module-honeycomb where the grids are most repetitive and it’s likely to be empty. Somewhere that you can be sure not to be *already occupied by metal*.

### 1.2. Don’t expect a return jump quickly

This place might be hard to open out of. Physics there are strange enough for light to seem “sticky” and water to track along cables against its will. Your portal device may be slower than usual to prime.

- **Treat yourself as stranded for at least several days.** Plan for supplies and contingencies with that in mind.
- Bring **two redundant methods** of calling your exit (if you have them).
- Decide **minimum‑time contingency** in advance: if you can’t open a way out after X days or if you’re too injured to function, you trigger your emergency exit device even if it costs a lot.

### 1.3. Don’t count on being noticed or rescued

The humans here are likely not going to think of you as a visitor, but as a problem or a tool. Expect no help except from their own operational habits.

## 2. Physical environment: what it will do to you

### 2.1. Temperature and cold

- **Expect** high‑cold — think maybe 5–10°C / 40–50°F, but with windless dampness that makes it feel sharper.
- **Cold** comes from:
  - the metal conducting in from the outside night sky,
  - and the dampness making your skin lose more heat than it would in dry air.

Plan **insulated layers** and a plan to keep your core warm.

### 2.2. Constant dampness

- Everything is coated with condensation film.
- **You will sweat under your insulation** from exertion and from humidity, but the outside surface will be cold.

This is a classic **hypothermia‑by‑perspiration** scenario. You must be able to vent heat but keep dry.

### 2.3. Noise and vibration

- Deep, structural **humming** from the engines below.
- **You may feel it before you hear it clearly**. It will be in your chest and teeth.
- High noise over long periods will slowly fatigue you, making mistakes more likely.

### 2.4. Light and time sense

- **Unchanging low-level industrial light**:
  - pale yellow/orange lamps above each module door,
  - intense spotlights on specific machines above.
- **No natural cycle**. This severely messes with your circadian rhythm:
  - you’ll feel tired “on time” but the environment will never acknowledge that.
- **Lagging light**: the visual “after‑image” of machines staying longer than you’d expect.
  - Could be simple sensory overload, or something weird.
  - Assume you **might misjudge distances or motion**.

### 2.5. Altitude/pressure

- You might feel a **pressure imbalance** in your ears:
  - sense of tightness,
  - mild headache,
  - slightly harder to hear clearly.
- **Do not** violently pop your ears by holding your nose and forcing hard if you feel pain or pressure — that’s a recipe for burst drums.
- **Yawn**, gently swallow, or try gentle jaw movements to equalize.

### 2.6. Microbiological risks

- **Rust stain** where condensate runs is not necessarily a health hazard by itself, but:
  - a wet, cold machine‑environment with organic condensate might be hosting **mold, bacteria, or uni‑cellular inhabitants**.
- **Pale “lichen” stains**:
  - treat as unknown biology.
  - do not touch without barrier.
  - do not drink the water near them unless you sterilize it.

### 2.7. Antimicrobials and corrosion

- The air itself may be slightly corrosive:
  - **Your metal tools** will not rust away in hours, but some dulling and tiny localized spots are possible.

## 3. Clothing and armor: what to wear into the condensate cathedral

### 3.1. Layering strategy

Use **three-layer** system, but adapted for constant wetness and cold:

1. **Base layer: moisture-wicking, synthetic or merino wool**
   - **Function**: move sweat away from your skin quickly.
   - Avoid cotton; it stays wet and cold.
   - Long sleeve top and long pants.

2. **Mid layer: thin insulating layer**
   - Lightweight fleece or synthetic knit.
   - You can vent this by taking off the outer if you need to dump heat.

3. **Outer layer: waterproof and abrasion-resistant**
   - Use **hard shell** waterproof with sealed seams, not “water‑resistant”.
   - Some structural padding on elbows and knees wouldn’t hurt if you fall on steel plating.
   - Taped seams and high waterproof rating are worth the cost here.

### 3.2. Hands and feet

**Feet:**

- **Insulated waterproof boots**:
  - Steel toe or composite plate if possible (not for the city, for yourself when you kick something).
- **Socks**: 
  - Thin **liner** sock + thick wool **outer** sock.
- **Spare socks**: keep at least two pairs in waterproof bags inside your pack.
  - Change socks regularly to prevent maceration and trench foot.

**Hands:**

- **Thin synthetic inner gloves** for dexterity:
  - You want to be able to handle latches, small tools, and not slip on wet metal.
- **Insulated waterproof shell gloves** / mitts over:
  - When you’re not actively manipulating tools, you can keep these on.
- **Your glove liners must be synced with your sleeve**:
  - Don’t let water run down your arms into them.

### 3.3. Head and face protection

- **Close‑fitting toque or balaclava**:
  - You lose a lot of heat through your head and neck.
- **Lightweight mask or scarf**:
  - To pre‑heat air a bit before you breathe it.
- **Safety glasses or goggles**:
  - Condensation might drip from above or splash from your movement.
  - Corrosion byproducts or dust shaken loose from high machines could be irritating.

### 3.4. Overall: you’re not going to war, but you’re going under machine‑water

- Think of yourself like a **submarine/mechanic hybrid outfit**: waterproof, durable, not heavy duty armor, but ready to kneel on wet metal and not instantly freeze.

## 4. Foot and body safety on the wet floor

### 4.1. Walking surfaces

- Everything is **metal**:
  - flat plating on the “floor”,
  - **gutter channels between housing modules**,
  - **basins with short trumpet rims** where condensate gathers.
- **All of it is slick with water film.**

### 4.2. Footwear strategy

- Look for boots with **deep lugs and high-traction soles**:
  - something like mountaineering boots or industrial rubber boots with rugged pattern.
- **Avoid heavy smooth rubber soles** — they’ll be sliding on the film faster.

### 4.3. Movement technique

- **Shorten your stride**:
  - Less opportunity to slip on the wet steel.
- **Weight centerline over your feet**, especially going over the gutter channels.
- **Small test steps before full-weight transfer**:
  - If the floor feels unexpectedly slippery or unequally wet, shift to another path.
- **Avoid the edges of basins**:
  - Even if they are flared and not sharp, they’re likely wet, and falling into one of them is going to be cold, messy, and potentially loud or disruptive to creature behavior.

### 4.4. Fall protection

- **Knee pads**:
  - Very useful if you need to kneel to inspect or reach something.
  - A hard impact on metal is not pleasant.
- **Helmet**:
  - Even if just a lightweight industrial hard hat or reinforced climbing helmet:
    - protects from low headroom girders,
    - from unexpected things falling from gantries above (tools not properly tool‑tethered, if anyone else does maintenance),
    - from accidental contact with a machine or cable.

### 4.5. Don’t add “wrong” beings to the floor

- Don’t spill things:
  - chemical spills,
  - strong-smelling substances,
  - strange materials your world might think are harmless.
- **Everything that hits the metal floor will be channeled** by the condensate system:
  - some might end up in the basins where the creatures are drinking.
- Wipe your feet before sleeping on your own material surfaces so your gear doesn’t become contaminated.

## 5. Heat and shelter: fighting the cold‑sink

### 5.1. Body heat is your main resource

In the absence of fireplaces or trees to burn, you’re going to rely on:

- **good insulation**
- **safe metabolism**
- **maybe some electrical or chemical tool for supplementary heat**

### 5.2. Portable insulation (sleep system)

- **Closed‑cell foam pad**:
  - Provides insulation barrier between you and the metal floor.
- **Insulated sleeping pad**:
  - Something like an inflatable, insulated mat (not a simple yoga mat).
- **Light sleeping bag or insulated quilt**:
  - Rated for temperatures slightly below what you expect (maybe 0–5°C comfort).
  - Put it inside a waterproof bivy sack if you can:
    - keeps the condensation film off your bag.
    - keeps the bag from leaching heat into the wet metal floor through contact.

### 5.3. Chemical and electrical heat

- **Hand warmers**:
  - Not just for hands; they can be placed in your pockets or at core areas to keep your torso temperature up.
- **Small rechargeable heater**:
  - If your device tech can support a compact heater element, maybe a rechargeable or battery-powered one:
    - Use cautiously, because electrical fields might interact with the machine room and its creatures.
- **Emergency blanket (mylar)**:
  - Lightweight, reflective, and can help a lot with keeping heat in.

### 5.4. Adjusting exertion to avoid sweat

- When you have to move:
  - **Strip layers before you start climbing or hauling.**
  - Put them back on before you stop and cool down.
- Remember the paradox of cold:
  - **You are safest when you are slightly cool and not sweating.**
- Don’t push yourself to “work hard to stay warm” — that traps sweat and then freezes you later.

### 5.5. Avoid the machinery as a heat source

- The machine bodies might be warm, but:
  - you risk electrical shock,
  - you might trigger a relay or someone’s maintenance system.
- Also, the creatures might feel drawn to the “warm” space you pick.

### 5.6. Don’t rely on human housing units

Trying to break into a module:

- might be misinterpreted badly by the owners,
- might be watched by whatever system monitors the worker “sleep boxes.”
- **They might not be actual houses you can just “enter” like a room.**

If you want to check out a module:

- Ask **first**.
- If not possible:
  - Peer through the gaps; maybe the door has no handle, but a latch from the outside.
  - That might indicate you’re not supposed to be opening them casually.

## 6. Water and food: what not to trust

### 6.1. Water source

You technically have **condensate everywhere**.

Pros:

- **Plentiful**, if you just collect what drops regularly.
- **Low likely biological contamination** from the outside if the system is sealed and the roof tiles are cold metal.

Cons:

- **Possible trace contaminants from machines**:
  - oils,
  - rust,
  - microbes from the *room’s interior air*.
- **No prior knowledge** of what chemical films might be on the surfaces.

### 6.2. Cleaning strategy

- **Collect water**:
  - directly from dripping into a container you can hold up to a source, rather than scraping it off machine surfaces.
- **Don’t collect directly from basins where creatures are clustering**:
  - Even if they behave passively, they are not harmless.
- **Filter**:
  - A fine mesh filter to remove particulates and rust flakes.
- **Disinfect**:
  - **Boiling**: if you can find a safe way to heat water to boiling point without starting a fire or using electrical equipment near the machine grid.
  - **Chemical purification**: use tablets or drops suitable for clear water.
    - Check instructions and make sure you’re not adding more than recommended.
- **Store**:
  - In rigid containers with tight caps.
  - Not in containers that might corrode easily.

### 6.3. Food

Bring:

- **Dense, compact calories**: 
  - nuts, trail mix,
  - energy bars,
  - jerky or high‑protein dried foods.
- **Packets of sealed food**: 
  - not glass jars that break,
  - not foods that require cooking unless you can.

This environment is not friendly to cooking. Don’t plan on elaborate meals.

### 6.4. Hydration discipline

Cold tends to suppress your sense of thirst, but your body is still losing water: **regular schedule > your body’s signals.**

- **Small sips regularly** rather than waiting to be thirsty.
- **Monitor your senses**: if you feel dizzy, confused, or unusually light-headed, consider dehydration and/or cold stress.

## 7. Creatures: coexist, don’t provoke

The creature described is not overtly hostile, but it is *alien* and *very numerous*.

### 7.1. What they *look* like they’re doing

- **Filtering** from the condensate:
  - the “water” you might not drink would be their environment and maybe their food.
- **Perhaps energy‑feeding**:
  - from stray fields of the machines,
  - from photoelectric emissions from the lamps,
  - maybe from the faint nodules in their bodies.

Their **glowing nodules** are not just lamps:

- **They might radiate something subtle**:
  - light not just your eyes sense,
  - “weirdness” that tightens your skin and dries your eyes when you stare closely.

### 7.2. Interaction protocol

Treat them like **electrical wiring or cables** rather than animals:

- **Don’t grab**.
- **Don’t crush or step intentionally**.
- **Don’t try to “clear” a path by removing them**.

If you must move through where they are dense:

- **Nudge very gently with your boot or implement tool**, not your hand.
- **Wait to see if they will move slightly**.
- **Step around** rather than over if you can avoid it.

### 7.3. Don’t disturb them at night‑simulation

Since you won’t have “night” in the normal sense, maybe you’ll simulate a sleep phase by dimming your own lights. When you’re “sleeping”:

- **Don’t let yourself roll into them.**
- **Don’t set gear near clusters where you might crush them accidentally.**

### 7.4. Don’t try to befriend them

- **No feeding experiments.**
- **No drawing blood or sample collection without serious protection and containment.**
- **No “touching the glowy bits to share the power” nonsense.**

### 7.5. If they *react*

- **They are mostly not moving to avoid you**.
- **If you see a wave of motion or a sudden reorientation of a group**:
  - Stop moving.
  - Back slowly away.
  - Adjust your angle to see if you’re casting something they react to:
    - light,
    - a tool that might be emitting a field,
    - your own body capacitance.

If you sense something like an **attitude shift** — not just “moving away from you” but *becoming more mutually aligned* or *forming a pattern* — retreat quickly and carefully.

### 7.6. Glow proximity

If you’re working near a dense cluster:

- **Turn your headlight / handheld light away** or off occasionally.
- **Don’t stare into the nodules of the nearest one**; short glance only.
- **Dry eyes and tight skin** around your face or neck = **step back**.

If you feel *unusual* effects like:

- a sudden feeling of time being “wrong”,
- vertigo,
- visual trails that are not normal after‑images,

treat that as **danger signal** and move away from dense clusters.

### 7.7. Hygiene around creatures

- **Don’t touch your face or eyes after handling anything where they might have been.**
- **Wash your hands if you can**, with the cleanest water you have.
- **Use sanitizer** if you’re not sure water is clean.

### 7.8. Rare “blooms” or swarming

If all of a sudden you see **many more creatures than you thought possible**:

- **That’s not a good time to be curious.**
- **Back away to the nearest structural edge**:
  - not in the middle of the floor,
  - not near a complex machine.
- **Stand still and observe**:
  - if they are moving to **form patterns around something else** rather than you, maybe it’s not your fault.
- **If you see the pattern pointing to you or following your motion**, treat it as a threat until you can prove it’s not.

## 8. Air and breathe: what you’re inhaling

### 8.1. Air quality

- **Humidity** near saturation.
- **Ozone smell and machine oil**:
  - not immediately poisonous,
  - but might irritate over long exposures.
- **Microbial shadow from the condensate**:
  - keep from inhaling deeply right near dripping basins or very wet surfaces.

### 8.2. Respirator mask?

- A **basic mask** (like a dust & particulate respirator) might help:
  - **not** full chemical gas mask,
  - **enough** to filter possible droplets and dust from rust and scale.
- **N** or better, **P**‑series particulate mask offers some protection if you’re going to be in close contact with machine surfaces.

### 8.3. Smelling stone in nowhere

- **“Phantom stone smell”**:
  - could be:
    - trace chemistry from the machine insulation,
    - solvent from paint or sealants,
    - some kind of subtle perceptual trick of the weird physics.
- **If you notice a strong sudden shift in smell**:
  - be cautious of possible gas from the machine system,
  - or if it’s associated with a loud unseen change, like a valve release or failure of something below.

### 8.4. Ventilation

- There are **vents** with grilles.
- **Do not open**:
  - those might be connected to pressure regulation systems for the building below.
- **Don’t block them**:
  - you might interfere with the air pressure difference that keeps the chamber stable.

### 8.5. Breathing technique

When you’re exerting yourself:

- **Deep nasal breathing in, controlled out**:
  - helps to pre‑heat and humidify air better,
  - lessens shock of cold air to your lungs.
- **Pause periodically**:
  - to feel your own heartrate,
  - to check if you’re getting lightheaded,
  - to adjust your pace.

## 9. Sickness and injuries: what’s likely to happen

### 9.1. Hypothermia and slow cooling

- **Onset**: you’ll not always “feel” freezing.
  - Might be:
    - shivering,
    - confusion, difficulty making decisions,
    - clumsiness, more frequent dropping of tools,
    - irritability or “I don’t care anymore” feeling.

If you notice:

- **Stop immediately**.
- Get **more insulation** on:
  - hat, hood up,
  - add layer, cover neck.
- **Get into your sleeping bag** if you can.
- **Move gently** to generate heat but don’t sprint wildly.
- **Have warm (not hot) liquid if available**.

If you’re further deteriorating:

- **Call your exit early if you can.**
- **Don’t try a “heroic return” when you’re already halfway confused.**

### 9.2. Trench foot and maceration

From constant wetness, your skin can:

- become soft, white, wrinkled,
- crack open, leaving painful sores.

**Prevention:**

- **Keep feet dry as much as possible.**
- **Change socks frequently.**
- **Air dry feet** by taking off boots and socks in a safe spot when you can.
- **Use barrier creams** if you have them.

### 9.3. Slips, trips, and trauma

- **Common injuries**: twisted knees, ankles, sprains from slipping on metal floor or gutters.
- **Possible head injuries from low ceiling on girders or hitting machine corners.**

What you want:

- **A basic first aid kit**:
  - bandages,
  - elastic wrap for sprains,
  - antiseptic wipes,
  - pain relief medicine (if your body tolerates it),
  - an adhesive wound closure such as steristrips.

### 9.4. Eye infection risk from creatures

You might not see them “biting” you, but:

- **if you rub your eyes after touching anything near them or their condensate channels**,  
- **or if they get close to your face**,

your eyes might show irritation:

- **Redness, itching, unusual dryness or “tightness,”**
- **Sensitivity to light beyond what’s already weird.**

Don’t ignore this:

- **Don’t scratch.**
- **Flush with clean water if possible.**
- If you’ve got antibiotic eye drops or something equivalent from your world that you know how to safely use:
  - consider them if irritation doesn’t clear quickly.
- **Watch for worsening vision** — if it gets worse, you’ll have to leave early if you can.

### 9.5. Stress from sound and time distortion

Over time in the static hum and unchanging light:

- **Time sense might distort**:
  - you’ll underestimate or overestimate how long you’ve been there.
- **Noise fatigue might cause you to make mistakes.**

**Countermeasures:**

- **Keep a simple mechanical timer**:
  - a cheap watch with an alarm is better than nothing.
- **Set artificial intervals**: sleep “shift”, “day shift”.
- **Take short breaks from motion**:
  - sit or lie quietly,
  - not only to rest but to recalibrate your sense of time.

### 9.6. Unusual sensitivity from nodules

If exposure to the glowing creature nodules causes:

- **nauseating sensation of “time lagging”**
- **a sudden sense of joylessness or “you’re being drained”**
- **unusual surge of emotion** you can’t explain,

then that’s a **sign**:

- **Back away**.
- **Do not sleep pressed close to dense swarms**.
- **Move your “camp” away** if you must.

### 9.7. Mental health

In this environment, you may feel:

- **claustrophobia**
- **dread of unchanging noise**
- **paranoia about being watched by the system** or by the creatures.

Coping:

- **Structured routine helps a lot.**
- **Manual journaling**:
  - even if it’s just “wake / check / rest / sleep”,
  - marking out “days” helps you hold on to human time.
- **Don’t interpret every machine click as a “message aimed at you.”**
  - it is likely not personal.
  - Even in this place, it might just be engineering.

## 10. Tools and gear: what to bring

### 10.1. Light source

- **Headlamp**:
  - Must be waterproof or resistant.
  - Hands-free operation is vital for keeping balance on wet floor.
- **Backup light**:
  - Small handheld flashlight.
- **Extra batteries or power banks**:
  - Assume any device will not be able to draw power from the machine room unless you know exactly what you’re doing.

### 10.2. Navigation tools

- **Notepad & pencil**:
  - To sketch map of:
    - where you arrived,
    - how you move between the module rows,
    - where you’ve seen “interesting” things like particular valve clusters or heavily populated creature zones.
- **Compass**:
  - Might not be accurate near all metal and machine fields,
  - but perhaps you can detect gross distortions and get some sense of direction by watching.
- **Tape of some kind**:
  - To mark small **post-it notes** or labels of your own:
    - not on the room itself; but on your gear or maybe small temporary markers you carry.

### 10.3. Climbing and safety

- **Short lightweight climbing ladder or collapsible ladder hook**:
  - If you’re going to access the gantries above, you’ll want:
    - **not** to climb the machines themselves.
    - **not** to put weight on cables.
- **Helmet**:
  - Already mentioned above.
- **Gloves**:
  - Already mentioned above.
- **Tool tethering lanyard**:
  - If you’re using anything you might drop:
    - tether it to your body so you don’t lose it into the machine floor below.

### 10.4. Basic tool set

- **Multi‑tool or light wrench and pliers set**:
  - Not for “breaking open the machine world”.
  - **For**:
    - helping yourself if you get stuck in a small space,
    - opening a stuck panel if you are authorized to look at it,
    - tightening your own gear.
- **Insulated tools** if you might be near electrical terminals:
  - **Touch only what you must**,
  - **Assume the whole system might be “alive” with more than electricity.**

### 10.5. Communication and recording

- **Audio recorder**:
  - To document the sound of the room and the dripping pattern.
- **Camera**:
  - To document:
    - the machine layout,
    - the creature gathering,
    - any “suspicious anomaly” you might want to look at later.
- **Watch/timekeeping device**:
  - To maintain your “time” even though the environment doesn’t.

### 10.6. Rope and tie-off gear

- **Small length (5–10 meters) of strong rope**:
  - For improvised safety line,
  - not necessarily climbing a huge distance but tying off to something sturdy if you must traverse an edge.
- **Carabiners**:
  - For fastening rope to existing anchor points like robust beams or **anchor**s you know are strong.

### 10.7. Containers and waste management

- **Sealed containers for trash and waste**:
  - Do not leave scraps of tape, wrapper, or anything else on the floor.
- **Urine container**:
  - If there’s no “restroom” module and you’re not going to use the channel system (which you shouldn’t, because it might connect to the basins).
- **Portable toilet bag** with solidifying agent:
  - To capture your body waste and keep it contained from the machine environment.

## 11. Social and psychological safety

### 11.1. If you meet human workers

- **Assume they are not “ordinary”**:
  - They live in those tiny metal modules.
  - Their sense of scale might be different.
- **Approach them**:

  - **Calm, slow movements.**
  - **Visible hands**, not wearing a mask that obscures your human face.

- **Talk**:
  - **Tell them you are not a threat**,
  - **Do not demand “help” immediately.**
  - **Ask for permission** before touching anything they consider theirs.

- **Don’t show them**:
  - **strange technology from your world** unless you’re sure of the consequences.
  - **anything that might be interpreted as weapon or tool they feel threatened by.**

- **Expect them** to be wary of anything:
  - “extraordinary”,
  - “new,”
  - **anything that might change the machine room.**

If they decide you’re a problem, they might call their system:

- **Those could be locks, doors, or some kind of “observation”**.
- **Do not assume “if there’s no jail, there’s no punishment.”**

### 11.2. What information to share and not to share

- **Don’t tell them you can “leave from anywhere.”**
  - If you can be held, they might try to force you to show them.
- **You can say**:
  - “I’m not from here,”
  - “I don’t know how your system works,”
  - “I’m not causing damage.”

- **Limit**:
  - “I can go anywhere instant.”
  - “I can open doors to other worlds.”
  - You might become seen as a tool to be “replaced” by someone else.

## 12. Leaving the chamber

### 12.1. When you’re ready to go

- **Before the last moment**, check:
  - **body temperature**: are you actually cold inside?
  - **hydration**: finish your water, but don’t drink too much of the room’s water.
  - **injuries**: inspect your body for hidden cuts or bruises.
  - **gear**: check nothing you brought with you is contaminated with strange growths from the place.

### 12.2. Clean yourself before exiting

- **Carefully wipe down**:
  - outer layers of your clothes,
  - your boots,
  - any tools you might bring back.
- **Inspect**:
  - **no visible creatures attached to you or your gear.**

We don’t know what they might become in another world’s environment.

### 12.3. Final stance

- Stand **clear of machinery**:
  - not in the middle of a dense module grid,
  - not under a big machine,
  - away from the main creature basins.
- **Stay away from**:
  - **gutter channels**
  - **low‑headroom beams**

If you’re carrying:

- **Any container with water or sample**, make sure it’s sealed and not overfull.

### 12.4. Open the door out

- **Do not** overload the machinery with your device’s power draw.
  - There might be feedback.
  - It might attract the glow of the creatures.

If you see:

- **creatures reorient to you when you activate your device**
- **the lights seeming to flicker or dim**

then **move quickly but not panic‑hastily** through the portal, and **close it behind you**.

### Closing summary

To survive and stay reasonably sane in that condensation chamber at the roof of the factory-city, you need to think like this:

- You are entering a **total machine ecology**, cold and wet.
- You are not the primary user; the creatures are.
- **Keep your body temperature stable**, your feet dry, and your hands clear of the unknown.
- **Respect the machine room’s rules and geometry**, even if you can’t see them.
- **Don’t test the creatures’ patience**, or they’ll show you what they’re for.

If you follow that discipline — careful layers, waterproof boots, sealed gear, clean water collection, and a cautious respect for the glowing inhabitants of this world — you might come out the other side of that chamber knowing something of what it means to live inside a machine that’s still breathing.
