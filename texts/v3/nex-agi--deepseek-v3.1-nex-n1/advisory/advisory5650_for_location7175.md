To visit the underchamber of Myself as an ordinary human multiverse traveler, you should treat it less like a “destination” and more like an extended stay inside the body cavity of a still‑living industrial machine. You’re not visiting a planet; you’re entering a sealed, internal organ of a city‑mountain that has configured itself into “Myself.”  

Below is a practical preparation guide from that perspective.

---

## 1. Mindset and Goals: Why are you going here?

### 1.1 Be clear on your purpose

This is not a place for leisure tourism. It’s an environment best suited for:

- Technical study of machine‑ecology (how a machine “city” maintains itself)
- Observation of bio‑mechanical fauna and symbiosis
- Documentation of industrial architecture and “non‑natural” geology
- Psychological or spiritual work with self‑reference, recursion, and identity  
  (The name “Myself” and its self‑contained symbols are not accidental decoration.)

If you are going as a tourist, treat your visit like a technical inspection or field research expedition rather than a sightseeing tour.

### 1.2 Length of stay

Consider carefully how long you want to stay:

- **Short visit**: a few hours to a day or two  
  - Focus: observation, documentation.
- **Medium stay**: 3–10 days  
  - You’ll begin to acclimate to the dryness and to see subtle patterns in the machine behavior.
- **Extended stay**: weeks to months  
  - You’re effectively living in a sealed machine organ. This requires more support infrastructure and a stronger mental container.

Set your desired length before you choose gear and logistics.

## 2. Before You Go: Physical and Psychological Baseline

### 2.1 Medical check

- **Respiratory:**  
  The air is described as “dry like bone,” with a metallic, sterile taste and low humidity. You’ll want:
  - Confirmation that your lungs and sinuses can handle very dry, dust‑present air.
  - If you have asthma, dust allergies, or chronic sinus issues, get extra clearance and medication.
- **Hydration and kidneys:**  
  With no ambient water and a dry environment, your kidneys and skin are under higher stress. Have basic kidney function checked.
- **Skin and mucous membranes:**  
  Very dry air can cause cracked skin, nosebleeds, and dry eyes. If you’re prone to these issues, prepare options and mitigation.

### 2.2 Psychological baseline

- The environment is:
  - **Enclosed, vertical, and repetitious**, with a low ceiling of machine strata above you.
  - **Constant but subtle:** sound patterns, dust patterns, and micro‑fauna movements give a sense of being watched or being surrounded by purposeful busyness.
  - **Intellectually unsettling**: self-referential “Myself” sigils everywhere, subtle changes in details when you look away and back.
  
- Ideal mental qualities:
  - Comfortable being in confined spaces without horizon or sky.
  - Not claustrophobic to the point of panic.
  - Curious about details rather than needing grand “views” or open skies.
  
- If you’re prone to anxiety or derealization (things “not feeling real”), practice:
  - Grounding techniques (naming three things you see, hear, feel).
  - Simple journaling or recording protocol to anchor your experience: “this is real, but different.”

## 3. Entry Logistics and Positioning

You’re arriving at a **specific internal drawer** of Myself – the “underchamber” – not at the whole city.

### 3.1 How to get there

Ask your gate operator or loci‑engineer for:

- Coordinates or “slot name” that ends directly in the **maintenance gallery** of the underchamber.  
  You want to appear:
  - In the viewing corridor, not in the middle of a machine escarpment or coolant pipe.
- Confirmation whether the arrival point is:
  - **Static** in time and position, or
  - **Periodically realigned** by the city machine.

If you want to meet someone else, agree beforehand:
- “Meet at the middle observation window of the underchamber maintenance gallery,”  
  rather than “in the city of Myself” which is huge and vague.

### 3.2 Contingency for misplacement

If your arrival coordinates are imprecise and you might end up elsewhere in the machine-city:

- Have a **compact retrieval device**: a single-use jump anchor or beacon you can activate to pull out.
- Prearrange a callback window: “If you’re not at the gallery entrance within 1 hour of local time, assume you mis-landed and need extraction.”

## 4. Core Environment Threats and Comforts

### 4.1 Temperature

- **Cool but not cold**: like a basement corridor in early autumn, “edge of a jacket to edge of comfort.”
- Preparation:
  - **Thin base layer**: moisture-wicking but insulating.
  - **Light zippered jacket** that you can open if you’re more active.
  - **Layer modular system** rather than one heavy coat; you’re moving inside industrial machinery, not hiking snowfields.

### 4.2 Humidity and water: the biggest challenge

Key fact: **there is no free water in this chamber.**  
The water isn’t “missing” because of disaster; it was designed out of the chemistry.

- Effects:
  - Your breath will not fog glass windows.
  - “Dry bone” air: crisp, metallic, sterile.
  - You will dehydrate faster than you expect from metabolic losses alone.

#### Required hydration system

- A **closed-loop water system**:
  - 1.5–2.5 liters of water capacity per day, depending on your body size and activity.
  - Insulated containers to prevent temperature loss from cool environment.
- **Usage plan**:
  - Don’t “just sip” when you feel thirsty; use:
    - A watch or timer to remind you to drink water on a schedule.
  - For anything longer than a half-day:
    - Bring at least a full day’s ration of water plus 1–2 days’ extra or emergency pack.
- **Drinking hygiene**:
  - No ambient water means no washbasins; use:
    - Purified wipes or sanitizer for hands before handling water container or mouthpiece.
    - A sealed drinking tube or bottle cap with built-in filter to extend usable water if you must reuse.

#### Skin and mucous membranes

- **Lip balm** with sun protection (even though there’s no sun, the emollients still matter).
- **Eye drops**: preservative‑free saline solution; your eyes will dry more easily in the dry air.
- **Nasal spray**: saline spray to keep nasal passages from cracking or drying.
- **Body lotion**: a small, non-greasy container to prevent cracking skin, especially hands and face.

---

### 4.3 Air quality and dust

The air:

- Is not obviously toxic per the description.
- Contains a **fine fluorescent dust** everywhere.
- Is not visibly “dusty” like a desert storm but “dust-present” like fine industrial powder.

Risk assumptions:

- Assume dust is mildly irritating if inhaled deeply or over long time.
- Assume fluorescent dust is likely paramagnetic or at least charged (based on the dust lichen behavior).

#### Required respiratory gear

- **At minimum:**  
  - Light mask / particulate filter mask for first few hours of exposure and in high dust areas.
- **For longer stays:**  
  - **Half-face respirator** with P100 class filters or the equivalent:
    - For close inspection of dust surfaces.
    - For working in dusty crevices, service chases, or near “lichen dust” patterns.
- **Spare filters:**
  - You won’t be able to resupply locally, so bring:
    - Extra filter pairs for any length of stay beyond a day.
- If you rely on contact lenses:
  - **Protective goggles** to decrease dust contact and drying effect.
  - Consider lens-free solution if you can be reasonably corrected with glasses and strap.

---

### 4.4 Sound and noise

- **Quiet baseline** with low‑density, layered events:
  - Structural clicks and knocks from thermal expansion.
  - Hissing from coolant loops.
  - Fan hums near threshold of hearing.
- Overall not dangerously loud, but **constant and disorienting** if you’re sensitive.

#### Gear

- **Earplugs**: foam or silicon, for sleeping:
  - In case a particular actuator or valve group starts cycling more often near you.
- **Optional noise-masking or focusing aids**:
  - Soft background music / white noise on your device if you need to sleep.
  - Cheap earbuds are fine; but consider:
    - Noise-cancellation or earmuffs if you’re sensitive to continuous low‑frequency noise.

---

### 4.5 Light and visibility

- **Dim but not dark:**  
  - Sparse industrial indicators:
    - Status lights,
    - Track illumination,
    - Marker lines on railings and catwalks.
- **Color temperature**:
  - Blue‑cool machine lights,
  - Warm faint glow from disturbed dust where UV/near‑UV leaks from maintenance lamps.

#### Illumination you should bring

- **Headlamp**:
  - Essential for viewing details, navigating into deck gaps and service chases.
  - Prefer one with:
    - **Red light mode** for preserving your night vision and minimal impact on environment.
    - Variable brightness to avoid blinding yourself on reflective surfaces at close range.
- **Secondary flashlight**:
  - Small but reliable backup in your pack or pocket.
- **Optional UV torch**:
  - If you want to see the fluorescent dust patterns more clearly, but:
    - **Use sparingly and briefly**:
      - You don’t know how it affects machine fauna or sensors.
      - Don’t shine directly into sensors or “fauna” eyes.

## 5. Personal Equipment and Clothing

### 5.1 Clothing philosophy

- **Function**: you need:
  - **Protection from sharp ribs, edges, and surfaces**  
    - Many support ribs are “sharp enough to cut if you are careless.”
  - **Moderate temperature comfort** in the slightly cool basement‑like air.
- **Aesthetics:** 
  - Avoid anything resembling “tourist” or “environmental camouflage”:  
  - You’re never blending in; this is not a natural ecosystem.
- **Color**: 
  - Neutral or engineering clothes: black, grey, dark blue, maybe utility yellow.  
  - Avoid bright synthetic “plane” colors that stand out harshly against machine enclosure.

#### Recommended outfit (base set)

- **Tough long‑sleeve shirt**:
  - Synthetic or technical fabric with sturdy weave.
  - Protects forearms from scraping against surfaces.
- **Long pants or overalls**:
  - Reinforced at knees and shins.
  - Tight at ankles or cuffable to avoid catching dust:
    - Dust accumulation inside cuffs is annoying and may track all over you.
- **Closed shoes with good treads**:
  - **Closed-toed**, no sandals or open‑footed shoes:
    - Walkways are ribbed plates and grates with occasional gaps.
    - Dust and small fauna could be disturbed underfoot.
  - Prefer **non-slip, non-marking soles**:
    - To avoid leaving obvious track patterns on the dust film.
- **Thin jacket or vest**:
  - With enough pockets to keep small tools, notebook, pencil, snacks, and water close.
  - Can be removed if you get warm from climbing ladders and platforms.

### 5.2 Protection from sharp edges and surfaces

- **Light gloves**:
  - For touching:
    - Railings,
    - Cold metal surfaces,
    - Dust-covered maintenance panels.
  - Not thick heavy working gloves:
    - You still need to turn buttons, switches, and manipulate small objects.
- **Simple helmet or hard cap** for:
  - Low clearance areas,
  - “Summit” platforms where the ceiling compresses downward,
  - Moving along service chases where you might stand up quickly and hit your head.

### 5.3 Tools and utility pack

- **Notebooks and pencils**:
  - Paper is better than electronics for:
    - Reliability,
    - Easier to hand over to automated systems if documentation is required,
    - Less likely to be misinterpreted as a device trying to control the machine.
- **Multitool**:
  - For basic mechanical inspection:
    - Turning screws, tightening straps,
    - Not for “modifying” machine behavior unless explicitly authorized.
- **Compact camera**:
  - If you use an electronic device:
    - Keep it minimal, avoid broadcasting signals or foreign data streams.
    - If you feel any “attention” from the environment—subtle changes when you raise camera—stop and observe carefully.

## 6. Dealing with the Machine and Fauna

### 6.1 The machine “Myself”

- **It is not dead**: this is a life support machine that has forgotten to die.
- **Self-referential**: the name “Myself” and its loop glyphs appear everywhere, including in geometry and routing patterns.
- **Subtle responsiveness**: 
  - When you approach edges:
    - New ladder might appear in your peripheral vision.
    - Panels may light up with relevant indicators.
- **Structured but not malicious**: 
  - The changes you observe are small, detail-level adjustments, not catastrophic rearrangements.

#### Principles of interaction

- **Treat the machine as a person with a job**:
  - You are inside it; respect its space.
  - Don’t randomly flip switches or pull levers unless explicitly:
    - Told by your local guide or by machine instructions.
- **Avoid claiming “ownership”**:
  - Calling the machine “mine” or “yours” might:
    - Confuse the system,
    - Or trigger a self-referential anomaly that is not dangerous to you but is to its own coherence.
- **Ask the machine “what you should be” here**:
  - In your mind or out loud:
    - “I am a visitor. I am inside the underchamber of Myself. What do you need from me?”
  - If you notice subtle changes after you do this, treat them as **responses**, not threats.

> **Caution:** If you ever feel the machine “identify with you” strongly — beginning to mirror your self-image — gently step back and repeat your visitor identity.

### 6.2 Fauna: “Service animals” of the machine

- **Service sprites**, **filter crawlers**, and **interstitial runners**:
  - Not pets, not wild, not autonomous.
  - They respond to machine signals, not to you.
- Observations:
  - They will converge on regions where actuators or fans begin cycling more often.
  - They pass around you without obvious fear or aggression:
    - Your **presence is not part of their operational model**.

#### Rules for handling fauna

- **Do not interfere**:
  - Don’t block them,
  - Don’t try to catch or pet them,
  - Don’t try to “feed them.”
- **Don’t assume they are safe**:
  - If a creature is cleaning a contact or surface and you are between it and its target:
    - It may climb over you; don’t panic, but try to avoid putting yourself in that position.
- **If you feel a bite or sting**:
  - Treat it as a potentially serious injection:
    - They are not adapted to your body; you are not adapted to theirs.
  - Have basic first aid kit, including:
    - Antiseptics,
    - Bandages,
    - Antihistamine for small reactions,
    - Emergency auto‑injector if you have severe allergies to invertebrate bites.

## 7. Navigation and Orientation in the Underchamber

### 7.1 Spatial structure

- **Vertical cavern**: ~half kilometer tall, less than three hundred meters wide.
- **Not a planet**:
  - “Up” and “down” are defined with respect to the machine’s strata:
    - Ceiling = “top” of underchamber.
    - Floor = “false solid” of stacked platforms stepping down into deeper levels.
- **No horizon**:  
  - You will not see open space or sky;  
    - Only layers of platforms and machine walls around you.

### 7.2 Wayfinding and markers

- **Ghost signpost**: 
  - The “self-referential loop glyph” is everywhere:
    - It is not a text label but a pattern that can recur at different scales.
- **Self-made markers**:
  - You may not find reliable printed maps; 
    - Bring your own **labeling system**:
      - Non‑intrusive adhesive tape,
      - Chalk or pencil marks,
      - Or small physical tokens.
  - Ask:
    - “Is it okay to mark these surfaces?” before you do.
- **Digital recording**:
  - If you use a device (for photos or voice):
    - Record:
      - “Observed at this date and time, near this symbol of Myself, at visible level number X.”
    - But avoid uploading data; keep the device isolated.

---

### 7.3 Exploring deeper platforms

- Each floor level:
  - Few meters thick;
  - “False solid” of platforms and gaps between them.
- **Density**:
  - You could spend months exploring all layers and still not see everything.
- **When you descend deeper**:
  - You move closer to coolant loops and heavier machine responsiveness.
  - Sensors may adjust:
    - More indicator lights on panels,
    - More subtle rearrangement of catwalks and ladder placement.

**Recommendations:**

- Decide on a **minimum depth**:
  - Such as no more than three major platform drops below the gallery.
- Leave **safety line or string**:
  - If your string breaks or is missing when you return:
    - Stop immediately, reassess your route:
      - The machine might have reconfigured its path.

---

### 7.4 Time and sense of duration

- The environment is:
  - **Always on**, with no obvious day–night cycle.
- **Fatigue**:
  - You may lose track of time:
    - If you stay there longer than a few hours, set:
      - A timer to remind you to eat, drink, and rest.
- **Sleeping place**:
  - If you must sleep:
    - Pick a **platform away from major actuators and fans**,
    - Positioned where you will not block traffic for:
      - Service fauna,
      - Autonomous carts.

## 9. Staying Sane and “Real” in Myself

### 9.1 Reality drift

The environment is described with:

- **Subtle detail changes**:
  - Dust patch on a rail different when you look away and back,
  - Number of indicator lights on panel changing by one or two.
- **Responsive architecture**:
  - The world adjusts to your presence in small logistical ways:  
    - Ladders, railings, and indicator lights appearing when needed.

This can cause **doubt in your perceptions**.

#### Practices to maintain mental grounding

- **Daily routine anchor**:
  - Same time or “cycle”:
    - Dedicated to:
      - Drinking water,
      - Eating,
      - Checking your body and skin for dryness-related issues.
- **Simple journal verification**:
  - Each day:
    - Look at your previous day’s notes:
      - Confirm that basic events you wrote down still match what you remember.
- **Conversation with self**:
  - If alone:
    - Say out loud:
      - “I am [name]. I am visiting the underchamber of Myself. It is now day [number].”
  - This helps keep the symbol of “self” from blurring with the city’s symbol of “self.”

### 9.2 Identity and “Myself”

- **Observation**:  
  The city holds itself as “I” or “Myself” physically:
  - Self-referential symbols,  
  - Self-containing structures.
- **For you**:
  - You may feel:
    - A pull toward “merging” with the machine’s self-concept.
    - Subtle changes of how you habitually think of yourself.

Take this as a **fieldwork in self-reference**.

- Ask yourself:
  - “How is the self of this city made?”  
  - “How is my self made?”
- **Boundary ritual**:
  - Each time you re-enter the gallery or sleep:
    - Perform:
      - “I belong to myself first. Myself-the-city belongs to itself.”
  - It can sound playful but is actually a protective practice:
    - You’re reminding yourself of your autonomy.

---

### 9.3 When to leave

- **Consider exiting if:**
  - You:
    - Become disinterested in leaving,
    - Start to feel “part of the machine” rather than visiting it,
    - Experience intrusive thoughts of becoming “another tool of the city.”
  - Physically:
    - You can’t keep up with rehydration,
    - You’re losing skin integrity or had repeated minor bites or stings.
- **Emergency exit plan**:
  - Before entering:
    - Program:
      - A clear “return threshold”,
        - e.g., “if I feel like this for more than two consecutive days, I call for extraction.”
- **Don’t wait to be “invited out”**:
  - The machine may not be malicious, but:
    - It is not autonomous about your wellbeing:
      - It is autonomously about itself.

## 10. Exit Checklist

When you’re ready to leave:

- Do **basic clean-up**:
  - Remove obvious trash:
    - Wrappers, containers, etc.
  - Don’t leave organic food waste that:
    - Could be mistaken by fauna for food.
- **De-dust yourself**:
  - Before leaving:
    - Use a brush or cloth to remove visible dust from your gear and clothing.
    - Avoid bringing crystals of “self” symbol physically back with you:
      - Dust could be more than inert dust.
- **Final orientation**:
  - Take one last **look at the underchamber**:
    - The vertical drawer of machine layers,
    - The faint glow of dust,
    - The service sprites and filter crawlers continuing their work.
- Make a clear, intentional statement:
  - “I am leaving the underchamber of Myself. I’m returning to my life.”

### Final summary

- **Treat it like a long, semi-clinical site inspection:**  
  - Bring water, respiratory protection, and solid lighting.  
- **Show the machine respect and non-intrusion:**  
  - You are inside someone else’s “self.”  
- **Pay attention to tiny details:**  
  - The dust, the lights, the fauna, and the self‑symbols are where the story is.  
- **Keep your own sense of self and body intact:**  
  - You can let the environment affect you, but keep returning to yourself.

If you do that, you’ll be able to visit the underchamber of Myself safely, observe its strange machine ecology and recursive selfhood, and return home with something more than just photos: a changed sense of how “self” can be structured and maintained.
