To walk into the New Cavern is to step into a world that’s still sketching itself in the margins of existence. It’s not gentle in the way of old forests or full-grown rivers; it’s “gentle” like a child’s hand that hasn’t yet learned to hold tightly or let go completely. This place doesn’t hate you, but it doesn’t know you either. It is still learning to be solid.

If you are an ordinary human traveler from the multiverse, you can survive here—if you prepare with the right mindset, the right gear, and the right humility.

---

## 1. What the New Cavern really is

Before getting into gear lists, it helps to understand what you’re actually entering:

- **Young geology, still reshaping itself.**  
  The stone is “not finished.” Water doesn’t just flow over it; it *softens* it, letting the rock slowly be rearranged. This isn’t a static cave you can memorize like a map. It’s a process, still choosing its shape.

- **No sun, no sky, no true weather.**  
  No rainstorms, no thunder, no seasons as you know them. Just subtle shifts of moisture and temperature and “time of day” defined by light from the stone itself: faint, diffuse, more like twilight than day.

- **A micro-world inventing its rules.**  
  Food is scarce, life is simple, and the ecosystem hasn’t decided what eats what. You’re not entering a fully balanced biome; you’re entering the first chapter of one.

- **People exist but live very close to the edge.**  
  A small, tight-knit community in stone homes, barely above subsistence level. No surplus to speak of. Their resilience is real but their buffer is thin.

You’re not walking into a museum exhibit. You’re walking into the first draft of a world.

## 2. Physical and mental health

### 2.1. Baseline check

Make sure you’re in good shape before you go:

- **Recent medical check:**  
  - Lung function (especially if you’re prone to asthma or bronchitis).  
  - Blood pressure (for climbs, low ceilings, and tight spaces).  
  - Vitamin D levels (if your prior world was largely sunless).

- **Basic endurance:**  
  This is not a place that will be kind to a very unfit body. You’ll be squeezing through narrow passages, climbing short ladders, and walking on damp, uneven stone.

### 2.2. Mental frame

The environment itself can subtly shift your mood:

- **Expect low-grade “tomorrow never comes” feeling.**  
  Everything is slow, continuous, not quite finished. It can feel like eternal waiting, if you’re used to clear sunsets and defined seasons.

- **Prepare for mild sensory deprivation:**  
  - Limited color palette (mostly gray-green stone with touches of pale green).  
  - No soundscape except drips, echoes of your own movement, and faint water murmuring.  
  - No smell of rain, earth, or ozone; just cool dampness and occasional faintness of stone dust.

If you’re prone to:
- depression in low-stimulus environments  
- claustrophobia  
- anxiety when not sure “what’s next”

then mentally prepare:

- Bring **a journal**, **a sketchbook**, or something to give narrative structure to your time.
- Decide on **small daily goals** (“walk to that next stone rib,” “learn three new words from the locals,” “map one alley of the city”) rather than big, abstract ones like “understand this world.”

## 3. Light and visibility

The light in the New Cavern is fragile but present:

- **Natural sources:**  
  - The faint glow from the stone itself.  
  - “Stoneflower” and similar mineral-plant growths with a faint luminescence.  
  - Local stone lanterns made from sealed glowing stone.

You will need to see:

1. **When the main cave light is “on” (if it has cycles of intensity):**
   - Ask locals if the light dims and brightens over time. If so, treat dim periods as night-time rest.

2. **Your own lighting (critical):**
   - A **strong, rechargeable headlamp** with a low-red mode to preserve night vision and not blind others.
   - At least one **backup flashlight**.
   - If you have access to advanced tech, consider:
     - A **very small, low-power lantern** you can sterilize after use.
     - Some travelers carry “everlight” stones or similar small persistent devices as a supplement if multiverse travel is possible.

3. **Avoiding pollution:**
   - In the dim light of the cave, your brights are *blinding* to others.  
   - In such a sensitive world, avoid dumping anything that might act as a “light fertilizer” to the stone; if the walls glow, you don’t yet know what affects that.

## 4. Air and water

### 4.1. Air quality

The air is cool but damp:

- **Humidity:**  
  - Close to 100% in many areas.  
  - Your clothes, equipment, and skin will never fully dry in the field unless you create heat and airflow.

- **Approaches:**
  - Keep **spare dry base layers** in sealed waterproof bags.
  - Use **quick-drying clothes** where you can.
  - If you have asthma or allergies, be cautious:
    - Stone dust or spores from new lifeforms can be a trigger.
    - Bring a **mask** and your **inhaler or antihistamines**.

- **Ventilation:**  
  - The place seems to have airflow through cracks from above.  
  - If you feel short of breath or unusually “heavy,” retreat upward to a known passage where air more clearly exchanges.

### 4.2. Water – your greatest ally and risk

The Spine is not just a “river”; it’s the world’s first decision.

**Treat all water as suspect until you know better:**
- Even if it looks clear:
  - You may be drinking from an ecosystem that has never encountered your human microbiome.
  - The locals’ immune systems are adapted to what’s here; you are not.

**Essential water gear:**

- **Water filter or purifier**:  
  - A filter that removes bacteria and protozoa.  
  - If you’re from a more advanced world, add:
    - Small UV purification light or chemical tablets (as a backup only if you know how to use them safely).

- **Storage vessels:**
  - Two or three **collapsible water containers**, 0.5–1 L each.
  - Keep them **always sealed** when not in use to avoid unseen contamination.

- **Testing (if possible):**
  - If you can obtain a field lab kit that quickly tests pH and basic minerals, do so:
    - This is stone *reforming* water; you don’t yet know what it dissolves.

**Drinking discipline:**

- Never scoop from pools where stoneflower or other unknown life grows directly in the water.
- Prefer water from:
  - Fast-moving sections of the Spine (even if they “move fast” by local standards, not by yours).
  - Places the locals clearly indicate they draw from.
- Ask a local what water they drink “raw” and what they “treat.” (Their treatment may be boiling, letting settle, or filtering through stone cloth.)

## 5. Food and energy

### 5.1. Your food supply

The New Cavern’s “pantry” is not ready to feed you.

**Bring from your world:**
- **High-density, sealed rations:**  
  - Nutrient bars that have long shelf lives and complete nutrition profiles.  
  - Vacuum-sealed pouches of meals if you can carry the weight and trash.
- **Nuts and seeds** if you are not allergic; lightweight but dense.
- **Electrolytes:** powder or tablets to dissolve in water.  
  - In damp, cool environments with constant humidity, you can still get quietly dehydrated if you sweat while climbing or working.

Plan **two weeks of food minimum** per person you’re bringing with you, even if you only intend to stay for “three days.” Time in new-world crossings can be slippery.

### 5.2. Local food (if offered)

- **Eat very, *very* cautiously.**
- This ecosystem’s “plants” and fungi are not yet tested by your biology:
  - Reactions might be:
    - Mild GI upset,
    - Allergic reactions,
    - Or even slow poisoning.

If you choose to try local food:

- **Tiny amounts first**, days apart.
- Eat only what you’ve seen:
  - The locals themselves eat regularly.
  - They eat with no visible ill effects over time.
- Ask about:
  - “Has this ever made anyone sick?”  
  - “Do you eat it every day, or only sometimes?”  
  - “Any part of it never eaten?”

## 6. Clothing and protection

### 6.1. Outer layer: moisture and abrasion

The rock is raw, sharp-edged, and often damp.

- **Good outer shell:**  
  - A waterproof jacket or poncho that allows breathability but blocks drips and humidity splash.
- **Tough trousers:**  
  - Not easily snagged or torn by rock edges.
- **Knee protection:**  
  - If you’re going to be crawling in low spots or working on stone floors, knee pads are not overkill.

### 6.2. Base layer: moisture and temperature

- **Quick-drying base layer** (thermal underwear if your world is colder than here):
  - Wool or synthetic, not cotton if you can avoid it.
- **Mid-layer if needed:**  
  - The air is cool but not freezing. A thin fleece or light layer that can be added/removed.

### 6.3. Feet and hands

- **Footwear:**  
  - Sturdy waterproof boots with:
    - Excellent grip on wet stone.
    - Reinforced toe and heel support.
    - Some ankle support to reduce twist injuries on uneven surfaces.

- **Spare socks:**
  - At least three pairs of:
    - Moisture-wicking socks.
    - Keep them dry in sealed bags.

- **Gloves:**
  - If you may be touching:
    - sharp-edged rock,
    - unknown surfaces,
    - new stonegrowths,
  then bring thin, protective work gloves.

### 6.4. Head protection

- A **light helmet** or hard hat if you:
  - Are at all clumsy.
  - Expect to be moving under low ceilings.
- This is not over the top; low ceilings are described as “just enough to press you into a sense of being under something.”

## 7. Health and first aid kit

### 7.1. Core supplies

Pack at least:

- **Basic wound care:**  
  - Adhesive bandages of various sizes.  
  - Sterile gauze pads and rolls.  
  - Medical tape that sticks to damp skin.  
  - Antiseptic solution, wipes, or gel.

- **Support and protection:**
  - Elastic bandage or wrap for sprains.  
  - Small splint if you have access to one.  
  - Pain reliever, if safe for you to take.

- **GI issues:**  
  - If you’ve consumed any local food or water, upset stomachs are likely.  
  - Have appropriate remedies for nausea and diarrhea if you know how to use them safely.

- **Allergies and eyes:**
  - Allergy medication if you use it.  
  - Eye drops if you’re sensitive to dust.

- **Chronic conditions:**  
  - If you have any:
    - Asthma, diabetes, heart conditions, etc.
    - Bring enough medication for the entire trip + a buffer.

### 7.2. Infection hygiene

This is a new world to your body:

- **Any small cut**:
  - Clean immediately and thoroughly.
  - Keep covered and dry (as dry as anything in this cave can be).

- If you:
  - Develop unexplained fever,  
  - Persistent rash or swelling,  
  - Cognitively feel “off” or more ill than the injury suggests,
  
  then **you must be prepared to leave early.**

The world is not hostile to you; it is simply indifferent and unknown to your immune system.

## 8. Tools and equipment

### 8.1. Navigation and time-keeping

- **Map or recording device:**
  - If electronics:
    - Waterproofed, with backup power.
    - Use for:
      - sketching rough layout of the cave system,
      - marking “safe return” waypoints to the known tunnel that led you down.
  - If not:
    - Carry a **notebook and pencil** (waterproof if possible).
- **Marking tools:**
  - If allowed:
    - Small temporary marks of **nontoxic paint** or chalk that can be removed later.  
  - Do not carve permanently into the wall if you can avoid it; this stone is literally learning its shape, and your carvings might be “remembered.”

- **Timekeeping:**
  - Without sun or moon, your sense of time will drift.
  - A reliable watch or equivalent device is important:
    - Set it to a “home” cycle if you’ve come from a world with clear days and nights.
    - Use it to create routine for yourself:
      - Sleep schedule, meal times, etc.

### 8.2. Basic safety gear

- **Knife/multi-tool:**  
  - For cutting food, cord, etc.
  - Not necessarily for “defense” – more likely to be used for chores.

- **Cord and line:**  
  - Some lightweight, strong line:
    - For temporary handholds,
    - Attaching items together,
    - Making simple repairs.

- **Containers:**
  - Sealed bags for:
    - Trash,
    - Samples of stone if you are authorized to take them,
    - Wet items that you don’t want to contaminate dry ones.

## 9. Social and cultural preparation

The people of the New Cavern are few but known to each other by name. They are tight-knit and not primitive in mind, only in tools.

### 9.1. Approaching without disruption

- **Arrive openly but not loudly:**
  - Use your main light on low, not “spotlight” mode.
  - Step into visible areas but only after a pause; if you see them:
    - Stop a short distance away.
    - Wait.
    - Show empty hands (if your body language is compatible).

- **Wait to be invited:**
  - If they’re wary of newcomers:
    - Don’t push past.
    - Don’t try to “help” by moving into housing spaces uninvited.
    - Let the first contact be calm.

- **Assume scarcity of everything:**
  - Don’t expect:
    - Regular bed-and-breakfast style hospitality.
    - Food to be shared without cost to them.
    - A “guide” without any request of something in return.

### 9.2. Language and communication

You likely start not speaking their language.

- **Simple body language and shared tasks:**
  - Point + question.
  - Show things you need:
    - Drinking water,
    - Shelter,
    - Directions to more “empty” areas where you might tent safely.

- **Learn a few key words quickly:**
  - “Water,” “food,” “danger,” “safe,” “yes,” “no.”
  - Names of:
    - The Spine,
    - The city,
    - Any key “landmarks” they point to.

- **Offer to help:**  
  - If you can:
    - Carry,
    - Move,
    - Or assist with small physical tasks without inserting yourself too deep into their routines.

### 9.3. Economy and trade

If they trade at all, it’s likely to be barter or labor exchange:

- **Goods you might bring if allowed:**
  - Strong, durable tools:
    - A knife that can hold edge better than their stone tools.
    - A hand drill or awl if your world’s tech doesn’t break their “no big machine” rule.

- **Things they likely don’t need:**
  - Complex trinkets,
  - Over-elaborate packaging,
  - Anything that requires chemicals they can’t replace.

**Key rule:**  
Bring *just enough* not to overwhelm their fragile world and not to break the “no metal yet” mood of the place, unless your whole purpose is known and approved by higher-level interworld rules.

### 9.4. Respect their space and “rules of stone”

They are not yet thinking of themselves as metropolis builders, but as “people who live near the water.”

- Learn:
  - What paths are not to be blocked with your gear.
  - Which parts of the Spine are not to be touched or “improved.”
  - Where you can and cannot set up temporary shelter.

- Ask:
  - “May I stay in this area?”  
  - “What is allowed to cross the water, and how?”  
  - “Is there anything I should not do here?”

Their awareness of the world’s ongoing formation might be intuitive more than scientific, but that is still *expertise*.

## 10. Environmental safety and “world ethics”

This is a newborn world. You are not just visiting a place; you’re walking into a geology lab that is also a home.

### 10.1. Do not accelerate their geology

- **No large interventions:**  
  - Don’t build dams or “fix” the flow of the Spine.
  - Don’t try to “shape” the stone more than you’ve been asked to.
- **If you can’t help**, that’s often better than “helping too much.”

### 10.2. Leave the smallest footprint you can

- **Pack out all non-native materials:**  
  - If you have plastic, metal, or electronics:
    - Do not leave them in this cave.
    - Even biodegradable “modern” trash might not behave as expected in this strange environment.

- **Do not take more than you’ve been allowed to:**
  - Stone samples,
  - Plant samples,
  - Even water and “soil” can be part of a delicate balance.

If you are collecting samples for any purpose, ensure:

- You are not removing the *only* of something.
- You’re not disrupting the locals’ food base or tools.
- You’ve been given permission by someone who actually has the right to give it.

### 10.3. “Don’t be a disease vector”

You are as much a risk to them as they might be to you:

- **Observe visible hygiene:**
  - Wash hands with clean water if possible.
  - Keep coughs/sneezes covered if you are ill; consider mask if you’re sick.

- If you:
  - Have any current infections,
  - Are running a fever,
  
  consider *postponing* your visit. This world’s immune system is not proven to handle anything you carry from your world.

## 11. Practical itinerary for a short stay

Assuming you plan to spend only a few days to a week:

**Day 1–2: Orientation and contact**

- Scout:
  - The main tunnel you used to enter.
  - The nearest dry-ish ground where you can set up temporary shelter.
- Contact locals:
  - Introduce yourself and explain purpose if you can:
    - “Visitor from other lands,” or “wanderer,” if multiverse is not a concept they know.
  - Ask if you can set up:
    - In a known corner of the cavern,
    - On a side of the Spine they approve,
    - Not in their most crowded space.

**Day 3–4: Learning and minimal mapping**

- With permission:
  - Follow a small segment of the Spine:
    - Notice how it might change even during your short stay.
  - Sketch, but do not carve permanently into the rock.
- Help:
  - With small tasks (if offered):
    - Fetching water,
    - Carrying stone,
    - Some menial repair or support.

**Day 5–6: Deeper observation and rest**

- Allow:
  - Time to simply watch:
    - How the water and stone interact,
    - How the stoneflower grows,
    - How people’s routines fit the slow flow of light.
- Check yourself:
  - For any signs of illness, skin changes, persistent mood shifts.

**Day 7+: Exit or longer-term negotiation**

- If you want to stay longer:
  - Ask:
    - “May I remain longer?”
    - “What can I do to be a useful guest here?”

- If leaving:
  - Check your gear:
    - Remove any setup you placed.
    - Leave the ground as you found it.
    - Follow the same tunnel out to ensure you’re not accidentally “opening” new paths into the world.

## 12. What to expect emotionally

When everything else fades away on the last day, you’ll likely remember:

- **The quietness:**  
  - No shouting of wind,  
  - No roar of traffic,  
  - Just the faint, steady deliberation of water and stone.

- **The sense of witnessing being born:**  
  - You’re seeing the beginning of a world that’s not yet sure what it wants to be when it grows up.
  - That’s not something you often get to witness from “inside.”

- **The humility:**  
  - You cannot be the center of this world’s story.
  - You are just passing through a space that has already decided its first love:
    - to keep its water slow,
    - to keep its stone close,
    - and to leave room for whatever comes next.

If you prepare carefully—physically, medically, and ethically—you can walk into the New Cavern as a guest who does not outstay their welcome, who does not break the world in their passing, and who can leave changed by having seen a world take its earliest philosophical breaths.
