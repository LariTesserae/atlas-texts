Preparation for the Station of the Leaking Sky  

For someone coming from an ordinary, mostly non‑magical world, this place isn’t just a location, it’s an environment that requires a specific kind of body and mind to visit safely and sanely. Think of it as planning to visit a living cathedral-ecosystem-factory that has very strong preferences about time, noise, moisture, and human behavior.

Below is a practical, “field guide” style of preparation you’d want to follow before going through those doors.

---

## 1. When to arrive

### “At the appointed hour”

This place, and its city, treat time as a contract. You are not allowed to just wander in whenever you please.

- Don’t show up early or “around” the specified hour. Arrive at the *exact* minute written on your ticket or invitation.
- If you’re unsure of the local timekeeping system, ask:
  - Is the “appointed hour” measured on the city clock, the station clock, or the engine clock?
  - What is the signal that begins the hour (bell, steam whistle, a change in the dripping sound)?
- In the day or two before you visit, align your sleep schedule with the station’s rhythms:
  - If the appointment is at night, start staying awake part of the night beforehand.
  - You want to be sharp and attentive when you enter, not fighting sleep.

### Time of day and season

- The station’s temperature is constant “day and night”, but your *comfort* and the *light* will vary:
  - **Daytime:** More light from the windows (unless the wings are dense). Easier for your eyes to adapt.
  - **Nighttime:** The “skyline” fungus glows faintly, but not enough for safe navigation without light.
- If this is your first visit, choose a daytime appointment if possible:
  - The visual cues and the sheer number of creatures will be easier to process.
  - You will be less likely to misstep and fall into a basin.

## 2. Clothing and gear

### Purpose of your outfit

You need:
- Waterproofing from above (the leaking roof) and below (the basins of the floor).
- Protection from the constant wet noise and the potential of escalations in the station’s energy system.
- Something to secure your movements and keep your footing in an always-wet, always-crowded environment.

### Basic layers

- **Underlayer:** Soft, quick-drying synthetic or treated wool fabric.
  - Avoid cotton that stays cold and wet.
  - Long-sleeved top and long trousers: to avoid bare skin touching cable-snakes, wings, or “skyline”.

- **Midlayer:** Thin insulating layer:
  - A light fleece or thermal shirt if the outside world is cold.
  - The station is not cold, but the city air is perpetually cool and damp.

- **Outer shell:** Proper oilskin or rubberized rain gear:
  - Jacket with hood and storm flap.
  - Overtrousers for legs and boots.
  - Make sure seams are sealed or double-stitched.
  - Choose colors in dull blues, grays, or dark greens to avoid startling the wings with sudden bright patterns.

- **Boots:**  
  - Knee-high waterproof rubber or well-oiled leather boots with *thick, non-slip soles*.
  - The tiles are uneven, often coated with transparent or near-invisible skyline, and can be dangerously slippery.
  - Consider half-gaiters if you have to step into water basins.

### Hands and head

- **Gloves:**  
  - Light, waterproof gloves or fingerless gloves with rubber palms.
  - You may want to touch railings and pipes; they are always wet and often vaguely rusted.
  - The gloves reduce direct contact with cable-snake plating and any unknown residues on surfaces.

- **Headgear:**  
  - A close-fitting cap or beanie and a rubber hood to keep the drips from running down your neck.
  - The constant rain will be annoying anywhere else; here it’s part of the structure of the place.

### Protection from noise and sound anomalies

- **Hearing protection:**  
  - The sound of “God being wrong” is not just a description; it is a constant pressure of sound from water, engines, and animals.
  - High-fidelity, musician-style earplugs (not just foam) will allow you to hear the polyphony without damaging your hearing.
  - Consider noise-reducing earmuffs if you find sustained noise fatiguing.

- **Optional acoustic shroud:**  
  - If you have access to the multiverse’s more advanced gear, a lightweight acoustic hood or “hearing helmet” tuned to damp ultra-low frequencies can be helpful:
    - The engine(s) below the floor may produce subsonic or very low-frequency sounds that can disorient you over time.

### Vision and lighting

- **Goggles or clear glasses:**  
  - To protect from droplets falling directly from three stories above.
  - The wings have metallic “lenses” over their eyes; the station might treat your uncovered eyes as a vulnerability.

- **Light sources:**  
  - Even in daytime, you might be under dense areas of roof “roots” and wings.
  - Bring:
    - A headlamp with adjustable brightness (red light mode is less likely to disturb animals).
    - At least one backup handheld light.
    - Chem-lights or glowsticks that can be sealed to reduce disturbance to skyline and glassfish.

You will not be allowed to use very bright, harsh lights; the station’s “eyes” are accustomed to a muted, gray-blue light, and sudden bright flashes will upset the inhabitants.

## 3. Medical and physiological prep

### Water and noise exposure

- **Hydration discipline:**  
  - Even though you’re surrounded by water, it is *not* safe to drink without the engineers’ explicit permission.
  - Before entering:
    - Hydrate well the day before and just before the appointment.
  - Bring:
    - A sealed water bottle.
    - Electrolytes to avoid lightheadedness from the constant noise and humidity.

- **Noise-induced fatigue:**  
  - The station’s soundscape will be fascinating at first, then oppressive.
  - Plan:
    - 10–15 minutes of “quiet break” every hour or two if you stay a long time.
    - This may mean stepping near the doors (where roaring sound is less dense) or simply closing your eyes and focusing your breathing.

- **Motion sickness and disorientation:**  
  - The rotation of turbines, the wheels of the spinners, and the swirling of glassfish schools can create vertigo-like effects.
  - If you are prone to motion sickness:
    - Take a mild anti-motion sickness remedy before entering.
    - Use fixed, vertical lines like columns and catwalks to reanchor your sense of direction.

- **Skin protection:**  
  - Apply a barrier cream or silicone-based gel on areas prone to prolonged moisture exposure:
    - Feet, ankles, lower legs.
    - Wrists, neckline.
    - This lowers the chance of chafing, maceration, or fungal issues.

## 4. Mental and psychological preparation

### Recalibration of expectations

This place defies ordinary ecological categories; “normal” doesn’t apply. You need to treat that as more than a metaphor.

- Remember:
  - There might be no “young” and “old” animals in the way you expect.
  - Time and regeneration are not happening the way your world understands.
  - Change *is* happening, but it is being held by intent and perhaps the engine below.

### Prepare for “the soup of life”

- You are not a visitor walking “through” somewhere; you are walking into a dense living fluid:
  - Thousands of moss rats.
  - Spinners rolling along edges.
  - Cable-snakes on the floor.
  - Wings hovering and folding all around you.
- Decide:
  - You will not startle or strike out at animals that are “very close to your face”.
  - They will not attack you intentionally, but they also will not respect your personal space.

- Practice **slow movement**:
  - Before visiting, spend a day deliberately moving in slow motion:
    - Slowing your steps, turning your head gently instead of jerking.
    - Your natural flinch is to recoil from something near you; the station needs the opposite: calm patience.

### Meditation or grounding exercises

- Before entering:
  - Do a short grounding exercise:
    - Feel your feet on the ground.
    - Note your breathing pattern.
    - Notice sounds around you without judgment.
- While inside:
  - If you feel overwhelmed by the noise, “camera focus”:
    - Pick one small sound (e.g., one droplet hitting a basin).
    - Listen to it until your awareness of the whole polyphony softens and becomes manageable.

## 5. Footing, floor, and basins

### Understanding the floor layout

- It is not “puddles” vs “solid ground”; it is a shallow maze of:
  - **Concrete and tile basins**
  - **Low walls separating basins**
  - “Eyes” of water that are all connected.
- Visually:
  - The wet floor reflects the ceiling; watch that you don’t step into a deep reflection thinking it is an extra tile.

- Walking strategy:
  - **Pause before each step** to check:
    - Is there a cable-snake in your path?
    - Is there a moss rat running just where you want to place your foot?
    - Are you seeing a reflection of a ceiling rod that looks like solid ground?
- Step lightly but firmly:
  - Avoid heavy stamping or stomping; the tile is not fragile, but the creatures are sensitive.
  - Where you must cross a wet patch:
    - Slide your foot slightly to test for depth before committing weight.

- If you lose balance:
  - Do not grab hanging tubing or “roots” from the roof; you don’t know their load bearing.
  - Try to fall into damp tile rather than into a basin with glassfish schools.

## 6. Interaction with the creatures

### General principles

- **Assume non-hostility but not safety from accidents:**  
  - None of them are designed to eat you or attack you.
  - But a collision with a cable-snake or a wing at face level can be dangerous if you are startled.

- **Do not touch unless you are told otherwise:**  
  - Even moss rats and glassfish should be observed from a distance.
  - The cable-snakes are “roots of the engine below”; interfering with them might be like tampering with the heart of the place.

### Specific groups of inhabitants

- **Moss rats (the algae-blue runners):**
  - They are nervous, “always first” everywhere.
  - Do not block their paths or corner them.
  - If you need them to move away from you:
    - Step slowly and predictably to one side.
    - Do not chase them or try to catch them.
- If you need to cross a dense patch of moss rats:
  - Move very slowly, giving them time to adjust their flow around you.

- **Spinners (translucent, shelled, with wheels):**
  - They seem built to roll around edges of basins.
  - Do not interfere with their routes.
  - If you see them clustered around a particular “rain-root” in the ceiling:
    - That’s a zone of higher importance; be extra careful not to disturb the water there.

- **Glassfish (finger-length, with turbine organs):**
  - Schools hum with a low engine noise.
  - Avoid stepping directly into their deeper basins.
  - The engine-organs inside them are moving; the water vibrates.
    - If your body is submerged with them:
      - There might be electrical or magnetic effects that are uncomfortable or even harmful to you.
  - Do not attempt to drink water from their pools.

- **Cable-snakes (metal-sheathed “roots”):**
  - They are warm to the touch but not enough to burn, apparently.
    - Even so, do not place your bare skin on them.
    - They are anchored to the **engine below** and might react to your touch in ways you can’t understand.
  - Step carefully over them; they do not yield.

- **Wings (folding banners of membrane and metal):**
  - They filter sound, vapor, and temperature.
  - They are closer to your face than you’d like.
    - If one is near your head, **freeze briefly and then slowly move your head away**.
  - Do not wave at them or try to scare them off:
    - They are part of the climate system; the engineers rely on them for the constant temperature.

- **“Skyline” fungus/bacteria:**  
  - It looks like shadow lines at first, then map-like trace phosphorescence when light is low.
    - Do not walk through it if you can avoid it.
    - It’s a record of the water system and possibly the engine’s rhythm.
    - Smudging or scraping it might be akin to erasing the station’s memory of the city’s water network.

## 7. Safety and guardianship

### Professional engineers: your only human authority

- The engineers are the only human inhabitants who *understand* this place.
- **Obey them immediately**:
  - “Don’t step there.”
  - “Don’t touch that.”
  - “Close your eyes now.”
  - “Don’t look down that stair.”
  - These are survival instructions, not polite suggestions.

- The engineers are wary:
  - They may have seen things below the floor that changed them.
    - The woman with the algae-blue eyes warns you’d “know” once you’ve gone down there.
    - If the engine below is revealed to you, the impact is not just information; it is potentially **existential**.

- Do not:
  - Ask for “proof” of their claims.
  - Try to go down to the engine floor on your own.
  - Argue with their decisions about where you can and cannot go.

### If the engine below affects you

- “When you’ve gone down there” you might:
  - Understand something about the city, water, and God being wrong that reshapes your worldview.
    - This can be mentally destabilizing, especially if you believe strongly in a single theology or physics.
    - The station itself, with its invariant rain and temperature and eternally regenerating animals, already hints at this.

- Prepare yourself emotionally:
  - Before arriving, consider your own anchors:
    - Who you are beyond your beliefs or your world’s “normal.”
    - What you would do if some of those assumptions were broken for you.

- After you leave:
  - You may need a debrief period with trusted people who can handle you saying:
    - “The rain was never weather.”
    - “The animals didn’t have children; they just changed.”
    - “There is an engine under the city that drinks sound and water and time.”

## 8. Logistics and supplies you should carry

- **Sealed supplies bag:**
  - Spare pair of socks and a thin towel in a waterproof bag.
    - Even good waterproofing can fail at the ankles.
    - Change socks before you travel back; walking in wet socks for hours after visiting is a bad idea.
  - Sealable plastic bags for:
    - Phone, camera, or note device.
    - Any paper notebooks you insist on carrying.

- **Notetaking tools:**
  - The humidity is high and water drops fall from above.
  - Use:
    - Pencil or waterproof pen.
    - Waterproof paper notebook or a few waterproof cards.
    - Do not rely on electronics without waterproof casing; they will be compromised by ambient moisture.

- **Simple snacks and hydration:**
  - You must not eat or drink from the station’s surfaces or water.
    - Sealed energy bars and nuts.
    - Sealed water bottle.
  - Plan your visit to keep blood sugar stable:
    - The noise alone burns energy through your brain’s attempt to process everything.
    - Eat something small before you enter and after you leave.

- **Basic first aid:**
  - Band-aids or waterproof bandages.
  - Antiseptic wipe packets.
  - Light tape or gauze roll.
    - You might not get cut often, but if you do, the wet environment will favor infection.

- **Optional: cheap commitment device**
  - A small, token object you can leave behind:
    - A coin, a small carved stone, or a simple metal badge from your home world.
    - The station might appreciate “an exchange” rather than “an extraction.”
    - Do not leave anything plastic or artificial unless you are certain it won’t break down into unfamiliar contaminants.

## 9. Listening and time discipline inside the station

### Learning the soundscape before you move

- When you first walk inside, **stop** and close your eyes for a short while:
  - Listen to:
    - Single droplets hitting copper basins.
    - The chatter of galvanized spillways.
    - The deeper hum of the main conduits.
    - The continuous-running of the turbines high above.
    - The faint, engine-like vibration through the floor.
  - Let your brain map the sound as a structure, not a noise.

- **Resist the urge to rush your visit**:  
  - The place wants to be taken slowly:
    - Each area of the roof drips into its own basin.
    - Each basin hosts its own community of animals.
    - The engineers have their patterns of catwalk and ladder.

- If you hurry too much:
  - You’ll misstep into basins.
  - You’ll startle the wings.
  - You’ll miss the very subtle lines of skyline that map the city’s water system.

## 10. Leaving and aftercare

### When your appointment ends

- The station might not have “closing hours” in the ordinary sense, but:
  - **Do not overstay your appointment** unless a engineer explicitly tells you to remain.
  - Something about the engine’s “intention” might be tied to the idea of *allowed duration*.

- On your way out:
  - Take one last moment to look up at the roof of “inverted trees” and down to the map-like lines of skyline.
  - If you have been told to leave something at a particular basin or table, do so carefully, without disturbing the water or animals.

### Post-visit processing

- You will likely feel:
  - **Overstimulated but strangely empty**, as if your brain was trying to decode an entire cathedral of information.
  - Emotionally unsettled, particularly if you’ve glimpsed the engine below or the “eternal” animals.

- After leaving:
  - Find somewhere quiet and warm to change into dry clothes.
  - Drink warm fluids.
  - Sleep if you can; the brain processes a lot during sleep after this kind of experience.

- If you find yourself:
  - Hearing phantom water drops or engine sounds in your sleep:
    - This will likely fade over a few days to a week, but it’s a normal sign that your mind has been deeply impressed by the station.
    - If it persists for longer than that or interferes with your ability to function, seek guidance from someone who understands “threshold places”.

- Documentation:
  - Write down your impressions of:
    - Which basins you saw; which cable-snake locations; where you might have seen “skyline” glowing faintly.
    - The engineer’s exact words, especially anything about “engine below,” “spinners,” or “God being wrong.”
    - Any anomalous temperature sensations, despite the gauge always being the same.
    - Any changes you think you perceived in the glassfish or moss rats over time, even though you were told nothing changes “except that which someone has intended to stop.”

---

In summary: visiting the Station of the Leaking Sky is not like seeing a factory or a museum. It is more like being allowed inside a body that is also an engine and also a church. You prepare:

- By protecting your body and senses from the noise, wet, and density of life.
- By aligning your time and movements with the station’s unyielding rhythms.
- By accepting that you may be changed by the encounter—especially if you ever “go down there.”

Approach it with slow, careful steps, profound respect for the engineers and inhabitants, and a willingness to let God be wrong at least once inside those walls.
