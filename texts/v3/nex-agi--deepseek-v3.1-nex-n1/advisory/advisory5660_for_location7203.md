This city is a body. It sweats, breathes, and circulates moisture and heat through old stone veins. Your job as a traveler is not to conquer it or revolutionize it, but to learn how to *step carefully* inside its skin and not get dissolved by it.

Below is a detailed preparation guide for an ordinary human traveler who wants to visit, survive, and maybe leave again.

---

## 0. Before You Start: What You Are Walking Into

- **Expectation:** An old, low, dense city of stone and brick and moss, built and rebuilt over itself so many times that it feels like a geological formation rather than a human project.
- **Climate:** Cool, damp, not exactly cold but not comfortable. “Perpetual misty edge-of-evening.” Visibility moderate but colors muted.
- **Tech level:** Quietly industrial in a buried way. No roaring engines; instead, hidden machine systems under the stone. Steam or gas pressure, valves, pipes, channels.
- **Weirdness:** Moderate but subtle. The city behaves like a living system with rules of heat flow, moisture flow, and architecture that you don’t immediately understand.
- **Your status:** You are small, temporary, and soft. The city will outlast you.

Adjust your mindset from “tourist” to “microorganism in a body.” You don’t control the body; you learn where its warmth and safety are.

## 1. Physical Health & Physiological Prep

### 1.1. Core Conditioning

- **Cardio & stamina:** The city is not vertical in a skyscraper sense, but you are constantly climbing and descending shallow steps, walking uneven, polished stone surfaces, and navigating subtle changes of level.
  - Aim: 30+ minutes of brisk walking per day; weekly longer hikes with uneven terrain.
  - Include stairs or step-ups to prepare legs for low-level ascents/descents.

- **Strength & stability:**
  - Focus on ankles, knees, and hips: single-leg balance, step-ups, step-downs, small lunges.
  - The stone streets are polished smooth by centuries of use; slips are less likely than you’d assume from debris, but you still need strong joints.

### 1.2. Respiratory & Moisture Resilience

- **Damp-loving microbes:** Wherever there is persistent moisture and biofilm-coated pipes, assume microorganisms. Your first lines of defense:
  - Make sure your vaccines are up to date (especially tetanus).
  - Ask your medical specialist for a broad-spectrum antibiotic to keep on hand only in case of severe infection, not for casual use.
  - Understand basic wound hygiene.

- **Lung protection:** The air is not poisonous by default, but:
  - Mild respiratory irritants from metal, steam, and biofilm biofilms may trigger sensitive individuals.
  - If you have asthma or chronic bronchitis, bring your usual inhalers or medications plus *extra*.
  - Consider a lightweight filtration mask for *short-term use* in particularly thick steam or valve-areas, not the whole city.

### 1.3. Eyesight & Night Adaptation

- **Vision:** The city lives in “perpetual edge of evening”; light is low but not dark.
  - Make sure you have an updated glasses prescription if you wear glasses.
  - Consider a pair of clear, protective eyewear (like lab or safety glasses) to keep condensation and droplets off your eyes in narrow alleyways where roof drip is persistent.
- **Night vision:** The city is not pitch-black, but your eyes will need to adapt.
  - Avoid staring at bright screens before you arrive; they’ll degrade your adaptation.
  - On arrival, give your eyes 10–15 minutes in the streets before you decide you “can’t see enough.”

### 1.4. Allergies & Skin

- **Moist vegetation & biofilm:** There is moss everywhere, and green surfaces clinging to stone.
  - If you’re highly allergic to mold or spores, bring antihistamines and pack quick-drying clothes to minimize damp skin contact.
- **Skin hydration:** Your skin will be constantly touched by cool mist; paradoxically, that plus indoor heat can leave dry patches.
  - Bring a simple moisturizer, especially lip balm and hand cream.
- **Boot rash & friction:** If your boots aren’t perfectly fitted, you’ll feel it after many days of damp walking.
  - Test your boots extensively before the trip; moleskin or blister pads are essential.

## 2. Clothing & Equipment: How to Wear Its Environment

### 2.1. Outer Layers: The Shell

- **Waterproof vs. “water-resistant”:**
  - You don’t need full storm gear; there’s no heavy rain.
  - You *do* need a breathable waterproof shell layer (like a rain jacket or parka shell) that can handle persistent mist and sideways moisture.
  - Hooded jackets are useful: droplets stock on your shoulders and head, especially in narrow alleys under roof drip.

- **Color & visibility:** The city is already dark slate, gray, and moss-green. Avoid:
  - Stark black (unless you’re deliberately trying to be invisible).
  - Neon (it will just look weird).
  - Instead:
    - Dark muted colors: charcoal, dark olive, deep browns.
    - A small reflective patch or band on your bag is not for vehicles but for other humans in low light.

### 2.2. Inner Layers: Temperature Patching

- **Core strategy:** The city’s temperature is *variable* on your ankles and lower body:
  - You’ll be walking through subtle gradients of warm and cold air from grates and vents.
  - **Base:** Breathable wicking layer to manage moisture without trapping sweat.
  - **Mid:** A thin insulating layer (like fleece or synthetic) you can strip or add in.
- **Avoid cotton:** If it gets wet (from mist or sweat), it stays wet and cools you.
  - Use synthetics or wool for socks and underwear.

### 2.3. Hands & Feet: The Critical Points of Contact

- **Hands:** They are among the first parts exposed to cold damp air.
  - Thin waterproof gloves or even wind-resistant liners can help.
  - If you work with your hands (taking notes, sketching maps), gloves with good touch sensitivity are worth the extra cost.

- **Feet:** The most important contact surface.
  - **Boots:** Sturdy waterproof hiking boots with good grip:
    - Walking is on polished stone channels and shallow steps.
    - Extra ankle support reduces strain over long days.
  - **Socks:** Wool or synthetic blend that can handle moisture:
    - Bring several pairs and change daily.
    - Consider thin sock liners to reduce friction blisters.

- **Spare shoes:** If at all possible, bring a second, lightweight pair of shoes or sandals for:
  - Resting your feet in drier quarters.
  - Walking around indoors where you may be asked to remove shoes.

### 2.4. Head, Eyes, Ears

- **Head:**  
  - A simple hat or beanie that is not bulky but still keeps moisture off your scalp and helps retain warmth.
- **Eyes:** Light tinted or clear protective glasses can reduce irritation from drifting mist and biofilm droplets.
- **Ears:** No special need, but if you’re sensitive to noise anomalies:
  - Subtle, low-frequency sounds from the buried machine can be disquieting.
  - Consider simple earplugs for sleep and for deep tunnels where resonance can be strange.

### 2.5. Belt Pack & Everyday Carry

When you’re walking the city, you want a small, easily accessible belt pack or sling bag *inside* your main layer or jacket for:

- **Small notebook & pencil:**  
  - Ink may run; pencils are better.
  - Use to sketch maps or patterns: indirect seeing of strange patterns is easier when you draw them.
- **Water bottle:** Metal or insulated; the city has water but it’s always prudent to filter your own.
- **Lip balm & hand cream:** Your skin will be persistently exposed to moisture and air.
- **Basic first aid kit:** Bandages, antiseptic wipes, tape, small scissors, tweezers.
- **Flashlight/headlamp (with red mode):**
  - Even though the city is not dark-night, corners and interiors can be very dim.
  - Red mode helps preserve your night vision and doesn’t draw as much attention or distort subtle colors.

## 3. Gear & Supplies: Practical Tools

You don’t need heavy expedition gear; but you need *right-sized* tools.

### 3.1. Navigation Tools

- **Paper map:** Preprinted or hand-drawn from any description you can get before arrival.
  - Steel city + subtle geometry anomalies = electronic devices prone to drift.
- **Compass:** Simple but reliable.
- **Chalk or small, removable markers:**  
  - You may want to leave temporary subtle marks on stone corners or steps:
    - Numbering alleys,
    - Marking safe routes toward warmth or known hosts.
  - Be aware of local customs and avoid permanent graffiti.

### 3.2. Light Tools

- **Headlamp with diffused or low settings:**  
  - Bright lights are rare in the city; you don’t want to be the blinding outsider.
  - Use low settings whenever you’re not alone or in deep cellars.
- **Small backup LED:** Always carry one.

### 3.3. Water & Food

- **Water filter/purification tablets:**  
  - Even though water channels are everywhere, you don’t know what’s in the biofilm or pipes.
- **Simple snacks:**  
  - Compact, high-energy: nuts, chocolate, energy bars, dried fruit.
  - You don’t know how available food is in the city; carry a day or two of emergency rations.

- **For longer stays:**  
  - Learn to recognize local sources of food:
    - Markets,
    - Communal feeding houses,
    - Any place where machinery heat leaks and people might gather warm food.
  - Be cautious about unknown foraged greens—even “familiar” looking herbs in this world may be mildly different.

### 3.4. Sleep & Shelter

- **Sleep system:** Lightweight sleeping bag rated for cool, damp conditions:
  - Synthetic insulation is better than down if it gets damp.
- **Bivvy or emergency shelter layer:**  
  - If you’re caught out unexpectedly, you want at least a waterproof layer between you and the drips.
- **Insect netting (if you’re sensitive):**  
  - There are low-hum, insect-like things around warm vents.
  - A net can be hung over your sleeping area if you’re staying in rough or temporary shelters.

## 4. Mental & Psychological Prep

This environment affects *how* you think.

### 4.1. Understanding the City’s Character

- **Not hostile, but *indifferent*:**  
  - The city doesn’t hate you. It just *doesn’t care*.
  - You’re like a bacterium on its skin: if you find a warm patch of wall, lucky; if not, you freeze.
- **Low anxiety, not no anxiety:**  
  - Everything moves quietly, but there is constant motion underneath.  
  - Some people find this soothing; others find it subtlety unnerving.
- **Prepare for “chronic mild stress”:**
  - Always having slightly cold hands;
  - Always having slightly damp sleeves;
  - Always needing to watch where your feet land.
  - This is not catastrophic danger; but it’s *constant*.

### 4.2. Emotional Strategy

- **Expect a small but steady “why” question:**  
  - The city is not built for human comfort; it’s optimized for its own internal circulation.
  - You’ll need small comforts:
    - A warm drink at intervals,
    - Dry things to look at,
    - A notebook to jot impressions.
- **Sound discipline:**  
  - The city is mostly quiet. You don’t need to whisper, but shouting or loud music will be jarring and draw attention.

### 4.3. Sense of Scale & Time

- **Time moves slowly:**  
  - You never *see* the city age; but you feel *your* time spent.
- **Ambient light:**  
  - It doesn’t shift as much as you’re used to, so your circadian rhythm can become confused.
  - Keep a simple schedule:
    - Regular meals times,
    - Set times to sleep and wake,
    - Avoid naps that are too long if you’re trying to integrate local time.

- **Mental “landmarks”:**  
  - Because the architecture is similar everywhere, you may rely on:
    - Texture: that one wall with a particular moss pattern.
    - Temperature: “go warmer” or “follow colder” until you find what you’re looking for.
    - Sound: certain valve exhales, rhythm of dripping water.

## 5. Cultural & Social Prep: Being Human Among Low-Strange Urbanity

You don’t yet know who built this city, or who else lives here, but some patterns can be anticipated:

### 5.1. Interpersonal Assumptions

- **Expect inhabitants to be quiet, low-key, and cautious:**  
  - People used to a subtle environment may be less open to loud noise or sudden movements.
- **Respect for thresholds:**  
  - Doorsteps are “dish-shaped” and concave thresholds “like sleeping backs”:
    - Don’t block them;
    - Don’t sit or sleep on them without explicit permission.
- **Privacy and windows:**  
  - Narrow windows and thick amber panes:
    - People are visible only faintly from the outside.
    - Don’t press close to windows; it’s likely considered rude or invasive.

### 5.2. Etiquette of Space & Pathways

- **Street channels are water pathways:**  
  - Don’t stand or sit in obvious channels, especially when they show wetness or moss.
- **Sharing space:**  
  - If you see someone consistently step to a specific side, or avoid a particular patch of ground:
    - Follow their pattern,
    - Ask later if you’re curious.
- **Noise discipline:**  
  - Keep sounds low:
    - Soft spoken voices,
    - Controlled movements of your pack and equipment,
    - Headphone volumes at levels that don’t leak sound.

### 5.3. Gifts and Exchange

- **Bring small, lightweight, durable gifts:**  
  - Not everything familiar to you will have value here, but:
    - Good tools: small sharp knife, strong needles, thread.
    - Simple, durable objects: pegs, clips, containers.
    - Things that can help *conduct* or *manage* moisture in small ways (like small containers or seals) might be particularly appreciated.
- **Ask before giving:**  
  - Some things may be sacred, dangerous, or simply unknown.
  - Observe before you interact.

## 6. Safety & Risk Management

### 6.1. Physical Risks

- **Slips & falls:**
  - Smooth, polished stone steps and shallow dishes.
  - Always test the surface with your weight before committing fully.
  - Watch your footing in corners where condensation is thickest.
- **Hidden infrastructure:**
  - Valve chambers, pits, and sunken wheelhouses are warm but *not* playthings.
  - Do not:
    - Turn random valves,
    - Enter obvious machine rooms without permission.
- **Structural risks:**  
  - The city is ancient but *finalized*:
    - Roofs sag concave; some stone is depressed.
    - Avoid standing under obviously deeply concave roofs unless you’re sure they’re stable.

### 6.2. Biological Risks

- **Unknown fauna & their eyes:**
  - Small elongated creatures with amber glowing eyes:
    - They are clearly watching you;
    - They are not likely a threat to your *size*, but they are predators of smaller things.
    - Assume no touching unless local custom says otherwise.
- **Avoid touching biofilm on pipes:**  
  - Even if you see faint glowing colors:
    - Don’t assume they’re safe.
    - Wash immediately if you accidentally contact them.

### 6.3. Environmental Risks

- **Temperature gradient crossings:**  
  - Sudden warm to cool air can:
    - Trigger condensation on your glasses and lenses,
    - Slightly disorient you,
    - Stress your body if you’re not layered appropriately.
- **Deep industrial spaces:**  
  - If you find your way into deeper tunnels:
    - Never go alone if possible,
    - Always mark your path,
    - Always tell someone where you’re going,
    - Have a light and some basic air quality monitoring if you can carry it.

### 6.4. The “Low Strange” & Subtle Weirdness

- **Patterns that appear when you’re *not* looking directly:**
  - Don’t panic; this is not necessarily hallucination or madness.
  - Treat them like a peripheral-understood system:
    - Sketching,
    - Indirect observing,
    - Marking places where patterns appear.
- **Temperature anomalies:**  
  - Some arches and windows correspond to warmth:
    - Use them as clues:
      - “Warmth means here someone might be inside or machinery is close.”
    - Don’t mistake them for supernatural threats unless behavior suggests otherwise.

## 7. Practical Arrival & First Steps

When you first arrive, you want a slow, deliberate start.

### 7.1. First Hour

- **Find an edge space:**  
  - Not the deepest core of the city but not entirely exposed.
  - A small courtyard or low set of buildings with visible windows but not too many people.
- **Orient:**
  - Identify your key reference:
    - A low tower (if visible),
    - A particular roof pattern,
    - A strong smell of metal and steam that indicates a machine-dense zone.
- **Body check:**  
  - Are your feet dry?
  - Are your layers balanced?
  - Are you overheating or getting cold?
  - Fix small discomforts *before* they become big.

### 7.2. First Day

- **Do not rush deep into the city:**  
  - Spend your first day exploring:
    - Narrow streets,
    - Lighted windows,
    - The behavior of condensation patterns.
  - Note:
    - Where you feel warmest,
    - Where you feel safest.
- **Identify potential safe zones:**
  - Places with visible light:
    - Courtyards with amber windows,
    - Alleys with lanterns,
    - Warmer regions around certain machine outlets.
- **Do not interfere:**  
  - Observe how:
    - People move,
    - What they avoid,
    - What they treat as sacred or dangerous.

## 8. Living With the City’s Limits: Buildings and Lodging

### 8.1. Finding Shelter

- **Look for signs of life rather than luxury:**  
  - Narrow slot windows glowing amber,
  - Gentle warmth seeping through stone foundations,
  - Any visible doorway that doesn’t look actively abandoned.
- **Expect low doors:**  
  - Buildings squat and dense; you may need to bend or duck slightly.
- **Expect shared walls:**  
  - Sound may carry through stone in strange ways:
    - Keep conversations low,
    - Be aware that your movements might be heard nearby.

### 8.2. Managing Your Space in a Building

- **Condensation management:**  
  - Inside surfaces will show damp:
    - Keep your sleeping bag and clothes slightly away from walls,
    - Use air channels if you can find them,
    - Avoid storing important items directly against cold surfaces.
- **Use warmth gradients:**  
  - If you find a warm patch of floor or wall:
    - Drying socks,
    - Warming your hands,
    - Not necessarily sleeping directly on (unless the locals say it’s safe).

## 9. Maintaining Your Own Systems: Hygiene, Hydration, Sleep

### 9.1. Water Use & Hydration

- **Always treat water before drinking:**  
  - Even if you’re sure it’s “just” condensation channel water:
    - It’s been in contact with biofilm and pipes.
    - Filter or purify to avoid stomach illness.
- **Monitor urine color:**  
  - You’re breathing damp air but not necessarily absorbing internal moisture.
  - Pale yellow is good; darker means you need to drink more.

### 9.2. Hygiene Under Dampness

- **Change socks regularly:**  
  - Even waterproof boots keep internal moisture from your feet; sweat builds.
- **Dry out clothes at least once daily:**  
  - Use any warm machine areas or vents if you’re allowed to be near them,
  - Rotate two layers of socks and underwear if you can.
- **Basic cleansing:**  
  - You won’t have access to showers as you know them:
    - Use a small towel and minimal water for sponge baths,
    - Focus on areas prone to bacterial or fungal buildup (feet, groin, underarms).

### 9.3. Sleep & Rest

- **Sleep environment:**  
  - Choose warmer than you think strictly necessary:
    - The damp can feel colder than the thermometer suggests.
  - Ensure your sleeping bag or cover is not directly contacting any wet surface.
- **Sleep hygiene:**  
  - Even without time-of-day cues, keep:
    - Regular sleep hours,
    - A short “wind-down” routine,
    - A small, safe light source you can reach without opening your eyes to total darkness.

## 10. Leaving: The Final Step

You’ve learned to walk with this city’s breath and blood. When it’s time to go:

- **Take your trash with you:**  
  - The city doesn’t need your plastic, wrappers, or broken tools.
- **Document your observations:**  
  - Sketch those patterns you only half-saw.
  - Write down what temperature anomalies guide you.
  - Record any unusual sounds or rhythms.
- **Check your body:**  
  - Any lingering skin issues,
  - Any breathing issues,
  - Any emotional drain from the low-light environment.
- **Plan recovery time:**  
  - After leaving, your senses may need:
    - A bright day,
    - Open space,
    - Time away from low, damp, stone environments.

---

### Final Mindset

Preparing for this city is not preparing for storm or avalanche; it’s preparing to swim quietly in a low, slow, damp environment that will still be here long after you’re gone.

You pack light because you must move with its currents; you layer clothes because the temperature shifts under your feet; you keep quiet because the city itself invites listening; you learn to watch condensation and moss and valve-breaths because these are the city’s own language.

If you do it right, you won’t conquer the city or “beat” it. You will, for a short while, be allowed to walk in its bloodstream and leave with only the softened edges of your own memories changed.
