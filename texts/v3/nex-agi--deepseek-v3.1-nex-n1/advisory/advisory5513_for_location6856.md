You’re going to a half‑drowned coastal metropolis where the ground breathes and the birds own the skyline. If you’re an ordinary human multiverse traveler who doesn’t want to become a footnote in the city’s long sigh, you don’t need magic. You need *preparation* and obsessive *attention to detail*.

Below is a practical guide: what to bring, how to move, what to expect, and how to keep yourself from ending up quietly dissolved in this soft, wet world.

## 0. Before Anything Else: Adjust Your Expectations

### The weirdness is in the *scale*, not in the impossible

This world isn’t going to hit you with sudden physics‑breaking phenomena.  
What will mess with you are:

- **Wet** that never quite stops  
- **Slow** that lulls you into underestimating danger  
- **Scale** – big city, big water, lots of animals, and very few humans  

You’re entering a place that is:

- **Quiet** – but not empty  
- **Gentle** – but relentless  
- **Simple** – in the way a long tide is simple: always coming, always changing everything  

You’re not built like the locals. You didn’t evolve here. Don’t imitate the birds. They have wings and hollow bones; you have ankles and a fragile body temperature.

## 1. Physical and Mental Health

Before crossing, be honest:

### 1.1. Medical Conditions

This place is **not** forgiving to:

- Any respiratory condition (asthma, COPD, bronchitis, mold allergies)  
- Poor temperature regulation (easily overheated or chilled)
- Poor circulation or diabetes-related foot issues  
- Any immune system problems or open wounds

If you fall into any of these categories, you should:
- Fix what you can medically before you go.
- Leave extra margin of safety in everything.
- Assume you will be slower, need more rest, and need more help.

### 1.2. Psychological Prep

This environment can:

- Lull you with a kind of *humid calm* and then suddenly wear you down with constant background noise and dampness.
- Disorient you with lack of strong contrast: everything slightly gray, dim, and wet.
- Make you feel isolated, because humans are sparse and mostly hidden.

Before you go:
- Know **exactly** why you are going there and what “success” looks like.
- Set a clear **time limit** for your stay.
- Decide **in advance** what kinds of risks you will refuse to take (e.g., “no swimming after dark,” “no entering totally submerged buildings,” “no accessing roofs without a safety line”).

Write these down and keep them with you. Your mind will be softened by humidity and entropy; you need hard rules.

## 2. Clothing and Personal Gear

### 2.1. Headline: You Must Stay Dry and Warm, Not Just Comfortable

“Wet” here is not a splash; it’s a condition. The entire city is trying to become water.

#### Essential clothing system

- **Base layer:** thin synthetic or merino **moisture‑wicking** longsleeve top and leggings  
    - Avoid cotton. It absorbs water and never dries.
- **Mid layer:** light fleece or thin synthetic insulation layer  
    - For the times a wind breaks through and you suddenly lose heat.
- **Outer layer:**  
    - **Breathable waterproof jacket** with sealed seams and good ventilation.  
    - **Waterproof trousers** with reinforced seat and knees.

- **Footwear:**  
    - **Waterproof boots** reaching at least to mid‑calf, with strong grip soles.
    - Extra **insoles**.
    - Multiple pairs of **synthetic or wool socks** – change them regularly.  
      - Rotate socks and let them dry if you can, but plan as if they won’t fully dry.

- **Gloves:** thin waterproof or quick‑dry gloves for contact with rusted metal, salt, and debris.

- **Head:**  
    - Light **cap** or beanie to manage moisture/heat loss.  
    - Wide **brim** or a hood with good rain coverage when it is actually wet.

- **Eye protection:** simple sunglasses or even clear glasses if you’re sensitive to glare off the water surface; it helps in the dimmer light as a contrast filter.

#### Accessories

- **Neck gaiter or small scarf:** to pull over face and neck in gritty wind, or to wipe sweat without a cotton towel.
- **Lightweight pack cover:** to keep your backpack from getting soaked through.
- **Optional (but strongly recommended):** a **compact micro‑spike or grip aid** if you’re going to cross metal walkways, salt‑rusted staircases, or slick rooftops.

### 2.2. Color and Visibility

You’ll want to be:

- **Not black** – in the dim light you might vanish to potential rescuers.
- **Not too bright** – if you want to avoid drawing attention from locals who don’t want visitors.

Aim for **neutral**:

- Faded gray‑green, dull blue‑gray, brown-gray.
- Avoid stark reflective materials.  
- You want to be seen by someone looking for you, but not shouting at the eyes of thousands of birds.

## 3. Pack and Protection Against the Elements

### 3.1. Shelter (Even for “Short” Visits)

Conditions:
- Sudden rain, heavy humidity, and nowhere truly guaranteed dry.
- Indoor spaces are partially submerged, structurally compromised, or occupied by others.

**Recommended shelter:**

- **Ultra‑light tarp** and **bivy bag**:
  - You can string a tarp over concrete windowsills or across rooftops to catch moisture and keep the wind off you.
  - A bivy protects you from ground moisture and bugs and can be used inside derelict buildings if necessary.

- **Optional:** lightweight **hammock**:
  - In areas where you can’t or don’t want to sleep directly on floors (due to mold, shallow water seepage, or broken glass).
  - Get **tree‑like anchor** strategies: window frames, solid pillars, etc. — but *only* after you’re sure they’re structurally sound.

**Always assume:**

- Any “floor” you see may be hiding mold, water seepage, weak spots, and jagged debris under the surface of dust or water.

### 3.2. Water and Hydration

**The seawater‑flooded channels are salty.** You mentioned **salt‑tinged** and **saltwater**, so assume:

- You **cannot drink the street water** untreated.
- You **cannot** reliably desalinate it without proper equipment, and boiling alone won’t remove salt.

Plan water:

1. Bring **portable water filter** rated for bacteria and protozoa.
2. Bring **purification tablets** or drops (e.g., chlorine or iodine) as backup.
3. Bring **collapsible water containers**.
4. Identify **rain** as your likely freshwater source:
   - Collect rainwater from clean rooftops or your tarp.
   - Use **pre‑filter** cloth or bandana to avoid debris and bird droppings going into your filter.

**Hydration discipline:**

- You’ll be sweating constantly in the humid warmth.
- Set **regular intervals** to drink, even if you don’t “feel thirsty.”
- Avoid alcohol: dehydrates and blurs judgment in a place that requires sharpness.

### 3.3. Food and Rationing

Given:

- You are a guest in a place where wildlife (birds, fish, etc.) is the primary owner.
- Humans are discreet and likely subsistence‑living.

You **should**:

- Bring **all** the food you need.
- Assume you **won’t** be relying on local handouts or scavenging.

**Good choices:**

- Calorie‑dense, **water‑resistant** foods:
  - Nuts, trail bars, sealed jerky, hard cheeses, vacuum‑sealed meals.
- **No** fresh foods that require refrigeration or are easily spoiled by damp.
- Consider **MREs** or similar dehydrated meals if you’re staying more than a day, but be aware of their salt content.

Store everything:

- Inside **zip‑sealed bags**, then inside **hard** or **waterproof dry‑bags**.
- Don’t let food be exposed to the humid air longer than necessary.

## 4. Navigation and Movement

### 4.1. Understanding the “Breathing Ground”

You described:
- The ground sighing: air and water bubbling through cracks and shifting slabs.
- Concrete slabs moving slightly underfoot.
- Water levels in cracks and channels rising and falling slowly.

This isn’t poetic; it’s physical hazard.

**Guidelines:**

- **Never** treat the ground as fully stable.
- Avoid walking on:
  - Slabs that are visibly cracked into patterns or tilted.
  - Areas where you can hear or feel water moving underneath.
- Always **test** weight before committing fully:
  - Place a foot lightly, then gradually more weight.
  - Look for any water seepage emerging around the edges of the slab.

If you are carrying **something** that can act as **weight dispersal** (like a wide board or even a pack), you can use it to bridge small gaps or suspicious surface.

**Don’t** trust:

- Any “concrete path” that shows:
  - Rebar poking through.
  - Salt crusts that are cracked and hollow.
  - Dark, algae‑covered spots where the water clearly overflows regularly.

### 4.2. Streets and Elevations

You said:

- The elevated sections are *where the streets still dry off between rains.*
- Low streets are submerged and channels now.

**Use elevation:**

- Whenever possible, move **along** the ridges instead of crossing the depressions.
- Plan **routes** on:
  - Fire escapes (if solid).
  - Rooftops with accessible access.
  - Overpasses, if intact.
  - Upper‑story walkways or balconies that may connect buildings.

Avoid **shortcuts** across:

- Unknown flooded streets.
- Areas where water is “only” ankle‑deep but you cannot see the bottom.
  - You may stumble on unexpected debris, broken glass, or open holes in the pavement.

Think **3D**:

- The city’s old floor has been partially eaten by water and time.
- Humans and birds now live **vertically**; you should too.

### 4.3. Tools and Navigation Aids

You **must** have:

- **Waterproof map and compass**:
  - Even if you have a GPS device, you should assume:
    - Batteries fail.
    - Electronics can corrode in the humidity if you don’t protect them.
- **Waterproof notebook** + pencil (for sketches and notes).
- **Markers** – to leave notes **if you must** communicate with others:
  - Assume you don’t speak the local language.
  - Simple symbols (arrows, dates, “safe” versus “unsafe” marks) can help.

**Navigation strategies:**

- Mark every **elevation gain** on your map:
  - These are your safe zones and potential viewpoints.
- Note **areas of obvious instability** (e.g., leaning lampposts, submerged cars).
- Avoid moving **after** heavy rainfall – the water level will temporarily rise and new slabs may fail.

## 5. Dealing with Buildings: Structural Safety

### 5.1. Doors of Salt, Rust, and Rot

You described:

- Buildings with lower floors submerged.
- Saltwater eating their foundations and metal.
- Metal turned into fragile “lattices” and “salt crusts”.

You **cannot** assume:

- Any doorframe or railing is safe to grab.
- Any staircase is intact.

**Guidelines:**

- Test **before** you commit:
  - Tap rails and supports lightly with something hard (e.g., a tool).  
    - If they sound brittle, or move easily, or shed flakes: **do not use them** for support.
- Avoid **hanging your body weight** on:
  - Exposed rebar.
  - Rusted handrails.
  - Salt‑covered window frames.

### 5.2. Entering Buildings

You **should**:

- Prefer **upper‑story** access from **external** routes:
  - Fire escapes, if they’re solid.
  - Adjacent rooftops or balconies.
- Avoid:
  - Lower stories submerged or water‑logged.
  - Basements entirely. They are likely flooded and structurally unstable.

If you *must* enter a building:

- Check **signs** of:
  - Cracks in the walls, especially near the corners.
  - Sloping floors.
  - Mold smell that indicates long-term humidity.
- **Stay** near the **exit**:
  - Don’t go deep into corridors if you can avoid it.
  - Have **light** and a way to mark your path back.

### 5.3. Shelter and Overnight Stays Inside

- Choose **upper floors** but:
  - Avoid top floors if you see signs of ceiling collapse or water leaking through the roof.
- Check **ceilings**:
  - Look for stains, swelling plaster, or sagging.
- Avoid **windows directly** onto:
  - Large bird congregations – you don’t want to wake up to agitated thousands of eyes watching you.

## 6. Biological and Chemical Hazards

### 6.1. Bird and Droppings

You described:

- Every cornice, ledge, and window sill occupied by birds.
- Droppings streaking the walls like “tears of chalk”.

**Risks:**

- Histoplasmosis and other fungal diseases from inhaling dust contaminated with bird droppings.
- Slippery surfaces – bird droppings are treacherous.

**Protections:**

- **Dust mask** or N95 respirator when cleaning around droppings or sweeping dry surfaces.
- Gloves when handling surfaces contaminated with feces.
- Avoid **sleeping** directly beneath areas heavily covered in droppings unless you can cover and protect yourself.

### 6.2. Water and Rust

You said:

- Water “silverish, greenish lead‑gray”.
- Industrial life still slowly functioning and exhaling smoke and steam.

You must assume:

- Water may contain:
  - Industrial chemicals, heavy metals, and bacteria.
- **Do not**:
  - Drink it.
  - Wash wounds or eyes with it unless purified and treated.
  - Bathe in it unless absolutely necessary, and even then quickly rinse with freshwater afterward.

For **skin**:

- Avoid **open wounds** entering that water.
- If you get cut, **immediately**:
  - Clean with purified water or antiseptic wipe.
  - Apply a disinfectant and cover.
  - Watch for infection signs.

### 6.3. Mold and Mildew

Assume:

- Everywhere that stays wet and warm is likely to have mold.
- Inside closed buildings, especially lower floors, you will encounter spores.

So:

- Use **mask** when cleaning.
- Don’t sleep directly on moldy surfaces; use your bivy or cot.
- If you’re highly sensitive, consider **antihistamine** options.

## 7. Dealing with the Locals: Human, Avian, and Otherwise

### 7.1. Humans: Discreet, Not Absent

You said:

- Hint of light in windows of submerged buildings.
- Occasional tendril of smoke from a roof.
- People “frequent particular places, but not loud.”

This suggests **survivors** who are:

- Private.
- Probably cautious with outsiders.
- Possibly relying on limited resources.

**Ethics and safety:**

- **Respect distance:** don’t approach their windows or rooftops unless invited.
- Avoid **blocking** access to:
  - Their water sources.
  - Their fishing or hunting grounds.
- If you need contact:
  - Signal **non‑threatening**:
    - Slow movements.
    - Hands visible and empty.
    - Avoid looming above or climbing directly onto their balconies without permission.
  - Observe **local** customs:
    - From a distance, notice how they move, where they go, and what times.

**Gifts:**

- If you have small, sealed, useful items:
  - Waterproof matches, salt tablets (if they matter locally), sealed food, a waterproof patch kit.
  - Offer sparingly and without expectation of being hosted inside.

**Safety**:

- Assume you are **not** the first outsider they’ve seen.
- Assume **previous** interactions might have been violent or exploitative.
- Don’t presume “hospitality” as your right.

### 7.2. Birds: The City’s True Inhabitants

You described:

- Thousands of birds on the ledges and sills.
- Everywhere, “a thousand eyes” turning when you move.

Behavior:

- They are quiet but **restless**.
- They are watching **constantly**.

**Guidelines:**

- **Do not**:
  - Get between them and the water while they feed.
  - Corner them or walk directly into roosting zones.
  - Approach nesting areas.
- **Do**:
  - Move **slowly and predictably**.
  - Avoid sudden gestures, especially with poles or sticks that might look like weapons from their perspective.
- If a **flock** suddenly erupts from a building:
  - Stay calm and cover your face and ears from flapping wings and potential debris.
  - Avoid flapping back at them or running wildly.

Their droppings are everywhere; you’ll never eliminate them, just manage your exposure.

### 7.3. Fish and Aquatic Life

You described:

- Many fish below the surface.
- Soft “swallowing” sounds when they surface.

**Humans** may fish:

- They are likely to have **spots** they rely on.
- Do **not**:
  - Fish where you see locals fishing.
  - Disturb their nets or lines.
- **If** you are invited to fish or shown:
  - Ask (through gesture and patience) where they want you.

**Personal** unease:

- Avoid stepping into areas with:
  - Unknown depth.
  - Strong sense of movement below you.
- You can’t know what else is in that water besides the fish you described.

  - Moving quickly over unfamiliar terrain in the dimmest hours.

  - Your peripheral vision more than “center focus” – movement in grey environments can be easier to detect at the edge of your sight.

### 9.2. Depth Perception in Water

- **Rinse** with freshwater when you can:
  - After accidental exposure to saltwater.
- **Dry**:
  - Areas where skin rubs (e.g., groin, armpits, waistband).

### 11.3. Eyes and Ears

- Protect eyes from:
  - Droppings and dust.
- Avoid **submerging** head unless:

- Re‑examine your last known location mentally:
  - What landmarks did you pass?
  - What elevation were you at?
- **Conserve** energy:
  - Find a safe, **elevated** spot.
  - Use **markings**:
    - Leave notes or visible signals if you know you will be searched for.

### 12.4. Extracting Yourself

If you must reach a **specific** extraction point:

- Re‑confirm:
  - Exact location and **time**.
- Avoid:
  - Cutting it close to darkness or incoming tides.
- Carry:
  - **Compact** space blanket or similar insulation:
    - If you have to wait overnight for extraction.
