### 1. Understand What You’re Actually Being Asked to Visit

You’re not going to a “house with plants in it.”  
You’re entering a *partially trained superorganism city* under stone, in a dark, cold, humid world where:

- Architecture = plant tissue.
- Light = living plants + tiny flying “stars.”
- Air = cold, dense, water-saturated.
- Floor = living root-mat.
- Everywhere = tiny, many-limbed creatures.  
You are not a god with technology; you are a soft, clumsy mammal in a biome that is already complete and competent without you.

Whatever you bring, you must be able to carry *by yourself*, on foot, across uneven organic surfaces and root-stairs, in near-darkness and high humidity, without shorting out or getting invaded by life.

---

### 2. Mentally Orient Yourself: The Environment

Before you even pack, set your expectations:

#### 2.1 Constant and Intense Moisture

- Walls, ceiling, plants: covered in water and slime.
- It’s not dripping *rain* exactly; it’s “fog precipitating on surfaces and then running.”
- Anything metal, paper, or fabric will be subject to 24/7 dampness.

#### 2.2 Darkness with “Glow”

- There is no sunlight.
- Light comes from:
  - Blue-glowing mosses.
  - Vein-glowing leaves.
  - Drifting flying creatures.
- This is not “bright enough to read without thought” level; think of it as perpetual twilight.
- Your sense of navigation and safety will depend heavily on light.

#### 2.3 Underground Coldness

- Not frozen, but a deep chill *plus* humidity.
- It feels colder than the thermometer implies because:
  - You’re in damp clothes.
  - Air is dense, so you feel heat being pulled off you more.
  - You aren’t moving as much as you would outside.

This is not a place to “wing it” in a thin shirt and sandals.

#### 2.4 Continuous Living Substrate

- Floors are living root mats.
- Surfaces are alive:
  - moss blankets,
  - root felts,
  - moisture-trapping membranes.
- Air is full of tiny motes and spores:
  - you will be constantly breathing them.
- The whole system operates:
  - water uptake,
  - “breathing,”
  - slow rearrangements of structure.

Assume you are interacting with a partly conscious animal-plant hybrid.

---

### 3. Core Objectives: What You Need to Survive and Not Harm the Place

You have three main goals:

1. **Stay alive** (temperature, moisture, food, water, safety).
2. **Stay healthy** (immune system, microbe load).
3. **Not break the world** (no toxic chemicals, no invasive species, no structural damage).

This is not a “campground” you can leave worse than you found it.

### 4. Clothing and Body Protection

#### 4.1 Layering for Cold and Wetness

- **Inner layer:** Tight-woven moisture wicking synthetic long underwear (top and bottom).
- **Mid layer:** Thin synthetic fleece or merino wool (insulating when damp).
- **Outer layer:** Waterproof *and breathable* shell jacket and trousers with sealed seams:
  - No gaping cuffs or vents where things can crawl inside.
- **Gloves:** Thin waterproof gloves with good grip:
  - For touching surfaces without direct skin contact.
  - Prevents scrapes from sharp stone edges or tool marks.
- **Head/neck:** Either:
  - A breathable balaclava, or
  - a hat + neck gaiter.
  This both:
  - keeps you warm,
  - and reduces *spores* entering your mouth and nose.

#### 4.2 Footwear

You need:

- Lightweight, waterproof, high-ankle boots.
- Non-mud pattern soles:
  - Soften the pattern slightly if you’re walking in new, “younger” root tunnels.
- You will not be able to see directly under your feet, so:
  - Good grip is essential.
  - Avoid rigid metal deep cleats that could damage root surfaces.

#### 4.3 Clear Goggles (Optional, but Wise)

- Not fully sealed gas-mask goggles but something like:
  - swimming goggles or lab safety goggles with anti-fog treatment.
- Why:
  - Moisture dripping from above will be constant.
  - Spores and droplets in your eyes can cause irritation or infection.
  - Unnecessary if you are not going to be climbing or looking up into active drip zones—but the city essentially *is* a drip zone.

#### 4.4 Mask/Filtration

- A lightweight filtering mask or simple half-face respirator with:
  - P100 filters or equivalent.
- Why:
  - You’re breathing in:
    - fungal spores,
    - tiny particles,
    - probably unknown microbes.
  - Important if you are immune-compromised or want to minimize risk of allergic reaction.

### 5. Light Sources: You Can’t Rely Only on the City’s Glow

You must be able to:

- See clearly when you need to.
- Modulate your light so you don’t:
  - blind locals,
  - disturb predator-prey patterns,
  - or disorient flying creatures acting as streetlights.

#### 5.1 Primary Light Source

- A headlamp with:
  - Red mode at low brightness,
  - white low/medium/high modes,
  - waterproof casing.
- Why red:
  - Preserves your night vision.
  - Less likely to disturb fauna or plants; many biologies are less sensitive to long red wavelengths than bright white.

#### 5.2 Backup Handheld Light

- A waterproof flashlight:
  - Narrow or focusable beam,
  - for checking specific surfaces:
    - tool,
    - artifact,
    - the edge of a water channel.
- A small reserve battery bank or spare set of batteries in:
  - waterproof case,
  - inside an inner pocket.

---

### 6. Atmospheric Protection: Temperature and Moisture Management

You will be at least *somewhat* cold and damp the whole time you are there unless you take special measures.

#### 6.1 Keeping Core Body Temperature Stable

- Hot water bottle, or:
- Small rechargeable hand-warmer that:
  - can sit inside a jacket pocket,
  - not scalding-hot enough to damage plant tissue if you press against it.
- Lightweight insulated blanket or emergency bivvy:
  - For times when you need to sit still or sleep:
    - this is not a camping tent but a “sit-and-watch” place.

#### 6.2 Moisture Management

- **Quick-drying towel** small enough to carry but:
  - large enough to wipe face, hands, and sometimes surfaces you sit or kneel on.
- **Extra set of base layer clothes** in a waterproof stuff sack:
  - If everything else gets damp, you can change.
- **Big plastic zip bags**:
  - For anything that must remain dry:
    - electronics,
    - notebook,
    - medicine,
    - food.

### 7. Hydration and Food

You will not know what is safe to eat or drink on this world.

#### 7.1 Water

- Bring sealed, known-safe water:
  - Bottles with sport caps or a hydration bladder with a tube.
- If you must drink water from there:
  - assume:
    - It is biologically loaded.
    - Possibly high mineral content.
    - It might contain microscopic organisms you are not adapted to.
  - If you *must* treat:
    - A small UV sterilizer pen,
    - or filter rated for viruses and bacteria,
    - *after* local consultation if possible.
- **Stress:** Drinking local water should be treated as risky.

#### 7.2 Food

- Bring sealed, long-shelf-life rations:
  - Trail mix,
  - energy bars,
  - sealed nut packs,
  - sealed jerky or freeze-dried meals for longer stays.
- Plan:
  - You do not graze on glowing plants.
  - You do not eat:
    - shellfish,
    - glowing nodules,
    - “things that look like fruit.”
- You will not know:
  - Which things are safe,
  - which are hallucinogenic,
  - which are symbiotic with other lifeforms.

### 8. Environmental Protection: You vs. The World’s Lifeforms

The environment is not hostile to you personally; but it is very dense with life.

### 8.1 Physical Protection

- Insect repellent–like:
  - DEET or picaridin (if you are in a universe where these are safe):
    - applied on **outside** clothing and not on the living root walls.
- If you are worried:
  - A thin spray of permethrin (on your clothes only) before you travel.
  - Keep *all* of this away from plant surfaces:
    - Even if your local chemistry is fine for humans and Earth insects, you don’t know what it does to symbiotic glow-moss.

### 8.2 Skin and Wound Care

- Small medical kit:
  - antiseptic wipes,
  - alcohol-based hand sanitizer,
  - waterproof bandages,
  - tape.
- **Any cut you get:** treat immediately and cover:
  - This is not a place to “let air heal it.”
- Steroid cream or anti-histamine for unexpected skin reactions:
  - If you touch something wrong, you might react.
- If you have severe allergies:
  - Epinephrine autoinjector if available to you.

---

### 9. Biological/Safety Protocol: Don’t Break Their World

You are crossing a boundary: not just of space but of ecosystems.

### 9.1 Before You Leave Your Earth

- Shower thoroughly:
  - Remove soil from other planets,
  - seeds,
  - foreign spores from your clothes and body.
- Wear clean, laundered clothes:
  - This reduces the chance you bring in:
    - Earth microbes,
    - invasive seeds,
    - tiny insects that could harm this new ecosystem.

### 9.2 When You Are Inside the City

- Do not:
  - scrape walls,
  - break roots,
  - “feel around the nodules” on the root floor.
- Do not try:
  - to “sample” glowing creatures,
  - to capture one,
  - unless explicitly invited by locals and guided by them.
- Do not:
  - use strong solvents,
  - alcohol wipes,
  - or sprays directly on plant surfaces:
    - you have no idea what their chemical baseline is.

### 9.3 When You Return Home

- For *your* world’s safety:
  - Wash exposed clothing separately,
  - possibly consider quarantine for your gear:
    - you don’t know what spores or organisms you may carry back.
- If you are from a world with sensitive agriculture:
  - treat everything you’ve worn/contacted as potentially contaminated.

### 10. Environmental Tools and Measurement

### 10.1 Basic Instruments

- Small, rugged thermometer:
  - To note:
    - baseline temperature,
    - changes as you go deeper or higher.
- Hygrometer (if you have one):
  - To know how wet you actually are:
    - The humidity will be at near-saturation levels.
- Compact carbon dioxide (CO₂) monitor:
  - In a closed-up plant cavern, you want to verify:
    - no CO₂ accumulation,
    - especially if you are going to use any combustion device (which you shouldn’t).

### 10.2 Time and Navigation

- Watch with:
  - luminous face,
  - waterproof.
- Compass (optional but not critical):
  - You won’t be under a sky, but:
    - you can track whether you turn consistently,
    - for simple mapping.
- Small notebook + pencil:
  - Not pen:
    - ink might smear in humidity.
  - For:
    - sketching maps,
    - noting water patterns,
    - recording impressions of “streetlights” and creatures.

### 11. Interaction with Locals and Their Artifacts

Humans are here. They are “primitive” in the sense of being low-tech and closely watching one plant-city for generations.

### 11.1 How to Present Yourself

- You don’t want to be mistaken for:
  - a supernatural,
  - or a threat.
- Wear:
  - neutral colors,
  - no glaring armor,
  - no light-emitting gear except during controlled use.
- When you meet people:
  - Show empty hands,
  - slow movements,
  - head slightly lowered,
  - not touching their tools or artifacts.

### 11.2 Learning Their Tools

- If you are allowed to look:
  - Observe **how** they:
    - chisel stone or root,
    - shape water channels,
    - guide plant growth.
- Do not:
  - grab or “test the sharpness” of their carved bone tools.
- If you are invited:
  - You might use *your own tools* to work:
    - a small chisel you brought,
    - to help gently guide water flow,
    - always under supervision of someone who understands the plant.

### 11.3 Communication

- Prepare:
  - Simple vocabulary notebook of:
    - gestures,
    - basic words,
    - if you learn them beforehand.
- Tone:
  - Quiet,
  - soft,
  - very little volume:
    - the world is designed for sound to travel strangely.
- You may be allowed:
  - to lodge in a spare chamber or alcove,
  - but this:
    - is not a hotel,
    - is a living system that they are responsible for maintaining.

### 12. Dealing With The Psychological Weight of the Place

### 12.1 Mental Preparation

- You will be:
  - never truly alone,
  - always aware of tiny moving things around you,
  - under a pressure of constant “aliveness.”
- Some people find this:
  - soothing,
  - others find it subtly terrifying:
    - like standing inside someone else’s lungs.
- Before going:
  - If you have issues with:
    - claustrophobia,
    - entomophobia,
    - or mycophobia (fungal environments),
    - you need to plan for it:
      - strategies,
      - breathing exercises,
      - maybe a companion if you have one.

### 12.2 During the Visit

- Keep a routine:
  - sleep times,
  - “surface” activity:
    - maybe you return to your own universe or an external camp for breaks,
    - or you simply have a defined rest cycle in your chamber.
- Physical:
  - Simple stretching exercises.
- Mental:
  - Short sensory grounding exercises:
    - name things you see,
    - touch,
    - hear,
    - to keep from getting overwhelmed by the world’s sensory texture.

### 13.1 Pack Logistics

### 14. How You Might Actually Use This in Practice

Imagine a day:

1. **Before you step through:**
   - You shower, clean clothes,
   - check pack: waterproofed, sealed, nothing loose.
2. **You arrive in the chamber:**
   - Cold weight hits your chest,
   - water smell is strong,
   - glowing mosses appear.
3. **First actions:**
   - Remove headlamp, set to red,
   - put gloves on,
   - slip mask up if you want it,
   - listen to water channels,
   - stand still for a minute:
     - let your eyes adjust,
     - let the living creatures adjust to you.
4. **During stay:**
   - You move slowly:
     - Not to crush anything,
     - staying to visible paths,
     - asking where you can walk (if locals are present).
   - You take notes:
     - patterns of glowing creatures,
     - position of water channels,
     - any tool marks on stone.
   - You periodically:
     - warm your hands,
     - hydrate cautiously,
     - wipe your face and neck.
5. **Before leaving:**
   - Pack everything,
   - leave no trash,
   - no foreign objects.
   - Back home:
     - You wash everything,
     - inspect yourself for cuts,
     - and maybe let your gear air out in a quarantine space.

### 15. Final Attitude

You are visiting an ecosystem that has been shaped by people who have been watching the same plant for generations.

- You come:
  - as a learner,
  - not as a scientist to be “served data.”
- You carry:
  - enough of your own world’s safety to protect yourself,
  - but not enough to force the world to behave like yours.
- You treat:
  - the root floor,
  - glowing leaves,
  - floating “streetlight creatures,”  
    - as beings you are visiting in their home,
    - not as decorations or “experiments.”

If you approach that way—patient, prepared, and respectful of the wet, stone, root, and the creatures forming the city’s living tissue—you will be far safer, and the experience will be far less frightening than it otherwise could be.
