Based on your description of this place, I'll call it **the Basin of Perpetual Engine-Rain**. Here's how an ordinary human traveler should prepare.

---

### 0. Before You Go: Common Mistakes

Most travelers die in this place not because of monsters or machines, but because of four basic errors:

1. **Assuming anything will ever dry.**
2. **Assuming “tropical” = “easy movement.”**
3. **Assuming “river sound” = “normal river.”**
4. **Assuming you’ll find dry firewood, dry ground, or dry shelter if you just improvise.**

Everything you plan should be planned around *continuous, unrelenting moisture* and *engineered water*.

---

## 1. Core Mindset Adjustments

### Treat this like an ocean, not a forest

You are not walking into a forest; you are walking into a **wet engine**.  
There are three main forces to respect:

- **Rain-that-never-stops** → constant water load from above.
- **Jungle-that-never-stops** → near-total occlusion, constant pressure from living and dead matter.
- **River-machine** → engineered energy far below, capable of swallowing anything that falls into its system.

Your mindset: anything you leave out gets wet. Any slip on slope becomes a fall. Any careless approach to the river becomes death.

### Treat height as deceptive

You are on steep mountain slopes, but:

- Air is thick, humid, and warm – not thin and cold.
- Altitude sickness is still possible, but less obvious than in alpine mountains.
- Exhaustion comes more from **heat + humidity + climbing** than from pure elevation.

Respect the height, but don’t rely on it giving you clear signals (“I feel fine” = still potentially hypoxic, plus soaked, plus heat-stressed).

## 2. Clothing and Body Protection

### Layers – for wetness and warmth, not for cold

- **Outer layer: waterproof and tough**
  - Full rain suit or jacket + pants with **taped seams**.
  - Not just water-resistant; “waterproof” rating minimum 10,000mm.
  - Prefer light or medium-colored outer fabric: you’ll need to see dirt, mold, and parasites against it.

- **Mid-layer: fast-drying insulation**
  - Synthetic fleece or thin primaloft.
  - Avoid cotton at all costs.
  - Even wool is second best here; synthetics dry much faster under constant rain.

- **Base layer: moisture-wicking**
  - Synthetic or merino wool (if you can afford multiple changes).
  - Change base layer daily if possible; at least strip and dry it at camp.

- **Underwear and socks**
  - Bring many sets. **Rotate and attempt dry them** every night.
  - Fungi and bacteria love this climate: do not rewear the same soaked underwear for long.

### Extremities protection

- **Gloves:**
  - Two types:
    - Lightweight, quick-drying gloves for steady climbing and handling wet rocks.
    - Tougher gloves for handling concrete edges, rusted metal, or vegetation with spines/hidden thorns.
- **Hat:**
  - Wide-brimmed rain hat or helmet with brim.
  - Something that keeps water off your neck and face.

- **Neck gaiter / microfiber towel:**
  - To wipe off continuous water streaming from helmet/hat.

- **Goggles/clear glasses**
  - Not sunglasses; the light is muted.
  - You need protection from:
    - Water dripping into your eyes.
    - Debris when you slip or brush vegetation.
    - Rain spray and splashes off surfaces.

### Footwear – critical

- **Waterproof boots:**
  - Full-grain leather or modern synthetic with waterproof lining.
  - Not low-top: water must stay out, not invited.
  - Prefer boots with:
    - Deep tread pattern that can clear mud.
    - Toe protection from rocks.
    - Stability around the ankle for uneven slopes.

- **Gaiters:**
  - Full height gaiters to seal over boot tops.
  - Prevent water from pouring down your leg into the boot.

- **Camp footwear**
  - Lightweight sandals or water shoes that you only wear in camp.
  - Allows your feet *time out of soaked boots*.
  - Keep feet inspected and aired daily.

You’re still going to be wet; but you can avoid *cell-deep waterlogged* feet and blisters.

## 3. Shelter and Sleep System

### Tent

- Lightweight **tunnel tent or trekking pole tent** with:
  - Taped seams.
  - Full coverage rainfly.
  - Raised floor bathtub.
- **Footprint or groundsheet:** required to protect from:
  - Ground moisture.
  - Sharp stones hidden under soil.
  - Rust stains / chemical residues from the machine-area below.

- Ensure:
  - All vents are usable even in rain.
  - You know how to set it up **fast**, under constant rain.

### Site selection

Avoid:

- Directly below obvious drip channels (tree branches, funnels).
- Tops of ridges where wind might be sharper if the weather pattern ever changed.
- Anywhere you can feel a slight rumble through the ground; that suggests you’re too near the machine-river system.

Prefer:

- Small **natural terraces or ledges** where water runs *around* not through.
- On ground with visible soil/debris rather than bare rock (which may be constantly slick).
- Away from obvious animal trails marked by disturbed moss.

### Inside the tent

- Place **all gear** in dry bags or waterproof stuff sacks.
- Use a **condensation towel**: small synthetic cloth you wipe interior walls with.
- Expect your sleeping bag/quilt to get damp no matter what you do; bring **synthetic insulation**.

## 4. Gear and Tools

### Backpack

- **Internal frame or frameless**, but with:
  - Waterproof liner **and** dry bags inside.
  - Outer pack cover (even if it doesn't truly keep out rain, it helps with splash).
- Prefer dark colors or camouflaged but not **leaf** pattern; you need to find it if you drop it in the vegetation.

### Essential tools

- **Fixed-blade knife:** full tang for cutting wood (if you’re allowed to harvest), and self-rescue situations.
- **Multi-tool** with:
  - Pliers, for mechanical systems if you encounter them.
  - Screwdriver, in case you need to interact with anything man-made.
- **Saw or pruning saw:** light but functional, for clearing vegetation where you *must* pass.
- **Trekking poles:** essential for stability on wet, greased basalt slopes.

### Lighting

- Headlamp with **white and red light**.
- Bring **extra batteries** stored in waterproof containers.
- Assume light is needed more often than you expect; the mist and canopy create illusions of dusk even at midday.

### Water system

- **Filter/purifier:** for bacteria/protozoa, even though the river looks engineered.
- **Chemical backups:** chlorine tablets or iodine as emergency.
- **Collecting rainwater:**
  - Use a rain fly edge or funnel to collect into clean containers.
  - Rainwater here likely safer than valley water if you catch it clean.

Do not drink directly from any unknown stream, especially near the machine-river channel; you don’t know what oils, residues, or materials are in the system.

## 5. Navigation and Communication

### The visibility trap

- Mist can move in and out **within seconds**.
- You can lose line of sight to your last landmark immediately.
- You cannot rely on stars or sun; cloud cover is constant.

### Required navigation tools

- **Compass:** non-electronic, reliable.
- **Detailed topo map:** if available from your multiverse supplier.
- **Marked route:** preplanned bearing with back-bearings.
- **Notebook + pencil:** waterproof paper notebook for recording bearings, landmarks, and sound changes.
- **Optional modern:** GPS device with preloaded maps and extra battery packs.

### Trail markers

- Use natural marking:
  - Bright, compact flagging tape **tied tightly** around branches.
  - Stone cairns on rock slopes where you must avoid.
- **Do not mark your path with items that scream “human”:**  
  Avoid plastic waste, colors wildly out of place with the environment.

### Communication

- **Satellite messenger or emergency beacon:** if you can afford it.
- **Pocket whistle:** for sound signaling under heavy rain noise.
- **Pre-agreed emergency plan:** three whistle blasts, wait, repeat.

## 6. Food and Food Storage

### Calories and moisture

- Expect higher energy expenditure:
  - Heat + humidity drains.
  - Constant climbing on slippery slopes.
  - Slogging through wet vegetation.

- Bring **high-energy, dense, dry foods**:
  - Dehydrated meals.
  - Nuts and seeds (if no allergies).
  - Energy bars.
  - Hard cheeses and cured meats (if shelf-stable).

### Avoiding spoilage and mold

- Store everything in sealed, dry containers:
  - Silica gel packets in each container to absorb moisture.
  - Check regularly for signs of mold or damp.

- Assume:
  - Any fresh food you bring molds within days.
  - Your bread will be soft then wet then slimy then gone.

### Cooking

- **Canister stove** or liquid fuel stove with:
  - Extra fuel; efficiency is lower in constant humidity.
  - Windshield that also works as rain shield.
- **Cook under tent vestibule** if weather allows or make a small tarp shelter for cooking.
- Always have:
  - **Emergency fire starter:** waterproof matches, ferro rod.
  - **Tinder:** stored in waterproof bags; cotton balls soaked in petroleum jelly, etc.

## 7. Health and Hygiene in a Wet Jungle Engine

### The skin threat

Your main enemies are:

- Fungi (tinea, athlete’s foot).
- Bacterial infections in cuts/scratches.
- Maceration (your skin turning permanently soggy and weak).

### Daily care routine

- **Change socks and base layers every day if possible.**
- Dry feet off, **inspect for cuts, blisters**, red patches.
- Use talcum powder or antifungal powder **lightly** on feet and groin area.
- Don’t sleep in wet clothing if you can help it.

### Medical kit essentials

- Antifungal cream/ointment.
- Antibacterial ointment.
- Gauze pads, medical tape, bandages (all in waterproof packaging).
- Iodine or antiseptic swabs for cleaning cuts.
- **Oral rehydration salts (electrolytes)**
- Medications:
  - Antihistamines for reactions.
  - Any personal allergy meds.
  - Mild painkillers (ibuprofen).
  - Antibiotics (if available and prescribed by you, pre-trip).

- Small scissors, tweezers, tick remover tool.
- Sunscreen: even under mist, UV can still be strong at altitude.

### Waste and hygiene

- Pack out all toilet paper or use natural methods that follow strict low-impact:
  - No waste near water sources.
  - No waste near the machine-river system.
- Use a trowel for burying solid waste if allowed; otherwise pack out.
- **Always wash hands with treated water** after toilet and before eating.

## 8. How to Move in this Terrain

### Slope and greased basalt

The slope is steep, with dark basalt that is water-slicked.

Technique:

- **Three points of contact:** at least three limbs in contact with stable surface when moving on truly steep or wet sections.
- Use trekking poles:
  - Test each placement before fully weight it.
  - Distribute weight on them horizontally to test grip.

- Move in:
  - Short, steady steps.
  - No sudden jumps or rushes.
- Avoid:
  - Stepping on visibly smooth, dark, wet stone slabs without edges.
  - Stepping on roots that look like they might be floating over air.

### Vegetation and route choice

- Don't assume a clear animal trail = safe human trail.
- Some animal trails may be:
  - Overhanging edges.
  - Directly above steep drops.
  - Involving narrow ledges.

- Use machete/saw sparingly:
  - Only cut where you cannot pass otherwise.
  - Examine before cutting for:
    - Hidden thorns.
    - Epiphytes that might fall.
    - Live branches giving you structural stability you’ll miss after cutting.

### Crossing wet ground and roots

- Whenever roots cross your path, treat them like railings:
  - Test before weight.
  - Use them to steady yourself.
  - Be aware they might be rotten inside despite external appearance.

- Step on:
  - Base of trees or roots anchored deep.
  - Exposed soil that still seems firm.
- Avoid:
  - Stepping on moss-draped surfaces that look suspiciously “floaty.”
  - Stepping on large leaf areas that may be only water over void.

## 9. The Machine-River System (Below You)

### Listening to the river

Your most important tool with respect to the river-engine is **your ears**.

- Notice:
  - Regular rhythms of water sound.
  - Any changes in pattern or pitch:
    - Higher pitch = higher velocity?
    - Low throbs = different structures underwater?
- **Full stop and listen:** On steep slopes approaching any edge of the valley, stop periodically and just listen.

### Approaching engineered intakes and channels

- Assume:
  - All exposed concrete edges are wet, slippery, and potentially algae-coated.
  - Rust edges might be sharp; they are hardened metal exposed.
  - Any water flowing into a channel may be channeled into **intake structures** that you cannot see.

- **Do not step directly onto the concrete edge** if you can avoid:
  - Approach from stable side ground.
  - Stay far back from where edges drop into the water system.
- Use a safety line:
  - If you must approach near intake edges, have a companion and a line attached.
  - If no companion, then work slowly, with great distance, or don’t approach at all.

- Signs to be cautious of:
  - Concrete structures with visible cracked edges.
  - Any area where the water’s transparency reveals **metal grills, bars, or mechanical structures** below.
  - Any place where you smell:
    - Oil.
    - Metallic tang.
    - Chemical smell underneath the normal mold and stone smell.

### Treat the machine-river as a predator

It does not chase you; but it:

- Is strong enough to pull anything under.
- Uses engineered shapes to maximize energy extraction.
- Likely contains submerged machinery that you can get caught on.

Your rules:

- **Never enter the water voluntarily.**
- **Never cross without knowing where you’re stepping to.**
- **Always assume the edge nearest the machine is unstable.**

## 10. Ecology and Living Beings

### Plants

- Expect:
  - Leaves to be edged with water; be ready for them to dump a load on you.
  - Epiphytes and orchids to be anchored above; they might fall, dislodge, or have insects living in them.

- Don’t:
  - Grab vegetation blindly for support:
    - Some may have hidden spines.
    - Some may be fragile and break in your hand.
    - Some may detach entire mats of moss and soil.
- Use sturdy trunks and large roots where you can verify their strength.

### Insects and small animals

- They are not swarming you like a plague, but:
  - You might find:
    - Small beetles crawling over you from time to time.
    - Small predatory spiders or ants where you extend your hand.

- Check:
  - Your gloves before putting them on.
  - Your boots before wearing them.
  - Your bedding before entering your sleeping bag.

### Larger animals

You’re never entirely sure they are there, but:

- They have been here:
  - Disturbed moss pattern.
  - Bent vegetation.
  - Occasional roll of sound deeper in the underbrush.

- Behavior:
  - Stay aware of sounds and changes in pattern.
  - If you notice:
    - A pattern of noise that seems to move when you move.
    - Repeated signs of tracks in your direction.
  - Then move:
    - With more noise.
    - Avoiding aggressive or sudden gestures.
    - Potentially taking detours to avoid confrontation.

## 11. Time of Day and Perception

### Light

- The light is muted white under perpetually clouded sky.
- Shadows:
  - Not sharp.
  - Easily confused with dark patches of moss or stone.

- Don’t rely on:
  - Sun position for orientation.
  - Shadows for depth perception.

- Use your:
  - Compass.
  - Timekeeping.
  - Map and landmarks tied to slope and landforms.

### Timekeeping

- Bring:
  - Waterproof watch.
  - Spare battery or mechanical watch.
- Note:
  - Start and end times of your day’s movement.
  - Approximate rest times.
- This helps:
  - Estimate distance traveled (if you know average speed).
  - Track how long you’ve been in heat + humidity stress.

## 12. Psychological and Emotional Preparation

### Aesthetic and mental pressure

This place is:

- Beautifully oppressive:
  - Everything is wet.
  - Everything is growing onto itself.
  - There is no dry, clear space of “open sky.”

You might feel:

- Claustrophobic from the canopy and mist.
- Overwhelmed by the unending noise of drip and river-engine.
- Disconnected from time because day and night feel similar under cloud cover.

### Daily mental practices

- Simple routines:
  - Morning and evening checks of body, equipment, and plan.
  - Small journal entries of mood, weather patterns, and sound changes.
- Maintain your:
  - Schedule of sleep even if light doesn’t change clearly.
  - Eating discipline.

- Acknowledge:
  - You may be seeing never-before-seen lifeforms.
  - This place is ancient, accomplished, and will not adjust itself for you.

You’re the visitor; you adapt to it.

### 13. A Final Checklist Before Stepping Into That World

- **Waterproof everything:** backpack liner, dry bags, clothes in waterproof stuff sacks.
- **Check boots:** waterproofing, treads, laces.
- **Check body protection:** rain jacket, pants, gloves, hat, eye protection.
- **Check shelter:** tent fly, footprint, groundsheet, stakes.
- **Check navigation tools:** compass, map (protected), notebook, pencil.
- **Check light:** headlamp, extra batteries.
- **Check health:** med kit, rehydration salts, antifungal, antibacterial.
- **Check food:** high-calorie, sealed, stored against water.
- **Check tools:** knife, multi-tool, trekking poles, emergency fire starter.
- **Check your plan:** known route, expected camp locations, escape plans back to higher safe ground.

If you respect moisture as your main opponent, treat the mountain slope as a greased surface, and the machine-river as a silent engine you can hear but never fully see, then you stand a good chance of leaving this place alive, with the memory of its endless rain, green layered living, and the sound of water harnessed into something almost like a machine.
