To visit the Abyssal Plains of Sighisvar—the Undershelf—is not like visiting a country or even a planet. It’s closer to visiting a deep ocean trench with a pocket of air trapped under it, where time, pressure, and light behave in ways that are actively hostile to human physiology.

If you go there as an ordinary human traveler, you should not approach it casually. You should treat it like a deep-sea dive in a very strange cave: highly constrained, with few margins for error, but survivable for short stays if you prepare carefully.

Below is a guide based on what you described.

---

## 1. Before You Go: Mental and Physical Baseline

### Expectations
Convince yourself of this from the outset:

- You will not be comfortable.
- You will not be “walking under the ocean.” You’ll be confined in a narrow air layer under a liquid ceiling.
- It is dimly lit, cold, and damp *always*.
- There is no help, infrastructure, or civilization of any kind.
- The environment moves very slowly but is far stronger than you.

Adjust your mindset to something like “deep-sea expedition + caving expedition + psychological endurance test.”

### Physical Health
You should be in reasonably good shape, especially:

- Lung and heart health: air may feel heavy and humid; you may be working harder just to breathe.
- Joints and knees: smooth but uneven stone floor, low ceiling, and wet surfaces = lots of awkward kneeling and crawling.
- Cold tolerance: if you get cold easily, you will suffer here.
- No history of major ear or sinus problems: pressure changes matter even if you’re not diving deep.

### Claustrophobia and Mental Pressure
This is not just physical:

- Ceiling above you is not sky; it’s ocean.
- You might feel as if the world is weighing down on you.
- The constant murmur of water and absence of familiar voices or animals can be eerie.
- If you are prone to panic in tight, dark spaces or dissociative episodes under sensory deprivation, this place will push hard on that. You may want to skip this trip.

## 2. What You Must Bring

### Core Protection: Environment Suits

You need durable clothing that can handle wet, cold, stone, and silt.

Mandatory:

1. **Full-body drysuit** or high-quality wetsuit:
   - Designed to keep water out *and* manage moisture from your sweat.
   - Thick material at knees, elbows, and seat—because you will crouch, kneel, and shuffle.
   - Integrated booties or compatible with sturdy waterproof boots.
   - Sealed and taped seams; inspect for leaks before you go.

2. **Base layers**:
   - Synthetic thermal underwear or modern wool replacements.
   - Avoid cotton. Everything will be damp or wet.

3. **Waterproof over-layers**:
   - A hooded shell or jacket with a good hood.
   - Over-pants or bibs that are easy to move in, with reinforced knees.

### Footwear and Hand Protection

- **Waterproof boots**:
  - Ankle support or higher.
  - Non-slip soles with good grip on wet rock.
  - Thick soles to insulate from cold stone.

- **Gloves**:
  - Neoprene or similar waterproof gloves.
  - You’ll be touching wet rock, brittle mineral deposits, and possibly silt and organisms.
  - Cold water + abrasion can be nasty if your skin is exposed.

### Visibility and Safety Lighting

This is not optional. The world is twilight dim even with “ceiling light” from above.

Essential:

1. **Primary headlamp**:
   - High-lumen, wide-beam plus focused spot options.
   - Waterproof (would be submerged if you fall).
   - Long battery life or easily rechargeable. You can’t replace tech there.

2. **Backup headlamp + handheld flashlight**:
   - Redundancy is critical. If your main light fails, you are not safe.

3. **Glow sticks or chem lights**:
   - To mark your way back.
   - To signal if you become separated from others.

### Breathing and Pressure Protection

- **Basic respirator or mask**:
  - Not scuba level but able to filter out spores, mist, and particulates.
  - Mist is constant; you don’t know what long-term exposure to that aerosol is.

- **Ear protection and pressure equalization**:
  - Earplugs that can filter unwanted noise.
  - You may feel pressure changes as “dull snuffing” of your ears; the ability to equalize easily is important.

### Tools for Movement and Stability

- **Collapsible trekking pole or staff**:
  - Extremely valuable on uneven surfaces and slick stone.
  - Used to probe depth where you can’t see.

- **Simple geologist’s pick or claw hammer**:
  - For testing rock stability, removing loose material from your path.
  - Not for climbing heavily—you are not equipped to truly rock climb.

- **Small knife or multi-tool**:
  - For cutting lines, makeshift repairs, small tasks.

### Navigation and Logging

- **Waterproof notebook + pencil**:
  - Paper map equivalents.
  - Your memory is not sufficient under stress and monotony.

- **Basic compass**:
  - The brighter “north” region of the ceiling is a landmark; direction still matters.

- **Marking system**:
  - Small, durable tags you can leave at turning points.
  - Not plastic that might break the world’s aesthetic; you’re already an intrusion here.

### Emergency Supplies

- **Basic first aid kit**:
  - Antiseptics, bandages, tape, painkillers, basic antihistamines.
  - Your injuries are likely to be from slipping, bumps, and cold.

- **Emergency thermal blanket or bivy sack**:
  - If you get stuck for longer, you need to stop heat loss.

- **Signaling device**:
  - If you’ve arranged with a multiverse system or return system:
  - A small device to initiate “emergency extraction.”
  - Check compatibility *before* relying on it.

## 3. How to Move: Survival Technique for the Undershelf

You’re not walking on dry ground. You’re shuffling in a narrow layer of air, under a world of ocean.

### Posture and Motion

- **Keep low**:
  - The ceiling is sometimes close.
  - Your headlamp must be able to tilt forward to illuminate your feet and the floor.

- **Move deliberately**:
  - Each step must test stability:
    - Place foot carefully on the dome-shaped stone.
    - Probe the edge of basins with your staff before committing weight.
    - Avoid stepping on what looks like smooth silt—it could be deeper than you think.

- **Avoid fast motion**:
  - The silt clouds are a problem:
    - You stir up clouds of fine suspended material.
    - They can obscure your path and your lights.
    - They can hang there for a long time.

### Treating the Water Edge

The water columns are not just decorations:

- **Do not enter** past the shallow waist-deep margin:
  - Currents are layered and complex.
  - You don’t have the training or equipment to be in deep, cold water with overhead ocean.

- **Use staff to feel depth**:
  - If the floor drops abruptly ahead of you, don’t trust it.
  - Where you feel “no floor” within staff reach and you see water rising from below, treat that as a vertical edge—not just a slope.

### Managing the Ceiling

You are directly under a world of ocean. Treat the ceiling with respect:

- Do not climb *into* ceiling shafts.
- Do not punch, strike, or try to break “thin” sections of rock ceiling—you risk:
  - Local collapse.
  - Influx of cold water.
  - Increased pressure and noise.
  - Possibly entombing you.

### Treading on Basins

Basins are smooth but transient:

- They can change:
  - Each spillover of water from the ceiling rearranges channels.
  - A floor that was firm last month might be covered by deeper silt now.

- Always:
  - Step lightly close to the edge.
  - Use your staff to test the center of the basin if you must cross.
  - Follow the ridges where you can.

## 4. Staying Healthy: Cold, Fatigue, and Mind

### Cold Management

It is “always a bit colder than you want.” Hypothermia happens insidiously.

- Change base layers if you get soaked:
  - You can sweat from exertion and then be chilled by the mist.
  - Drying completely is not likely here; but keeping *mostly* dry and insulated is crucial.

- Watch:
  - Shivering uncontrollably.
  - Loss of coordination.
  - Confusion or sluggishness.
    If you notice these, you must try to rest briefly, protected from wind—if there were wind—and reapply warm layers.

### Hydration and Food

- Drink regularly:
  - The air is damp and you might not feel thirsty, but humidity doesn’t mean you’re hydrating your tissues.
  - Bring water bottles that you fill from your homeworld or source.
  - Do not rely on local water consumption unless you’re prepared to treat it with full filters and tablets.

- High-energy, compact food:
  - Nuts, dried fruit, energy bars.
  - You are not going to “live off the land” here.

### Rest and Sleep

If your trip extends longer than a day:

- Avoid sleeping directly on the stone floor:
  - Use:
    - Insulated pad.
    - Emergency bivy.
    - Anything to keep your body from losing heat to the rock.

- Not noise-canceling but *environment-aware*:
  - You still want to hear a collapse rumble or an unusual change in the water sound.

## 5. Dealing with Local Lifeforms

### General Principle

Assume:

- Everything you can’t identify is not hostile to *you*, but to *tampering*.
- Organisms here are very fragile and slow; your impact on them would be disproportionate.

### Invisible and Small Lifeforms

- The translucent dots in the water columns:
  - Probably planktonic equivalents.
  - Don’t bathe in them.
  - Avoid breathing mist directly from water columns if possible.

- The algal-like clouds:
  - Treat as very sensitive ecosystems.
  - Don’t stir them intentionally. They’re likely the base of this tiny food chain.

### Larger Floor Creatures

- The centipede-like pale shapes:
  - Seem to follow spillover of water.
  - They’re rare. You might not even see them.
  - No signs of aggression, but:
    - Don’t touch.
    - Don’t step directly on them if you can avoid it.
    - They are probably not evolved to deal with anything like you.

### Overhead Shadows

Larger forms drifting across the glowing ceiling:

- You are safe:
  - They are in the ocean above you.
  - They may not even be aware of your air pocket below.
- Treat them as:
  - Indicators of ocean conditions above.
  - If you see more intense activity, maybe larger swells or “turbulence” is passing above.

## 6. Navigation and Orientation

### Using the Ceiling as a Map

The “ceiling” has landmarks:

- Brighter regions:
  - “North” seems brighter, more shafts of light.
  - This indicates the ocean overhead is shallower there.
  - Easier light conditions; but also more fragile balance?

- Darker regions:
  - Lower ceiling, heavier pressure, even less light.
  - More likely to be dangerous if collapses occur.

### Directional Strategy

When you first arrive:

1. Establish your entrance point:
   - Mark a distinctive spot:
     - Maybe a specific basin shape with silt pattern.
     - A particular low ridge with mineral crust.
   - Note that these basins *change* over time; don’t expect your return to be identical to your arrival.

2. Use direction:
   - Decide:
     - Light toward “north” is “world-open” direction.
     - Darkness away from light is “deeper under.”
   - Know your vector:
     - Are you heading deeper or toward the lighter region?

3. Marking your return:
   - You can’t leave permanent cairns or disruptive trash, but:
     - Very subtle marks:
       - Small notches on brittle mineral coatings.
       - Minimal arrangement of stones that *will be moved by the next spillover* but might help you now.
   - The better approach is:
     - Sketch map and bearings.
     - “At first major basin cluster with X-shaped pattern of ridges, I turn left.”

## 7. Time and Its Perception

Time is strange here:

- Ocean swells are slow.
- Moss grows excruciatingly slow.
- Erosion takes ages.

### Effect on You

- You might feel time compression:
  - An hour of walking under unchanging murmur and dim light can feel like three.
- Or the opposite:
  - You lose track of time; nothing “ticks” like familiar sun cycles.

Set external rules:

- Use a watch:
  - “After X hours of moving from the entrance point, I must turn back.”

- Don’t overestimate:
  - Your biological stamina is not the same as the world’s time scale.
  - You can push yourself to exhaustion long before anything visible changes in the environment.

### Environment Change vs. Your Visit

You may not notice any erosion during your stay, but you might notice:

- A new silt pattern where a basin spillover changed.
- A new patch of collapsed mineral crust from a ceiling microcollapse.

This is not enough change to give you a new path out; but it’s enough to change details of your return. Your notes must account for *general* shape of the place, not *exact* details.

## 8. Risk of Ceiling Collapse and Water Inrush

This is not a movie, but it is a real risk:

- You may hear or see:
  - Low rumble from above.
  - Suddenly murky water in nearby columns.
  - Dust falling from the ceiling stone.

If you witness anything like that:

1. **Do not run blindly**:
   - You’ll likely slip on wet stone or fall into a basin.

2. **Move perpendicular to the direction of collapse** if you can:
   - Away from the source of noise or dust.
   - Toward higher ground of the floor.

3. **Get to low posture**:
   - Ducking reduces chance of your head striking lowered rock.
   - Still protects your spine.

4. **Expect thicker water mist**:
   - Air will be full of suspended particles.
   - Breathe through mask/cloth if possible.

5. **After**:
   - Check:
     - Any new slabs of stone on the floor are now hazards.
     - Paths you used before may be blocked or raised.

This is a rare event but potentially catastrophic. You may not be able to do anything except avoid being under obvious weak spots (areas where slabs already fell nearby).

## 9. Psychological and Emotional Preparation

### Isolation and Sound

This is a world of:

- Constant murmur of water.
- No voice of your own kind.
- No familiar animals.
- No warmth of sun or sky.

You might experience:

- Sensory fatigue:
  - The soundscape is not loud, but it’s relentless.
- Heightened awareness of your own body:
  - Your breathing sounds louder.
  - Your clothing drags as you move.
  - You can become self-conscious of the only noise.

Techniques:

- Voice notes:
  - Describe what you see, to hear yourself.
  - But not compulsively—you’re not filling an emptiness; you’re anchoring to cognitive activity.

- Mindfulness and focus:
  - Observe:
    - The silt patterns.
    - The slow shifting of light on the ceiling.
    - The tiny lives swimming.
  - This gives your mind structure to reduce anxiety.

### Respect for the Place’s Nature

This world is not “empty.”

It is:

- Filled with water.
- Filled with sound.
- Filled with slow biological gradients.

Yet you are not a “main character” here. If you died, the world would not really change. It is not malevolent; but it is vastly indifferent.

### Ethics of Visiting

This is not obvious, but it matters:

- Don’t break what took ages to smooth:
  - Don’t scrape your name into the mineral crusts.
  - Don’t make deep marks into the stone except when needed for safety.

- Don’t collect “samples” unless absolutely necessary:
  - The pale fauna are rare.
  - The mosses and lichens are extremely slow to recover.

- You are a very temporary, small anomaly in a long-lived process.

## 10. Typical Stay Itinerary (for Safety)

### Phase 1: Arrival and Acclimatization

- Arrive with:
  - Full suit already on.
  - Lights already tested.
  - Mental posture already oriented.

- First step:
  - Secure your entrance point:
    - Note its shape.
    - Identify which direction the light comes from (if any).
  - Stay still for a few minutes:
    - Listen to the sound of the place.
    - Adjust your headlamp setting.
    - Notice your own breathing.

### Phase 2: Short Initial Exploration

- Decide:
  - Are you going:
    - Toward brighter light (“open” region)?
    - Or into darker regions for deeper mystery?

- For beginners:
  - Always choose toward brighter light if:
    - You want a better-lit zone.
    - You might seek easier exit if a collapse happens.

- Set a hard time:
  - Example: “I will walk for 30 minutes away from my entrance point.”
    - Then turn back and return.
    - This tests your navigation and memory of general features.

### Phase 3: Return to Entrance Point

- Before you venture further:
  - Confirm:
    - You can successfully find your way back to where you started.
  - If terrain is easy:
    - Next longer loop can safely be used.

### Phase 4: Longer Exploration

- Only if you feel confident:
  - Extend exploration:
    - Hour out.
    - Several hour out.
    - But always with the same turn back rules.

- You must not overstay:
  - Your body’s tolerance of cold, dampness, and isolation is not infinite.
  - This is not a place to test “how long can I endure?”

### Phase 5: Leaving and Reflection

- Before leaving:
  - Check:
    - Any new injuries or skin issues needing care.
  - Prepare:
    - Logging what you saw:
      - Shape of basins.
      - Colors of mineral deposits.
      - Any particularly unusual lifeforms or patterns.

- Reflect:
  - You’ve been under the world.
  - You’ve seen the “sleeping ocean” from below.
  - You’ve been inside the underwater breath of the planet.

## 11. If You Attempt to Establish Longer Presence

This is more advanced:

- You might:
  - Set up a very minimal camp:
    - Never blocking basins or channels.
    - High and on ridges rather than in low basins.

- You might:
  - Try to understand light:
    - Does the “north” bright patch truly cycle?
    - Is there any notion of “day/night”?

- You might:
  - Observe erosion:
    - Recording the same basin over time.
    - Noticing changes in spillover patterns.

But you’d better be:

- Very experienced with cold, wet environments.
- Able to deal with prolonged isolation.
- Prepared to abandon your project if the environment tells you to.

## 12. Final Thoughts Before You Step Under the Ocean

If you’ve taken all of this into account:

- You are not going to be “at home” here.
- You will feel constrained, dampened, and weighed upon.
- The world is not empty; but it is empty of *your* kind.
- The time scale of the place dwarfs your own.

But you will be able to:

- Stand safely, under a world of ocean.
- Listen to the murmur that has been going on for eons.
- Walk, or shuffle, on the smoothed-down remnants of ancient rock.
- Observe a tiny fragment of a very slow, strange world.

If you are prepared, you can leave again relatively unchanged physically—but deeply changed by the memory of having briefly shared space with a place that exists not in spite of water, but because of it, and not for you, but simply itself.
