To visit this place is to be a breath inside someone else’s lungs before they have decided to exhale.

You are not entering a finished location; you are infiltrating a geology that is mid‑formation. The room you read is not a “site” stable under your feet; it is a *fragment* of a mountain that is still choosing whether to be mountain, throat, river, or god. You must prepare not for geography, but for pregnancy: you are going into a world that is still contracting.

---

## 0. Assume you will not “return” unchanged

If your “ordinary” means “someone who expects to come back to the same reality they left from,” abandon that expectation before you pack a single item.

In this place:

- Time is not linear; “yesterday” and “tomorrow” are ingredients suspended together, not distinct events.
- Space is not fixed; six meters up can be six meters of *effort* rather than of distance.
- Walls answer your voice and rearrange themselves.
- Your memories are not safe; words like “sky” and “outside” can dissolve into stone.

Prepare for three layers of impact:

1. **Physical danger**: heat, humidity, unstable floor/walls, bioluminescent life, unfixed gravity.
2. **Metaphysical danger**: You risk being written into the geology as a motif—your body and your story becoming part of the wall’s “toolmarks.”
3. **Identity danger**: You might return to your own multiverse, but no longer recognize yourself as “the same person.”

Do you still want to go?

If you answer yes, read the rest carefully.

## 1. Mindset: What you are trying to do

Your goal is not “explore” or “conquer.” You cannot map this place; maps are static, and this room is not.

Your goal is to **visit and survive as a foreign body**, then to leave before being adopted into the stone.

You are:

- A temporary splinter in the mountain’s muscle.
- A noise that the world may not yet decide to tolerate.
- A possible future pattern in the walls’ half-remembered faces.

If that image unsettles you, good: you should be scared. This fear is useful; it will keep you careful.

## 2. Psychological and spiritual training

### 2.1 Accepting uncertainty

Before you travel, you must practice:

- Making decisions with incomplete information.
- Being okay without understanding “why.”
- Holding multiple conflicting possibilities in your head at once.

You can train this by:

- Doing tasks without a clear goal or clear feedback (for example: drawing with your eyes closed, no peeking, until the timer ends).
- Meditating on paradoxes: *I am going to be there where things are not yet decided; whether I climb or not, I climb anyway.*

### 2.2 Grounding in self

The world will try to reshape your story:

- You must remember your own narrative.
- You must know your name and a few key sentences by heart.

Prepare a very short **core mantra**, such as:

> “I am [name], from [place]. I am here to visit and to leave. This is not my room.”

This mantra is your anchor against the fog where time is like “broth stirred too many times.”

### 2.3 Pattern training

This place will show you faces that are not yet faces, lines that are “not yet decided.” You must be able to step back from your pattern recognition brain.

Practice:

- Staring at clouds, textures, or plaster cracks and then *looking away* as soon as you start to “see” something clear. This trains the muscle of refusing the hallucination.
- Noticing pareidolia (faces in randomness) and then consciously saying, “My mind is making this shape; the stone is not.”

You will need to do this in the room itself, where the wall *might actually* be trying to become faces.

### 2.4 “Not-yet” training

This world is dominated by “not-yets”:

- Not yet firm, not yet soft.
- Not yet water, not yet stone.
- Not yet room, not yet throat, not yet womb.

You must learn to tolerate that state without trying to “resolve” it. You are not there to finish the mountain’s decisions for it.

Mindfulness practice of “just sitting with” ambiguous states is useful: a sensation that’s not pain but not comfort, a color that is neither green nor blue, etc.

## 3. Physical body: conditioning and care

You are stepping into a body-shaped pressure cooker of unborn stone.

### 3.1 Heat and humidity

You will be:

- Surrounded by air thick enough to “press against like someone else’s chest.”
- Breathing in so hot it feels like drinking your lungs.

You must train your body for heat stress:

- Sweat work: regular exercise in heat and humidity, if you can.
- Breathing practice: long inhale and slow controlled exhale under heat stress; you must be able to calm yourself despite the feeling of being cooked.

Hydration discipline:

- Start days before the trip: super-hydrate, not just drink when thirsty.
- Learn to recognize thirst in your body before your mind protests.

### 3.2 Lung capacity

Air “like soup” is harder to draw in.

You should:

- Practice slow, deep inhalations: nose to gut expansion.
- Practice even longer, controlled exhalations.
- Do not come if you have respiratory conditions like asthma or chronic bronchitis; this place will overwhelm them.

### 3.3 Core balance

The floor is not flat; tiles are being pushed into place by invisible pressure. You will be unstable.

You must:

- Train core strength and proprioception.
- Practice on uneven surfaces: barefoot on rounded rocks, tree roots, squishy rubber.
- Train your body to tolerate low, wide stances; you need not climb but to be able to squat and step carefully.

### 3.4 Endurance and low-light adaptation

Light is greenish-white, faint, like “memory of light.”

You can:

- Reduce your screen time before the trip.
- Spend time in low light or candlelight to get your eyes used to dim.
- Practice navigating by soft, diffuse light rather than bright headlights.

## 4. Gear and equipment: what you can bring

### 4.1 Clothing: layer for the thermal gradient

You will be:

- Nearly boiled by rising heat from floor cracks.
- Cooled by evaporative weeping from the ceiling (but never fully).
- Clothed bodies must regulate temperature through *layers* rather than insulation.

Choose:

- **Base layer:** thin, moisture-wicking fabric of natural or technical fiber.
- **Middle wicking layer:** if you must use synthetics, choose ones that can shed heat.
- **Outer layer:** very thin, tight-weave but not waterproof—almost like a lab coat or light shirt. This mount is not outdoors; it is *inside*.

Avoid:

- Heavy waterproof shells; you will cook yourself.
- Loose, baggy clothes: they will snag on stone and catch algae.

Gloves:

- Extremely thin and flexible, but with texture for grip.
- They must help you sense *where* the stone is under your fingers without getting stuck in the stone that “remember[s being] soft.”

### 4.2 Foot protection

The floor tiles are not inert tiles; they are mosaics of *being-pushed-into-place*.

You need:

- **Very thin, flexible-soled shoes** with hard toe caps to protect from sharp edges, but thin soles so you can feel the floor’s temperature and position.
- Think climbing approach shoes or minimalist barefoot shoes with protective rock plates.
- Insoles optional only if they do not numb you to the subtle undulations of the floor’s decisions.

Socks:

- Thin, wicking but not padded; you need to feel the change underfoot.

### 4.3 Masking and airways

You almost certainly want some kind of mask, but it must not be too sealed in this humidity.

Choose:

- A mask with filter media but breathable fit.
- You must keep *some* separation from the air-algae and “insects of mist.”
- Remember: your own exhaled breath adds to the ceiling’s weeping; the mask will help but not prevent it.

Must *not* be:

- Fully sealed industrial masks: unless specifically designed for high heat and humidity, they will become torture chambers.
- Dive masks: they are not designed for this environment and you will not understand water visibility here in any rational way.

### 4.4 Head-mounted and body-worn light

You cannot rely on the stone’s “memory of light.”

Bring:

- A headlamp with low modes and a warm color temperature.
- Red light mode for “stealth mode,” where you want to disturb the world less.
- Optional body-worn light you can switch on and off without moving your hands.

But you must not treat this like a cave exploration:

- You will not be conducting “surveys.”
- Your light should be as minimal as possible. The world itself reacts to stimuli.

### 4.5 Tools of minimal intervention

The stone is not going to obey your levers or chisels in any predictable way.

Bring:

- **No heavy tools** except a very small knife (if regulations allow).
- Maybe one simple, thin-edged blade of inert material. You don’t want to introduce metal that confuses the “metal veins that broke free of the ore.”

Do not bring:

- Complex gadgets or electronics.
- Things that require precise mechanical laws.
- This place will humiliate them: you may get a “choir” of corrupted devices, or worse, they may become themes in the wall’s future.

### 4.6 Marking and navigation (without graffiti)

You cannot trust a line drawn on the wall to persist.

Instead:

- Use small, temporary personal markers you can retrieve:
  - Tiny colored fibers of a known non-local polymer.
  - Non-permanent ink or dye on your own skin.
  - Not on the wall itself; if you must, use faint chalk marks that can be brushed away by the room’s “first breath.”

Maintain:

- A short, physical **notebook** with pencil and paper pages, not electronic devices.
- Pencil survives moisture better than ink; if pages get damp, you can still read.

But you must not expect to map “true” paths; you can only map your own decisions.

### 4.7 Water and electrolytes

You cannot fully rely on the “weeping” water as drinkable in the same sense you are used to.

- Bring a sealed, simple container with water from your own multiverse.
- This is your anchor. Drinking your own water helps remind you where you came from physically.
- Replenish sparingly; you cannot tell if “local” water will be you or you will be it.

Electrolyte salts in small packets:

- To help your body manage sweat and heat shock.
- Taken before and after intense heat exposure.

### 4.8 Temperature and environment monitoring

You cannot “trust” appearances of temperature.

Bring:

- A simple mechanical thermometer if you can find one.
- Humidity indicator strips (color-change based) rather than complex electronics.
- You may not need “precise” numbers; but you need to know when you are approaching your body’s limit.

Monitor yourself:

- Heart rate, dizziness, nausea—more reliable than instruments in this place.

### 4.9 Time-keeping in the broth-stir of time

Wristwatches and digital clocks are likely to be corrupted.

If you can, bring:

- A simple **hourglass or sand timer** from your own world.
- Even better, a small mechanical device whose function is visible to the eye.
- Use it to set “exit intervals,” not to measure “true” time.

Your “day” will be artificial:

- A visit of, say, three or four “hours of your own timer” is enough to start to change you.
- You should leave before the timer’s sand runs out completely.

## 5. Handling interactions with the world itself

### 5.1 Voice

“When you speak, the sound does not travel like echo; it collapses into the stone like heat into metal.”

This is crucial:

- You must not shout, unless in extreme emergency.
- Use whisper, not speech.
- Practice your communications before arrival:
  - Hand signals.
  - Slow gestures.
  - If companions are with you, agree on signals for “stop,” “help,” “retreat.”

Treat your voice like a weapon you can barely control:

- Excess words produce new seams in the stone, “blisters of rock,” or “questions that forget to be spoken.”
- Use your mantra only if absolutely necessary.

### 5.2 Gaze and attention

“If you look too long, the lines become faces that have not yet decided if they are going to be faces.”

- You must not stare at features for too long.
- You will not be able to stop yourself at first; that is fine.
- The key is not to avoid seeing but to avoid building *expectations* around what you see.

Technique:

- Blink deliberately.
- Shift your focus from feature to feature without lingering on one.
- View the wall primarily with your peripheral vision; in central focus, it is more likely to *change* according to your mind’s pressure.

### 5.3 Floor and elevation

“The floor is not a floor, but a mosaic of unborn tiles.”

- You will not get “flat ground” anywhere.
- Step lightly; test each step before committing weight.
- Avoid pressing directly over cracks; “you can feel something almost… almost” beneath the shimmering air.

“The distance between features is tiny, but the distances themselves are treacherous.”

- You may not think to climb “six meters” to a shelf, but the “distance” becomes the effort.
- Do not assume you can climb back easily.
- If you climb to a shelf or “something that might be a window or mouth,” you must assume you might not be able to return down the same path.

### 5.4 “Climbing anyway”

The narrator’s line is not a guide, but a symptom:

“I climb anyway. We always do.”

You can *choose* to respect this compulsion or not, but know:

- In this place, climbing changes the world around you.
- If you climb, you must:
  - Visualize always being able to return down.
  - Keep track of where your hands are; the “wall” is not consistently stone or body.
  - Be prepared that the wall you go up on may not be the same wall you come down on.

### 5.5 “He” on the shelf

There is a body on the shelf:

- “half-pot half-mountain.”
- “algae like second skin.”
- “stone of the shelf rising like wax around his legs.”

You must not assume you can rescue him or that he is really dead.

Visor protocol (if you are using any):

- Do not touch him unless you are fully resolved to risk being absorbed into the same pattern.
- Test the shelf with tools before you approach: deposit something of material from your own universe first, like a tiny piece of fabric.
- If the shelf behaves “gently,” like wax, you may be able to approach; but remember that wax is melted by heat.

## 6. Understanding and dealing with the “ecology”

You are not the only life here.

### 6.1 Air-algae and insects of mist

“The algae settles on your skin like second dew.”

You must not treat this as purely harmless or purely dangerous.

Plan:

- Avoid letting large areas of exposed skin sit under the “weeping” for too long.
- Do not rub your eyes or mouth with bare fingers after touching algae.
- If you must touch, use gloves and then remove the gloves carefully, not allowing them to touch your face.

The “insect of mist” are barely more than suggestions.

- They likely cannot hurt you on their own.
- But you cannot be sure what they are doing:
  - “they feast on algae, and the algae feast on steam, and the steam feast on whatever lies beneath.”

Maintain a distance in the sense of:

- Don’t “play” with them or try to catch them.
- They are the world’s rehearsal; you are late to the dress rehearsal but not ready to be on stage.

### 6.2 “Bioluminescence” and visibility

The algae glow where they touch stone.

This may help you see:

- You may never be in full darkness.
- Your light source should be used sparingly in order not to disturb the room’s “ecology of light.”

## 7. Time and duration: how long you can stay

“Time is not linear; you get pieces of yesterday, tomorrow, and never-at-all in the same spoonful.”

Practical consequence:

- You must not think “I will spend three days here.”
- Decide on **short intervals**:
  - Maybe one “session” of one or two hours by your external timer.
  - After each session, return if possible to a more stable region, or at least to the “threshold” where you entered.

If you stay longer:

- You may find yourself caught in a “time-stew” where your arrival and departure are less distinguishable.
- Your memory may start to behave like the broth of the room’s time.

Exit plan:

- Before you enter, decide:
  - A maximum number of “timer sessions” you will allow yourself.
  - A signal or self-rule: “When I feel this particular way (for example, when I start to believe the wall is talking to me personally), I leave immediately.”

## 8. Companions, if any

You likely do not want to go alone.

Choose companions carefully:

- People who can remain calm in heat and humidity.
- People who will not shout or “provoke” the wall.
- People who can accept your “I climb anyway” as theirs as well in worst case.

Group protocol:

- Never separate more than you can see with your own eyes.
- Do not allow “one of you goes to the shelf to investigate” while the other stays below.
- Things you intend to keep together can be kept by something as simple as a very thin tether line—but not anchored to the wall; the wall might decide to grow the anchor into itself.

Communication:

- One word, “stop,” if not whispered.
- You might instead agree on a single sharp sound (like two small stones tapped together), but remember that noise is still sound to the stone.

## 9. This world’s technology and artifacts

“The technology of this place is primitive and not primitive at the same time.”

You will see:

- Toolmarks on the wall not made by you.
- Half-pot, half-mountain forms on the shelf.
- Patterns of “not-yet-artifacts” that are clearly being trimmed by some pressure that is “not us.”

You should not:

- Pick “artifacts” as if you are in archaeology.
- Bring anything from this room into your own multiverse if you can help it.
  - It may import “pregnancy” of the world into your own.
  - Even if inert where you take it, the pattern of stone being born into being might be repeated.

If you *must* take something:

- Make it small, not uniquely shaped.
- Wrap it in materials from your own universe and store it separately.
- Do not keep it near your skin or near your sleeping area.

## 10. Health and safety after you return

“Reality breaks down, and I climb anyway.”

When you leave:

- There may be no obvious “cut” from the room to your stable world.
- You might still be in the fog of the “broth” time.

Prepare for:

- **Heat shock rebound:** your body may be subtly cooked from the inside; watch for signs of heat stroke even if you “felt” fine during the visit.
- **Psychological blurring:** You may find yourself “remembering” the wall’s “attention” as you turn your head to see.
- **Sleep disturbances:** dreams of climbing, halfway faces, weeping ceilings.

You should:

- Give yourself a “decompression period” in a safe, stable environment.
- Talk to someone you trust (if you can) without having them interpret everything as fantasy.
- Keep journal notes of how you feel, but accept that your memory of the room may be as incomplete as the stone’s “memory of being riverbed.”

### Final: A simple procedure for visiting this “room-world”

Before departure:

1. Memorize your name, origin, and purpose:
   > “I am [name], from [place]. I am here to visit and to leave. This is not my room.”

2. Prepare:
   - Thin, flexible shoes and gloves.
   - Mask with minimal protection.
   - Simple headlamp with low-light mode.
   - Small notebook, pencil, and short timer (from your own world).
   - Sealed water from your own universe.

3. Set clear constraints:
   - Decide your maximum time interval before you enter.
   - Agree on signals if you are not alone.

When you arrive:

4. Enter slowly:
   - Take shallow breaths at first; acclimatize.
   - Move slowly and with low profile.
   - Do not speak loudly unless absolutely necessary.
   - Do not stare at the wall for long.

5. Present:
   - Use small, clear steps.
   - Test the floor before fully weight-bearing.
   - Remain open to the “not-yet” without helping to decide it.

If you decide to climb:

6. Climb with maximum caution:
   - Observe the wall’s “memory of being soft” before committing.
   - Do not assume you can return by the same path.
   - Do not climb to the shelf unless you are fully ready to risk being written into the wall’s pattern.

7. Leave before you feel:
   - Like you *understand* the room.
   - Like you *want* to stay longer than you planned.
   - Like the wall’s “attention” is becoming personal to you.

When leaving:

8. Exit decisively:
   - Use the same path in time if not in space; you may not get the same geometry, but you can choose the same “direction of story.”
   - Release your impressions of the room as you go; do not cling to them.

9. Remember:
   - You may not be the same person who went.
   - That is not necessarily a failure.
   - The “room” is not done; you are only one of the thousands of things that will happen in it before it is decided.

---

This room is the world.

It is the world *not yet* done.

Your visit should be:

- Short.
- Careful.
- Respectful of the “not-yet.”

And then you should leave, before you have become one of the faces that the stone has not yet decided to stop being.
