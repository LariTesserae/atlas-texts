To walk this continent as an ordinary human traveler is to step into the body of a dead engine the size of a planet. You are not visiting a ruin so much as climbing into the ribbed plate of a world-sized furnace that has cooled down only “coiled-spring hot” instead of “white-hot.” Your preparation, therefore, should look more like a deep fieldwork survival kit than a tourist backpack. It’s less “what will I see?” and more “how do I not get broken by the environment itself—height, humidity, monotone darkness, and engineered stone?”

Below are notes on how to think about your visit and what to bring.

---

## 1. Mindset and Purpose

### Why you’re going
This isn’t a place for casual sightseeing. People—at least those who survive—go here for:

- **Exploration and mapping**  
  Trying to understand a continent-sized engine substrate, where even “geology” is laid out like machine ribs.

- **Resource extraction / prospecting**  
  The ubiquitous metallic threads and synthetic nodules in the rock are valuable to someone, somewhere. The heat gradients themselves are energy.

- **Scientific fieldwork**  
  Biologists, geologists, and engineers of multiverse-scale systems come here to study how life and materials work when sunlight is off the table.

- **Pilgrimage / weird tourism**  
  There are those who seek out worlds like this to confront a deep sense of *scale* and *absence*.

Decide which you are closer to before you pack, because it determines where you go, how long you stay, and how much you need to carry.

### Emotional posture
Expect:
- A sense of being **inside something** rather than on top of it.
- A subtle, constant background disorientation: no sky, no horizon, no “up” in the usual visual sense.
- Monotony: amber light, low contrast, soft shapes—everything rounded by fatigue.

Mental resilience matters at least as much as gear. You’re not going to be afraid so much as *worn thin*.

## 2. Time, Season, and Duration of Stay

### Timekeeping
- No suns, no stars, no moon.  
- You will need **external timekeeping** that:
  - Works even if your local electronics go partially blind.
  - Tracks your *body*’s circadian cycle independently of the environment.

**Recommendations**
- Bring:
  - A robust wristwatch with a clear local-time display.
  - A separate *sleep-cycle tracker* that synthesizes “light” cues (e.g., a wearable that emits appropriately timed light toward your eyes).
- **Explicitly sleep by schedule**, not “when tired.” The amber light and the absence of day-night cues are easy to underestimate.

### Season
Seasons are driven by thermal inertia:
- **“Warm phase”**:  
  More heat from the crust.  
  - Warmer air, more humidity.  
  - More thermal activity in fauna, especially **heat-feeders**.  
  - Mist and condensation events more frequent.
- **“Cool phase”**:  
  Heat stored in the crust is released more slowly; air feels cooler.
  - Less thermal activity, but some **moisture-feeders** might be more visible in certain basins.
  - Ground might be barely cooler to touch.

**Recommendation**
- For a first visit:
  - Aim for **early cool phase** if possible:
    - Air marginally cooler.
    - Ground still warm but not “maximal.”
    - Fauna slightly less active; easier to learn patterns.
- If you’re *doing* fieldwork:
  - Plan **two overlapping seasons**: one warm, one cool.
  - You’ll see different ecosystems and different thermal sag.

### Duration
- Very short visits (< 1–2 days):  
  You’re just grazing the surface, literally and figuratively.
