Visiting the Underwell is less like arriving at a destination and more like entering a very small, very humid clock that never needs winding.

For an ordinary traveler from the multiverse—somebody with bones, lungs, and a need for dry socks—this is not a dangerous place in the usual sense, but it will punish assumptions. You will not be mauled, starved, or frozen. You will be gently tested by humidity, time, and silence until you either adapt or decide to leave.

Below is a practical guide to preparing yourself.

---

## 1. Understand what you’re actually walking into

### Space and scale

- The chamber is only a few dozen paces across and maybe half again as high.
- You can see across it from one side to the other.
- You cannot stand upright everywhere. The floor and ceiling are curved, uneven shelves of rock; sometimes you’ll be crouching, sitting on ledges, or half-wedged into hollows.

### “Weather” and conditions

- No sky, no stars, no sun, no seasons.
- No rainstorms, but the air is thick and moist.
- The temperature is warm and very stable, but humidity gradients near the walls and corners will feel cooler and tighter.
- The air does not move strongly; most sensation you feel will be subtle pressure changes, not true wind.

### Time and speed

This is the most important thing to understand:

- Everything visible that happens inside happens slowly.
- If you stay for a few days or a week, you will not watch moss grow or stone-lines creep upward.
- People used to constant change (traffic, chatter, screens) can find this slow pace of existence subtly destabilizing. It can feel like time itself is leaking out of your world.

### Light and visibility

- All light comes from faintly glowing mosses.
- It is dim enough you can comfortably see patterns but too dark to read small print comfortably.
- The color is greenish-gray, with faint hints of jade when your eyes adjust.
- There are no deep shadows like under the sun; everything is softened and diffused.

### Soundscape

- One dominant sound: the drip of the basin—*pit* … silence … *pit* … silence.
- Soft minor sounds: a leaf scratching against stone, a tiny animal falling through moss, a distant sigh of air moving.
- Everything else is stillness.

### Relationship with the city above

You are underneath a city, but:

- No city noise comes through clearly.
- You might feel subtle vibrations through the stone; you’ll notice more pressure-related sensations than audible sounds.
- If you come from a noisy world, this kind of muffled near-silence will be unusual.

### Life in the chamber

- This is *not* empty rock: it’s a sparse, living community.
- Plants are pioneers, making their first home on stone.
- Animals are small, slow-moving, and often blind or translucent.
- Nothing here is evolved to hunt humans; but everything is fragile.

### Your fragility vs. the world’s

Your biggest danger:

- You are bigger, more fragile, and more temporary than anything else here.
- A careless slip, a brisk touch, or a misplaced boot can erase decades of growth that you didn’t even notice.

Understanding *that* changes how you should behave.

## 2. Physical and sensory gear

### Light and vision

Bringing artificial light is tricky, because it’s like turning on stage floodlights in a library.

**Functional basics:**

- A small, dimmable headlamp or lantern.
- A narrow-beam torch for inspecting details.
- Red filters or low-intensity modes to avoid overwhelming your eyes or the ecosystem.

**Use guidelines:**

- Use only as much light as you need for safety and movement.
- When you stop to simply be there, move away from any strong light source. Let your eyes adapt to the moss.
- If you must use bright light, try to keep it away from the glowing moss and the living-wells; they exist based on subtle humidity and light gradients.

**Consider options:**

- Chemlight sticks or small candles while walking, to avoid sudden blinding flashes.
- Night-vision or monoculars if you want to see details without flooding light everywhere.

### Footwear and clothing

You are going to be wet-footed and damp at least part of the time, but you can limit misery.

**Footwear:**

- Waterproof sandals or light shoes with good grip on wet stone.
- Non-slip soles are important: the rock surfaces will be uneven and sometimes moist.
- Avoid heavy boots that you can’t wash and dry thoroughly afterward.

**Clothing considerations:**

- Choose lightweight, quick-drying, synthetic or natural fabrics that pull moisture away from your skin.
- Consider layers you can peel off:
  - A thin base layer for next-to-skin comfort.
  - A loose outer layer that dries quickly.
- Colors:
  - Neutral, dark, or earth tones will help you visually blend into the environment.
- Avoid:
  - Cotton-heavy garments that will stay wet the whole time you’re there.
  - Bright fluorescent colors unless you like feeling like an alien tourist at a monastery.

### Tools for movement and safety

Because you’re dealing with an uneven, “not-quite-a-floor” surface:

- **Compact trekking poles or a sturdy staff:**  
  - Useful for balance on uneven rises and drops.
  - Gentle on stone surfaces if you cap the tips.
- **Simple chalk or tape markers:**  
  - For marking subtlely-safe paths or marking points of return.
- **Basic bandana and a small towel:**  
  - For wiping sweat and moisture from your face and neck.

## 3. Temperature and humidity: handling “sweat of the earth”

The air sits warm and humid:

- No sweat-chill unless you sit still where condensation is thick.
- If you stay near the basin or where moisture seeps along walls, you will feel a layer of damp on your skin.

### Prepare for:

- **Hydration:**  
  - Despite humidity, you will lose moisture through respiration and pores.  
  - Bring your own water bottle, even if you plan to drink only from the basin’s measured source.
- **Skin comfort:**  
  - Light powder or anti-chafing products if you’re prone to irritation under damp clothes.  
  - A small tube of moisturizer if your skin tends to dry out after being humid for long.

### If you feel cold:

- Near the narrower parts of the chamber, air may feel cooler and closer.
- A thin wind layer or long-sleeve shirt helps here.
- The main discomfort is not usually cold, but being damp for long stretches.

## 4. Time and your mind

### The pacing of the place

The Underwell is not “empty” or “dead,” it is just slow:

- The moss glows and faintly shifts.
- The stone-lines grow in imperceptible increments.
- Drops fall into the basin in intervals that can vary from minutes to seconds.

### Psychological effects

You might encounter the following:

- A sense that time is passing more slowly than you’re used to.
- Boredom or restlessness if you expect “spectacle” or “progress.”
- Guilt at being too loud, too fast, or too bright when everything else is so slow.

### What you can do about it

**Before you go:**

- Accept that you are visiting a *mood*, not a parade of events.
- If you have a habitually busy mind:
  - Consider short daily meditation or breathing exercises for days or weeks beforehand.
  - Reduce your consumption of rapid blinking media before you head in.

**During your stay:**

- Bring a notebook or sketchpad:
  - Not just to write about, but to *time your thoughts* by.
  - Writing down tiny details can help anchor your awareness of time passing.
- Practice small rituals:
  - Count drops of the basin; make small measurements of intervals between *pit* sounds.
  - Watch for tiny changes over the days of your stay (if you stay longer than a few hours).

**If you start to feel unmoored:**

- Do not inject noise or artificial activity into the space.
- Step quietly to a different part of the chamber where the “feel” of the air is slightly different.
- Listen to your own breath and to the drip of the basin; this often restores a sense of rhythm.

### How long to stay?

This depends heavily on you, but some guidelines:

- **Short visit:** a few hours to a day.  
  - Best for a first introduction to the chamber.
- **Medium visit:** two to four days.  
  - If you want to experience shifts of perception and to feel what “living quietly” means under stone.
- **Longer stay:** more than a week.  
  - Requires training yourself beforehand in slow living, meditation, or writing.
- **Not recommended:**  
  - If you strongly depend on external stimulation and social contact to feel stable.

### Essential time-related tool

- A simple, silent watch or timer:
  - To set gentle reminders for yourself (e.g., check in at intervals).
- Do not keep alarms constantly on; if you must use them, set very low-volume sounds.
- Find out what time “does” to you when you don’t have the usual cues of day and night.

## 5. Food and water

### The basin’s water

The water in the basin:

- Comes slowly and from an unknown source above.
- May be subtly different from what you’re used to.
- Has been shaped by stone, minerals, and organic matter from above and within the chamber.

Whether you drink from it directly depends on your body and tolerance, but if you do:

- Use **common sense:**  
  - If you’re from a world with very clean water supplies, consider filtering or boiling the water before using as drinking water.
- Respect that:
  - This basin is one of the key anchor points of life in the chamber.
- Do not use:
  - Soap, chemicals, or anything that might damage the water’s balance for the tiny invisible swimmers below and surface skippers on the water.

### Bringing your own food

You cannot rely on foraging in this place:

- Nothing edible is large or human-nutritious.
- Tiny animals and fragile plants are not suitable as food for you.

So pack light:

- Nonperishable foods that:
  - Don’t require much cooking or heating.
  - Are stored in tightly sealed containers so no crumbs escape into the environment.
- Trail mix, compact bars, or nutrient-dense foods that don’t require hot preparation.
- Water containers of your own; you may not want to rely entirely on the basin’s drip.

### Waste handling

This needs to be carefully planned:

- **Absolutely do not leave waste or food scraps in the chamber.**  
- Bring a small, tightly-sealed bag for:
  - Organic food waste.
  - Any other packaging.
- Plan your exit so that:
  - You take out more than you brought in.
  - Nothing is left except your footprints and maybe a few subtle marks that you’ve decided to leave.

If you need to relieve yourself:

- Not in the chamber itself; ideally exit before or after your time there.
- If you are unable to leave:
  - Use a container method and remove it entirely, leaving no trace of biological matter that could reshape the ecosystem’s nutrients.

## 6. Movement and protection: taking care of the Underwell

You are walking into a very delicate environment:

### Respect the scale and age of things

- Remember that:
  - The plants you see may be older than you are.
  - The stone basin is being slowly deepened, each drop over centuries, not hours.
  - The living-wells condensing water droplets are animals, not decorations.

### How to move safely

- Move slowly and deliberately; this is not a place to rush.
- Watch your foot placement:
  - Avoid obvious moss mats and their edges.
  - Avoid stepping directly over obvious seams of moisture.
- Use your hands lightly:
  - If you need to touch the wall for support, use open palms rather than fingertips.
- Avoid leaning:
  - On the thin-stemmed ferns and stone-lines; they do not support your weight.

### Treat animals as if you’re in a museum of fragile glass

- **Do not disturb:**  
  - The slow-moving arthropods.
  - The living-water droplets on the leaf clusters.
  - Anything under the water surface you cannot see clearly.
- No poking or collecting; this is a living space, not a display case.

### If you must interact

- The only acceptable interaction is:
  - Gentle observation from a distance.
- If you must touch something:
  - Use the back of your fingers or knuckles rather than fingertips.
  - Press lightly; if you feel resistance, stop immediately.

### Protection of the stone

- Avoid marking the stone:
  - No carvings, graffiti, or scratches into rock surfaces.
- No anchors:
  - Do not attach anything to the stone-lines or other plants.
- No bolting:
  - Do not drill or affix anything into the walls or floor.

### Preventing contamination from outside

- If you’re traveling from worlds with different environments, consider:
  - Brushing off shoes before entering.
  - Ensuring nothing on your clothing or gear carries seeds, spores, or hitchhiking bugs from outside worlds.
- The Underwell has its own slow history of adaptation; you don’t want to accidentally introduce “invasive time.”

## 7. Sanity and solitude: how to handle being deep under stone

### The psychological environment

You are under stone and under city:

- Not completely sealed from them, but insulated from them.
- Your mind may be more aware than you expect of:
  - The weight of rock above.
  - The occasional muted vibration from the city above.

### To manage potential unease or claustrophobia

- **Preparation before visiting:**  
  - If you know you tend to feel trapped in enclosed spaces, try smaller, well-lit caves or basement rooms first.
  - Practice being alone with no sound except for one or two simple noises (like your own breathing or a clock).

- **While you’re there:**  
  - Focus on the space between you and the walls rather than “the thickness above.”
  - Treat the ceiling’s stone-lines and moss glow as a kind of living sky; you can look up at it instead of thinking constantly “rock above.”
  - Spend time near the basin where the sound of the drip gives a sense of open space rather than confinement.

### The silence of the chamber

- The silence is vast enough that you may feel:
  - Your own heartbeat more clearly.
  - The sound of your own breathing.
- This can be uncomfortable at first, especially if you’re used to constant background noise.

**To befriend the silence:**

- Try deliberately matching:
  - Your breath to the drip of the basin.
- Close your eyes intermittently for:
  - Short periods of simple listening.
- If you can’t sleep at first:
  - Use the drip and your own steady breath to create a pattern rather than fighting against the quietness.

### Timekeeping without sun

- This is key:
  - The chamber doesn’t have day and night in the usual sense.
- To keep track of time:
  - Use a simple, discreet watch; not to constantly check time, but to avoid completely losing orientation.
- Consider marking “artificial days”:
  - Light routines you control yourself:
    - Dim light for “sleep.”
    - Full but gentle light for “reading or writing.”

### Social isolation and you

If you go with companions:

- Set clear shared rules:
  - Talk softly if you must.
  - Design times for “no speaking at all.”
- If you are alone:
  - Understand that you will not see anyone else for days if you stay long.

### What to bring if isolation feels overwhelming

- A notebook for:
  - Writing letters to yourself or others.
- A small symbolic object:
  - From your world, to remind you of where you come from and that you can return.

### When you should leave

- If you feel:
  - Persistent, increasing anxiety or dread.
  - Strong urges to disturb or damage the chamber out of frustration or boredom.
  - Physical symptoms like constant headaches, disorientation, or unusual emotional swings that don’t match your usual pattern.
- Then:
  - It’s time to exit carefully and quietly.

## 8. When you arrive: your first hours in the Underwell

### Step-by-step first arrival

- **Enter the threshold:**  
  - Move slowly and deliberately.
  - Pause to allow eyes to adjust from ordinary light to the dim green glow.
- **Find a safe posture:**  
  - Sit or kneel somewhere stable with a clear view of the basin or the walls.
- **Listen and breathe:**  
  - For the first half-hour, simply absorb:
    - the drip of the basin,
    - the faint breath of the air,
    - the subtle motions of glowing moss.
- **Scan gently with your eyes:**  
  - Notice the moss’s velvet patterns.
  - The stone-lines reaching upward.
  - The faint trails of water along seams in the rock.

### Avoid the “tourist reflex”

- Don’t rush to:
  - “See everything.”
  - “Cover everything.”
- Treat it like a slow, quiet conversation rather than a tour.

### Creating your basecamp

- Choose a spot:
  - Not directly on the largest moss patches.
  - Not directly blocking any moisture trails or the basin’s lip.
- Lay down:
  - A thin pad or mat (if allowed in your own rules for impact).
- Organize your belongings:
  - In one compact, easily movable pile.
- Keep paths:
  - Minimal and repeated, so that you don’t spread wear across multiple surfaces.

### Your first rituals

- Mark time in a small way:
  - Count a number of drops; then pause.
  - Create a simple pattern or “day” of your own making using this sound.
- Introduce yourself mentally:
  - To the space as if it were a host.
- Decide silently:
  - What you will do to take care of this place while you’re here.

## 9. Ending your visit and leaving

### How to leave respectfully

- Before departure:
  - Check for your own traces: footprints’s marks are hard to erase everywhere, but you can remove:
    - Any litter.
    - Any brought objects you intended to remove.
- Make sure:
  - You haven’t left anything behind that doesn’t belong.

### Last rituals

- Take a few moments:
  - To sit quietly again by the basin or near the moss you’ve become most aware of.
- Listen:
  - To one last sequence of drops and air shifts.
- Decide:
  - What you want to carry with you in memory, rather than in objects.

### When you return to your own world

- Expect:
  - The first moments of noise and light to be jarring after the quiet under-stone.
- Acknowledge:
  - That what you’ve seen is not something you can easily explain to others who were not there.
- The chamber:
  - Will go on without you, unchanged by your leaving in any visible way, but still quietly shaped by everything it has touched.

---

## 10. Final mindset: what you’re preparing for

You are not merely preparing to travel to a location; you are preparing to encounter an *aspect of time itself*, made visible in stone and moss and dripwater.

You want to arrive ready to:

- Notice slow movement rather than expect rapid events.
- Listen carefully rather than make noise.
- Protect what you’ve been allowed to see.
- Allow yourself to be changed by being quiet under a world of stone and city.

The Underwell:

- Does not need to be impressed.
- Does not need to be impressed upon.

You just need to be present enough to learn something from what happens when you sit quietly inside the faintest green light of a world breathing beneath a world.
