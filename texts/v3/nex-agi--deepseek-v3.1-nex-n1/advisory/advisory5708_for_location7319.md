Before you even think about stepping into this place, you should understand what it is not.

It is not a wilderness.  
It is not a ruin.  
It is not a garden in the ordinary sense.

It is a completed environment.

Everything you see is designed, sealed, and set to run within a narrow band of parameters: humidity, temperature, light, pattern. Change is not forbidden, but it is clearly not intended. You are the only variable.

---

## 1. Mindset: You are entering a sealed artifact

### The single biggest danger is cognitive drift

This place will not assault you with storms, predators, or radiation. It will assault you with *absence*:

- No wind.
- No sound except your own body and voice.
- No visible time of day.
- No visible decay or erosion.
- No clear reference scale.

Over time, that can unbalance your sense of self more than obvious danger.

Before going, you should:

- Clarify your purpose for being there.
- Set clear anchors: “I am here to observe and leave,” not “I will wander until I feel done.”
- Decide on a maximum time limit you will not exceed without explicit external consultation.

You are entering a kind of psychophysical greenhouse. You need to manage your own head with the same care you would give to your physical body in a vacuum.

---

## 2. Pre‑visit: Training and conditioning

### 2.1 Low sensory variation training

If you have access, spend time in environments with minimal variation:

- A large, empty, indoor space with constant lighting and no external noise.
- A float tank or sensory deprivation setup (if you can tolerate it).
- Long walks in uniform fog, where sound and visual distance are muted.

This helps you recognize the early signs of your own mind trying to “fill in the gaps” with hallucinations, patterns, or intrusive narratives.

### 2.2 Pattern recognition and self‑checking

You’ll be surrounded by perfectly repeated patterns. Your mind will start finding meaning where there is only geometry. You should:

- Practice basic math or logic puzzles daily for a week before the trip.
- Build a habit of checking your own reasoning:
  - “Am I seeing this because of a pattern, or because I want to explain it?”
  - “If I remove all emotion from this impression, what is left?”

Keep a simple checklist of cognitive distortions to look out for:

- Overattributing intent to patterns
- Interpreting coincidence as message
- Feeling that the environment is responding personally to you

## 3. Psychological equipment

### 3.1 Written grounding protocol

You should not enter this place without a clear, written list:

- Who you are (name, date of departure, home reference).
- Where you came from and a clear description of your nearest safe anchor world.
- Brief statement of purpose for visiting.
- Conditions under which you will abort immediately (e.g., waking hallucinations, time feeling lost, strong sense of becoming “fired into the world”).

Laminate this. Keep it accessible. You *will not* reliably remember this under the long, quiet pressure of the place.

### 3.2 Time anchors

Since there is no day/night and no visible celestial motion:

- Bring a very reliable, preferably battery‑powered watch.
- Consider two separate timers:
  - One for duration: “Leave this world after X hours.”
  - One for self-check intervals: eg every 30 minutes you pause, look at your written grounding note, and speak aloud where you are and what you’re doing.

### 3.3 Recording tools

Bring:

- A notebook and pen (pencil) that are unlikely to leak or break down.
- A simple audio recorder for your voice.
- Perhaps a simple camera (if allowed—be cautious of misusing images).

Use these to externalize your perceptions. The environment’s “meaningless coherence” will be easier to handle if it’s captured outside your head.

## 4. Clothing and protection

### 4.1 Temperature and humidity

- The temperature is described as warm, not hot—like baked ceramic that is slowly releasing heat.
- Humidity is high, but not dripping; it feels more like a greenhouse mist than a tropical storm.

You should wear:

- Light, breathable layers:
  - Long‑sleeve base shirt and thin trousers to protect skin from any ceramic or glaze that might be irritant.
  - A thin overshirt or jacket that can be tied around the waist if you feel warm.
- Nonabsorbent fabrics:
  - Avoid heavy cotton that will soak up humidity and feel wet.
  - Choose synthetics or blends that dry quickly.
- Slightly over‑sized shoes with good grip:
  - The glaze surface may be smooth in some areas and cracked in others.
  - You want traction to avoid slipping into the water basins.

### 4.2 Skin and moisture management

Because humidity is high but droplets rare, you are not likely to “get soaked,” but your skin might feel sticky, especially around eyes, lips, and hands.

Prepare:

- Antifog wipes or a small cloth for your face if you tend to sweat around your eyes.
- A light lip balm.
- If you wear contacts, consider wearing glasses for this journey instead; the humidity and uniform brightness will strain your eyes less that way.

## 5. Environmental hazards: What you actually face

### 5.1 Psychological slipping

The biggest risk is not ill intent but design. The environment is *patterned*:

- Reptiles, frogs, fish, moths all in fixed positions.
- Mounds of vegetation identical and spaced on regular lines.
- Cracks in the glaze with perfectly uniform spacing.
- Water basins with perfectly spaced reeds and suspended fish.
- Light embedded in the glaze itself.

Your mind will tend to:

- Try to “decode” the patterns as language.
- Feel that being there is significant, chosen, or foreordained.
- Implicate itself in the design (“you are the only maker mark this world will ever have”).

You should decide in advance:

- “I will not treat any perceived pattern as a message addressed to me.”
- “If my thoughts start to break into fragments or tics, I will leave.”

### 5.2 Physical stability of the surfaces

- The ground appears firm and stable: a thin ceramic glaze over underlying fired material.
- Water basins are shallow, with sharply defined rims and still surfaces.

There is no overt sign of fragility or collapse. However:

- Avoid jumping on bridges or arches; you gain nothing by testing them.
- Step carefully around the edges of basins where glaze and water meet.
- Do not kick the lizards or frogs or anything else fused to the surface; you do not know what might happen structurally or psychologically.

### 5.3 The water

- It is described as like warm bathwater.
- It appears clean but not “natural” in origin.
- It does not seem to be actively hostile or corrosive, but it is described as “intentional.”

Practices:

- Do not drink from any basin or film unless you have no other choice.
- Even if prospects appear desperate, assume you will not be drinking local water.
- If extremely necessary, you can wet your lips or external skin surfaces (non-mucous membranes) but avoid ingestion unless you’re facing true dehydration risk and you’re ready to accept that risk.

## 6. Survival kit: What you need to survive with no resources from the world

### 6.1 Water and food

Prepare as if this world provides nothing usable:

- Water:
  - Bring all you need for the full planned duration plus a margin:
    - At least 2–3 liters of water per day.
- Food:
  - Nonperishable, simple rations:
    - Energy bars or nuts.
    - Compact, sealed items that won’t leak or smell.
  - Nothing you will need to cook or prepare.
  - Enough calories for your planned stay plus at least 24 hours more.

### 6.2 Lighting

Even though the sky is described as always brightly lit, there may be structures or regions you can’t see where you may want light. You are not guaranteed that everywhere feels safe.

- A headlamp with fresh batteries.
- A small backup flashlight.
- Optional: light sticks to mark your way back if you are allowed to leave markers and they don’t feel “disrespectful” to the environment.

## 7. Sanity and self: How you should behave there

### 7.1 Moving through the world

When you walk:

- Stay aware of your own path:
  - Pick clear landmarks, such as specific arrangements of mounds or bridges.
  - Mark your route with small, nonpermanent mental notes:
    - “Third mound of vegetation with the circle pattern lizards around it.”
- Avoid repetitive looping patterns of your own making unless you deliberately decide to:
  - The environment already loops in design; endless looping in your stride might reinforce your sense of timelessness.

### 7.2 Touching things

Limit touch to what you need to know:

- You may touch:
  - The surface to verify its temperature, texture, and stability.
  - Plants at the edges of mounds to assess their flexibility.
  - Water edges for testing temperature and reflection behavior.
- You should not:
  - Break, remove, or “take” any objects (including stones, if you find any).
  - Disturb the water films or fish suspended in the basins.
  - Attempt to pry or break the lizards or frogs free from the glaze.

### 7.3 Using tools found there

Place the strange tool you described in the category of:

**Objects not meant for you.**

Unless you explicitly understand how to use it safely and ethically:

- Observe the tool:
  - Its position, angle, relation to the basin.
  - Take note as if you are recording data, not interacting.
- Treat it like a museum artifact:
  - Study without altering.
  - If it must be moved, do not do it lightly.

If you have a specific reason given you before you travel to use that tool, you should be given clear instructions from that source before you go.

## 8. Leaving the world: Exit strategy and debrief

### 8.1 When to leave

- When you reach the agreed time limit.
- If you feel:
  - Your thoughts becoming fragmentary, “glitchy,” or drawn to pattern tics.
  - You begin losing track of why you are there.
  - You start to feel that being “fired into the glaze” is acceptable or appealing.
- If you show signs that the environment is starting to feel more “real” than your home or self.

### 8.2 How you should leave

- Do not simply “walk out gradually.”
- Use an explicit transition device or method, if you have one:
  - Doorway, anchor object, or token.
- Follow a clear exit protocol:
  - Return your attention to your grounding note.
  - State out loud:
    - “I am [name]. I am leaving the ceramic world now.”
  - Do not allow the idea of “I will leave just one more loop” to linger; it will be very easy for the environment’s static equilibrium to make that loop recursive indefinitely.

### 8.3 After you return

- You should not attempt to return immediately.
- Take time to:
  - Rehydrate properly.
  - Sleep in a place with normal time cues (sunlight, clocks, sounds).
  - Talk with someone else if you can:
    - Describe the place, not as “the meaning of everything,” but as a constructed environment you observed.
- If you are troubled by:
  - Lingering patterns.
  - Seeing glaze-like patterns in your home world.
  - Fragments of language breaking your sentences.
  then seek professional psychological support rather than attempting to “return and solve it.”

## 9. What you will not be prepared for, and you must accept that

No matter how you prepare, there will be things you cannot anticipate:

- Exactly how “thick” the absence of sound and time will feel.
- How your mind will respond to seeing perfectly identical objects at perfectly identical distances.
- The feeling that you are the only maker mark this world will ever have, that you are the only thing that has touched it and not been fired into it.

This fact doesn’t make preparation useless; it makes it more important.

You will not be able to fully prepare the first part of your *self* that will encounter this place, but you can prepare the part of you that will decide:

- When to stop.
- When to retreat.
- When to reassert that you are not just another element in the glaze.

  - Watch and timer.
- Recording:

  - Light, breathable layers with non-slip footwear.
- Self-rescue:
  - Your clear, pre-decided exit statement and method.

A fired world  
where you are the only thing that has not yet been fired into it  
and you intend to keep that distance intact  
so you can leave when you must.
