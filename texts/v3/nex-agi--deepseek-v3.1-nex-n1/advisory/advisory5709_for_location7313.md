Visiting Vent‑Stone is not impossible for an ordinary traveler, but it is punishing in the same way living on an ocean rig or in a deep mine is: survivable only with preparation, discipline, and respect for the environment’s rules.

Below is how you should prepare: gear, habits, mindset, precautions.

---

## 1. Understand what you’re walking into

### The world in a phrase

You are entering a **self‑eating metal world**, clamped vertically onto a giant pillar-construct whose heat and moisture are recycled through layers of decks and shafts like blood in a body.

Key qualities:

- **No ground**: only decks on top of decks.  
- **No plants**: only “metal mosses,” films, and strange stains.  
- **No sun**: only a uniform gray-white glare from a low sky.  
- **High, but not open air**: high elevation from the world’s core, but you feel enclosed by clouds and structure above and below.
- **Hot but not hellfire**: heat seeps up from below all the time; warm floors, warm railings, warm walls.
- **Damp but not flooded**: water drips, fogs, condenses, and is engineered; never quite enough to feel safe, but enough to rust and rot.
- **Very mechanical, but quiet**: the city is a giant machine that tries to be quiet.

This is not a place to improvise your survival strategy en route.

## 2. Physical and health prep

### Heat and humidity

**Temperature:** high–2.7/3.  
**Humidity/water:** 1.2/3—damp but not soaked.

**What you need:**

- **Lightweight, long‑sleeve, moisture‑wicking base layers**  
  Avoid cotton; anything that stays damp will become a skin problem.  
  Synthetic or quick‑dry fabrics are essential.
- **Breathable outer layer**  
  Something thin that protects from metal edges, rough welds, rust without trapping heat.  
  Think stiff workwear rather than heavy winter gear.
- **Heat acclimation beforehand**  
  If you have time, train in heat (saunas, hot workouts). Vent‑Stone’s heat is constant and low-level but exhausting over long days.
- **Hydration strategy**
  - Assume you need to drink **at least every 20–30 minutes**, even without feeling thirst.
  - Electrolytes are essential; salt, especially. You’re sweating constantly even without noticing—hot air, contact with warm surfaces.
  - Bring **electrolyte tablets or powder** and ration them carefully. You can’t rely on local food.

### Lungs and air

The air is “wrong to breathe,” not clearly poisonous but clearly not natural.

You should bring:

- **Basic particulate filter mask or half‑mask respirator**  
  Not a full scuba-style rig, but protection against fine dust, rust particles, metal spores, and whatever “metal moss” sheds into the air.
- **Backup filters / spare cartridges**  
  You’ll be there longer than you plan.
- **Optional: lightweight filter mask for “good” areas**  
  Something easier to wear for long periods than a full respirator; but have at least one heavy-duty respirator for when you descend into confined spaces.

If you have asthma or any respiratory sensitivity, assume Vent‑Stone is **medium‑risk** and only consider going with serious support (medical preparation, possibly oxygen contingency).

### Skin and body contact

- **Everything is warm to the touch**: railings, floors, walls.
- Many **rusted surfaces** and “metal moss” films.

**You should pack:**

- **Work gloves**  
  Durable enough to grip rough surfaces, but light enough to not overheat your hands.  
  You’ll be constantly touching handholds, ladders, railings.
- **Long trousers and sleeves**  
  Protect against:
  - Abrasions from scuffing on rough welding.
  - “Hot” contact from surfaces that are hotter than expected.
  - Unknowns from the weird films and organisms.
- **Basic cuts/abrasions kit**  
  - Antiseptic wipes or spray.
  - Bandages and gauze.
  - Impetigo‑class antibiotic ointment if you can get it (skin infections from humidity and metal dust).
- **Foot powder**  
  Sweaty boots in a damp environment are rot and fungus factories.

### Noise and vibration

- **Low noise level**: 0.9/3.  
  But there is a constant low mechanical vibration and metal groan.

**Pack:**

- **Basic earplugs** for sleep and for times when you rest near machinery shafts.  
  Not deafening, but the constant low hum leads to fatigue.
- **Optional: lightweight earmuffs with noise reduction**  
  For if the “beneath” sounds rise suddenly.

## 3. Gear and clothing

### Core gear list

- **Backpack**: modest-sized, durable, preferably waterproof or at least water‑resistant.
- **Lightweight waterproof over-jacket or shell**  
  For rain showers and dripping condensation.  
  Doesn’t need to be warm; it needs to shed water.
- **Work gloves** as above.
- **Closed-toe boots with excellent grip**  
  - Metal decks often **slick with condensation or light rain**.  
  - Need grip, but also support for climbing ladders and uneven surfaces.
- **Headlamp + spare batteries**  
  The “night” isn’t dark in the gloom sense, but corridors are shadowy and “dark” sections of the city are very dim.
- **Basic respirator and/or filter masks** as above.
- **Notebook + waterproof pen or pencil**  
  For mapping, timing cycles of ventilation or rain, noting “safe” zones.
- **Multitool** (with knife, screwdriver, pliers, maybe file).
- **Duct tape or gaffer tape**

### Specialized extras

- **Thermal camera or at least a simple IR thermometer**  
  Enables you to:
  - Know where floors are truly hot.
  - Identify vents before they open.
  - Avoid “hot” decks that might be dangerous to your soles or equipment.
- **Small humidity + temperature sensor**  
  Vent‑Stone’s “sweat” varies; know where it is dangerously wet or hot.
- **Optional: lightweight climbing harness + basic carabiners**  
  If you plan to go near deep edges or internal shafts.

### Electronics

- **Assume low power availability**: bring robust battery packs and solar charger if you can.  
  The city is wired like a body—but you don’t know how to plug into its veins safely.
- **Everything should be water-resistant** or in waterproof cases.
- **Expect no electromagnetic compatibility**:  
  Your devices might glitch or show false readings near certain shafts or supports—where gravity feels “off” or flux fields are strong.

## 4. Water and food

### Water sourcing

The water rating is 1.2/3—**enough water to keep from dying of thirst**, but not enough to be casually generous.

**Sources:**

- **Condensation rivulets, engineered troughs, and basins.**  
- **Localized rain showers within shaft vents.**  
- **Fog dripping from overhead beams.**

**Rules of thumb:**

- **Never drink from any visible rust-colored water or anything with iridescent films.**
- **Collect only water that**
  - is clearly moving;
  - is not sitting in stagnant pools;
  - is not under “metal moss” or suspicious stains.
- **Always treat or filter water**
  - Filtration: e.g., portable filter with ceramic element.
  - Chemical treatment: tablets or drops.
  - If possible, **boil** (using a heat source from the environment—many surfaces are hot enough to heat water).

**Storage containers**  
Bring lightweight, durable water containers or collapsible bladders.  
Vent‑Stone is full of moisture, but not necessarily “easy drinking” water.

### Food and nutrition

- There is almost **no vegetation**, and any fauna is rare and likely strange or mechanical.
- Assume **no local agriculture**, and any local foodstuff will be preserved, engineered, or scavenged from unknown sources.

**Bring:**

- **High-density, shelf-stable rations**: nuts, seeds, energy bars, dried proteins.
- **Compact cooking set**: small pot, heat source if you don’t want to rely on heating surfaces (which might be unsafe).
- **Electrolyte supplements**: you’ll be losing salt through sweat everywhere.
- Multivitamins.

**Attitude to local food**:  
If you encounter any form of “food” the locals consider normal (tastes metallic, smells like engine grease, looks like pressed sheets), be extremely cautious. Assume you **should not** eat local unless absolutely necessary and not until you have tested small quantities with long waits between doses.

## 5. Navigation and movement

### Knowing where you are

- **No horizon**
- The city is **stacked decks**: you’re never “on ground”, but “on a layer”.
- No “north” or sun to track; time is ambiguous.
- Directions are “coreward,” “edgeward,” “up-deck,” and “down-deck.”

**You should:**

- **Always know where your edge is**:  
  Never lose track of the platform railing or drop into blindness without knowing how to return to “edge” or “major deck.”
- **Use markers**:  
  - Paint chalk marks on walls at heights others won’t easily erase.
  - Tie tags or fabric strips on railings.
- **Sketch small maps**:  
  - “Up” vs. “down” ladders.  
  - “Core” vs. “edge” tunnels.
- **Time “cycles”**:  
  The city moves slowly (0.6/3 dynamic), but there are cycles:
  - **Faint temperature changes**  
  - **Occasional opening/closing of vent panels**  
  - **Pattern of rain showers**

Learning to sense “urban breathing” will help you know *time*: when a vent panel last opened, how long since a rain shower, whether the metal is warming or cooling.

### Gravity and deck “tilt”

- Gravity feels uneven. In some areas near deck edges, your weight seems lighter.
- In others, closer to core supports, you might feel heavier or “pulled” into the floor.

**Practical precautions:**

- **Always use three points of contact on ladders.**
- **Assume small handrails and edges might be slightly weaker or “off” in their response to your weight.**  
  Don’t lean casually out over empty space.
- When you feel “lighter,” treat it like being in a low-gravity environment: your body may not intuitively sense how far you can fall or how hard you will hit.

### Openness vs. claustrophobia

- Scale is open–2.2/3 in the sense of wide decks and interlocking structures.  
- But you are **always in a structure**, never outdoors under blue sky.

If you dislike confined spaces, this will be psychologically hard. Prep:

- Mindfulness or grounding exercises.
- Regular checkpoints where you can return to “your edge” platform, where you feel at least one open direction of view.

## 6. Survival and risk mitigation

### The edge / fall risk

- **Elevation is 2.9/3—extreme.**  
  You are not just above a ground plane; you are somewhere above an unknown depth of cloud-covered decks below.

Treat **every edge** like a cliff:

- Always stay **at least one full body length away** unless intentionally viewing.
- **Never sit on railings**.
- When leaning near edges, keep **your center of gravity low**.
- Never test railings or “temporary” guardrails by putting weight into them.

If you walk to the edge:

- Plant yourself, **stable feet**, wide stance.
- Keep one hand on anchored metal structure.
- Don’t “pose” near the edge; gusts or shifts of the deck might happen.

### Metal, rust, and sharp edges

Everywhere you are:

- **Rusted seams**
- **Welded joints, rough and sharp**
- **Exposed bolts**

Behave as if you’re in a factory yard:

- Watch where you place your hands.
- Assume “finished” surfaces are actually **work surfaces**, not decorative smooth skin.
- Don’t “brush” along walls casually; your clothes and skin can be torn or burned.

### Heat compartments

You know the heat comes from below:

- **Closer to core = hotter.**
- More **engine access, shafts, and vents.**

**Safe zones:**

- Edges of the city, away from major shafts, where heat radiates but less intensely.
- Areas where **water condensation channels** run visibly; these often indicate heat exchange being actively managed.

**Avoid:**

- Confined corridors near **coreward shafts** where the temperature shoots clearly higher.
- Metal “bubbles” or **small enclosures with no visible ventilation**: they could be heat pockets or steam traps.

### Water “slip” hazard

- Whatever decks look dry, remember **constant condensation**.
- After rain showers or near vents, metal **will be slicker than you expect**.

Walk deliberately:

- Short steps, flat-footed on ladders, not heels on smooth metal.
- **Always brace one hand or arm against something.**

## 7. Fauna and the “metal life”

### What you might encounter

- **“Thin‑bodied, insect‑like things with chitin and metal‑plated limbs”**
- Possibly integrated into the city’s “automation”

**Assume:**

- They are **not pets**.
- They are **not guaranteed to ignore you**.

**Basic rules:**

- **Do not provoke**:
  - Don’t throw things at them.
  - Do not block their vents or access shafts.
- **Avoid touching them**
- **Avoid being close when they are disturbed**
- **Do not eat them or put them near your mouth**

If you find “metal moss” or films, **don’t scrape or chew** samples; use gloves and tools, and seal them carefully.

### The “urban organism”

You might interact with low‑level inhabitants of the city itself:

- **Human or not?** unclear.
- They likely live inside the **service shafts** and internal corridors.

You should:

- **Assume the city is inhabited** until proven otherwise.
- **Never assume “nobody lives here” because it looks quiet.**
- Leave **no obvious trash** or damage:
  - You might not be the only one to pass.
  - The “urban organism” might resent intruders who don’t show respect for the machine.

### Noise and respect

The city tries to be quiet. **You should too:**

- No shouting unless you absolutely need to attract someone.
- Avoid slamming doors or plates.
- If you must leave signs or “calling cards,” do it quietly and not destructively.

## 8. Mental and emotional preparation

Vent‑Stone’s reality edges on **high weirdness** (2.4/3), but its rules are consistent once you learn them.

### What you’ll feel psychologically

- **Weightlessness without space**: low gravity feelings at edges, but still vertically constrained.
- **Time without sun**: no “morning” or “afternoon” lights to anchor your circadian rhythm.
- **“Young-old” city**: surfaces are aging while the structure feels “still being built.”

You will:

- Feel tired **more than you should**, from heat, humidity, and the lack of natural cues.
- Experience **time dilation**: cycles of machinery and “breathing” panels operate at timescales of hours, not minutes.
- Over days, you may start to **hear the machinery as a rhythm**: your heart syncing to its faint hum.

### Coping routines

- **Strict sleep schedule**  
  As best as you can, enforce roughly “night” and “day” for yourself.
- **Mark the days**
- **Keep a journal**  
  - What you saw.
  - Any changes in temperature, “respiration” of the panels.
  - What you felt emotionally.
- **Limit delirium**:  
  The combination of heat, strange light, and constant noise can cause “machine delirium” over time.  
  - Regular breaks.  
  - Visibility of the sky (even gray) from time to time.  
  - Something “human” around you: photos, small comforts from home.

If you find yourself thinking of the city as “mother,” or “machine love,” or feel compelled to “touch its heart,” you should consider **pulling back to a safer deck or edge platform** for a while. This is not a place to go “too deep” without strong support.

### Dealing with isolation

- You might go **many hours or days without seeing another person or anything clearly alive**.
- Fauna rating 1.4/3—rare.

You’ll need:

- Internal discipline: routines, rituals, small goals.
- Outlets: reading, writing, music through headphones occasionally.
- A “plan” for each day—even something simple: “map two decks,” “follow water channels,” “document rust patterns.”

## 9. Day-to‑day behavior in the city

### Moving around

- **Always carry at least one liter of water.**
- Always **carry your headlamp, even in “daylight”.**  
  The “night” dims uniformly but some corridors are fully dark.
- Never **walk barefoot**: floors are hot, sometimes slick, sometimes jagged.

### Clothing and changing

- At least one **dry set of clothes** kept in waterproof bag.
- Change **socks frequently**.
- Air dry clothes on **warm surfaces** but not scalding ones.

### Eating and drinking

- Eat **small, frequent meals** rather than big ones.
- Keep water close **at night** as well—the heat can still dehydrate you.
- Avoid “drinking” from random surfaces like “licking condensation” from beams—might be coated with metal films.

### Where to sleep

- Choose **less coreward**, less “hot” spaces.
- Prefer **wider decks with some view of the gray “sky”** rather than deep inside tunnels.
- Stay away from **major vents**, where they might breathe unpleasantly at night.
- Use **thermal ground mat or insulation layer between you and the floor**—floors radiate heat.

### Personal hygiene

- Wash hands **after** touching any rust or “metal moss.”

If you develop any unusual rashes or metal “taste” in your mouth, **reduce exposure time** and rest in more neutral edge environments.

## 10. Leaving Vent‑Stone

When your time is over or you need to return:

- Take **only samples** that you can securely and safely seal:
  - Rust fragments.
  - Possibly flakes of “metal moss.”
  - Never put them into your mouth.
- **Dispose of waste properly**:
  - Don’t just dump your trash into vents or “empty” shafts.
  - Pack out what you can; if nothing else, leave it in a **neat, sealed, unobtrusive** spot near a “used” compartment.

### Post‑visit health

Expect **lingering effects**:

- Sleep disturbances:
  - You’re used to the low hum and now it’s gone.
- Temperature perception:
  - Normal air might feel “cold” and “empty” for a while.
- Dreams of decks:
  - You may see infinite platforms in your sleep.

Do **gradual re‑entry**:

- Re‑hydrate properly when you’re out.
- Maintain a regular sleep schedule.
- If you feel “off” (fever, unusual fatigue, breathing issues), consider medical evaluation and **bring samples of anything you inhaled or touched**.

---

### Final summary for an ordinary traveler

If you’re going to Vent‑Stone:

- **Prepare for heat and humidity** like you’re preparing for a hot factory and deep cave system at the same time.
- **Assume the city is alive**: its machine is its body; its condensation is its blood.
- Treat **every edge as a cliff** and every railing as suspect.
- Drink **treat water only**, from trusted sources.
- Map **up/down**, core/edge, **never** north/south.
- Keep **quiet**: the city whispers—you should whisper too.
- Bring **markers**, light, insulation, and filter masks.
- Bring **patience and discipline**: the change here is slow, but the consequences of carelessness can be immediate.

It’s not a place that “hates” you; but like standing on the outer skin of a breathing machine, you must be careful where you step, when you lean, and how long you stay.
