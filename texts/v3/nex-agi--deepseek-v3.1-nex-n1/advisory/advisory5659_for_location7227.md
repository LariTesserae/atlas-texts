Assume you are an ordinary human traveler, without engineered biology or built-in tech. You get tired, cold, sick, and bored like anyone else. You also have access to roughly modern-level gear and transport support (from your multiverse travel agency, backers, or your own kit), but you yourself are fragile.

Going to the Basin Engine in the Deep Cut is not a hyper-lethal instant death sentence, but it is not a safe tourist destination. It’s a wet, semi-living machine that is constantly in motion. The danger is slow corrosion, subtle pressure shifts, unpredictable humidity, and the fact that nothing ever stays put.

You will want to prepare in three layers:  
1. Body: clothing and protection.  
2. Toolbox: gear and supplies.  
3. Mindset: rules and expectations.

---

## 1. Clothing and Protection

### Skin barrier first

Everything in the Cut is wet and slightly corrosive. The air is thick with moisture and rust; metals “rust” into weird foams and semi-liquids. The water itself has thin slicks of oil and metallic residues. You want as little of that as possible directly on your skin.

Pack:

- **Full-body waterproof layer**  
  - A shell suit (like industrial-grade rain gear but better seam-sealed, or a dry suit used by divers).  
  - Not breathable fabric is fine here—the environment is already humid. You don’t “vent sweat” much when you’re effectively in a low cloud anyway.
- **Durable under-layer**  
  - Tightly woven synthetic fabric that doesn’t stay damp as long as cotton.  
  - Thermal layers if you chill easily; the Cut is “cool hush,” but you can lose heat to continuous moisture.
- **Gloves and boots**  
  - **Gloves**: thick, waterproof, chemical-resistant gloves that still allow some finger movement. You need to touch metal surfaces, valves, and strange objects without getting direct contact with rust or residues.
  - **Boots**: knee-high waterproof boots with excellent grip. The floor is a thin sheet of water over metal plates, often slick with residue or a thin layer of rust-foam. Consider light crampons or heavily textured soles if you can get them.
- **Face and eye protection**  
  - **Splash goggles or tight-sealed mask**: Not hardcore gas masks needed necessarily, but something that keeps moisture droplets out of your eyes and prevents rust dust from getting into your contacts or under your lids.
  - Optionally a **half-face mask** with particulate filters: rust flakes and metallic residues in the air are not what you want to be inhaling deeply.

**Don’t wear**: anything you care deeply about preserving. The combination of moisture, rust, and oils will stain and degrade most fabrics and metals over time.

## 2. Gear and Supplies

Think of your pack contents as “support systems for a body that doesn’t belong here.” The basics are still: warmth, water, food, light, navigation, first aid, and communication.

### Water and filter

- **Bring sealed water from outside**  
  The Basin Engine has water everywhere, but it is a metallic, oily film of condensation with strange residues: “ground moisture” that is continuously recycled by the machine. You don’t want to drink directly from it without extreme care.
- **Small filter rig or distillation device**  
  A filter alone isn’t enough; you want something that can handle:
  - Suspended rust particles.
  - Possible oils or metallic slicks.
  - Unfamiliar dissolved salts.
  
  If you can’t purify reliably, rely strictly on stored water from home.
- **Collapsible container**  
  To collect and treat water if you need to extend your stay.

### Food

- **Sealed, long-term, stable calories**
  - Energy bars, meal bars, dehydrated meals in waterproof packaging.
  - Avoid anything that goes mushy or rancid easily; moisture will get into any packaging flaws over time.
- **Minimal waste packaging**  
  Whatever trash you generate, you carry out. The Cut’s “environment” doesn’t need candy wrappers or plastic packets to become part of its rusty ecosystem.

### Lighting

- **Multiple light sources**  
  The light is described as “uniform pearly gray” with “no clear sun.” No proper shadows, but that doesn’t mean you can see details. You need your own light:
  - **Headlamp** with both broad and focused beam modes.
  - **Handheld light** as backup.
  - **Chem lights or low-powered markers** to leave a trace you can see later.
- **Waterproofing**  
  Use waterproof cases or pouches for all lights and batteries. The air is condensation-heavy; things will feel wet even if you aren’t “in rain.”

### Footing and movement tools

- **Trekking pole(s)** or a sturdy staff  
  - Helps test the depth and slipperiness of the film-covered basins.  
  - Some basins are “finger-width deep”; others are almost shallow bowls; all are slick metal underneath.
- **Kneepads**  
  You may need to kneel or sit to get under low pipes or engine structures; you don’t want to rest directly on wet metal plating.

### Navigation and mapping

- **Map**  
  You might not have an existing map of the Cut Basin Engine. If you can, sketch from:
  - Long-distance observation or local intel before you arrive.
  - Mark major engines, “sentry box,” scaffolding platforms, and pipe-clusters you can see from threshold areas.
- **Compass + environment sensors**  
  - The **standard compass may be unreliable or erratic** because of metallic structures and possible electromagnetic hums; but it’s still worth taking.
  - **Altimeter and barometer** (if you can get them) are extremely useful:
    - The Cut is essentially “a pressure machine” rather than a weather machine. Barometric shifts can help you anticipate **pressure storms**—before valves hiss open and air “flicks past like a film played in a hurry.”
    - Watch the pressure: **falling = potentially storm, rising = possible settling but then a new turbulent burst**.
- **Marking your path**
  - Use **temporary markers**:
    - Tape that can stick even on wet metal but comes back cleanly.
    - Water-soluble paint or chalk (if your ethics support leaving temporary marks).
  - **Don’t expect permanent tracks**:
    - Your footprints fill back with condensation from the air.
    - The ground itself is slowly reorganizing. Rust moves; “erosion is established but not static.”
    - You might find “your way back” not by footprints but by recognizing constellations of engine structures, “plants,” and basins.

### Data collection and measurement tools

- **Notebooks and pens**  
  - Cheap paper will suffer in the humidity; use waterproof paper or store notebooks inside sealed plastic bags.
  - Keep track of:
    - Time you entered and paths.
    - Notable changes in “plant” structures.
    - Rust-thing behavior patterns.
    - Pressure storms.
    - “Mouths” of the Basin Engine—these whirls of air and moisture that are more than just eddies.
- **Basic tools**  
  - Multitool or knife.
  - Small tongs or pliers to handle objects you don’t want to touch directly (rusty things, “sensor” gauges, unusual trinkets).
- **Sensor device**  
  - If you have access to one:
    - A device that can measure humidity, temperature, and perhaps barometric pressure and electrical fields.
    - The Basin Engine’s “tech” operates on pressure, temperature, humidity, and faint electricity; being able to see what changes can help you predict and avoid.

---

### First Aid and Medical

- **Standard hiking kit**, but:
  - Add extra **bandages and antiseptic** for minor cuts and scrapes.  
  - In a wet, rust-rich environment, small wounds can easily get infected or irritated by metallic residues.
- **Allergy meds**  
  You might react to airborne spores, rust particulates, or oils from the “plants.”
- **Eye rinse solution**  
  For when rust flakes or droplets splash your goggles or mask and some get through.
- **Medication for motion sickness or vertigo**  
  The “never-still” air, layers of mist, and faint pressure changes can cause nausea or dizziness in sensitive people.

---

### Communication and Safety Backup

- **Two-way radio or similar device**  
  - If you have support outside the Basin Engine (team base, ship, etc.), set clear:
    - Check-in intervals.
    - “If you don’t check in by X time, we do this.”
    - **Have a rescue plan** before you go.
- **Lifeline / guide line**  
  - If it is possible to anchor **outside** the Basin Engine and carry a line with you:
    - Spool of thin, strong cord (like high-quality climbing line) inside a waterproof bag.
    - Anchor outside the Cut threshold and then pay out line as you move into the Basin.  
    - This gives you a literal lifeline to return along—especially if you become disoriented by the misty “no clear sky” environment.

---

### Containment and “Take Nothing, Change Nothing”

- **Ziplock or dry bags**  
  - For:
    - Samples (if you are collecting any).
    - Trash.
    - Your own devices that must not be exposed continuously.
- **Respect for “autopoetic machine”**
  - Treat the Basin Engine as a **self-regulating system**:
    - Do not jam valves just to “see what happens.”
    - Do not smash rust structures or “plants” for “samples” unless it is absolutely necessary.
    - The “rust” is not “decay”; it is a form the world itself has settled into.
    - The “storms” are not just danger; they are how the system keeps breathing and **growing new structures**.

## 3. Mindset, Rules, and Behavior Expectations

### The core rules to memorize before you step in:

1. **Nothing is ever still**
   - The air, the water, the “rust,” even “time” in pulses of pressure—none of it is stationary.
   - You will not find “calm” in the sense you are used to.
   - Your sense of equilibrium depends on knowing that and going with it.

2. **Air and water are not separate fluids**
   - The air is wet; the water is nearly airy.
   - Don’t expect to “get dry.” It’s not a place to dry out clothes, hair, or gear.
   - Plan your exposure time. Prolonged stays will risk waterlogging even the best gear.

3. **Rust is a form, not a decay**
   - The rust-foam on pipes and plating is “old rust,” a “settled erosion pattern.”
   - It supports structure as much as corrodes it.
   - The **structure** may be held together by rust and pressure as much as by solid bolts and plates.

4. **Plants are not plants**
   - They “photosynthesize humidity” instead of sunlight.
   - Their leaves are membranes; some trunks are cables-in-resin.
   - They are not designed by you, and they don’t care about you.
   - Don’t lean on them, break them, or “fix” them.

5. **Rust-things are shy**
   - Don’t chase them.
   - Don’t try to capture them.
   - Don’t assume they are simple “robots”; treat them as rare animals adapted to a weird machine-ecology.
   - If you harm them, you are not just “breaking a toy”; you are removing one of the few clearly moving creatures in this system.

6. **Pressure storms are normal**
   - You will experience:
     - Sudden hiss and valves opening.
     - A sharp “pressure storm” that feels like a brief film acceleration.
     - A “drizzle” of metallic water droplets afterward.
   - If possible:
     - Move **out of the thickest mist layers** before the pressure falls; you might avoid being directly in the “mouth” of the Basin Engine.
     - **Don’t run** while the air is flicking past; you could slip easily or fall into a basin, or into a sharp edge of metal plating.

7. **“Mouths” are not mouths**
   - They are whirlpools of air and moisture, “more than eddy, less than creature.”
   - They:
     - Thicken the humidity.
     - Concentrate mist droplets.
     - Sometimes **lock in** structures of vapor and electricity.
   - When you see one:
     - Don’t panic.
     - Don’t touch its center (if you can see it).
     - Use your barometer and your senses; **anticipate** before they fully form.

8. **“Growing” storms are special**
   - Sometimes enough turbulence triggers the **spawning of a new structure**:
     - A thread of moisture.
     - Then rust building up into a new slender pipe or branch of the engine.
   - It is not safe to stand directly where the air pressure is focusing; you might be **nudged** by invisible air currents or lightly struck by the forming structure.

### Survival Protocol: Inside the Basin Engine

Before entering, you and your support should agree on:

- **Time limit**:  
  - 4–6 hours maximum for a first, “short reconnaissance” entry.
  - The longer you stay, the more your gear will saturate, and the more the subtle effects of humidity, rust residues, and pressure/noise will wear you down.
- **Check-in intervals**:  
  - Every hour or at every major threshold built structure you enter.
  - Leave messages **outside** the Basin Engine:
    - On paper.
    - On a device.
    - Verbally with someone: “Here is what I saw last; this is where I am going next.”

Once you are inside:

- **Stay at low angles**
  - The floor has low, basin-like depressions and water film; you are always moving slightly up or down into another basin.
  - Move carefully:
    - Test each step with your pole or staff.
    - Avoid edges where you can’t see how the metal plating under the water is shaped.
    - If water is “finger deep” in a basin, you can usually walk through it; deeper than that and you might be sliding on wet metal under the water. Slow down.

- **Watch your “sky”**
  - There is no real sky, but the **layers of mist** above:
    - If you see a layer of mist **lowering** and thickening near you, that can be a sign of a pressure “event” or a forming “mouth.”
    - Move to a clearer, “less busy” airspace if you can.
