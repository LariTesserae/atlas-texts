To visit this cavern is to accept an invitation into a very specific kind of body: the body of the earth at rest.

You will not “conquer” this place. You will not be its hero or its discoverer. You will be more like a guest entering a sleeping house at night, asked to take your shoes off at the door and move quietly so the dream is not disturbed.

### 1. Before you go: understanding *what* this place is

This is not a dramatic cave of roaring water and echoing chambers. It is the opposite:

-   **Small scale:**  
    You can cross it in thirty slow steps. The ceiling is low. The space is *intimate*. You will not be overwhelmed by size, but by closeness.

-   **Warm, humid, and still:**  
    Think “bathroom after someone has taken a long, hot shower but never opened the door.” No wind, no drafts. The air feels like it has been holding its breath for a long time.

-   **Almost no life:**  
    No plants, no insects, no animals in any usual sense. The “life” is more like metabolism: warmth holding steady, mineral films forming, water quietly maintaining equilibrium.

-   **Near-complete silence:**  
    No dripping, no wind, no machinery. The sound of your body—breathing, swallowing, clothes shifting—will be audible.

-   **Softly sacred:**  
    The low stone structures at the water’s edge and the back of the cave are clearly human, but primitive and respectful. This place has been treated as a shrine or sanctuary by someone, even if no nameable god is claimed.

So you are preparing to enter a *small, warm, humid, silent, almost-lifeless stone chamber with a quiet pool at its center*.

---

### 2. Mindset: why you are going there

You might go:

-   to rest in a place that feels like the earth is holding its breath  
-   to listen more deeply than you usually do  
-   to meditate, or simply to be alone  
-   to see if your own body, after a time, begins to slow down to match the pace of the cavern

Some people might go seeking visions or “messages.” This is possible, but not in the way you may expect. There are no angels carved into the walls. The messages will be subtle, like shifts in your own breathing, or the faint realization of how long you can sit without thinking.

For this trip, assume:

-   **You are not here to change or claim the place.**  
    You are here to be allowed into *its* condition.

-   **You are not here to find dramatic meaning.**  
    You are here to experience *continuity*: warmth, stillness, darkness, and water in a long, unchanging moment.

Adjust your expectations. If you can accept that “nothing might happen,” and that “nothing” might be deeply significant, then you are ready to go.

---

### 3. Physical and logistical preparation

#### a. Clothing and footwear

Choose clothing that:

-   **Breathes but doesn’t get heavy when wet.**  
    Cotton is fine, but cotton will stay damp if it gets soaked. A light synthetic blend that dries quickly is better if you might touch water.

-   **Is modest and simple.**  
    This place feels like a natural chapel. Bright colors or slogans on t‑shirts might feel out of place. Soft earth tones: gray, brown, mossy green, black.

-   **Covers you lightly but fully.**  
    Long sleeves and trousers are better:  
    - They protect you from scrapes and from the slight chill that can settle when you sweat and then stop moving.  
    - They protect the cave from your sweat and skin oils.

-   **Does not rustle.**  
    Avoid loud synthetic fabrics or rain jackets that *shshsh* when you move. In silence, that’s jarring.

For footwear, choose:

-   **Closed shoes or sandals with good grip**  
    - The floor may be slick near the water line.  
    - Light shoes you can remove easily so you can sit barefoot if you wish.

Socks to change into after the visit are helpful—your feet will appreciate being dry outside.

---

#### b. Light and visibility

The description is very clear: there is no torches, no lamps built in. The light is *dim, from above*:

> The faint light from above is enough to see the outlines of things and not much more.

Bring **your own light source**, but choose it carefully:

-   A small **LED headlamp** or **flashlight** with:
    -   **very low modes** or a **diffuser** so you aren’t shining a spotlight into the cavern’s face.
    -   warm or warm-white LED; cool blue-white light feels harsh here.

-   A **candle lantern** or **single tea candle and holder**:
    -   Provides gentle, flickering light.
    -   More respectful to the atmosphere.
    -   Better if you plan to stay longer.

When you arrive, **start with minimal light**. Let your eyes adjust to the faintness from the opening above. Use only enough light to avoid stumbling and to see where you are stepping. Think “nightlight,” not “searchlight.”

---

#### c. Water and snacks

Even though the cavern is full of water, **do not plan to drink from it**.  

Bring:

-   A **small bottle of water**  
-   A light snack like **nuts, fruit, or an energy bar** if you plan to stay longer than an hour or so.

The air humidity might make you less aware of thirst, but your body is still using water. Sip quietly.

If you do eat, *eat before or after you enter*, or sit right at the entrance, not at the water edge, so that crumbs or wrappers do not become part of the chamber.

---

#### d. Hygiene and respect for the water

This is a **still** pool, *warm* and *unmoving*:

-   Do **not** swim in it.  
    Your oils and skin cells would disturb the water in a way that might take weeks or months to equalize.

-   Do **not** spit, urinate, or leave any trace in the water.  
    This is not a river; it won’t carry your waste away.

-   Do **not** wash your hands or face in the pool.  
    If you need to wash, use a small amount of water from your bottle and a towel.

-   Avoid wearing perfume, strong deodorant, or insect repellent.  
    These residues will linger in the air and on the water surface.

Think of the water as the **still center of the room**, not a resource to be used.

---

### 4. Medic and health considerations

The air is warm, humid, and still. This is:

-   Wonderful for lungs and skin in many ways  
-   Potentially uncomfortable if you are sensitive to heat or humidity

Especially if you have:

-   heat intolerance  
-   asthma  
-   claustrophobia  
-   circulatory issues  
-   joint problems (from sitting long)

…you should prepare:

-   **Go slow.**  
    When you arrive, let your body acclimate near the entrance or on the threshold rather than rushing straight to the center.

-   Bring **inhaler or medication if you need it**, stored in a waterproof bag.

-   If you feel faint or dizzy from the heat and humidity, **move closer to the entrance** where cooler air may meet the warm, and sit or lie down. **Do not force** a longer stay than your body can tolerate.

The place is not “dangerous” in itself; but *stillness + humidity + heat* can catch you by surprise if you push yourself.

---

### 5. Timing and duration of the visit

This cavern feels outside of time in one sense, but your body still works on a schedule.

Good choices:

-   **Morning or early evening:**  
    When the outside world is quiet and the light from above is least likely to crash harshly into the cave.

-   **Stay as long as feels natural.**  
    - For some people: 20 minutes.  
    - For others: several hours.  
    - Stay as long as you remain **present**, but not so long that you feel trapped or restless.

Plan **no schedule**, but *do* tell someone else on the outside when you go in and roughly how long you expect. That’s just safety, not drama.

---

### 6. How to enter and move around

When you arrive at the cavern:

1.  **Pause at the entrance.**  
    - Listen.  
    - Let your eyes adjust to the dimness.  
    - Let your body temperature and breathing begin to settle.

2.  **Step in quietly.**  
    - Move slowly, as if entering a library or place of worship.  
    - Your shoes will likely make sound; accept that, but don’t rush.

3.  **Use the “edge.”**  
    There is only a thin strip of stone floor around the pool:
    - Walk carefully, especially over the damp stone near the water.  
    - Keep low if you are near the lower-curved ceiling; one hand trailing lightly on the wall can help steady you.

4.  **Respect the water horizon.**  
    - The edge of the pool is marked by that faint mineral film “sheen.”  
    - Do not step or kneel directly in it.  
    - Walk around rather than shortcutting across the water.

5.  **Notice the alcoves.**  
    Areas where the ceiling dips lower are like *mini-rooms*. They might be:
    - Where you sit to be more hidden  
    - Where you leave your small belongings if you want the center to remain uncluttered

---

### 7. Ways to be with the water

You are allowed to **touch** the water, but only *gently*:

-   Sit near the edge, on the stone, or on the low stone crescent that looks like a bench.

-   **Hand only:**  
    - Dip your fingertips or palm into the water quietly.  
    - Do not splash.  
    - Feel the weirdness of its warmth: *like skin that has been lying in the sun.*

-   **Notice what happens:**
    - Sound: tiny, almost swallowed by the silence.
    - Ringlets: they spread out slower than you expect, because the water is thick and calm.
    - Reflection: your hand will break the pale disk of light from the opening above.

-   Do **not** drink, swim, or bathe.

If you feel drawn to stillness, **simply watch the surface**:
-   The disc of light from above.
-   The faint mineral sheen at the edges.
-   The way the water barely moves.

---

### 8. How to sit, breathe, and be quiet

The air is already doing very little. This is your cue:

1.  **Choose a place to be.**  
    - By the water edge, on the low stone crescent if it feels sturdy enough.  
    - Or the stone platform further back.  
    - Or simply sitting on the bare floor, where you can feel the warmth of the rock under you.

2.  **Settle your body:**  
    - Sit cross-legged or with knees drawn up.  
    - If you prefer, lie on your back on the platform or stone—where you won’t roll into the water.

3.  **Reduce noise:**  
    - Turn off your phone or leave it outside.  
    - Move metal buckles, keys, or jangling items to a quiet pocket or bag.

4.  **Listen inward** as the silence grows:
    - Heartbeat  
    - Breath  
    - The soft sound of clothes against stone  
    - Your swallowing, your shifting weight  
    - The faint sound of light water movement when you touch it

This is where the cavern *does* something to you: your system starts to synchronize with its stillness.

---

### 9. Ritual, if you want it (and how to not ruin it)

The stone crescent and platform have clearly been used by someone as *something* like a sanctuary or sleeping place.

If you want to mark your presence respectfully:

-   **Bring only very small objects**, if any:
    - A single smooth stone carried from a place you love  
    - A small piece of cloth or cord tied in a simple knot  
    - A handwritten word on a piece of paper you later take away  
    - A flower or leaf you *do not leave*; bring it out with you

-   Place anything **away** from the water edge:
    - On the platform  
    - In the dry alcoves  
    - For **temporary** presence, then remove it when you leave  

-   **Do not leave offerings that will rot:**  
    - No food  
    - No liquids besides plain water if you must  
    - No candles unless you plan to burn them completely or take them away

The best offering you can give is **to leave nothing** behind except perhaps your footprints on damp rock.

---

### 10. Leaving and re‑emerging into the world

When you feel it is time to go:

1.  **Rise slowly.**  
    Your body will have relaxed deeply; don’t shock it by leaping up.

2.  **Check your place:**
    - Have you left anything physical?  
    - Is any wrapper, cloth, or stone out of place?  
    - If you moved anything, *return it* how you found it.

3.  **Take one last look at the water.**  
    Notice if the disc of light from the opening is still there, unchanged, or if your eyes have adapted to see it differently.

4.  **Walk out quietly.**  
    - No dramatic goodbye; the cavern doesn’t need your ceremony.  
    - Just leave with the same care you came in with.

5.  **Notice transition when you emerge:**
    - The air will likely feel cooler, drier.  
    - Your body might feel lighter or strangely heavier.  
    - Your senses may be sharper or muffled for a moment.

This is when many travelers *feel* what they just experienced—not in the cave itself, but in the contrast between its stillness and the moving world outside.

---

### 11. Inner preparation: what you are actually carrying in

This is the most important form of “preparation”:

-   You are carrying in **the expectation to be quiet**, not to be entertained.
-   You are carrying in **the willingness to be slowed** to the pace of stone and water.
-   You are carrying **a respect for a place that has chosen not to change** unless you change it—and therefore, *you choose not to change it*.

You might journal before you go in, or after you come out—not during. The middle of the visit is not for writing, but for being in the place that is already itself.

Think of this cave as **a body already perfectly balanced**, holding its own breath in a long, steady exhale that hasn’t ended yet.

Your job is not to fix it or save it or decode it.

Your job is to visit, be quietly present for a while, and then leave without altering it—except in the soft, invisible way that being in its presence might alter *you*.
