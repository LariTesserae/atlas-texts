To visit the world under the vertical storm, you should not think of it as a trip—you should think of it as making a controlled, temporary insertion into a machine that was not meant for you.

Below is guidance framed as an orientation manual for an ordinary traveler: what you’re walking into, what to bring, and how to prepare yourself mentally, physically, and technically.

---

## 1. What you actually are stepping into

### 1.1 The geometry

Imagine you’re standing on the floor of a room where the ceiling is an ocean, and above that ocean is another ocean.

- **Above you:**  
  - 0–30 m: air  
  - 30–150 m (approx): the boundary fog layer  
  - Above that: clear, very high-pressure water, compressible to many kilometers depth  
  - Suspended in that water: a stack of disk-shaped cities ascending toward a distant “surface” where the storm happens.

- **Below you:**  
  - Black glassy basalt plateau stretching to the horizon, occasionally broken by sharp ridges and a few glass towers.

You are *not* standing on the bottom of that sea. You’re standing on the *underside of the sky*, or the roof of the world. Gravity in this layer is roughly “normal” downward (into the rock), but the water column is *above* gravity, towering upward like a skyscraper of ocean. Nothing is designed around you:

- The cities are sealed for aquatic pressure-beings.
- The engines calibrate equilibrium for *them*, not you.
- The temperature, the sound, the light—everything is optimized for a system that is not alive in your way.

You’re visiting the inside of a permanent machine whose inhabitants are somewhere else entirely.

**Suitability:** This is **not** a safe destination.  
- **If you have a choice:** choose another world.  
- **If you must go:** treat it as militarily supervised expedition.

---

### 1.2 Time and change

- **There is no change.**  
  - Storm stays at constant intensity.
  - Sound of the engines: constant deep tone, no volume or pitch change.
  - No regenerative cycle: day–night is vague shifts of light through clouds.
  - No temperature gradient.

That’s more than unnerving—it’s dangerous to your mind:

- **Perception of time** is easily disrupted by environments without change.
- Hours can feel like minutes or days like benumbed eternity.
- Without sensory anchorpoints, you can drift into disorientation or dissociative states.

Treat this like long-duration sensor deprivation or shiftwork in a bunker. You need **external clocks**, **structured routines**, and **scheduled exits**.

---

### 1.3 The soundscape

A constant deep, resonant tone from the engines:

- It is **always** there.
- People do speak in low voices near that tone—whisper-levels, but still understandable.
- This means:
  - Long-term exposure risks **severe tinnitus and/or hearing damage** without protection.
  - **Sleep fracturing**, even if it doesn’t feel loud: the brain never gets “quiet”.

You need:

- **Custom-filtered hearing protection**, not just foam plugs.
- Some form of noise masking at night (if you can sleep).
- Practice **talking at low volumes without straining**.

---

### 1.4 The pressure and medium: air and upper water

- **Below you:** rock and random glass towers.
- **Around you:** basalt plateau and air.
- **Directly above you:** first air, then fog, then water ocean.

Important **physiological** realities:

- If you ascend *into* the water (see the section on climbing), you’re **not going to be able to breathe water**. Anyone who tells you “it’ll be fine” is lying.  
- You will need full **underwater life support**, and:
  - You must assume **continuous pressure increase** as you climb higher through the city stacks.
  - **Returning downward** requires **controlled decompression** like a deep dive.

**Traveling “vertically” in this world is like deep-sea diving.**  
Do not treat this like branching tunnels in a cave.

---

### 1.5 The inhabitants of the water

The creatures in the vertical ocean:

- **Density-adjusters** (cylindrical with rotating limbs):  
  - Move by changing internal density, not by swimming.
- **Segmented ribbons** with metal cores:  
  - Swim as precise, mechanical-looking bands.
- They feed on **dust and metal particles suspended in water.**
- They **do not appear to eat each other**—no predator-prey behavior you can recognize.

What you need to know:

- For **them**, the water is life support; for you, it’s a hostile high-pressure environment.
- You may be **invisible to them** sensorially:
  - They probably detect fields, pressure differentials, engine signatures.
- **Do not assume:**  
  - They must ignore you.
  - They cannot be startled by your fields, thrusters, or other equipment.

---

### 1.6 The air beneath the ocean: dryness and metal dust

- The air is **cold**, often **dry**, and carries:
  - **Metal dust filaments** hanging from engine-to-city cables.
  - Possibly metal-fines in the air itself.
- **No dust storms**, no wind strong enough to knock you over, just dust lines suspended like threads.

**Hazard profile:**

- **Fine metal dust**: inhalation risk over hours.
- You need at least N95/P2 filters or better, preferably **PAPR or powered air-purifying respirator**.
- Assume metal dust accumulates **everywhere it can settle**—in your hair, on gear, in your pack.

---

### 1.7 The psychology of a place that wasn’t built for you

Features:

- No welcoming doors, ladders, or signs.
- A scale that makes you feel insignificant:
  - Ocean above swallowing continents.
  - Cities like vertical vertebrae of bones.
  - Engine root-systems bigger than buildings.
- Everything conveys **indifference**, not hostility or hospitality.

Psychological effects:

- **Existential vertigo:** “What is the top of this ocean holding?”
- **Being an aberration:** You might be the only soft, air-breathing thing that ever stood on that basalt.
- **Time brittleness:** Without cycles, you can feel cut off from your own *internal* clock.

**You must prepare:**

- A clear **mission**, ideally **time-limited**.
- **Anchor routines:** wake times, sleep times, micro-goals.
- **Mandatory breaks** from “environment exposure”: return to a known, similar baseline world for deconditioning.

### 2. Physical preparation: fitness and resistance

### 2.1 General conditioning

You need:

- **Strong core:**  
  - A lot of your motion will be on glassy flat stone with subtle balance challenges.
- **Good leg and joint support:**  
  - Days walking on featureless, hard basalt “glass floor” can wear down joints.
- **Mental stamina for endurance:**  
  - This is not a sprint; it’s a long awareness watch in a subtly hostile environment.

**Minimum baseline:**

- Can you do:
  - 6 km walking over flat hard terrain with full pack without intense joint pain?
  - Squat and stand easily to inspect equipment, pick items up?
- Can you wear:
  - **Semi-rigid or rigid helmet** and/or **pressure mask** for several hours?

### 2.2 Temperature adaptation

You won’t be comfortable:

- **Air:** cold enough to require **insulating layers**, but not freezing cold.
- **Water boundary:** colder than you expect; it feels *glacier-damp*, not merely icy.

Prepare:

- Layered clothing:
  - Base layer: moisture wicking.
  - Mid layer: thermal insulation.
  - Outer shell: wind and water-resistant.
- **Always carry dry backup:** if you get wet from the boundary layer, you need fast change before hypothermia creeps in.

### 3. Equipment and gear

### 3.1 Hard constraints: what you can’t skip

1. **Waterproof pressure suit (if you want to reach the cities):**
   - Sealed helmet with integrated pressure mask.
   - Communication system inside.
   - Small depth and pressure-monitoring system.
   - Decompression algorithm ready-accessible (trained or automated).

2. **Standard suit if you stay on the plateau (surface suit):**
   - Full helmet/facemask for air filtration.
   - Respirator with filters for fine dusts.
   - Secured goggles and hearing protection.

**You cannot safely “go just for a look” without protection.**

---

### 3.2 Hearing protection and sound management

- **Canal-clearing earplugs:**  
  - If you have to enter pressure environments, your ears must be able to equalize.
- **Noise-cancellation or attenuation headsets** tuned for:
  - Extreme low-frequency cancellation.
  - Clear voice transmission with your group.
- **Low-level white noise or pink noise generator** for sleep:
  - To create *change* in the soundscape so your brain isn’t trapped in one frequency.

---

### 3.3 Light source and navigation tools

- **Horizonless deprivation:**  
  - The basalt and ocean vanish into indistinct distance.
- **You need:**  
  - **Depth perception:** headlamp with *distance* focus.
  - **Laser range-finder or LIDAR device:** to know how far things are; *never* trust your eyes when space scale is broken.
  - **Digital compasses, inertial navigation:** because visual reference points will be misleading.
- **Marker systems:**  
  - Temporary reflective tape flagging.
  - **Remove everything you bring with you**, especially if it is synthetic: metal dust plus plastics is not something you want to leave into a system that never erodes.

---

### 3.4 Safety and tooling

- **Tripod-based anchoring** if you need stable station:
  - Basalt is glass-smooth; tripod feet need adhesive pads or self-leveling jacks.
- **Harness and tethering gear:**  
  - If you are going to bring in *any* vertical gear, remember:
    - You can’t simply anchor screws into the basalt without damage.
- **Foldable micro-environment:**  
  - A **tent-like** structure with own air handling and external noise damping:
    - Give your eyes and body a *bounded* world for rest.
    - Provides both temperature and sound control.

---

### 3.5 Measuring instruments

- **Pressure & depth gauge:**  
  - Even if you’re not entering the water, monitoring subtle pressure changes from the engines is important.
- **Temperature probe:**  
  - To track your body heat loss and environmental temp.
- **Environmental sensors:**  
  - For detecting metal dusts, unknown gases, or field fluctuations.
- **Timekeeping:**  
  - **Multiple external clocks:** atomic or multiverse-stable chronometers.
  - Make sure you **declare**:
    - “I am not staying here more than X hours/days.”

**Humans are not reliable counters of time in a constant environment.**

---

### 3.6 Medical supplies

- **Basic trauma kit:**  
  - Bandages, antiseptics, splints, eye flush.
- **Pain relief and anti-inflammatory:**  
  - Joint strain from walking on ultra-flat dense basalt is high.
- **Cold-weather-related:**  
  - Skin lubricants, balms, and antifungals if you’re exposed.
- **If you plan to breach water boundary:**  
  - **Otorhinology and decompression FAQ:**  
    - Simple ear clearing instructions.
    - Guidance for what *pressure or time depth class* you are entering.
- **Sedation for sleep:** optional but considered:
  - The constant tone can be maddening.
  - Use under guidance of a physician in multiverse travel.

**Always assume** there is **no native medical infrastructure** that you can understand.

### 4. Psychological and mental training

### 4.1 Simulation before departure

You should:

- Spend **multiple hours** in a controlled simulation:
  - A dark, immersive environment with sound frequency similar to the engines.
  - Minimal visual change, no obvious day/night cycle.
- **Monitor yourself**:
  - How quickly you get irritated.
  - How you feel: time distortion, anxiety, mind racing.

Adjust your **visit length** based on that:

- If you feel uncomfortable after 2 hours:
  - Don’t plan more than 4 hours of exposure per “visit session”.
- **Structured exposure:**  
  - Short visits first, longer intervals later.

---

### 4.2 Routine anchors

- **Set “daylight”:**  
  - Even if there’s no real day-night, you:
    - Declare waking “morning”.
    - Set “evening” wind-down activities.
- **Micro-routines:**  
  - Every 4 hours: check your logs, eat a small portion of food, mark time point in your journal.
  - Every hour: check suit integrity, pressure sensors, or temperature.
- **Stop timelines:**  
  - Before you go in:
    - “After 12 hours, we’re out regardless.”
    - If you stay *inside* the water layer:
      - Set maximum dive time, mandatory decompression time, and **zero exceptions**.

---

### 4.3 Reality checkpoints and external contact

- **Regular external checkpoints:**  
  - Every 2–4 hours:
    - Reach out to someone outside the world.
    - Describe what you see and *how* you feel.
- **Prepared questions:**  
  - Ask them:
    - “Does my description sound coherent?”
    - “Does my time sense match your external time?”
- **Exit criteria:**  
  - Before entry:
    - List clear criteria:
      - If you report disorientation or time distortion → **mandatory exit.**
      - If you report *indifference to danger* → **mandatory exit.**

---

### 5. Entering and navigating the plateau world

### 5.1 Insertion site

Assuming you can appear on a stable anchorpoint:

- **Choose a flat, unobstructed basalt area** away from:
  - Close edge over the sky-sea.
  - Engine shafts directly overhead.
- **Expect:**  
  - No paths, no roads, no visual cues.
- **Set your “anchor”:**
  - A distinctive **tripod, beacon**, or **regulated transponder** at your insertion site.
  - Tie *every* outward movement from there, so you can always return.

**You cannot rely on “turning back and looking” because everything looks the same.**

---

### 5.2 Moving on the plateau

- **Basalt texture:**  
  - Extremely smooth, polished.
  - Slightly uneven in places:
    - Sharp ridges where it flows broke.
  - **Your shoes should be:**  
    - Not-smooth treads, with high grip and ankle protection.
- **Navigation:**  
  - Use **compass heading + fixed range**:
    - “Walk north-northeast for exactly 500 m from base beacon.”
    - Mark turnpoints; triangulation back to base must be possible.
  - **LIDAR mapping:**  
    - Real-time mapping of microtopology will help you avoid repeating paths.

---

### 5.3 Approaching the edge of the world

- **Do not go near the drop-off into the sky-sea** unless:  
  - You are fully harnessed with **reliable anchor point**.
  - You have **someone on the line** watching you.
- **Psychological edge:**  
  - Looking out over a horizon with sky “below” and an ocean above you will cause disorientation:
    - Fix your eyes on **close reference points**:
      - The edge of your helmet, boot laces, your own hands.
    - Breathe slowly; don’t stare at horizon for more than seconds at once.

**The edge is not a place to be cavalier about.**

---

### 5.4 Interacting with the boundary layer fog

**Observing the boundary layer:**

- You *can* look upward through the fog ceiling:
  - Bright headlight on *low* to avoid disorienting yourself by reflected light.
- **Touching the water:**
  - Your hand *should* be protected:
    - Gloved, ideally:
      - With cross-contamination minimized:
        - You don’t know what microscopic ecosystems are in the water.
  - As you raise:
    - Your arm enters **air**, then **wet fog**, then **free water**.
    - You feel the **cold** increase, and then the **drag** of water.
  - **Do not:**  
    - Stick faces or heads into water without full helmet.
    - Assume you can pull back out “easily” once pressure starts.

**You’re not just dipping your hand into a pond; you’re entering a column of ocean above.**

### 6. Ascending into the cities—choosing to go deeper

**This is the least safe part.** If you must attempt:

### 6.1 Pressure and dive system

- **Full closed-circuit diving system:**  
  - Gas supply controlled and monitored.
  - Counter-lungs and depth control.
  - Oxygen content sensors.
  - **Automatic ascent-rate and depth alarms:**  
    - You must not be forced to do math in your head.
- **Depth and time calculations:**  
  - **Mandatory:**  
    - Before you first break water:
    - Set **max projected depth**, **max duration**, and **required decompression schedule**.

**Never** override return depth or time limit once set.

---

### 6.2 Approaching the engines

**Understanding:**

- The engines:
  - **Helical turbines** drawing water inward, not pushing out.
  - Fields around them maintain **dry** area below the ocean.

**Behavior you must avoid:**

- **Do not touch** engines’ outer surfaces:
  - Their field may be part of what keeps the ocean from collapsing.
  - **Do not implant attachments**, screw anchors into shell, or attach lines.
- **Observe only:**  
  - From ~10 meters or more.
  - Use **oblong or far-zoom optics** for detailed inspection.

**There are no instructions manual you can understand; assume you don’t know what you’re doing.**

---

### 6.3 The cities and connectors

**If you reach a city disk:**

- You might be able to **externally inspect the transparent walkways and structures**, but:
  - There are no external doorways designed for you.
- **Do not attempt** cutting or welding into:
  - The translucent ceramic blocks.
  - The cables connecting disks.
- **Why:**  
  - You *cannot* predict how the pressure system and field supports will react.
  - You might:
    - Cause local field failure,
    - Or partial pressure collapse,
    - Which you would not survive.

**Your goal is:** *light external touch*, no structural alteration.

---

### 6.4 Life in the vertical ocean: don’t interact

**Safe patterns:**

- Just watch:
  - Cylindrical creatures with rotating limbs.
  - Segmented ribbons moving.
- **Do not:**  
  - Use powerful lights directly on their bodies.
  - Use electrical fields or sonar from your devices which they may detect.
  - **Do not:**
    - Reach out to touch them.
    - Try to capture samples without **thorough containment planning**.

**What you *should* do:**

- Take notes:
  - How they move.
  - How many you see in a given volume.
  - Whether you see any *changes* over time:
    - Somebody has to be the first person to notice what happens when they *do* change.

**Collecting samples:**

- If authorized:
  - Use **closed sample containers**:
    - Isolated from water by your suit.
    - Captured while minimizing external contact.
- **Assume you are contaminant:**  
  - You don’t know if your tools are edible to them.
  - Or if they will react to anything you’ve introduced.

**They’re not predators, but they are alien sensors; you are not a neutral element.**

### 7. Risk and contingency planning

### 7.1 Entry and exit lane

Before you set foot in this world:

- **Define your exit point** clearly:
  - Location and exact coordinates.
  - **How** you will leave:
    - Multiverse transit device respawns you,
    - Or you return to a known secure “window.”
  - **If you are in water:**  
    - Underwater exit cannot be used casually:
      - You must *surface* to the air layer below ocean *with* controlled decompression.

**You cannot be impulsive.**

---

### 7.2 Emergency scenarios

1. **Equipment failure:**  
   - Loss of pressure in suit:
     - Dropping to bottom of vertical ocean is not safe:
       - You may go to depths you can’t ascend from.
       - Set **automatic abort to nearest air or safe zone** if sensors fail.
   - Loss of hearing:
     - First, protect **vestibular system**:
       - Avoid sudden head movements.
       - Go back to known-anchor and base.

2. **Psychological decompensation:**  
   - Panic, confusion, or time distortion:
     - **Stop movement.**  
     - Return to base station.
     - Contact external observer.

3. **Environmental**:
   - Any change in the *constant* engine sound:
     - Means **something** has changed.
     - Treat it as a warning to:
       - Move to safer zone, ideally **exit the world**.

**Any change = anomaly, not curiosity.**

---

### 7.3 Decompression and re-entry

**When you leave:**

- **Go through decompression if** you have been:
  - Under high pressure in the water column,
  - In any medium that your multiverse tool requires **buffering**.
- **Physical debrief:**  
  - Ear check.
  - Joint and muscle check.
- **Temporal debrief:**
  - Ask yourself:
    - How long does it *feel* you were there?
  - Ask external observer:
    - How long were you actually there?
  - Note discrepancies:
    - If they are large, your **next visit should be shorter or cancelled**.

**You must become vigilant of your own perception distortions.**

---

### 8. Ethical and system-level considerations

**You are in a world not meant for you:**

- There is no visible “eco” system you might recognize, but there is a system.
- **Principles of safety to apply:**
  - **Leave no trace, where physically possible.**
  - **Do not modify structures:** ceramic disks, cables, engines.
  - **Do not drag contaminants:** from your world or others—
    - Foods, oils, plastics, foreign dusts.

**If you must interact, you must be as near as possible to a ghost.**

**Long-term contact is not safe for you or for them (if they are capable of being affected at all).**

---

### 9. Returned mission—how you leave yourself

**Before you depart:**

- Write a clear **report**:
  - What you saw, heard, measured.
  - How you felt.
  - How long you felt you were there.
  - How long you actually were.
- **Lock this report away** and:
  - Compare it with any later visit you make to watch:
    - How your perception of the world changes,
    - And whether you are becoming more tolerant or numb to its dangers.

**The goal of your visit is not to become comfortable there.**

---

### 10. Final word—a concise kit of mind and body

You should treat this world as:

- **A machine inside ocean and rock**, built by someone else for someone else.
- **A constant-state environment** where your time and sense of self are easily distorted.
- **Not a place of hospitality or danger**, but one of **indifference.**

**Essential preparation:**

- **Fitness:** good core, capable joint and limb support.
- **Protection:** hearing, breathing, pressure, and temperature.
- **Tools:** external clocks, navigation devices, sensors, depth and pressure gauge.
- **Mind guard:** structured time, external anchors, strict exit criteria.
- **Ethics:** deep respect for the stability of a world that doesn’t change and **has not invited you.**

**You do not conquer it or adapt it to you.**  
You visit briefly, watch carefully, and retreat carefully—before its unchanging storm becomes too seductive to leave.
