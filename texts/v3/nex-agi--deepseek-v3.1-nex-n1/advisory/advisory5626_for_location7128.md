To visit this place is to step into a blueprint that hasn’t decided yet what it’s building.

It’s not a disaster zone or a warfront or a monster-haunted wilderness; the danger here is *structural indecision*. You will not die from a single wrong step as much as from many small collapses and bad assumptions. The world itself is trying out versions of itself in real time, and you need to behave like you’re inside a half-dried sculpture that is still being shaped.

---

## 1. Before You Go: Choosing the Right Person

### Physical and mental health

You don’t need to be an athlete, but you should be:

- **Comfortable with heat and humidity**  
  This is like walking inside a mouth: warm, damp, constant. If you faint easily in heat or humidity, or your body does poorly in “stifling” conditions, this place will grind you down.

- **Reasonably stable on your feet**  
  The footing is uneven concrete, unseen edges, and sometimes weirdly tilting surfaces. Good balance is more important than raw speed.

- **Mentally resilient to uncertainty and “trick” environments**  
  If you can’t stand being gaslit by your own surroundings, you will suffer here. The world *will* shift around you, and you will question yourself. This plays into the most important trait:

- **Capable of trusting instruments and protocols more than your eyes**  
  Your eyes will be wrong sometimes. The horizon will be wrong. Distances will be wrong. You need enough self-control to say “my map and my compass are more reliable than my sense of ‘that looks like five minutes away’.”

### Optional but helpful traits

- **Experience with confined or “no sky” spaces:** caves, tunnels, deep indoor spaces.
- **Basic construction sense:** knowing what “rebar,” “formwork,” “concrete slab” mean helps you parse what you’re looking at.
- **No severe claustrophobia:** The sky isn’t a sky but a low, luminous ceiling. It doesn’t constrict like a narrow tunnel, but it never “opens out” into normal sky, which some people find disquieting.

If you have panic attacks under uncertainty or need very clear structure to be okay, this world will be quietly psychologically hostile to you.

## 2. Equipment and Clothing

### Clothing

Think: *light + modular + quick-dry + protective*.

You’re going to be damp all the time, but not from rain—more like being in a shower room after someone’s left the hot water running.

#### Recommended clothing layers

- **Base layer**
  - Synthetic or moisture-wicking T-shirt and long underwear.
  - Avoid cotton as anything you’ll be depending on; cotton will stay wet and clammy against your skin.

- **Mid layer**
  - A thin synthetic long-sleeve shirt and pants.
    - Purpose:
      - Protect from mild scrapes on concrete edges.
      - A layer between you and the faintly warm rebar you might touch or brush against.
    - Choose materials that dry quickly and resist tearing on rough surfaces.

- **Outer layer**
  - A rain jacket and pants (waterproof but breathable) if possible.
    - Not because you get rain in the normal sense, but:
      - **Protect your skin from the water that hangs in sheets and curtains.**
      - You might need to push through dangling threads of water that are being drawn sideways by non-standard gravity. You do not know what happens if these water curtains are allowed to saturate your skin long-term.
    - Even a cheap poncho will help.

- **Head protection**
  - A hard hat or lightweight helmet if you can obtain one.
    - Protects against occasional dripping from overhead, accidental head bumps into columns in low-visibility fog, or from unexpected sharp edges as you stand up from crawling under a rebar grid.

- **Hands**
  - Light work gloves with grip.
    - You may be bracing yourself on concrete, edges, or rebar.
    - The metal is warm and not rusted but you don’t want to cut your hands on sharp edges.

- **Feet**
  - Waterproof boots with good grip:
    - **The concrete floor is not uniformly wet,** but you can suddenly find yourself standing on slick, beaded surfaces where moisture has been gathering “invisible lines.”
    - You need grip even on smooth surfaces, and protection from:
      - Stub toes on jagged edges of slabs.
      - Step into holes where rebar is exposed.
    - Ankle support is highly recommended because of uneven surfaces and hidden height changes.

- **Optional accessories**
  - A bandana or scarf:
    - Can be wetted and wrung out to clean your face.
    - Can be wrapped around your mouth and nose if the air ever thickens into something more than just humid.
    - Not a mask, but a layer between you and the odd metallic-taste air.

- **Avoid heavy, insulated garments**
  - The temperature hangs near “want to strip a layer.” If you over-insulate you will overheat.
    - You’re better off with thin layers that add up to warmth than a single “heavy” coat.

### Instruments and tools

This is where you *counteract* the world’s tricks.

Bring **non-electronic tools where you can** because even if you had electronics, you have no idea what instability would do to them. The world itself behaves like a half-rendered simulation—you should not trust your life to a delicate digital device here.

#### Essential instruments

1. **Good analog compass**
   - With:
     - A clear baseplate.
     - Declination scale you can set.
   - You will need to believe this compass when your eyes say “that wall looks closer than the map suggests.”
   - You will need to believe your bearings when the horizon subtly shifts after you blink.

2. **Watch with clear second hand**
   - Mechanically powered if possible.
   - Needed:
     - For timing short intervals when you’re observing the world’s small changes.
     - For estimating travel times when you feel like you’ve been walking forever but the map says you’ve barely covered any distance.

3. **Notebook and pencil**
   - Paper:
     - Stable to temperature.
     - Won’t crash.
     - Won’t be corrupted by tiny geometry shifts.
   - Pencil:
     - Ink will run in the humidity.
     - Graphite doesn’t care.

    Use it:
    - Sketch maps of what you see.
    - Mark unusual columns, rebar ribs, pits, and where you first noticed them.
    - Note time, bearing from compass, and description of what’s different.
    - If you come back and find that column has moved, or that floor edge has a new curve, you *record* that.

4. **Measuring tape**
   - 10–20 meters long is fine.
    - Use it:
      - To measure distances to suspicious objects like “that nearby wall” that appears to be moving.
    - If your compass says you’ve walked 100 meters on a straight line, but the tape says you’re now 50 meters from the last marked column, you know something is wrong with your perception of distance.

5. **Simple elevation tools**
    - At minimum:
      - A small spirit level (or a level app on a device, if you must).
    - Helps you:
      - Detect whether the floor still matches your map’s contour.
    - If you were sure that stretch of floor was slanted uphill but your level shows it’s flat now, you have learned something about how reality shifts.

6. **Sunlight-bright flashlight**
   - Even if no real night falls:
     - Useful to brighten your path when the fog overhead thickens and “twilight” becomes “very dim grey.”
     - Useful to explore:
       - Dark edges under overhangs.
       - Pits where the floor has been left unfinished.
    - Bring:
      - Spare batteries or a separate backup light.
    - Attach a wrist strap so you don’t drop it into a deep rebar pit.

7. **Water containers**
   - Multiple:
     - Water bottles that you can close tightly.
    - Even though:
      - The world is full of water, it hangs in the air and not in rivers or lakes.
    - You may not find safe standing water you can easily drink.
    - Use:
      - Water from home.
    - If you must tap into the world’s moisture:
      - Use only water collected in clean vessels from condensation surfaces in clean concrete.
      - Filter lightly with cloth or a filter straw if you can.
      - Do not experiment with the water that’s drawn along invisible lines by sideways gravity.

8. **Food**
   - Energy dense:
     - Trail bars, nuts, chocolate, sealed dried meals, etc.
    - Assume:
      - There is no food in this world.
      - Nothing you can eat will grow here.
      - The longer the stay, the more you must rely on your own supplies.

9. **Medical supplies**
    - At least:
      - Antiseptic wipes or solution.
      - Bandages and gauze pads.
      - Medical tape.
      - Blister treatment pads.
    - You will likely:
      - Scrape hands on concrete edges.
      - Rub feet from walking over uneven surfaces.

    - Optional but helpful:
      - Electrolyte tablets or hydration salts in case you sweat heavily in the humidity.
      - Painkillers if you get headaches from the metallic air and oppressive humidity.

### Optional but useful tools

- **Mask or respirator**
  - N95/N99 or equivalent.
    - You might move into zones where the air feels thicker and more “like soup,” or where metallic taste in the air is stronger.
    - You don’t know what’s suspended in that fog or droplets; you might want to filter some out.

- **Simple rope (10–20 meters of cord)**
  - Uses:
    - Tying yourself to a column if you explore near unstable edges.
    - Lowering a pack into a curious floor hole before you commit to climbing.
    - Simply marking a path unambiguously (you won’t lose a rope the way you might a chalk line).

- **Chalk or reflective tape**
  - For marking:
    - Your route on the concrete.
    - Which side of a column you passed.
    - Which end of a broken slab edge leads to solid floor.
  - The world has not decided to be stable or permanent; if you behave *as if* you’re navigating a building that’s being redrawn around you, you’ll never be surprised.

## 3. Navigation and Perception

### Your big problem: The world doesn’t sit still

You will walk into spaces where:

- Columns that seemed vertical now lean a bit.
- The floor interface that once looked like a gentle slope becomes a sharp edge.
- The horizon seems to flicker between two different angles before settling.

You cannot stop this. You *can* refuse to be fooled by it.

### Basic navigation protocol

1. **Set a clear reference point before you leave**
   - Some landmark:
     - A column.
     - A distinct configuration of rebar.
     - A broken slab edge that you photograph or sketch.
    - This is your “return point” if you lose your way.

2. **Always navigate with a compass and time, not distance “by feel”**
   - Don’t say:
     - “That looks like 100 meters away.”
   - Count:
     - Number of steps and time taken.
    - Use:
      - The map area you’ve written yourself, if needed.
    - Example:
      - If you walk at say 100 meters per minute steady:
        - 1 minute = 100 meters.
        - You time that and align with compass headings.

3. **Mark your way clearly**
   - Use chalk or tape:
     - Arrows pointing back to your origin.
     - Number or initial letters on each marker you leave.
    - This:
      - Helps you verify if anything else has moved.

    Even if geometry changes:
    - Your marks might be gone, but their absence tells you something.
    - If they’re still there but the column they’re attached to has a new slope, that’s valuable information.

4. **Don’t trust sudden “shortcuts”**
   - If you see:
     - A path to a “nearby” column that looks much shorter than expected.
    - Assume:
     - Your geometry is wrong.
    - Treat that as:
     - A signal to be extra cautious, not as a convenient shortcut.

### Deal with deceptive scale and horizon

The world was designed on huge scales:

- A “wall” you think you see near to you might be a huge far away foundation.
- The slope you think is a hill might be something meant to support a building the size of a city block or more.

Your rule:

- **Always assume that you underestimate distance and overestimate height.**  
  You will likely be closer to flat things than you think, and further away from large features than you want.

Walk prepared:

- If you don’t reach what you thought was “nearby” within a reasonable time:
  - Stop.
  - Check your compass and map.
  - Check any measurements you may have taken.
  - Consider that you didn’t misjudge your own speed; you misjudged the world’s geometry.

### Handle shifts in geometry

You will *notice*, eventually, that :

- Objects you *remember* being straight now lean slightly.
- Part of the floor you thought was flat now has a slight tilt.

At this point:

1. **Don’t panic**
   - There is nothing immediate you must do, except:
     - Stop reckless forward movement.
     - Reconfirm your position.

2. **Check your instruments**
   - Compare:
     - Level of floor with your level.
     - Compass bearings with your notebook.
    - If things disagree:
      - Trust your instruments and notebook more than your memory.

3. **Update your map**
    - Write:
      - Time of observation.
      - What you observed changed.
    - This does not solve the problem, but:
      - It gives you data over time.
      - It helps you detect patterns of change.

    If you notice:
    - Columns are always rotating slightly in a particular direction, or floors tilting toward a particular section of the world, you might be detecting:
      - A larger structural change underneath.
    - In that case:
      - Move away from that region instead of toward it.

## 4. Safety and Risk

### Physical hazards

Mostly low-intensity but persistent:

1. **Trips and falls on uneven surfaces**
    - Solution:
        - Always watch your feet.
        - Go slow over unknown terrain.
        - Don’t rush because you *think* you can see that the next part is “flat.”

2. **Hidden edges and pits**
    - The floor sometimes:
        - Drops away into a grid of rebar over black water.
    - You’ll be:
        - Walking on what you thought was reliable floor, then be at an edge you didn’t see coming.
    - Solution:
        - Use your light:
            - To scan ahead of you.
        - Always probe with:
            - Your foot or tool before you commit weight to a new surface.

    If you are near an edge:
    - Don’t:
        - Walk backward to line up a “photo,” or peer over your shoulder to look at something else.
    - Always:
        - Keep the edge in your field of view.

3. **Reinforcement bars (rebar)**
    - Hazards:
        - Puncture risk if you fall onto them.
        - They’re mildly warm and not rusted, but still sharp.
    - Solution:
        - Don’t:
            - Jump over pits where you might slip onto metal bars.
        - Do:
            - Treat them like hazardous scaffolding you’re not allowed to climb.
    - If you must cross near them:
        - Go carefully.
        - Use your rope to stabilize yourself where you can.

4. **Dripping water curtains and their weird gravity**
    - You will:
        - Encounter hanging threads of water that are distorted sideways by invisible forces.
    - You don’t:
        - Know what will happen if you walk through them.
        - Maybe they’re just water.
    - Recommendation:
        - Avoid them where you can.
        - If you must pass through:
            - Cover:
                - Your head and face.
            - Don’t:
                - Ingest water droplets from these “curtains.”
    - Don’t:
        - Use them as a source of drinking water unless absolutely necessary.

5. **Heat and humidity**
    - Even though:
        - The temperature alone doesn’t feel dangerous, you will be:
            - Sweating more.
    - In:
        - Thick, humid air.
    - So:
        - Overheat and dehydration can sneak up on you.
    - Management:
        - Drink:
            - On a schedule, not just when you feel thirsty.
        - Rest:
            - More than you think you need.

    If you feel:
    - Lightheaded.
    - Headache.
    - Dryness despite humidity.
    - Your heart pounding faster than expected.

    - Then stop:
        - Rest in the shade of a column or wall.
        - Drink water.
        - Cool down.

### Psychological hazards

This might be *more* dangerous than the physical risks:

1. **Doubt in your own memory and senses**
    - The world:
        - Moves slightly.
        - Changes between blinks.

    This will:
    - Make you feel:
        - Unstable.
    - You might start to:
        - Question yourself.

    Countermeasure:
    - Always leave:
        - Objective records:
            - Notes.
            - Sketches.
    - If you doubt:
        - Whether you really remember shape correctly:
    - Check:
        - Your notebook.

    You must decide:
    - That:
        - It is acceptable to see this place.
    - You:
        - Don't need to understand it fully to return safely.

2. **Disquieting atmosphere**
    - Ceaseless twilight:
        - No clear day or night.
    - No life:
        - No plants or animals.
    - Only:
        - Concrete, metal, water.

    This can:
        - Wear down your sense of time.
    - You may:
        - Become convinced:
            - Time has completely lost meaning.

    Countermeasures:
        - Keep:
            - Regular schedule.
    - Sleep when:
        - You would have at home because you’re tired.
    - Eat:
        - At set intervals.

3. **No horizon of relief**
    - You:
        - Can't wait “until dawn” or “until the storm passes.”
        - The world will be like this the whole time you’re here.
    - So:
        - You need an internal plan:
            - How long you will stay.
            - When you will return regardless.
    - Don't say:
        - “I’ll leave when it feels done.”
        - It never will.

### Sensory strangeness

- Air:
  - Warm thick soup.
  - Faint metallic taste.
  - Sometimes thicker bands as though moving between rooms.

- Light:
  - Diffuse and shadowless.
    - You won't see clearly defined edges.
    - You might feel depth perception is slightly off.

- Sound:
    - Doesn’t travel or echo normally.
    - Loudness:
        - Doesn't reveal how close you are to source of sound.
    - If:
        - You hear cracking noise:
    - Assume:
        - It could be from anywhere and not necessarily nearby.

    Behavior:
        - Treat:
            - Big sounds:
                - As warnings to be more cautious.
    - Not:
        - As directions to find something.

## 5. How to Explore (If You Must)

This world calls very strongly to be explored:

- You feel:
    - Like you are inside the blueprint.
    - You want to see what it’s building.
    - You want to test if it can recognize you as an occupant.

You should be:

- **Systematic, not adventurous**
    - You:
        - Are not in an action movie.
    - You:
        - Are in a place that has not yet decided what to do.

### Basic exploration protocol

1. **Choose a direction and commit, but with a return condition**
    - For example:
        - Walk:
            - 30 minutes in one compass heading.
    - Then:
        - The return time is at least:
            - As long as you walked out.
    - You:
        - Don’t extend:
            - Halfway through your supplies before you start back.

    Or:
        - Use:
            - Markers:
                - At least every 5 minutes of walking.
    - When:
        - You have deployed:
            - Half of your markers:
    - You:
        - Turn back.

    This is:
        - To ensure:

2. **Do not touch “interesting” architectures**

        - Large columns or:
