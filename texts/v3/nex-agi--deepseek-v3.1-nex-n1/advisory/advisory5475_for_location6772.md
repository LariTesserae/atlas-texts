You are preparing to visit a machine that has become a sacred grove, and this body is not.

Everything else is listening, so your first rule is simple: *you will be overheard.*  
You don’t need to be scared of that, but you need to be honest about it. This is not a place you pass through invisibly; it is a place that reads you.

Below is a practical guide for how an ordinary human traveler—with ordinary senses and limits—should prepare to walk safely and meaningfully into the courtyard of the weeping stones.

---

## 1. Before You Step Into This City

### 1.1. Decide why you are going

You are not just dropping coordinates on a map. You are entering:

- A **functional node** in a city-sized water and climate system;
- A **sensor array** that feels air, humidity, heat, and motion;
- A **space of contemplation**, built like a cathedral of water and stone.

Your reasons matter, because *this place will shape you more than you will shape it*. If you are:

- **A seeker of quiet**:  
  Prepare for a kind of silence that is densely packed—sound and pressure moving in very slow rhythms.  
- **A technician, researcher, or pilgrim to the city itself**:  
  You will be treated as part of the environment; the system will adjust around you.
- **Just curious and passing through**:  
  Whatever you expect, this place will gently correct your expectations.

Whatever your motive, name it clearly before you arrive. It will help keep you oriented when the space begins to *think* around you.

### 1.2. Understand your constraints

Expect:

- **High humidity**: air like a warm, wet veil.
- **Temperature**: comfortable but warm; close to the dew point.
- **No direct sunlight** on the floor of the courtyard—light is amber, filtered, and diffuse.
- **No “wild” nature**—every living organism is engineered in place, like notes on a staff.

Your body will be:

- A heat source.
- A moisture source.
- A sound source.
- A sensor node.

The system is not your enemy—but it is not indifferent. You are being input into a calculation.

## 2. Practical Preparation for Your Body

### 2.1. Clothing and gear

Assume your clothes will be *lightly damp but not soaked* within minutes of arrival.

**Choose clothing:**

- **Lightweight, close-fitting layers** that:
  - Won’t drip water everywhere.
  - Won’t brush accidentally against surfaces you should not touch.
- **Non-synthetic or blended fabrics that manage moisture well**:
  - Linen-cotton blends.
  - Base layers that move sweat away from your skin.
  Avoid anything that:
  - Gets heavy when wet.
  - Might leave fibers or lint on surfaces.
  - Makes rustling sounds with every movement.

**Colors:**

- Not black or pure white if you can avoid it.
- Choose soft greens/ greys/ earth-tones, to better understand how the light affects you.  
  This isn’t about disguise; it’s about learning to see how the place *sees you*.

**Footwear:**

- Flat, **non-slip soles**—the floor is polished smooth and slightly curved.
- Closed shoes (to avoid leaving skin-oils or salt on the floor).
- Leave shoes that:
  - Track dirt.
  - Have heavy treads that can damage the finish.

**Coverings:**

- If your hair is long, tie it back.
- If you wear jewelry:
  - Avoid objects that swing loose.
  - Choose pieces that cling close to your body.
    The system will respond to motion alone; extra movement is extra noise.

### 2.2. Scent and contaminants

This is a clean, closed system of water and membranes.

Make yourself as neutral as possible:

- Do not wear:
  - Strong perfumes, colognes, or oils.
  - Deodorant with strong scent.
- Use minimal:
  - Makeup or lotions that might flake, rub off, or transfer.
- Avoid:
  - Any solvent that outgases: nail polish remover, strong solvents.
  - Insect repellents, unless you are medically dependent on them.

If you can, shower before you visit, with unscented soap, and rinse very thoroughly.

**Tobacco smoke, vapes, heavy alcohol breath:**

- Avoid for several hours beforehand.
- The membranes are sensitive to chemistry in the air.
- You won’t be “punished,” but you will change the composition of the air the garden measures; and that will be written into the records of whatever system this feeds into.

### 2.3. Personal hygiene and health

- **Hydration**:  
  Drink water beforehand, but not enough to make you urgently need to relieve yourself during your visit.
- **Medications**:  
  If you need medications, bring them, but in containers that are:
  - Sealed.
  - Non-shedding.
  Avoid anything that crumbles easily or can be spilled into the water.

- **Open wounds or high viral shedding**:  
  If you have:
  - Fresh cuts or open sores, especially on your hands or feet:
    - Cover them carefully.
    - Do not let them touch water or surfaces.  
    This is not only for the sake of the system; it is for your own safety. The place is not sterile, but it is precise and alive.

- **Motion sensitivity**:
  - If you are prone to vertigo or claustrophobia:
    - Expect a low dome, indirect light, and a sense of being *inside* something.
    - Prepare grounding strategies: breathing slow, counting steps, having an exit plan.

## 3. Mental and Emotional Preparation

### 3.1. Attitude

You are entering the **inner ear of a machine city**.

The appropriate tone is:

- Quiet reverence.
- Respectful curiosity.
- Patient observation.

Approach this as you would approach:

- A library.
- A temple.
- A laboratory.
- An operating theatre.

Not *your* operating theatre—so keep your hands to yourself and your mind observational.

### 3.2. Set intentions before you arrive

Before crossing into the courtyard, decide clearly:

- What you want to learn.
- How long you intend to stay.
- What you will refuse to do (“no touching of the water-curtain,” etc.).

Write this down if possible, so after the visit you can compare how it changed you.

### 3.3. Listen to yourself before you listen to the garden

Do a short self-check:

- Are you anxious? If yes, why—and what signals do you want to avoid sending?
- Are you calm? If yes, can you maintain that under a low, steady hum?
- Are you overtired? If yes, this might blur into the surreal as the place’s clock starts to pull your sense of time.

It is okay to be nervous or excited. The point is not to be blank, but to be **aware of what your body is doing**, so you can separate what is *yours* from what is *the courtyard’s*.

Do not arrive blind, if you can help it.

- The **name of this city**, or its class (if it has one).
- Whether this courtyard:
  - Is a public structure.
  - Is a restricted maintenance node.
  - Is tied to a particular religion or order of engineers.
- Whether there is:
  - A code of behavior.
  - Known customs of greeting or silence.
  - Proscribed behaviors (such as touching the water-curtains or the walls).

### 4.2. Special cultural or legal meanings

- Are **weeping stones**:
  - A known symbol?
  - Part of a particular ritual or architecture?
- Are visitors:
  - Expected to leave offerings?
  - Expected to leave nothing?
  - Logged or monitored in a record system?

If you can find any oral history or technical description of how this system works, you will be better prepared for the kind of *attention* you’re under.

This will tell you how much the space has changed your mind.

## 6. How to Move in the Courtyard Itself

### 6.1. Entering

When you first step into the courtyard:

- **Stop just inside the threshold.**
- Take one slow, full breath:
  - In through the nose.
  - Out through the mouth.
- Notice:
  - The **temperature**.
  - The **smell**: green metal and fermenting flower petals.
  - The **light**: diffuse amber, from nowhere and everywhere.
  - The **hum**: low, steady, from the stones.

**Then take three steps in and pause again.**

This gives the system a chance to adjust to you. You are not a perturbation; you are a data point.

### 6.2. Walking around the ring of stones

The courtyard is small:

- Six steps from wall to ring.
- A dozen more steps around the stones.

Walk as if you were walking around the edges of a sleeping body:

- **Slow, smooth steps**.
- **Avoid sudden stops or changes of direction**.
- Do not rush.

**Stay clear of:**

- The water-curtains hanging from the inner sides of the stones.
- The base where the mechanical roots and “engine roots” coil.
- The walls and their patterned condensation.

**Where you may walk:**

- The floor alone:
  - It is polished synthetic stone, almost-blue grey.
  - The curve is gentle enough that you will not notice it except by your inner sense of balance.

**Respect the trough:**

- The shallow ring of water with the green metal stems is not a decoration or a fountain to play in.
- It is part of the system that feeds the stones and the city’s water.
- Do not:
  - Touch the water.
  - Lean over the trough.
  - Step into it.

### 6.3. How to listen to the weeping stones

The listening here is literal:

- The **stones** hum a low note.
- The **mechanical leaves** track your movement with slow clicks.
- The **condensation chambers** cycle with pressure changes.
- The **water-curtains** breathe as the meniscus in the pores rises and falls.

You will hear better if you:

- **Minimize your own noise**:
  - Avoid shuffling of feet.
  - Avoid jangling of keys or devices.
  - Avoid speaking unless you must.
    If you speak:
    - Use soft tones.
    - Do not shout or whisper frantically.

- **Pause often**:
  - Every 20–30 seconds, stop moving.
  - Every time you stop, you reset the gradient of the humidity field your body is injecting into the room.
    The system will adjust.

You can think of this like listening:

- Not just to the sound.
- But to the **silence between the sounds**.

You will begin to notice the **pressure-beat**:

- As if the room itself has a blood pressure.
- As if the water-curtains are its valves.

**Do not try to “fix” or “interpret” this while you are inside.**  
Just note that you feel it.

## 7. Human Safety and Risk Management

### 7.1. What not to touch

**Do not touch:**

- The **water-curtains** of the stones:
  - Even if you think you can “break the surface tension.”
  - The surface is not random; it is part of a control system.
    - Interrupting it might derange calibration at least.
    - At worst, it might trigger alarms or defensive responses from the city system.
    - Also, you do not know what is in that water chemically or biologically.

- The **greenglass stems and mechanical leaves** in the trough:
  - They turn like sunflowers.
  - They might be:
    - Sensors.
    - Data nodes.
    - Or even light sources.
    - Treat them like exposed nerves.

- The **wall mosses and engineered algae**:
  - They are not weeds to be picked.
  - They are not cushions to lean on.
    - If you are tired, sit on the floor if you are allowed to.
    - Do not use the walls as a backrest, unless you have clear permission to do so.

### 7.2. Your own body’s signals

The environment will bring you closer to your body’s limits:

- **Dehydration**:
  - You may lose moisture without realizing it.
    - If you feel fuzziness or a slight headache:
      - You may need to leave and drink water.
      - The humidity is not the same as hydration.

- **Overheating**:
  - Warm wet air can reduce your evaporative cooling.
    - If you feel dizzy:
      - Move slowly to the edge of the space.
      - Sit down.
      - If you can, leave the courtyard entirely.

- **Psycho-emotional overwhelm**:
  - If the constant *sense* of pressure and the low hum becomes:
    - Oppressive rather than calming.
    - Or like the pressure in a sealed room:
    - Then:
    - Step out.
    - Do not push yourself to stay longer “to prove you can handle it.”
    - This place is more than a decoration on your senses; it is a structure that will weave into your nervous system if you stay long enough.

### 7.3. Contingency for accidents

If you accidentally:

- Slip and fall.
- Touch something you should not have touched.
- Spill a container.

Then:

1. **Do not panic.**  
2. **Minimize contact.**  
    - If you touched something:
      - Step away from it.
    - If you spilled something:
      - Leave it as it is; do not try to clean it with your own cloths or water.
    - If you are injured:
      - Move yourself safely to the edge of the courtyard.
    - If there are **any visible interfaces**—panels or ports in the wall:
      - Do not touch them unless you have been instructed to.

**Record the incident:**

- Quietly note what happened in your memory or notebook once you have left.
- If you must report to any authorities, be:
  - Precise.
  - Factual.
  - Humble.

## 8. How to Be Present Without Being an Intrusion

### 8.1. Entering the relationship

This garden is not **empty**:

- The moss breathes.
- The condensation chambers breathe.
- The water-curtains breathe.

The place as a whole is a **single body** with many sensors.

Your relationship to it is not unlike your relationship to:

- A sleeping animal.
- A library.
- A laboratory where you are not the chief researcher.

You may be permitted here, but you are still **company**.

**Ask yourself silently:**

- “What am I asking of this place?”
- “What am I giving it?”

### 8.2. Your posture as communication

Watch how your body is positioned:

- **Are you looming** over the stones?
  - Then you are a pressure and a shadow at the same time.
    - Adjust your position: lower if appropriate, or step back.
- Are you **circling too quickly**?
  - Then you are disturbing the humidity field.
    - Slow down.
- Are you **ignoring the sensation of being watched**?
  - The mechanical leaves move, however slowly.
    - Acknowledge that your presence is being measured; not as a threat but as a fact.

You can send:

- Calm.
- Respect.
- Curiosity.

The courtyard will take them as input data.

### 8.3. Ritual and behavior, if allowed by local custom

If you are given:

- A specific **way of greeting the stones**:
  - Do not force it into something showy.
  - Follow the form given.
    If you are not given:
    - Then default to:
      - Stillness.
      - Silence.
      - Presence.

**Consider:**

- Before you leave:
  - Stand before the ring of stones for a few breaths.
  - With your eyes closed:
    - Listen to the water.
    - Listen to the breath.
    - Listen to your own breath.

This is not a mystical flourish; it is a **sampling method**.

When you do this:

- You are:
  - Taking a sample of the system.
  - And letting the system take a sample of you.

## 9. When You Leave: What to Take, What to Leave Behind

### 9.1. How you exit

- **Walk slowly to the exit**.
- Do not run, even if you felt overwhelmed.
    - If you need to leave quickly:
      - Move smoothly but purposefully.
- **Pause at the threshold**.
    - Take one last full breath.
    - Compare:
      - The temperature.
      - The smell.
      - The sound.
    - To what you remember when you first entered.

### 9.2. What you must not leave behind

- Physical objects:
  - No coins.
  - No notes.
  - No personal items.

Unless you are explicitly instructed to do so by someone who *knows this city’s rules*.

### 9.3. What you *should* leave behind

- **Expectations** that are not met:
  - This is not a ruin of nature.
  - This is not architecture without life.
  - It is something else.

Allow yourself to be *changed* by that.

You can leave:

- Your sense of urgency, if you can bear to.
- Your desire to control the environment.
- Your assumption that you understand the relationship between “machine” and “nature.”

### 9.4. What you should take away with you

**Record in your memory:**

- The low humming of the stones.
- The color of the almost-blue-grey floor.
- The scent of green metal and fermenting flowers.
- How your body felt holding the humidity of the place.

**Record later in your notebook:**

- What you noticed emotionally:
  - Did you become calm?
  - Did you become uncomfortable?
- Did you feel:
  - More like yourself?
  - Less like yourself?
    This is data on the environment’s effect on your nervous system.

## 10. Long After Your Visit

### 10.1. Integration of the experience

This courtyard will not be a “place you once visited”; it may be a place that stays *inside* you.

The system of which it is part:

- Listens.
- Records.
- Responds.

You will not walk the same way in water, rain, or rivers again:

- A rainstorm will sound like the weeping stones.
- A condensation pattern on a window may remind you of the delicate hexagonal honeycomb on the walls.
- A low hum in a city access tunnel will recall the stones themselves.

This is not coincidence; it is **pattern recognition**.

### 10.2. Preparing yourself for next time

If you intend to visit this place again:

- Ask yourself:
  - What changed you most from the first visit?
  - What did *not* happen that you expected?
  - What did you not expect that happened?

- Adjust:
  - Your clothing.
  - Your time.
  - Your intentions.
  - Your openness to being changed.

**You might prepare:**

- With breathing exercises.
- With practice in silence.
- With learning more about the city you’re entering.
- But mainly you need to be ready to be watched by something more than you can watch.

### Final Summary in Practical Steps

Before you visit the courtyard of weeping stones:

1. **Prepare your body:**
   - Wear neutral-smelling, moisture-friendly clothes.
   - Ready shoes with non-slip soles.
   - Cleanse without scent.
   - Hydrate, but not too much.

2. **Prepare your mind:**
   - Set your intention clearly.
   - Decide your limits and what you will not do.
   - Familiarize yourself with any known codes of conduct.

3. **Prepare your tools:**
   - A notebook and pencil.
   - A sealed bag for minimal sensors.
   - A mask if you want to control your breath and humidity output.
   - Nothing that will shed or leak into the system.

4. **When you arrive:**
   - Enter slowly and stop at the threshold.
   - Move quietly, around the floor, not touching the stones or water.
   - Listen to the sound the water is being.
   - Notice the leaves following your motion with slow patience.
   - Do not disturb the filters of air and water and stone.

5. **When you leave:**
   - Walk out gently.
   - Do not leave anything behind.
   - Carry home with you the memory of its hum and breath and the pressure in your chest.

You will not be only a visitor. You will be part of the data stream that this city turns to stone and water and time.

Prepare yourself, then, as someone who wants to be **good data**: honest, calm, and clear enough that the garden, listening through its weeping stones, can hear you without being harmed by what you are.
