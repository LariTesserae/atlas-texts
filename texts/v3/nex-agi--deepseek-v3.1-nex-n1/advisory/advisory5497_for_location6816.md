Here is a preparation guide for an ordinary multiverse traveler planning to visit this land of terraced highlands and fog‑shrouded towns.

-------------------

## 1. Big picture: What kind of place is this?

You’re heading to a world with a strong “theme”:  

- **Stone:** Dark, smooth, river‑formed rock, everywhere—underfoot, in walls, in terrace fields, under the central pool.  
- **Water:** Gentle, constant, pervasive moisture. Not storms and floods, but steady seepage, mist, channels, terraces.  
- **Silence & Absence:** No animals. No insects, birds, or anything else. Only plant, stone, water, and humans.  
- **Slow Time:** Very little seasonal change. Weather is stable. Erosion and river work are happening but feel slow even on a human timescale.  
- **Human scale:** Structures are small and modest. “Urban” in the sense of organized dense(ish) settlement, not towers or empires.  
- **Technology:** Simple stone tools, some rare metal, no machines beyond basic wheelbarrows and shallow water wheels.

Your mindset should be: **“I’m stepping into a quiet, damp, slow stone garden where water and silence are the main things.”**

-------------------

## 2. Physical health & environmental dangers

### 2.1. Climate and body stress

- **Temperature:** Warm and humid, fairly constant day and night.  
- **Air:** Moist, heavy. “Ocean‑plateau memory” humidity.

**Risks:**

- **Overheating:** With constant warmth and humidity, you can get heat exhaustion or heatstroke without realizing it, because you’re not sweating effectively in the damp air.
- **Dehydration:** Paradoxically, you can underestimate how much you’re sweating because you may feel “already wet” from mist.  
- **Fatigue:** High humidity plus warm temps can drain you faster than you expect.

**Prepare by:**

- Bring **lightweight, breathable clothing layers**:  
  - Loose, long‑sleeve shirts that protect your skin but still breathe.  
  - Short sleeves for hard work but with long sleeves you can pull over.  
- **Moisture‑wicking underlayer** if you have access to synthetic tech;  
  **loose linen** or similar natural fabric if you’re constrained to lower tech.
- **Wide‑brimmed hat or a hooded cloak** to protect from mist and sun (when the clouds thin).
- A **bandana or cloth** to wipe sweat/condensation from your face.
- **Plan to drink small sips of water often**—even when you don’t “feel” like exercising.

### 2.2. Feet and terrain

The ground is:

- Tussocky, **mossy turf over peat**.  
- Often **soft, saturated soil** just under the moss.  
- Stone paths polished **smooth and wet** by water and feet.

**Risks:**

- **Slipping on wet stone.**  
- **Wet feet constantly:** bad if you’re prone to blisters or fungal infections.  
- Twisting an ankle on uneven or hidden stones.

**Prepare by:**

- **Footwear:**  
  - Closed shoes that can be wet permanently:  
    - Modern: waterproof trail shoes or sandals with non‑slip tread.  
    - Low-tech: sturdy leather sandals or well-made boots with hobnails/cleats, if possible.  
  - Extra attention to **grip**. Smooth soles are dangerous here.  
- **Socks:**  
  - Synthetic or quick‑dry, if you can. Wool is fine if you can dry it occasionally.  
  - Bring multiple pairs; change often when you can.  
- **Skin care:**  
  - Antifungal powder/cream if you’re prone to athlete’s foot or similar infections.  
  - Daily foot check and cleaning if you’re staying long.

### 2.3. Breathing: damp, moldy, siliceous?

There are no obvious air poisons described, but you should assume:

- **Possibility of mold spores** from constantly damp peat, moss, and building interiors.
- **Potential for dust/silica** if any stone quarrying is happening.

For an ordinary traveler:

- If you have asthma or allergies, **bring your inhaler and antihistamines**.
- If you have access to advanced tech, a **small mask or filter** for dusty quarry work or musty rooms is not absurd; you don’t need to wear it constantly, but have it.
- Practice **good breathing hygiene**—sleep on a slightly raised platform (if available), avoid sleeping directly on the ground where moisture and mold may be thick.

## 3. Clothing and gear

### 3.1. Everyday clothing

Aim for “always damp-proof” rather than “totally waterproof.”

- **Upper layers:**
  - Light, long-sleeve **overshirt or thin jacket** that sheds water but breathes.  
    - Think poncho or cape in low-tech versions; light rain jacket in higher-tech.
  - A hooded or hat-with-cape setup works well.
- **Lower layers:**
  - Sturdy trousers that can get **muddy and moss-stained**.
    - Tight weave, not thin shorts (unless in truly private enclosed interior).
- **Footwear:** see above; key is **non-slip** soles and tolerance to being constantly wet.
- **Extras:**  
  - Small **headlamp** or lantern for when night and mist both thicken.  
  - Spare pouch or bag for **wet socks and clothes** so you don’t mix everything together.

### 3.2. Staying “dry” (or at least not hypothermic)

You’ll probably always be slightly damp. Focus on:

- Staying **dry enough to stay warm**, rather than perfectly dry.
- Quick **change of base layer** when you’re inside for a while.

Bring:

- At least **one full set of dry clothes** stored in a waterproof bag or wrapped in oilcloth/wax.
- A **small blanket or thin travel sleeping mat** to insulate you from the stone; stone will wick heat from you even if the air is warm at night.

-------------------

## 4. Water, food, and medicine

### 4.1. Water

Good news: water is everywhere.

**But consider:**

- Even if “fresh” and crystal clear, **your microbes may not be local microbes**.
- **Their bodies are adapted to local water. You are not.**

**Prepare:**

- For **first 1–2 weeks**, treat water:
  - Modern: **water filter or purification tablets**.  
  - Low-tech: **boiling** as core method; even if cloudy mist, it’s better than nothing.
- After you’ve observed:
  - If locals commonly drink from some channels and not others, follow **their pattern**.
  - You might slowly “tease” your gut into accepting local water by mixing small amounts of local water with boiled or filtered.

Don’t assume “water everywhere” means “hurl yourself into any runoff channel”—respect the locals’ understanding of which flows are safest.

### 4.2. Food and your stomach

They don’t have huge animals to farm, so local diet:

- **Vegetable‑based**:
  - Leafy mats of thick, succulent plants,  
  - “Grassy” but short crops,  
  - Possibly stored roots and seeds, dried if possible.
- **Little or no meat** (since there are no animals).
- Probably **dense, moist fare** that keeps well in damp conditions.

Your **gut microbiome is from elsewhere**, so:

- **Go slow with new foods.** Eat small portions of unfamiliar dishes the first few days.
- Find **cooked foods** rather than raw or “room-temperature” offerings if you’re sensitive.
- Have **antidiarrheals, basic painkillers, and possibly electrolytes**:
  - Rehydration salts or powders you can mix into water if you get traveler’s diarrhea.

### 4.3. Longer term health

Bring:

- **Any prescription medications** plus small extras if you can, because there are no pharmacies.
- **Basic first aid kit**:
  - Bandages that can work **even when damp**.
  - Antiseptic ointment or powder.
  - Gauze pads.
  - Medical tape.

If you’re from a world where vaccines are common:

- Make sure your **tetanus and other routine inoculations** are up to date if possible.
- Even if there are no animals to transmit rabies or plague here, you’re still exposed to cuts from stone and metal tools.

## 5. Risk and safety: what can actually kill you

### 5.1. Falls

The main obvious danger here is **not violence or predators, but terrain and water**.

- **Edges of the plateau:** sharp drop where “world drops off into cloud sea.”
- **Wet stone paths** that can be slick.
- **River channels and spill steps** in terraces.

**Prepare by:**

- Avoid **walking near edges at night or in thick mist**; if you must, stay on the inside of the path.
- Ask **locals** where:
  - “Do you walk near the edges in mist?”  
  - “Where are the dangerous places?”  
    Their answers are likely to be straightforward; they know the water and stone.

### 5.2. Water and drowning

Most “rivers” sound slow and shallow, but:

- The **central pool in town** is deep, with strange rock formations.
- Streets and channels have **water constantly moving**, even if slow.

**Assume:**

- Don’t **splash around blindly** in channels or the central pool.
- If you slip and get hurt, even minor injuries can complicate long-term travel.

### 5.3. Tool hazards

- **Rare metal tools**: means they are physically valuable but also sharp and maintained.  
- **Stone chisels and hammers**: flying chips or mis-strikes can happen.

**Prepare:**

- If you help work in stone or channels:
  - **Eye protection** if you have access (even a simple visor, mask, or goggles).
  - Mind your **stance**: feet on stable ground, not on slick rock.

### 5.4. Monsters and humans?

The text does not suggest violent wildlife or humans. However, as a traveler:

- Don’t **assume you are invisible or harmless**.  
- You may be watched closely because of:
  - **Strange gear**,  
  - **Different body language**,  
  - **Lack of knowledge about how water flows are managed.**

**Assume politeness and safety, but maintain:**

- A modest **self-defense tool** if your world allows it (small knife, pepper spray, etc.), more for your own sense of security than expected need.
- **Trust your instincts:** if you feel uncomfortable in a narrow or isolated water channel, don’t push it.

## 6. Social and cultural preparation

### 6.1. How to approach locals

You are an outsider in a stable, small-scale, slow-changing world. They:

- Move **quietly**,  
- Speak **little**,  
- Listen deeply to the **water language**.

You should:

- **Start small:**  
  - Ask to help with small, non-critical tasks: carrying, cleaning, adjusting small stones in channels.
- **Observe first:**  
  - How do they treat water?  
    - Do they avoid stepping into certain flows?  
    - Do they wash their hands in certain channels but not drink from them?
- **Mirror their tone of voice:**  
  - Keep your voice low and calm;  
  - No shouting unless necessary (danger).

### 6.2. Building trust and showing respect

Key things:

- **Water and flow:**  
  - Do not alter water flow channels or stones on your own.  
  - Ask: “Is this okay to move?” before touching anything that looks “managed.”
- **Trees and markers:**  
  - Trees are rare and honored;  
  - Treat any tree as a **public, living artifact**.  
  - Do not cut branches, break shoots, or disturb anything around tree bases.
- **Houses and thresholds:**  
  - Enter only when invited clearly (in gesture, if not language).  
  - If you have mud, moss or dirt on your feet, try to leave that at the threshold.  
  - Observe if they remove shoes indoors.

### 6.3. Language and communication

They may *not* share your language.

**Prepare by:**

- Learning **very basic “water and stone” vocabulary** before you go:
  - “water,” “river,” “stone,” “rain,” “safe,” “danger,” “yes,” “no,” “please,” “thank you.”
- If you can’t learn in advance, then:
  - **Use gestures**: pointing, open palms, bowing slightly.  
  - **Draw diagrams** in wet mud or sand to show ideas:
    - Channels,  
    - Source and destination of water,  
    - Time of day (if sun position visible enough).
- **Bear in mind:**  
  - In a world with very few animals, their metaphors might be centered around water, stone, and plants rather than animals.

### 6.4. Personal and ethical boundaries

- **Don’t treat the locals like “extras” in your scenery.**  
  - You may be fascinated by the quietness and “unreality” of their animal-free world;  
  - To them it is just reality.
- Avoid **taking photos or drawing** people if it might make them uncomfortable;  
  - Ask if possible; or do sketches of stonework and water first.
- **Do not secretly “collect” anything:** stones, chips, vegetation samples, unless explicitly allowed.  
  - The world may be simple, but it is **not less valuable than yours**.

## 7. Mental and emotional preparation

### 7.1. Coping with the silence and absence of animals

You are going from a world of likely noise (birds, insects, engines, voices) to:

- A **deep soundscape of water** and **wind in grasses and leaves**,  
- No animal calls,  
- Sparse human speech.

This might:

- Bring you **peace**, or  
- Make you **slightly uneasy or even anxious** at first.

**Prepare by:**

- Spending **quiet time in water-rich environments** before you travel (if you can)—  
  - near riversides,  
  - under constant rain if possible,
  to accustom yourself to the sound.

- **Bringing simple comforts:**  
  - Small journal,  
  - A pencil (ink liquids may be a problem in damp).  
  - A simple musical instrument if you play one, but be aware that locals may not have instruments with loud, sharp sounds.

- **Accepting that being “alone with water” will be normal:**  
  - If you usually self-distract with noise, you may need to adjust.

### 7.2. Time and patience

This place has:

- **Very little seasonal changing**;  
- Weather that is steady:  
  - Soft clouds,  
  - Gentle mist or rain,  
  - Diffuse light.

For you, that can:

- Cause **disorientation of time**:  
  - Days blend together;  
  - “Clock” of your old world (heat changes in spring/summer, etc.) doesn’t apply.
- Or it can be **calming**, if you treat it like meditation.

**Prepare by:**

- Keeping a **simple physical calendar**:
  - Mark days with a small line on paper or stone,
  - Keep track of “waking days” since arrival.
- Finding **one small daily ritual**:  
  - Check the water level of a particular channel or terrace,
  - Watch how the same plant looks,
  - Sketch the same patch of terrace from day to day.

This helps ground you against time slipping.

### 7.3. Avoid “romanticizing” or “depressuring” yourself

This world:

- Is not under “grand threat” from what you see,
- Has not obviously “gone wrong” by having no animals or seasons.

To avoid:

- **Don’t project:**  
  - Don’t assume they dream of having “more animals” or your “varied seasons” unless they show such signs.
- **Don’t assume they are “simple” or “less evolved”:**
  - Their craftsmanship of water and stone and their “art of maintaining equilibrium” is likely complex, even if not materially impressive.

## 8. Practical daily “how to be”

### 8.1. Moving around

- **On the plateau:**  
  - Stay on visible paths or known routes, especially in thick mist or low cloud.  
  - Edges of the highland are sharp drop-offs; keep a distance when you can’t see clearly.
- **In town:**  
  - Follow the **main pathways of smoother stone**.
  - Don’t step into the shallow channels unless you see locals doing so clearly and repeatedly.
- **On the terraces:**  
  - **Do not walk on the retaining walls of the fields** unless locals show you that’s allowed.
  - Step **only where soil is visible in cultivation beds** if you need to cross the field.

### 8.2. Personal hygiene and water usage

Consider:

- Most visible **water is in use**: gutters, channels, terrace fields.
- **Bathing and laundry:**  
  - Ask where locals wash themselves and clothes.
    - Usually this will be downstream from the main settlement or on specific channels.
- **Toilet needs:**  
  - Ask explicitly where you should relieve yourself:
    - They may have a compost or “night soil” system connected to fields.
    - Do **not** simply pee into channels or terraces without being sure—that’s a major intrusion into their water art.

### 8.3. Night and safety checks

Before you sleep:

- Know your **safe path from bed to the door**:
  - Interior stone might be slick as well if condensation is common.
- Check **your roof leaks if you’re in a local home**:  
  - If you’re allowed to help, you might work with them to adjust channel stones or redirect a flow.
- **Light your route:**  
  - Even a small lamp or headlight helps avoid stepping into unseen channels.

## 9. What to bring: a concise checklist

Tailor to your level of tech, but here’s a core set:

**Clothing & Protection:**

- [ ] Lightweight long-sleeve shirts and trousers  
- [ ] Non-slip waterproof shoes or sandals  
- [ ] Extra socks (multiple pairs)  
- [ ] Wide-brimmed hat or hood  
- [ ] Cloth or bandana for face sweat/moisture  
- [ ] Thin waterproof layer (cape, rain jacket, etc.)

**Health & Safety:**

- [ ] Basic first aid kit  
- [ ] Water purification method (filter/tablets or portable container for boiling)
- [ ] Antidiarrheal and basic pain reliever  
- [ ] Any prescription meds + extra  
- [ ] Eye protection (if you’ll help with stone work)

**Tools & Useful Bits:**

- [ ] Knife or multitool (if allowed)  
- [ ] Strong, cord/rope (their ropes are plant-based; your own can be useful if different)
- [ ] Portable light source (headlamp or lantern if available)
- [ ] Dry bags or waxed cloth to keep things dry
- [ ] Small notebook and pencil  
- [ ] Calendar or way of marking days  
- [ ] Simple map-making materials (paper, pencil)

**Optional:**

- [ ] Small musical instrument or personal object of comfort  
- [ ] Sketching supplies if you like documenting  
- [ ] Tiny containers of ointments: antifungal, skin protection cream

You can **multiply** certain items like socks, underclothes, bandages, depending on how long you plan to stay.

## 10. How to “leave” better than you found

This is a world where:

- Equilibrium is delicate,
- People’s survival **depends on their detailed understanding of water**.

So you should:

- **Leave no obvious trash**:
  - Anything you bring with you that is not biodegradable or “stone-like,” carry out if you can.
- **Don’t introduce “improvements” without consent and testing:**
  - Even “better” waterproofing methods or new tools could be disruptive.
- **Share what you know carefully**:
  - If locals show interest in your world, explain:
    - Your animals,  
    - Your seasons,  
  - But avoid **“selling” your world as “better”**.
- **Offer help rather than change**:
  - Help repair a wall,  
  - Clear a channel,  
  - Learn from them how they “read” the water landscape.

-------------------

If you take away one thing from all this, let it be:

**This is a place where water is the first law, and quiet is the second law.  
Prepare to stay wet, to be slow, and to listen to the water’s constant language as you move through a world that is essentially stone remembering the ocean.**
