You are not going to stumble into this place by accident.  

The travel itself is the first filter. If you can’t endure days of walking upriver valleys, under a sky that never really brightens or darkens, into high air and thin sound, you will never reach the weeping plateau at all. Most people who feel “called” here will already know they are being asked for something more than a weekend escape.

So you don’t “visit” this city in the usual tourist sense. You prepare like you’re going to live in a place that is slowly etching itself into you.

---

## 0. Before you decide to go

### Understand why you want to come

Ask yourself:

- Why do I want to go to *this* specific place—a city of wet stone, high and quiet and half-hidden by clouds?
- Am I seeking to understand something, or escape something?
- Can I stand being slow, damp, and alone for long stretches?

If you’re looking for thrill or spectacle, you will likely be disappointed. This place shows its power in subtle shifts: faint drip patterns, condensation knitting on stone walls, a slight change of temperature in the air. The drama here is geological time and quiet moisture.

If you are seeking answers to big questions—about pacing, meaning, or your own pattern—then this city can be useful. But it answers in its own medium: water, stone, and time.

---

### Get the body ready

You are walking to a roof-of-the-world city where the air feels thin and sound hollows out. Your body needs to do a lot of the work.

**Basic fitness:**

- Build up to walking 6–8 hours a day with a light pack on uneven ground (if your world has such training).
- Daily practice in:
  - Lung capacity: simple breath holds, or slow rhythmic breathing.
  - Balance: standing on one foot, eyes open or closed.
  - Quiet core work: plank, gentle bridges, so your joints are not hammered by endless days of walking.

**Acclimate to altitude-like conditions:**

Even though the description emphasizes “strange air” rather than altitude sickness, you behave as if heading to a high mountain:

- Train at higher elevation if you can (or in simulated hypoxic rooms if you have access).
- Learn to notice your own breath:
  - Does your chest get tight?
  - Do you get light headaches when you push yourself?
- Practice going slower than you think you should need to.

Your ordinary human heart and lungs are not built to walk on a roof of the world with a dome of invisible pressure above and a heartbeat-like tremor beneath, but they can adapt better than you think.

---

### Prepare the mind and emotions

This is a quiet, stubbornly *slow* city. You will need to be mentally prepared for that.

**Practice:**

- Sitting still for 20–30 minutes at a time without doing anything.
- Listening to faint sounds in your environment:
  - drip in a nearby bathroom,
  - distant traffic,
  - wind outside the window.
- Noticing your own thoughts without getting swept up in them.

This city’s rhythm is measured by drip and cloud-movement. You’re not going to be “entertained” so much as invited into *observation*.

## 1. Clothing and gear: what to bring

### Clothing principle: moisture, not cold, is the main problem

The plateau is not intensely cold, but it is relentlessly damp:

- The air is saturated.
- Mist never rises high, but it is always there.
- Droplets form constantly on surfaces—including you.

Think **“layers that breathe and dry”**, rather than **“heavy insulation.”**

#### Inner layers

- **Base layer:** thin synthetic or moisture-wicking fabric long-sleeve top and long bottoms.
  - Avoid cotton; it stays wet and saps warmth.
  - Purpose: to pull moisture away from your skin and toward the outer layers where it can evaporate or go into the air.

- **Optional mid-layer:** light fleece or thin wool sweater.
  - Wearable in the outer chill of the heights, but can be rolled back or stashed when you’re walking between sheltered buildings.

#### Outer shell

You need **water-resistant** but not impenetrable:

- A waterproof-breathable jacket or poncho with a hood.
  - It’s not for rain so much as for *condensation* and *continuous mist*.
- Trousers that shed water rather than soak it.
- Something not completely waterproof is better than plastic; if moisture can’t breathe out at all, you’ll be sweating inside your shell.

“Waterproof” shoes or boots are useful, but nothing is fully proof here forever.

---

### Footwear

You are going to be on **wet stone**, textured and curved:

Bring **non-slip, low-profile soles**:

- Hiking shoes or boots with:
  - Good grip on smooth surfaces,
  - Not overly thick or heavy.
- Avoid soles that are too high; the city’s “ground” is a curved, living slab, and you need to feel it underfoot.

Bring **two pairs of socks**:

- One pair of thin liner socks  
- One pair of thicker hiking socks  

Purpose: prevent blisters and help avoid trench foot from constant moisture.

---

### Small essentials specifically for this world

You are not hiking to Volcano Traverse or Dessert City. You are going to a place of *slow stone* and *low mist* with subtle but real rules.

**Essential tools:**

- Thin **gloves**:
  - Not so thick they take away feeling;
  - Enough to protect your fingers from sharp stone edges when sitting or resting.
- **Head covering:** a simple cap or hood with a brim.
- **Spare cloth or scarf:**
  - To wipe moisture from your face,
  - To dry off surfaces before you sit or lean,
  - Sometimes used to gently pat excess moisture off lichen or stone basins if you want to study them.

**Optional tools:**

- Small notebook, pencil or waterproof pen:
  - To sketch channels,
  - To record sequences of drips and patterns without disturbing the city.
- **Candles or small light**:
  - Not for “lighting up the night” (which never fully darkens),
  - but for reading your notebook under shallow arches or in low chambers.
- A **simple compass**:
  - The city is described as having a central spiral and water always flowing toward the center.
  - Knowing direction helps you orient yourself toward the “center” where the mist is thickest.

**Do NOT bring:**

- Heavy electronics or devices that rely on precise time.
  - Time here behaves oddly; you might feel longer or shorter than days.
  - Such devices will confuse you more than help.
- Crunchy or noisy packaging.
  - This city rewards quiet; you will want to be as unobtrusive as possible.

## 2. Provisions and water

### Food and drink

The river in the gorge valley is described with living stones with “slitted mouths,” breathing in the silt. This water is not necessarily safe for you.

**General rules:**

- If you drink water from the river:
  - Treat it like it’s from another world:
    - Filter and boil or
    - Use purification tablets or drops if you can get them in your multiverse travels.
- In the city itself:
  - Water seeps **from the stone**.
  - The basins look pure, but they are infused with living minerals and possibly with organisms you don’t know.
- If you test a small amount of city basin water:
  - Taste it only *after* it’s been collected and exposed to air for a time,
  - Watch your body: lips, tongue, throat.
  - If you feel any odd numbness, warmth, or tingling, stop drinking immediately.

**Pack safe food:**

- Dried fruits,
- Nuts,
- Hard cheese or cured protein,
- Flatbreads or dense crackers that won’t mind damp air.

Everything you bring should be sealed tightly against moisture. The air itself will try to soften your food.

## 3. Safety and physical health

### Walking on wet stone

The ground is described as “calloused skin” rather than dead rock:

- The surface is textured with shallow waves and ridges.
- It is constantly wet from beading moisture and condensing air.
- It is subtly curved, like the surface of a vast bowl.

You must behave as if walking on **slippery basalt**.

**Practice at home:**

- Walk on wet tiles or concrete after rain.
- Notice how your shoes behave.
- Test intentional slips:
  - Learn to fall safely:
    - Let knees bend,
    - Relax body into the slide rather than locking up,
    - Tuck your head.

On the plateau:

- Take smaller steps than normal.
- Glide your feet rather than stomp.
- Look *ahead* of the stone in front of you, to see where the gradient and curve will be, rather than staring right at your toes.

---

### Temperature and humidity

Your main problems will be:

- **Chill from damp**, rather than cold from freezing air.
- **Possible fungal or skin irritations** from contact with wet surfaces and living organisms.

**Basic precautions:**

- Change your socks and base layer regularly:
  - Even if you don’t “feel” soaked, you’re accumulating moisture.
- Air out your clothes:
  - Lay them flat on stone where you can be sure they won’t slip into cracks or basins.
- Learn basic wound care:
  - Minor cuts and scrapes become more serious if constantly wet.
    - Keep small cuts clean and covered.
    - Avoid soaking your feet in unknown basins.

---

### Interaction with living organisms

There are **low blue lichen**, spiral-like mosses and translucent algae ribbons in the water basins.

Treat everything unknown as **potentially subtle:**

- Avoid touching any organism with open cuts on your body.
- Do not taste any unknown lichen or nodules of light simply because they look luminous.
- If you *must* touch:
  - Use fingertips or cloth,
  - Observe carefully,
  - Avoid disturbing patterns or pulling growth from stone.

---

### Fauna and unseen beings

You may encounter:

- **Stone-treaders:** small slate-colored creatures like crab-crustaceans.
  - Very slow moving.
  - Not obviously aggressive.
- **Water-singers:** hidden in deep cracks and caves.
  - Faint ripple in basins, tones echoing through stone.
- **Large shadows above:** wingtips or hints of serpentine bodies in the mist clouds.
- **Occasional footprints:** broader than human, barefoot-like impressions near basins.

Your **role** is not to conquer or interact like a hero in a story. You are here as *guest*.

**Basic safety:**

- Do not corner or step on stone-treaders.
- Do not throw stones or objects into the basins, particularly those where ripples appear from below.
- If you see unusual footprints, you are not obligated to follow them.
  - This is a place where “curiosity” and “respect” must be kept in balance.

---

### Risk of getting lost and time confusion

Time behaves strangely:

- It feels longer than a day,
- Sleeps and wakes can confuse you.

Also:

- Sound doesn’t travel far horizontally, but into the stone very easily.
- Distances are softened by mist and low light.

You **can** get lost in the city of low basins and buildings. The descriptions strongly imply that it *guides* those who cooperate with its flow.

## 4. How to enter the city itself

### Before you step onto the plateau

When you finally climb out of the last valley and the world “opens up abruptly” onto the roof of the world:

- Stop **before** you first step onto the slab.
- Notice:
  - The curve of the ground,
  - The faint tremor of the stone,
  - The low mist ceiling.

**Observe before walking:**

- Sit down with your back to the slope of the valley you just climbed.
- Listen:
  - To the drip-sounds,
  - To the wind like pages being turned,
  - To your own heartbeat and whether you can feel it, faintly, through the stone.

**Anchor your awareness:**

- Take a moment of deliberate closure:
  - Say (to yourself or aloud) that you are entering this place *not* to take but to learn.
  - If you have religious or personal symbols of safe travel, use your customary practice in a low voice. This place doesn’t demand “beliefs” but it appreciates clarity of intention.

---

### First step

When you first step onto the weeping heights:

- Go barefoot for a short while **if** you feel safe doing so:
  - The stone is described as “cool but not cold,” like winter skin.
  - It’s trembling faintly, like a distant heartbeat.
- Acknowledge that you are now walking atop *something* that is something like a body, not inert rock.

## 5. How to behave inside the city

### General principles of the traveler

1. **Move slowly**
   - The city is inscribed by water, guided by patience.
   - The faster you move, the more you disrupt yourself and your perception of this place.

2. **Follow water**
   - Channels and basins and the slight movement of water are the “roads” and “signposts.”
   - Water *always* flows toward the center of the plateau.
   - If you feel disoriented, you can:
     - Choose a small basin,
     - Watch a droplet travel,
     - And align your direction with the flow of water.

3. **Stay near basins**
   - Footprints of “the unseen ones” appear only between basins.
   - Dangerous open ground is not described but implied:
     - The center of life is *around* these basins; stray away from them too long and you drift from context.

4. **Respect the silence**
   - Speak if you must, but:
     - Keep your voice low,
     - Avoid shouting or singing loudly,
     - Let your sounds become part of the drip and wind rather than override them.
   - If you’re with others:
     - Use words sparingly,
     - Learn to communicate by simply pointing and gesturing.

---

### How to rest and sleep

You will not find buildings with rooms as you know them.

The description mentions:

- Hollows at the base of buildings—“openings without doors or thresholds.”
- Low benches or curved surfaces.

**Choose a place to sleep that:**

- Is near a basin but not right *in* the main flow of a channel.
- Allows you to see a bit of sky:
  - The sky is “low and heavy,” but it still defines your sense of day.
- Avoids the deepest, thickest mist of the center:
  - The center is described as having “mistier, thicker air.”
  - Begin near the edges of the city to get used to its breathing.

**Preparing your bed:**

- Use your pack or folded clothes as padding between you and the stone slab.
  - The stone itself is not sharp, but it is damp and cool.
- Sleep with your feet slightly lower than your body if you’re lying in a curved depression:
  - This matches your body to the “great bowl” shape of the plateau.

**Before you sleep:**

- Listen:
  - To the drip pattern,
  - To the faint hiss of condensation on stone walls.
- You might notice:
  - The “slow concert” of city-breathing.
- Do not work yourself into a fear about it:
  - This is not a horror movie;
  - This is simply *another way of being alive*.

---

### How to interact with the buildings

The buildings are not just houses or shelters:

- They are *formations* of the same slab as the ground beneath,
- Rounded, like “smooth bones breaking the surface of skin.”
- Their surfaces are covered in water-carved script.

When you approach a building:

- **Notice the script from a distance first**
  - Look at how lines of text curve around the base, like channels of water.
- **Before touching**
  - Sit or kneel near the wall,
  - Watch how condensation traces the script over time.
    - Sometimes figures of moisture appear and then fade away on the stone surface.

If you touch the wall:

- Do not press or scratch:
  - The stone is being etched by patience;
  - do not ruin that with one impulsive gesture.
- Use your flat palm rather than fingertips,
  - Feel the cool, living tremor of the slab.
- Stay very still for at least the time it takes for:
  - A few droplets to form along the script,
  - or one to fall into a basin nearby.

**Do not:**

驱赶或打扰那些stone-treaders沿着墙壁阅读。
- They are tracing the script with their many claws as if reading.
- You are not here to interrupt them;
- Observe as if reading over their shoulders.

---

### How to understand the city “responding”

You are described sensing that:

- Flows of water around your feet may adjust,
- The basin around your feet “steers its flows slightly away from your shadow.”

The city is not hostile or enchanted in the way of fictions that seize you. It simply *resets* around your presence.

**Practical ways to interact with this:**

- Sit near a basin and:
  - Watch your own footprint or shadow position relative to the water.
- Notice:
  - Does condensation on your jacket mirror the patterns of script nearby?
    - If you hang your jacket near a wall, you might see droplets forming on it in faint lines.
    - This is not a sign for you to interpret in words.
    - It is more like the city saying: *I see you.*

**Remain humble:**

- Do not assume you are being “chosen” or given special power.
- You are being *observed* by the stone and water, just as you observe them.
  - That is enough.

## 6. Mental hygiene on the plateau

### Time and sense of “self”

You may lose simple sense of time:

- Sleeps don’t align neatly with “sunset” or “sunrise.”
- The sky only shifts through shades of slate and steel, never full bright or dark.

You will be tempted to:

- Doubt yourself (“How long have I been here?”),
- Or worry about home (“Are they still there?”).

Practical **time-keeping**:

- Choose **one** simple method and stick to it:
  - Counting:
    - “sleeps”
    - or meals (if you maintain regular meals),
    - or the number of times you notice a major drip pattern change at a particular basin.
- Do not try to force precise hours and minutes; this world will not cooperate.

---

### Attention and bias

You will see:

- Faint outlines of figures on stone walls,
- Patterns of ripples appearing from “nowhere” in basins,
- Chilled water-sounds from deep below.

**Basic rule:**

- Let *everything* be true and not immediately explained:
  - It is true that the condensation traces the shape of the script.
  - It is true that ripples appear from unseen centers.
  - It is true that stone-treaders gather as if in reading groups.

Do not rush to label:

- “Spiritual,”
- “Scientific,”
- “Real,” or
- “Not real.”

This is a **different** way of being real. You adapt to it by not *classifying* so much as *feeling*.

---

### Loneliness

If you are alone here:

- It may become tempting to interpret:
  - Every drip pattern as a message,
  - Every footprint as a ghost of your own future.
- Remember:
  - You are not necessarily the center of this story.
- To keep yourself sane:
  - Speak softly to yourself:
    - Name your sensations:
      - “This is my breath.”
      - “This is the drip of water.”
      - “This is the distant tremor of the stone.”
    - This keeps your mind from spiralling into stories that separate you from reality.

## 7. Practical list of “do”s and “don’t”s

**Do:**

- Bring many layers of clothing that can breathe and shed moisture.
- Bring shoes with soles that grip wet stone.
- Bring food and secure it from damp.
- Treat all water as suspicious until you test small amounts.
- Walk slowly, test each move on slippery slabs.
- Sit near basins and learn to read the water channels.
- Watch stone-treaders from afar and quietly.
- Listen to the drip patterns and let yourself be still.
- Respect your own physical limits: altitude-like air, wet chill, and time confusion.
- Keep a simple notebook and pencil for your own sanity and memory.

**Don’t:**

- Bring noisy equipment or devices that demand regular timekeeping.
- Drink from basins or river without testing or treating.
- Disturb lichen, mosses, or algae except with gentle touch.
- Step on or attempt to catch stone-treaders.
- Shout, sing loudly, or play music that would overwhelm the city’s quiet.
- Try to “interpret” the water script as if you were meant to decode secret messages.
- Push yourself to the center of the city in your first days; allow the place to invite you.
- Pretend that your being here is necessarily more important than the stone-treader perching on a basin’s edge.

## 8. Leaving the City

When it is time to leave, you will likely feel:

- A pull of curiosity toward the unseen center:
  - A thicker mist of deeper air,
  - A place where all channels of drip-water seem to be drawn.
- A pull of home:
  - The way you came,
  - The lower valleys where the sky is more familiar.

**Pay attention to which pull you feel:**

- If the center beckons strongly:
  - You may decide to go deeper.
    - Prepare:
      - Make sure you are warm enough,
      - Have food enough,
      - Have mentally accepted that you may lose track of time entirely.
- If the pull of home is stronger:
  - You may decide to return another time.

If you choose to leave:

- Choose **one** recognizable basin near your entry point:
  - Note:
    - its pattern of channels,
    - the approximate position of the nearest buildings,
    - the way the water flows into one specific crack.
- When you return to that basin:
  - Compare:
    - Has the script deepened slightly?
    - Has the condensation pattern changed?
- These changes are not messages to you, but they *are* a record.
    - This city records your comings and goings in its own medium.

Before you leave the city entirely:

- Stand at the edge of the plateau where it begins to slope down into the valleys.
- Turn back:
  - Look at the city from a distance:
    - See if you can now detect the faint spiral form of the buildings,
    - the convergence of water channels toward the misty center.
- Say a simple, internal farewell:
  - You may not know the local language;
    - Use your own words:
      - “Thank you for letting me stand here.”
    - The city does not need your praise.
    - But *you* need to remember your own place in this.

---

### Final return and “aftercare”

When you leave the plateau and return to lower valleys:

- You may be surprised:
  - How bright the sky is,
  - How sharp sound is,
  - How quickly your clothes dry out.
- You may feel:
  - A slight sense of *missing* the slow drip concert.
    - This is not an attachment you need to break.
    - It is more like you’ve made yourself a friend who is far away.

**At home:**

- Do not force yourself to explain this place fully:
  - It is built to be etched, not to be photographed.
  - It is understood by being *slowly* understood.
- You may keep:
  - A small bowl or basin of water:
    - Watch condensation patterns,
    - Use it to remember your time on the roof of the world.
- You may find:
  - Your steps have become a little more patient,
  - Your time a little less frantic,
  - The way you listen is changed.

---

If you ever choose to return to the weeping heights, you will not be coming back as a stranger. The city will recognize your wet footprints on its stone, and your breath will lie quietly between the slow drip of water and the deep heartbeats of the world beneath you.
