To visit this room is not like visiting a place. It’s like entering a body that’s not yours but is entirely alive.

If you’re an ordinary human traveler with the means to get between universes but not to change your own physiology, you must treat this trip as something between a deep scientific expedition and a sensory ordeal. You’re stepping into a low‑water, dry‑static, high‑touch ecosystem where “you are not alone” is an understatement.

Below is a practical guide, from mindset to clothing to what to do with your sanity.

---

## 1. Understand what you’re entering

### A. The core facts of the room

- Size: roughly 3 × 3 meters, floor about half a step below ground level.
- Shape: sunken concrete cube, open to the sky above (but the sky is not visible).
- Life: the floor and most of the volume up to your ankle–shin height is filled with “thread‑workers.”
- Structure: The room’s actual “structure” is mostly made of threads:
  - “Floor” is a dense carpet of threads.
  - “Ceiling” is a lattice of threads spanning the old ceiling height.
  - Doorway is blocked by a coral‑like column of hardened threads.
  - Window opens onto more of the same thread-world, not onto clear outside.

- Key physical facts:
  - Very dry: static-electricity dry, chapped‑skin dry.
  - No free water anywhere.
  - Static and dry air currents are the main energy source—not sunlight.
  - Thread‑workers are not carbon-based like you; they’re more like mineral-colloid filaments with organic films.

### B. The “intimacy” of the place

This is not a place you can observe without being part of it. Movement anywhere in the room is:

- Through thousands of tiny bodies.
- Across layered, living material.
- Ambiguous between “walking on floor” and “being held by the floor.”

You will not have clean separation between yourself and the environment. This will be psychologically intense if you’re not prepared for it.

## 2. Before you go: physical and mental preparation

### A. Mental conditioning

1. **Expect constant touch sensation, not pain**
   - There is nothing described as inherently painful or aggressive.
   - But you’ll feel:
     - Constant pressure and light movement against any exposed skin.
     - The sensation of being “bathed in fine threads”—like walking through thick hanging blankets or spider webs, but living.
   - If you dislike being brushed or touched continuously, you will need to prepare:
     - Gradual exposure therapy to light textures all over your body: soft cloths, brushes, etc.
     - Practice staying still with “buzzing” background sensations—fans, air currents, white noise.

2. **Expect psychological “crowding”**
   - Even though it’s a small room, you will feel surrounded in a way that isn’t spatial as much as numerical.
   - Remind yourself:
     - The room’s living system is not hostile to you.
     - It is old, slow, and indifferent to your presence as a person; it will adjust to you the way it adjusts to falling dust.
     - Your “threat” to the system is on the level of “footsteps on moss.”

3. **Expect a time distortion experience**
   - The room moves on timescales of hours to days to months.
   - To you, it can feel like:
     - Watching something very slow; if you’re not careful, you can fall into a sort of trance or frustration.
   - Prepare:
     - Practice meditation or observation tasks that train you to see value in slow change.
     - Bring explicit tasks (see below) so you’re not just trapped in your own boredom.

### B. Physical health check

Because you’re entering a dry, static, low-moisture environment:

1. **Ensure your skin and mucous membranes are healthy**
   - No open eczema, sores, or big cracks on lips or skin.
   - If you’re prone to dry skin, get that treated before you go.

2. **Check respiratory system**
   - You’ll breathe “air” thick with fine fibers and “static dust.”
   - A person with asthma or dust allergies shouldn’t go here without serious medical support and possibly a better filtration system.

3. **Consider hydration baseline**
   - Start your trip very well hydrated.
   - You’ll lose moisture to the dry environment more than usual, even if you’re sealed in clothing.

## 3. Equipment and clothing

### A. Protective suit

You’re not dealing with vacuum or poison gas, but with “living texture” and subtle dryness. I’d recommend gear modeled on a cleanroom suit plus dust-protection gear:

1. **Full-body suit**
   - Material:
     - Strong, smooth synthetic fabric that doesn’t easily trap lint/threads.
     - Anti-static treatment if possible.
   - Design:
     - One-piece suit with hood attached.
     - No exposed zips or pockets that tiny fibers can get into.
     - Gathered wrists and ankles with elastic seals or boots integrated, so no threads can get inside your clothing.

2. **Gloves**
   - Something:
     - Thin enough to feel details but tough enough to protect your skin.
     - Ideally smooth, not “grip‑patterned” to avoid snagging threads.
     - Long enough to overlap the suit sleeves.

3. **Boots or overboots**
   - Sole:
     - Soft enough to not damage the floor more than necessary, but still stable.
     - A textured but not overly sharp tread pattern.
   - Your bootprints will be:
     - Valleys of pressure “erosion” in the thread-world.
     - The workers will migrate back into them within seconds to minutes.
   - Expect to feel:
     - The floor depress under you, then “push back” as the threads rearrange.

4. **Face and eye protection**
   - A sealed visor or mask that:
     - Protects eyes from fibers and static dust.
     - Doesn’t fog easily (you need to see clearly to understand).
   - Don’t rely on open goggles or cloth mask alone:
     - Particles can be fine enough that you’ll breathe them if you’re not fully sealed.

5. **Optional nose filter / respirator**
   - If you’re not in full breathing mask:
     - A simple particulate filter rated to PM2.5 or better.
   - If you are sensitive:
     - Consider full respirator; but keep in mind that changes your experience of the place.

### B. Environmental gear

1. **Hydration pack with internal supply**
   - A sealed hydration bladder that you can sip from without opening:
     - Ideally tube routed to inside your mask/suit.
   - You’re not going to find any drinking water in the environment.
   - Don’t spill: water could be shock to the system here.

2. **Electrostatic precautions**
   - You won’t be handling high voltage, but:
   - Antistatic wrist strap or foot strap:
     - Use to ground yourself gently to some reference point if you have to touch sensitive equipment.
   - Avoid:
     - Synthetic materials that generate big static discharges if possible.

3. **Lighting**
   - The room has light “from above,” but dim:
   - Bring:
     - Headlamp or handheld lamp with low, medium, and high settings.
   - Use:
     - Low to adapt to the room’s natural light and avoid disturbing its behavior.
     - Higher to examine details like the walls, floor texture, or rings.

### C. Scientific tools (if you want to understand, not just survive)

1. **Magnification / lens**
   - A small magnifying glass or clip-on lens for your visor to see:
     - Segmentation of the threads.
     - Ring of “mouths” on the swarm columns.
     - Surface of the cradle rings around the iron anchors.

2. **Sample containers**
   - Very small sealed containers (if you are careful to avoid abusing the place):
     - To collect a few tiny threads from the outermost layer.
   - Don’t:
     - Tear out massive clumps or “hunt for specimens.”
   - You’re legitimately curious; not there to be a collector at the cost of the system.

3. **Notebook and pencil**
   - Digital devices might be affected by static or weird physics:
   - Paper:
     - Much better, unless the humidity-difference attacks it.
   - Write:
     - Timestamp notes.
     - Sketches of the ceiling lattice patterns.
     - Changes of the swarm states.

## 4. Entering the room

### A. Getting into position

If you can be transported directly to the center of the room:

- You will appear over the thread floor.
- Expect your feet to immediately:
  - Sink into what feels like a deep carpet of living silk, but far more textured.
  - Feel the surface of threads moving around your ankles.

If you must enter from above:

- You can be transported to what remains of the “floor of the factory floor,” above this room.
- Care:
  - Don’t break ceiling structure if you can avoid it.
  - Explore the edge of the ceiling hole, looking for where the lattice is thinner or less developed.
  - Try to enter through space between the threads, not through the strongest lattices.

As you pass through the ceiling lattice:

- Threads will:
  - Deform around you.
  - Press against you but not attack you.
- You might feel:
  - A strange combination of resistance and yielding—like dense, very fine curtains.

### B. Movement within the room

When you place your foot on the floor:

- The floor depresses slightly, then:
  - “Yields” like moss underfoot.
  - You feel many threads sliding against each other.
- After you lift your foot:
  - Crawlers move back into the depression.
  - The pattern reknits itself.

Remember:

- Try to step slowly:
  - Each step is a small “event” in this room’s quiet.
  - Too rapid walking might be felt like a storm over eons of relative calm.

### C. The first minutes of sensation adjustment

1. **Vision**
   - The room will look:
     - Initially blurry because of the density of hanging threads.
   - Let your eyes adjust:
     - Ceiling: lattice hanging above.
     - Corners: dense, packed thread-work on shelves.
   - You might not perceive individual “workers” at first:
     - They appear as noise, until your eyes resolve them.

2. **Sound**
   - Background:
     - A continuous low hiss, like rain on dry leaves.
   - Allow:
     - Ten minutes of silence to really hear the structure of the sound:
       - Crawling, swarming, wind flutes through formed lattice.
       - Occasional soft “thump” of a falling ceiling section.

3. **Touch**
   - Even if fully sealed in suit:
     - You feel pressure of the threads against you.
   - If not sealed:
     - Prepare for a shivering sensation as thousands of light contacts move across your skin.
   - Avoid:
     - Panicking and flailing; that’s just confusing yourself and the system.
     - Generally, the system is not reactive to aggression in kind.

### D. Room “laws” to behave by

1. **Move slowly and predictably, not the same way each time**
   - Don’t:
     - Overstep the same exact spot too often; you’re changing the balance of motion.
   - Do:
     - Vary your path slightly.
     - Touch the walls with a glove for support if needed, lightly.

2. **Treat the swarming columns with respect**
   - When columns form:
     - Their “side” ring of mouths align toward you or something else.
     - That is not targeting you.
     - It is the room monitoring your presence as a new mass of interest.
   - Don’t:
     - Grab, wave, or try to break the columns.
   - Do:
     - Pause your movement for a moment while they “adjust” to your presence.

3. **Be mindful of ceiling collapse**
   - Every time a section of the ceiling lattice falls:
     - You feel a deep, muffled thump.
     - The floor where you stand might shake slightly.
   - If you’re directly under it:
     - Move gently aside if you can.
     - You’re not in danger of being crushed by heavy weight, but:
       - Being covered in thick clumps will be uncomfortable and overwhelming.

## 5. Investigating and interacting with the room (if you want)

### A. Understanding the different states

1. **Dormant threads**
   If you want to feel them:

   - Kneel or sit down (if you can psychologically tolerate contact).
   - With gloved hand:
     - Run fingers over the surface.
   - You should feel:
     - Stretch, not snap, when pulled gently.
     - Faint dry crackle when rolled between fingers.
   - If you have a lens:
     - Look at the segmentations of the thread.
     - Note outer vs inner layers: darker and older near the floor.

2. **Active crawlers**
   - Sit still and watch:
     - Let your eyes resolve the pattern of moving threads.
   - You might notice:
     - Temporary “paths” where crawler density is higher.
     - Edge of your footprint becoming darker before it brightens again.

3. **Swarm-formers**
   - Wait:
     - Over time, you’ll see the motion of the room suddenly change.
   - Signs:
     - Texture of the floor becomes more organized.
     - Columns of workers rise from the floor to hang from the ceiling lattice.
   - When you see:
     - A column forming with a ring-like “face”:
       - That’s the swarm-formers’ alignment of attention.
       - They’re directing the group’s sensing to a point in space.

### B. Observing generation around the iron rings

The three rusted iron rings in the floor are the centers of reproduction:

1. **Before a discharge**
   - Iron rings are:
     - Flaked with oxide.
     - Surrounded by a “clear” loop where pressure from the swarm has been particularly focused.
   - Note:
     - The pattern of the workers around the ring—maybe sketch it.

2. **During a discharge**
   - The change of the room:
     - Tactile pressure on your skin intensifies slightly.
     - Sound of the room deepens by a semitone or so.
   - You might:
     - See faint sparks of bioluminescence along the columns.
     - Feel the “floor push up” as threads move under you.

3. **After a discharge**
   - Around each ring:
     - Swarm-formers stream in columns.
     - The leading individuals wrap around the iron ring.
   - Over time:
     - The ring looks more covered.
     - Eventually, what appears to be an embedded circle of smooth cream-colored material “over” the iron ring.
   - If you stay:
     - Many days or weeks:
       - The floor becomes more active around the ring.
       - “New” individuals appear as rough texture of the floor near the ring, before being integrated into the swarm.

### C. Crossing the threshold to “outside”

If you want to try to leave the room into the rest of the thread-world:

1. **The doorway column**
   - The doorway column is not solid stone:
     - It looks like coral, but it is actually a hard-looking but fragile accumulation of threads.
   - Approach:
     - Place one hand on the column carefully.
     - Press gently.
   - You should feel:
     - Yielding and deformation around you.
     - Resistance from the pressure of the structure behind it.

2. **If you decide to pass through**
   - Use:
     - Both arms to part or press the column.
   - Move:
     - Slowly, like parting curtains.
   - You will feel:
     - The sensation of being enveloped.
     - Threads pressing against your body.
   - Expect:
     - The texture to be overwhelming if you’re not well suited.
   - If you panic:
     - Stop moving.
     - Breathe slowly.
     - Remind yourself that nothing is trying to hurt you.

3. **On the other side of the doorway**
   - You will likely find:
     - Another corridor or room similarly filled with threads.
   - You may not find:
     - Clear sky, vegetation, or normal world—you’re still embedded in the thread-world.

### D. The window

- Look out the window:
  - You will see:
    - More threads.
    - Possibly the faint outlines of more brick or concrete ruins.
  - That is not:
    - “Outside” in the sense of sky and wind.
  - It is:
    - A continuation of the same thread-world.

## 6. Managing your own body

### A. Dehydration & dryness

1. **Lips and skin**
   - Even if sealed:
     - Your lips in particular will be vulnerable to chapping.
   - Bring:
     - Sealed lip balm inside your mask/suit if you can.
   - Do not:
     - Lick your lips; the dry air can strip moisture quickly.

2. **Drinking**
   - Use your hydration pack:
     - Drink small sips regularly.
   - Don’t:
     - Loosen your suit anywhere to drink: if fibers are in the air, they’ll enter small gaps.

3. **After you return**
   - Expect:
     - Very dry skin and possibly mucous membranes.
   - Rehydrate slowly:
     - Water, electrolytes.
     - Skin moisturizer if your skin is not too sensitive.

### B. Fatigue and sensory overload

1. **Time limit your visit**
   - Even if you love the place:
     - Set a clear maximum time before you go:
       - Maybe start with 1–2 hours, then longer if you tolerate it.
   - You’re not built:
     - To be surrounded by that texture endlessly.

2. **Resting inside the room**
   - If you sit:
     - Find a corner where the thread shelf is not too fragile.
     - The “floor” will reshape under you:
       - You might end up sitting slightly lower before you leave.
   - Listen:
     - The soundscape of the room is actually rhythmic if you accept it.
   - Don’t:
     - Expect to sleep here unless you’ve very accustomed to sensory overload.

### C. Mental health precautions

You might experience:

1. **Claustrophobia**
   - Even though ceiling open:
     - The feeling of being surrounded by billions of tiny bodies can be claustrophobic.
   - Counter:
     - Keep your gaze focused on specific points:
       - One corner of the ceiling lattice.
       - One of the rings.
       - The window frame.
     - Avoid staring at the floor texture if it feels “too alive.”

2. **Existential anxiety**
   - This ecology:
     - No water, no plant, no “outside” that you recognize easily.
     - The cycles of death and rebirth are slow and obvious.
   - Accept:
     - It’s not a model of decay; it’s a model of time and structure.
     - Your presence is just one more cycle of the room.

3. **If you feel panic coming on**
   - Stop:
     - Stand still.
     - Fix your gaze on a single stable point (e.g., the window).
   - Breathe:
     - Slowly in, slowly out; 4–6 seconds.
   - Decide:
     - You either leave the room or embrace the feeling of “being part of the floor” temporarily.
   - Remember:
     - The thread-workers are not reacting to you as an enemy.
     - You’re not being trapped by the room; it’s just keeping itself together.

## 7. Ethics and considerations of being “alien” here

### A. Your impact on the ecosystem

1. **You are not part of their energy system**
   - You:
     - Don’t share their chemistry.
   - They:
     - Do not feed on you or “use” you in a way you can understand.
   - Your:
     - Sweat, flakes, breath:
       - Are probably meaningless to them except as new mass and texture.

2. **Tread lightly**
   - Don’t:
     - Kick or stomp the floor.
     - Tear the ceiling for speed.
     - Try to excavate the floor blindly.

3. **If you take samples**
   - Take:
     - Only outermost, surface layer threads.
   - Try:
     - To be from a corner where the structure seems dense.
   - Don’t:
     - Dig into the deep layers where older workers are.
     - Break the porcelain cradle around the rings unless you’re very intent and even then, be gentle.

### B. Recording the visit

1. **You might be the only person who ever connects this place to a world of water and sky**
   - Take notes:
     - On the macroscopic patterns of the lattice.
     - On the timing of the swarm-formations.
     - On the changes around the rings.
   - Sketch:
     - The floor pattern.
     - The column of the doorway.
     - The window view.

2. **Think of yourself as a lens**
   - Not:
     - A conqueror or colonizer.
   - But:
     - Someone moving between two very different definitions of “world.”

## 8. Leaving the room and aftercare

### A. Getting out safely

1. **Use the same way you came in, if possible**
   - Exiting through:
     - The ceiling: gently push through the ceiling lattice and allow yourself to be lifted out.
     - The doorway: part the column slowly and be ready to re-enter your world.

2. **Expect resistance to detachment**
   - The threads will:
     - Cling to you as you leave.
   - You might feel:
     - A temporary “reluctance” of the room to let go of your mass.
   - It’s not:
     - Personal.
   - It’s:
     - The texture’s answer to a change in geometry.

### B. After you return “home”

1. **Clean your equipment**
   - Remove:
     - Surface threads; any collected dust.
   - Check:
     - If you managed to bring any dormant threads into your world:
       - They might not be able to survive in your humidity.
       - Keep them isolated if you’re not sure.

2. **Check your body**
   - Skin:
     - Any redness or itching from prolonged contact.
     - Any particles stuck in pores or hairline.
   - Respiratory:
     - If you inhaled any fibers:
       - Watch for symptoms—better yet, have checked by a professional if that’s possible.

3. **Processing the experience**
   - You might have witnessed:
     - The birth of new workers from etched rings.
     - A collapse of the ceiling lattice.
   - Reflect:
     - On the slow movement of life in this place.
   - Write:
     - Your emotional responses as well as your observations.
   - Consider what:
     - The “room of thread-workers” tells you about time:
       - That’s not linear, but cyclic, and always redistributed.
