To visit the Deep Canning of B‑73 as an ordinary human multiverse traveler is to accept a simple fact: you are going *into* a machine that was never meant for you.  

The designers had plants in mind, and water, and pressure gradients.  
You are the variable they forgot to constrain.

If you prepare well, this place becomes a cathedral of slow growth.  
If you don’t, it becomes a humid, mildly toxic, subtly dangerous aquarium you can’t leave.

Below are the things to understand and arrange *before* you enter, and how to behave while you’re inside.

---

## 0. Before You Commit: Check Yourself Against This Place

### Who should *not* go

- Respiratory sensitivity: chronic bronchitis, asthma that flares with humidity, mold allergies, emphysema, etc.  
  The air is a thick, warm, wet blanket. If you’ve ever been miserable in tropical humidity or in damp conservatories, you’ll hate it here on *day one*.
- Joint and skin issues:  
  - Rheumatic joints that don’t like warm damp  
  - Fungal skin infections that bloom with occlusive moisture  
- Claustrophobia and strong thalassophobia:  
  You *know* there is ocean, very deep, above your head. You can’t see it, but you feel it. If you have panic in submerged pressure hulls or “sunken submarine” scenarios, this may not be the trip for you.
- Acute need for “something happening”:  
  This is a slow chamber. Your brain is used to constant change; here, almost nothing *visible* happens on a timescale of hours. If you need novelty or stimulation to feel stable, the Deep Canning can stretch your mind like taffy.

### Who should go (if you still want to)

- People who can feel a quiet joy in controlled, industrial-scale biology.  
- Anyone who finds “weeping pipes” and “patience of plants” soothing rather than maddening.  
- Observers, not adventurers: those who enjoy measuring small, subtle changes over time.

## 1. Conceptual Prep: Understand What You Are Walking Into

### Structure of the world

- You are in (or under) an ocean, on top of a submerged seamount.
- The Deep Canning is a high-pressure vault: *a dome that is actually a hull*.  
  The world above you is full ocean; the world below you is geothermal vents and machinery.
- You are *inside* the water cycle:  
  - water in the tanks  
  - water under the floor grating  
  - water in the air  
  - water in your lungs, every breath you take.
- Everything is engineered to be *just this side of comfortable* for a human, but not designed *for* humans.  
  Expect small, cumulative edge cases: skin prunes, mild dehydration headaches, salt mouth, etc.

## 2. Tools & Equipment to Bring

### 2.1. Basic survival kit

- **Secure tether to home / your hub world**:  
  Ensure you *can* leave. The Deep Canning is not a place you want to be stuck in due to a broken portal device.
- **Durable, waterproof data recorder**:  
  - Camera (with excellent humidity sealing and anti-condensation optics).
  - Small notebook with *synthetic* paper that doesn’t curl or dissolve under humidity.
- **Compact timepiece**:
  - Mechanical or sealed electronic device with *two* modes:
    - local timekeeping  
    - elapsed time (since entry)
- **Personal locator beacon**:  
  If something goes wrong—structure failure, health issue—you need a way to signal your multiverse hub or rescue team *without* relying on local infrastructure.

### 2.2. Environmental protection

- **Breathing comfort**:  
  - Light mask/respirator with replaceable cartridges suitable for humid, salt-aerosol air and mild trace gases.
- **Skin & clothing**:  
  - Non-synthetic base layer (moisture-wicking but not clingy).  
  - Overlayer: thin, waterproof, *vented* rain jacket or lab coat.  
  - Feet: closed-toe shoes with *non-slip* soles. The floor is gratings and condensation; wet metal + wet algae = fall risk.
  - Gloves: thin, waterproof, for touching tank walls, algae, or lower-level machinery.
- **Salt & humidity protection**:  
  - Rehydration salts or electrolyte tablets for your water bottle.
  - Small towel of fast-drying material.  
  - Moisture barrier cream for skin prone to maceration from constant humidity.
- **Eye comfort**:  
  - Sealed goggles or wraparound glasses with anti-fog coating.
  - Saline solution eye drops for when salt aerosol makes your eyes dry and sting.

## 3. Health & Safety Baseline

### 3.1. Before you step in: medical checks

- Mild respiratory clearance check (especially if you have asthma).
- If you have any heart conditions that are affected by heat, humidity, or low-level oxygen shifts, *consult* before going.
- Current vaccination for whatever your baseline world requires, plus any broad-spectrum prophylactic you’d take for industrial wet environments.

### 3.2. Inside the chamber: monitoring

- Hydration:  
  You’ll be sweating and evaporating moisture constantly *from* your skin, but breathing air that’s already nearly saturated. You will not *feel* thirsty the way you do in dry heat.  
  - Set a timer to drink water *by* schedule, not by thirst.
- Core temperature:  
  The ambient air is slightly above comfort, rich with moisture. Pay attention to dizziness, nausea, or a feeling of “swimming head”—that is not “mystical alien atmosphere,” that is heat-strain.
- Salt intake:  
  You’ll be exhaling moisture and salt. Electrolytes help avoid headaches and muscle cramps.

### 3.3. Psychological requirements

- **Structured schedule**:  
  The human brain is not designed to “float” in featureless time. This place is the opposite of featureless—but *time* is. So you bring:
  - A fixed sleep cycle
  - A fixed work/walk/observation schedule
  - Internal “holidays” or mini-missions: “Today I’m drawing the moss patterns,” “Today I’m mapping the tank fronds at chest height,” etc.
- **Anchor objects**:  
  Bring something that feels like home. A small charm, a photo, a notebook you use only for this.  
  The Deep Canning is *good* at making you feel like a temporary shadow in a long memory machine—you need a reminder that you *are* someone outside, too.

## 4. Entry Protocol

### 4.1. Getting through the door (wherever “door” is)

- Confirm you didn’t open into *water*:
  - In your jump hub, set the destination height *above* the floor level of the Deep Canning.  
    - Coordinate: X/Y/Z; where +Z is “up into dome”.
  - Have a fail-safe: if you open your portal and see water, not grating floor—you shut it immediately and adjust.
- Check your anti-gravity or maneuver harness:
  - If something *does* drop you straight into water under the floor, you want to slow fall rather than plunk in.

### 4.2. First moments inside

- Stand near the edge of the platform, not directly over the submerged machinery:  
  If you faint (from heat shock, humidity, or plain shock), you don’t want to fall into the water through the grate.
- Orient:
  - Identify a visible landmark: the central knot cluster (vine/machine junction at ceiling height).  
  - Identify your exit portal anchor / safe zone.
  - Do *not* lean immediately on the tanks—give yourself a little time to feel the space.

## 5. How To Be Inside The Deep Canning

### 5.1. Movement

- **Always assume wet footing**:  
  - Walk deliberately, not hurriedly.  
  - Test footing weight before committing full weight.
- **Keep shoes on the grate**:  
  - No sitting on the floor with your feet dangling through the mesh into the water below.  
  - Remember: *something* lives down there—plants engineered to behave a bit like animals.
- **Use handrails where they exist**:  
  Everywhere there is a vertical rib or railing, you should use it as a guide. This is a place where *slow* movement is the default; we don’t need to fight that.

### 5.2. Touching (and not)

- **Do not touch the central vine-mech knot at the ceiling without reasons**:  
  That’s where the system’s brain and nerve cluster are. It’s not a toy.
- **Avoid touching fresh condensate on the ribs**:  
  - Drops form *slow*; they are from *salt* air; they can irritate skin and eyes.  
  - Also, you don’t know what is dissolved in that condensate.
- **If you want to touch tank walls or plants**:  
  - Use gloves *first*.
  - Make sure feet are stable.
  - Place your hand flat, *don’t* grab or pinch.
  - If you lean *against* a tank, remember that *you* are a heat and humidity signal.  
    The algae will remember you. Your outline of sweat and breath and warmth will be written into the growth pattern.

## 6. Red & Blue Lines: What You Never Do

### Never do this:

- **No swims**:  
  Do not go under the floor grating into the water.  
  - It is engineered to complex ion gradients and hormones optimized *for* these plants.  
  - You are not declared in the recipe.
- **No introductions**:  
  - No food crumbs, no seeds, no bacteria on your boots from other worlds.  
    - Before you step in, clean your shoes and gear of soil and other lifeforms.
    - This place is *animal-free* by design.
    - Any fauna you introduce is a pollution that the system will react to.
- **No “improving” the control panel**:  
  The panel at the center of the vine-mech cluster is *integrated with plant tissue*.  
  - Do not hack it.
  - Do not try to “fix” anything.
  - This is a *living* device, not an inert console.
- **No “mementos”**:  
  - Don’t cut off fronds.
  - Don’t break nodules.
  - Don’t “update” graffiti in the algae.
  - The *system* keeps track of you; it does not ask you to keep track of itself.

## 7. Describing & Studying Well

### 7.1. Observation missions

Assign yourself tasks like:

- **Day 1–3: “Chamber baseline”**  
  - Map the tank rows from one edge of the chamber to the other.  
  - Identify at least 5–10 distinct moiré of algae and mosses on the tank sides.
- **Day 4–6: “Patterns of growth”**  
  - Choose a small area of tank wall, mark out a square you can revisit (photo + notation).  
  - Over days, notice: any new nodules forming? *very* subtle changes in color? different edge geometry?
- **Day 7–10: “Water under”**  
  - From above the grating, choose a point of the underwater floor where you can see pipes clearly.  
  - Take repeated images at the same time of day, to see if the flow *looks* different when you compare later.

### 7.2. Taking notes

- Use **time stamps** for every observation:
  - Local date/time  
  - “Day since my arrival”
- Make note of:
  - **You** (how you feel: humidity headache, calm, anxious, etc.)  
  - **State of the floor**: water level beneath gratings, visible droplets on railings  
  - **Plant growth**: any new fronds you *notice*, not that you prove are new  
  - **Any anomaly of light**: unexpected dimming, brightening, colors of pressure pulses  
  - **Any new “remembers you” moments**: algae outline of your handprint, a moss patch where you knelt, etc.

## 8. Time & Stress: Mental Wellbeing In Slow Time

### 8.1. Time feels weird here because

- There is no “sunset” or “sunrise” in the usual sense.
- Light cycles are subtle fades of artificial spectrum.
- Sound is almost absent: no wind, no animals, just the drip and hum *you* can barely perceive.
- The *system* counts change in years and decades; *you* count in hours and days.

To avoid *time drift*:

- **Set a personal clock**:
  - 8 hours of “sleep”
  - 10–12 hours of active observation/notes
  - 4–6 hours of “soft” time: stretching, quiet, memory of home.

### 8.2. Handling “cathedral brain”

This place feels like a submerged temple of biomass. That can be:

- Deeply calming,
- Or sometimes numbing or even slightly unsettling.

To keep your mind clear:

- **Practice brief grounding routines**:  
  - “I am here. The floor under my feet is metal. There are _this many_ tanks to my left. _This_ is my marker.”
- **Talk to yourself** (if needed):  
  - Keep your own voice present in recordings or notes.  
  - The absence of sound can make your own inner voice feel loud, then brittle. *Speaking* helps.
- **Limit exposure**:  
  - If after days you feel irritation, spaceyness, or “time dissolving,” you may need active breaks *outside* the chamber in your home hub.  
    - This is not an “interdimensional” effect; it’s monotony and humidity and pressure.

## 9. Exit & Aftercare

### 9.1. Leaving without “staining” the place

- **Check you haven’t dropped anything**:  
  Equipment, tools, clothing.
- **Do a final body scan**:
  - Any sweat marks you left on railings? (Not a problem, but be aware: *you’re* now part of this room’s memory.)
- **Gently clean your gear**:
  - Any residue from salt, humidity, or algae spores goes *with* you.
  - You don’t want to accidentally seed another world’s wet-lab with Deep Canning algae.

### 9.2. Body recovery after exit

- **Dry off**:  
  - Change clothes fully *outside* the chamber, in a dry area.
- **Hydration check**:  
  - Drink at least one full bottle of water with electrolytes once you’re out.
- **Rest**:  
  - Give your body a few hours to renormalize to “dry world” physics.
- **Listen to your notes**:  
  - Sometimes your body was *storing* stress and not telling you in the moment.  
    - If you feel more exhausted or “over” the world than expected, schedule *some* decompression time.

### 9.3. Returning visits

- If you go back months or years later (your time):
  - **Go to the same place** where you leaned or touched the tank during your first visit.
  - Look *very* carefully at the algae and moss:
    - Chances are you’ll see faint patterns of your own actions preserved, *blurred* but present, like old handwriting under water.
    - The system *does* remember you.

---

## 10. Final Attitude

You are not going to the Deep Canning to *change* anything.  
You are going to *watch*.

Prepare as you would for a long, silent, wet, warm meditation retreat deep inside an ocean mountain, where the walls are green and the sky is black glass.

Bring your body protection, your timekeeping discipline, and your quietest curiosity.  
Then step in, look, and listen *slow*.

The Deep Canning will not remember you as a hero or an explorer.  
It will remember you as a faint humidity gradient, a heat shadow, and a gentle pattern of growth in the algae.
