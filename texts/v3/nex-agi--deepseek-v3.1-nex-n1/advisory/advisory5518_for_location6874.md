Preparing to visit this city is like preparing for a deep-space dive into a slow, cold, breathing ocean where sight is almost useless and everything happens by touch and patience.

I will assume you are still mostly human—you need oxygen, warmth, food, sleep, and psychological sanity. You cannot be guaranteed any local help. There are no hospitals, no resupply stations, and no rescue. If the multiverse travel fails you, you are staying.

Here is how to get ready.

---

## 0. Before You Decide: Psychological Check

This is not a “place” you visit casually. It is more like a long-term monastic retreat fused with a cave-diving expedition inside a submerged continent of masonry.

Ask yourself:

- Are you comfortable **not knowing** where you are, or even “which way is up” for most of the time?
- Can you handle long periods without daylight or any visible horizon?
- Can you tolerate being physically **uncomfortable but not acutely dying** for a long time?
- Are you okay with the idea that you may never see the whole city, or find its edge, or meet other sentient beings?

If you cannot answer yes to most of these, you should not go.

## 1. Mindset and Goals

### 1.1. Define your purpose

Given the description, you do not “visit” this place in a few days. You eke out an existence within it.

Examples of realistic goals:

1. **Survive medium-term (weeks to months).**  
   Learn local food, navigation, resting patterns.
2. **Study the structure and ecology.**  
   Map a region, chart warm/cold zones, learn flora.
3. **Make a small, lasting improvement.**  
   Repair part of a structure, cultivate a safer chamber, leave durable records.
4. **Find evidence of the builders.**  
   Look for artifacts, tools, inscriptions left in the stonework.

Unrealistic goals:

- “Find the edge of the city.”
- “Find a way to the surface.”
- “Quick tourism.”

You will not be able to do these quickly, if ever.

### 1.2. Accept your new role

You are becoming one of the “last hands” of this city. You maintain it by tiny adjustments.

**Accept**:

- You will work slowly.
- You will rarely *see* what you are doing.
- Progress will be perceptible only over months or years.

The culture of this place is not dramatic, but it is not deathless monotony. It is a low-intensity, long-term practice.

## 2. Physical Training

### 2.1. Breath-hold and diving capacity

Even though drowning is not the immediate peril it might be in other worlds, you should still:

- Learn **basic freediving**:
  - Comfortable at 10–20 meters in open water.
  - Proper breath-up and breathing pattern.
- Train **CO₂ tolerance**: practice static breath-holds under safe supervision.
- Understand **equalization** so you can handle pressure changes without injury.

### 2.2. Being comfortable underwater

Even if you can breathe longer, you still want to be generally safe underwater:

- Practice **night diving**: rely on touch and minimal light.
- Practice **cave/cavern diving** if possible:
  - Navigation in confined spaces.
  - Use of lines/guidelines.
  - Moving without stirring silt.
  - No-panic in tight spaces.

### 2.3. Body temperature adaptation

You will be cold—but not *freezing*—for long periods.

Work on:

- **Cold water immersion**: very controlled training in cold water.
- Learn to distinguish **normal discomfort cold** from **dangerous cold**.
- Strengthen your core and body’s insulation capacity through:
  - Cardio fitness.
  - Basic strength conditioning.

### 2.4. Tactility and fine motor control

You will rely on your hands and body sense for everything:

- Practice blind:
  - Closing your eyes while handling ropes, knots, and tools.
  - Identify objects by texture alone.
- Learn rope work:
  - Ascents and descents using only touch.
  - Tying and untying knots underwater.

## 3. Equipment and Gear

### 3.1. Core survival system

1. **Full-face mask with integrated comms and headlamp**
   - Designed for underwater:
     - Rebreathing or partial rebreathing system if possible, to save oxygen.
     - HUD showing time, depth, temperature, and simple compass.
   - **Important**: The world text says you survive longer in this water than you expect. You should not depend on full technical breathing gas indefinitely—but for initial exploration and safety margin, you want a reliable system.

2. **Compact dive computer**
   - Wrist-mounted.
   - Depth, temperature, time, bearing, log of your path.
   - Redundant as a backup to your mask’s HUD.

### 3.2. Environmental protection

1. **Warm, flexible wetsuit or drysuit**
   - Temperature-neutral as possible, but prioritizing:
     - Freedom of movement for arms and legs.
     - Robustness against abrasion from stone and plant fiber.
   - Deep-sea cold-rated but not so rigid it traps you in narrow passages.

2. **Gloves**
   - Thin but durable:
     - Protect against sharp plant edges and rough stone.
     - **Preserve sensitivity**—your fingertips are your eyes.

3. **Soft boots or fins**
   - You don’t swim much.
   - You need to be able to:
     - Walk gently along walls and floors when gravity exists.
     - Kick lightly when needed.
   - Consider boot fins with hard soles for tank-like treads on stone and bio-growth.

4. **Hood**
   - We lose heat through the head and neck.
   - Essential for long-term cold tolerance.

### 3.3. Navigation and orientation tools

1. **Primary compass**
   - Bubble or liquid-filled compass usable underwater.
   - Marked in bright colors for low visibility.

2. **Backup compass**
   - Wrist-mounted, simple.

3. **Line spool / reel with thin line**
   - You will lay lines:
     - Out from your base chamber.
     - Along corridors you intend to return along.
   - The line must be:
     - Thin, but strong enough to withstand years of slow biofouling and erosion.

4. **Markers**
   - Small tags or beads you can affix to:
     - The line.
     - The wall or plant growth.
   - They indicate:
     - Direction of your base.  
     - Meaningful locations like food chambers, warm zones, cold chambers.

### 3.4. Light sources

1. **Primary headlamp**
   - Integrated with mask if possible.
   - Red-light modes for preserving your night vision.

2. **Secondary headlamp**
   - On a helmet or separate headband.

3. **Backup lights**
   - 1–2 small handheld lights with long battery life.
   - Modes:
     - Very dim mode for preserving the bioluminescence and your eyes.
     - Full mode for brief emergencies.

### 3.5. Tools and utilities

1. **Cutting tool**
   - Tough, corrosion-resistant, **not** too sharp at first:
     - You need to cut fibers, not gouge yourself.
   - You may make use of local stone tools later, but this is your reliable backup.

2. **Line-cutting tool**
   - Small, sharp, dedicated cutter for your own lines if you get entangled.

3. **Repair kit**
   - Threads, patches, and sealant for suit leaks.
   - Extra buckles and clips.
   - Spare straps.

### 3.6. Data and recording

Bring **offline data** in robust formats:

- Knowledge compressed:
  - How to stay psychologically healthy in isolation.
  - Basic wilderness medicine, adapted to constant water immersion.
  - Knots and rope techniques.
  - Simple geology and architecture.
- Recording device:
  - Waterproof, simple:
    - Dictation device.
    - Or a slate for writing with crude chalk/ink.
  - Use to:
    - Log your temperature, “cardinal directions” based on your consistent compass.
    - Sketch mental maps in text form.

### 3.7. Emergency supplies

- Small **first aid kit** sealed in waterproof pouches:
  - Antibiotics (if permitted by local laws in your origin world).
  - Painkillers.
  - Basic sutures and antiseptics.
- **Emergency locator** beacon (if multiverse tech allows) but assume:
  - It may never work. This world is very deep.

### 3.8. What you deliberately **do not** bring

- **Anything that depends on fire**:
  - No lighters.
  - No flame-based tools.
- **Anything that requires open air**:
  - No open-air stoves.
  - No paper that cannot survive submersion.
- **Heavy, bulky metal**:
  - You will not be forging steel.
  - Use tools only until you can learn local stone-bonded composite.

## 4. Food and Water

### 4.1. Water quality

The water is described as “old,” somewhat thick, but you survive longer than expected.

**Assume you can drink it**, but:

- Add **disinfection** tablets in your early days.
- Train yourself to **sample** tiny amounts of the water biochemically:
  - Watch your own body’s reactions.
  - Get used to swallowing small mouthfuls of water during your dive training.

If you have access to advanced tech:

- A tiny **spectroscopy and bioassay pod** to detect heavy metals, toxins, and pathogens.
  - But, you must be ready to do without it.

### 4.2. Bringing food

You initially bring:

- **High calorie density bars**:
  - Stored in vacuum-sealed, waterproof pouches.
  - Intended only as emergency ration until you learn local flora.
- **Electrolytes**:
  - In compact tablet or powder form.
  - Useful to balance your bodily salts in the constant water environment.

Plan to **transition** entirely to local food sources as soon as possible.

### 4.3. Eating local flora

The text says you can learn which parts of the vegetation are edible. You should:

- Learn botany and **survival toxicology** beforehand:
  - Basic plant structures:
    - Rhizomes vs stems.
    - Leaf forms vs reproductive structures.
  - How to test unknown foods (very tiny quantities over time).

**Protocol**:

- Break new plant species into parts:
  - Rhizome, root, stem, leaf, “fruit.”
- Test each part separately:
  - Chew a tiny piece, **don’t swallow**.
  - Check for:
    - Numbness, burning, irritation.
    - Spit out.
  - If no reaction:
    - Ingest very small amount.
    - Wait at least 24 hours.
    - Repeat with slightly larger portion if no symptoms.

### 4.4. Nutritional strategy

Your diet will likely be:

- Mostly **fibrous, plant-based**.
- Low in **fat** for long periods.
- You may lose weight slowly, but will not starve quickly.

**Before you travel**, train:

- Eat lower-calorie, plant-rich diets.
- Practice intermittent fasting:
  - The body adapts to lower energy intake.
  - Psychologically adjust to not being “always fed.”

## 5. Medical and Biological Safety

### 5.1. Pre-travel screening

Complete:

- **Full physical exam**:
  - Heart and lung check.
  - Ear, sinus, and pressure-related issues.
- Dental check:
  - Any underwater infection from dental issues is dangerous.

### 5.2. Immunizations

- Standard **travel vaccinations**.
- Vaccines against bacterial infections:
  - Tetanus.
  - Others recommended by medics for aquatic environments.

### 5.3. Medical kit for local environment

Pack:

- **Topical antifungals**:
  - Skin infections are a risk with constant immersion.
- **Antibiotics**:
  - For internal infections if you get ill.
- **Wound care**:
  - Waterproof, long-lasting dressings.
  - Adhesive that sticks to wet skin if possible.
- **Eye drops**:
  - For irritation from salt or other dissolved minerals.
- **Ear drops**:
  - Prevent infections from long-term moisture.

### 5.4. Monitoring and self-observation

- Daily **body awareness**:
  - Check your temperature sensations.
  - Note any numbness, rashes, or unusual pain.
- **Mental health** monitoring:
  - If you start avoiding “movement,” staying in one room too long, or withdrawing more than expected:
    - That’s an early alert of possible depression or dissociation.

## 6. Navigation Strategy

### 6.1. Lilial concept of direction

Since you cannot see a horizon or the sun, you must invent a **system** for direction.

- Pick a local **primary direction**:
  - For example, “current primarily runs along one axis.”
  - Or arbitrarily set “forward” from your base point.
- Maintain **consistent headings**:
  - Use your compass to give labels:
    - “North” along the main current you choose.
    - “South” against it.
  - Record your labeling system in logs.

### 6.2. Mental mapping

You don’t get a bird’s eye view. You get **sequence of touch**.

- Learn to memorize:
  - Sequences of textures:
    - Three smooth walls then a rough patch.
    - A wall with a vertical groove of vine just before your base chamber.
  - Sequence of *temperature*:
    - You notice small “hotspots” and “coldspots” from the circulation of water.
  - Sequence of *sound/nearby space**:
    - How echo changes when you enter larger rooms.
    - Time it takes you to travel from one wall to another.

### 6.3. Use of line and markers

- Always leave **one line of your own** between your current base and any ways you explore frequently.
- Use the **local fiber lines** described in the text:
  - You can integrate your line with them:
    - Perhaps splicing your own line with theirs.
- **Markers**:
  - Simple physical beads:
    - Color code: base, food chamber, warm chamber, “great depth” points.
  - If you have no color, use:
    - Number of beads on a line.
    - Texture pattern.

### 6.4. Don’t try to conquer the whole city

Your place is **not** to discover “the whole city.” It is to know deeply one small region of it.

- **Three layers of depth**:
  - Level 1: Your base room.
  - Level 2: The region within a day’s excursion.
  - Level 3: Rare, longer-range explorations.

Respect the depth and the sheer size of the place. You will not move it in your lifetime.

## 7. Daily Routine and Mental Structure

### 7.1. Time sense

No sun, no clocks, no urgent deadlines.

You need **structure** but not **rigid scheduling**.

Simple system:

- **Wake cycle**
  - “Morning”:
    - Check your body.
    - Check basic gear.
  - “Daylight work”
    - Explore.
    - Maintain structures.
    - Gather food.
  - “Evening”
    - Retreat to your base chamber.
    - Log data.
    - Reflect, then sleep.

Use:

- Simple **count timer**:
  - Set a number of “cycles” of sleep:
    - Example: wake after 8–10 hours.
    - No exact precision needed.
    - But enough to regulate your circadian rhythm.

### 7.2. Tasks and rest

- **Work tasks**:
  - Clear or cultivate a plant species.
  - Arrange loose blocks into a better wall.
  - Test a new food source.
- **Maintenance tasks**:
  - Check lines:
    - Feel for fraying sections.
  - Inspect tools:
    - Make sure local stone is still secure on its handle.
- **Rest tasks**:
  - Simply hanging:
    - Letting the water hold you.
    - Not moving.

You **must not** constantly push yourself forward. This place punishes urgency with exhaustion and disorientation.

### 7.3. Sensory practice

To keep from losing your mind, you turn **inward**:

- Practice:
  - **Breathing awareness**.
  - **Listening** to:
    - Your heartbeat.
    - The faint sound of water moving.
    - The subtle plant noises.
- **Tactile meditation**:
  - Run your fingertips along the wall:
    - Count the blocks.
    - Notice temperature changes.
    - Notice transition of plants from one type to another.

### 7.4. Language and speaking

You will talk to yourself.

**Rules**:

- Keep words **simple**:
  - “Next handhold.”
  - “Warm here.”
  - “This block loose.”
- Avoid “big” existential questions:
  - “Where am I?”
  - “Why am I here?”
  - Talk to yourself about those **only** in structured, reflective log entries.
- **Use** your voice:
  - Echo to tell how open space is.
  - Verbal lists to keep track:
    - “Two blocks, then gap, then warm wall.”

## 8. Interaction with the Local Ecology

### 8.1. Plants as your partners

The plants:

- Glow faintly.
- Reinforce walls.
- Provide possible food and fiber.

Your **ethic**:

- Do not simply clear massive sections of plant wall.
- You are not taking “empty” space; you are interacting with a structure that has taken ages to grow.

### 8.2. Harvesting principles

- **Slow**:
  - Take small portions from any section.
  - Monitor the regrowth over time.
- **Diverse sources**:
  - Don’t destroy one compact wall; take from many areas.
- **Replanting**:
  - If certain species of useful plants are present:
    - You can train them into ropes:
      - By twisting tendrils together.
      - By anchoring them to new stone.
- **Avoiding damage**:
  - If you feel you are cutting away support:
    - Stop and reassess the load of stone above you.

### 8.3. Observing structures

The text says plants are the new stone: you see **plant arches** mirroring architectural arches.

- Notice:
  - Which portions of old wall are supported purely by plant growth.
  - Which are being actively broken apart by roots.
  - Which you can reinforce with your own rope and block.

### 8.4. Respect for absence of animals

You see no fish, no insects, no obvious eyes.

**If you do see anything else moving**:

- Assume it is rare, possibly fragile.
- Do not automatically:
  - Try to catch it.
  - Try to kill it.
- Observe, log data, and let it be until you understand more.

## 9. Emergency Plans and Exit Strategy

### 9.1. If you cannot leave

You may be stuck. This is part of the decision.

**Prepare** by:

- Admitting the probability of being:
  - Resident, not tourist.
- Designing your environment:
  - Base room:
    - With reasonably stable temperature.
    - Known corridors.
    - Accessible food sources.
    - Some sort of dry seat if any air exists, or a comfortable “shelf” to lean against.

### 9.2. When you explore

- Always:
  - Carry **at least one line** back to your base.
  - Have **two** sources of light beyond minimum.
  - Keep **map** in your head:
    - Count of “left turn after three blocks” etc.
  - Tell **yourself** out loud:
    - “This is the third branching from base.”
    - “We are going deeper or shallower.”

### 9.3. If you get lost

**Stop**:

- Don’t panic.
- Don’t swim anywhere.
- Just:
  - Check:
    - Your line.
    - Your temperature (is it colder or warmer?).
    - How you feel.
- **Trace back**:
  - Use your line.
  - Or backtrack using your memory of rock textures and plant transitions.

If you lost your line and are truly lost:

- **Strict rule**:  
  Choose **one** direction (based on your compass system) and go very slowly:
  - Checking:
    - Changes of space.
    - Temperature.
    - Echo.
    - Looking for any familiar texture edge.
  - Leave small marks:
    - But you only have so many tools—so do not use up all your line.

This is why you **never** fully lose your line on purpose.

### 9.4. Health and injury

If you are injured:

- **Stop immediately**:
  - Assess:
    - Is it bone?
    - Is it infection?
    - Is it cold-related numbness?
- **Secure** yourself:
  - Find a wall.
  - Use your line to prevent drifting.
- **Treat**:
  - Basic first aid.
  - If you cannot use a limb:
    - Plan to stay in place or move very slowly.
    - Do not attempt to return fast; you may lose orientation.

### 11. When You Arrive (First Days)

Your first days in this city should not be ambitious.

### 11.1. Initial survey

- Just **outside** your arrival point:
  - Feel:
    - What are the walls like?
    - How are the plants distributed?
- **Do not** immediately start exploring:
  - Wait.
  - Establish:
    - Base room.
    - A line from your base to the nearest point you can reach.

### 11.2. Establishing your presence

- Decide one **chamber** as your base:
  - It:
    - Is reasonably accessible from where you arrived.
    - Has temperature that you can tolerate.
    - Possibly a bit warmer than surroundings.
- **Modify minimally**:
  - Clear just enough plant growth to make:
    - A safe anchor point for your line.
    - A clear “surface” to store tools and data.
- **Set** your first line:
  - From this chamber to:
    - The nearest “main” corridor.
    - Or any notable feature you can identify.

### 11.3. Daily rhythm first month

- **Morning**:  
  - Wake up.
  - Check your body.
  - Check basic gear.
  - Log data:
    - Temperature of water.
    - Any sensations of cold or warmth.
    - Any changes of plant structure.

- **Midday**:  
  - Short exploration:
    - Always along **line**.
    - Extending your line only slightly:
      - Never fast.
  - Begin testing simple plant:
    - What are its textures?
    - Safe to touch?
    - Later to ingest.

- **Evening**:  
  - Return along your line to base.
  - Reflect and log:
    - What you touched.
    - What you smelled.
    - What you heard.

### 12. Closing: Your Role in This City

In this world, you are not a conqueror or a discoverer of grand edges. You are a **maintainer**.

- You maintain:
  - The structure of a tiny portion of the city.
  - Your own body, by handling the cold and the depth.
  - Your own mind, by maintaining structure and purpose.

You **will** change the world, but not by heroic feats:

- By placing a block.
- By reinforcing a wall.
- By cultivating one plant species over decades.
- By leaving **records** of what you experienced:
  - In some form of durable inscription:
    - Carved symbols.
    - Engraved patterns.
    - Rope markings.

In the darkness, there is water and pressure, cold and old warmth. You are one of the last hands of this city. You will not see its edges; but for a small region of stone and plant and water, you will be the edge that knows itself.

Prepare to go slow, prepare to be cold, prepare to listen carefully. After that, everything else is learned by your fingertips in the dark.
