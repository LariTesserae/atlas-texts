Here’s what you should understand before you go, and how to prepare for Sheol‑on‑the‑Shelf.

---

## 1. What kind of place this is

You are not going to a ruin; you are going to a living engine that has outlived its purpose and been turned into a habitat.

- **Time has stopped, but life hasn’t.**  
  The machinery of Sheol‑on‑the‑Shelf finished running thousands of years ago. The heat in the stone floor is the leftover memory of that work. The city is now a stable, fossilized engine that has settled into a thermal and chemical equilibrium. In human terms, it’s eternal.

- **It is sealed.**  
  The world has been closed “like a box.” There is no sky and almost no external weather, only a deep, basalt‑and‑glass ceiling far above the mountain shelf. That means:
  - no day/night cycle,
  - no stars,
  - very little air movement,
  - no rain, ever.

- **It is warm and very dark.**  
  The ambient temperature is warm enough that you won’t freeze, but cool enough that you will need clothing. You will be colder than the floor itself. Light sources are scarce and precious: the only real lights are the inspection ports of the boilers, glowing yellow‑green.

- **The ground is unreliable.**  
  You are walking on the roof of a city built on a mountain shelf. The floor:
  - black tiled stone where it is intact,
  - deep banks of fine dust/ash where it has failed,
  - occasional metal plates that ring underfoot,
  - edges that are crumbling away into the dark interior of the mountain.
  
  You will not be able to trust your eyes alone; you will have to learn by sound and by feel.

## 2. Psychic and spiritual preparation

### 2.1. Prepare for enclosedness

You will feel a deep, geological claustrophobia:

- There is a ceiling where you expect sky.
- The world outside is permanently cut off.
- The air is still and charged with the sense of a machine that has finally stopped.

To prepare:

- Spend time before your trip in environments that simulate this:
  - Long hours in basements or underground spaces with no external windows.
  - Practice tolerating the absence of weather changes.
- Train your mind:
  - Remind yourself: **this is not a tomb, but a habitat.**  
    People live here and have adapted for generations.
  - Accept that you will not “see the sky” for the duration of your visit.
- Consider bringing:
  - **Low‑tech timekeeping:** a watch that you don’t rely on for navigation but to keep your own circadian rhythm.  
  - **Meditation or grounding routines** you can perform without light, to steady yourself when you feel trapped by the lack of horizon.

### 2.2. Prepare for scale and silence

The boilers are huge, semi‑visible structures that fade into the dark. The soundscape is almost nonexistent.

You will hear:

- Your footfalls on tile and plate.
- The soft crunch of your boots in powder.
- Your own breath and heartbeat.
- Silence, deep, almost tactile.

To prepare:

- Practice **listening meditation**, so you can be comfortable with your own internal sounds instead of being spooked by them.
- If you rely on electronics for audio, recognize:
  - Sheol is a machine landscape; some devices may be very affected or might fail in strange ways.
  - Bring headphones that you can tolerate long‑term in silence; the absence of sound can be disconcerting.

### 2.3. Prepare for the absence of “nature”

There is no green world you would recognize. Vegetation, if you can call it that, is confined:

- Inside the boilers,
- As a **microbial mat** that covers the boiler floor,
- Maintaining a self-contained ecosystem.

You will not see trees, forests, or bodies of water. There is no open moisture.

To prepare:

- Adjust your aesthetic expectations:
  - This place is *engineered*, not pastoral.
  - Wherever you see warm yellow-green light from inspection ports, that’s your “forest.”
- Bring:
  - **Memory images:** photos, drawings of Earth environments on a device or paper, to remind yourself that this world is not the only one.
- Consider spending time before your trip in industrial spaces:
  - Factories,
  - Power plants,
  - Shipyards,
  - Underground tunnels.
  - Get comfortable with being inside of “machine architecture.”

## 3. Gear and physical preparation

### 3.1. Footwear and walking support

The ground is your main hazard.

You need:

- **Solid, closed boots with thick soles**
  - You need to be able to register by feel:
    - the tile underfoot,
    - the metal plate,
    - the depth of the powder.
  - The soles should be firm enough to transfer these textures but flexible enough not to tire your feet quickly.
- **Gaiters or dust guards**
  - The dust is fine and pervasive. You don’t want it inside your boots, socks, or clothing.
- **A sturdy walking staff**
  - Essential:
    - Probe the depth of dust before committing.
    - Test the stability of tiles near inner edges.
    - Provide balance when the floor flexes over voids.
  - Choose metal or strong composite, not wood: Sheol’s environment and its possible invisible chemistry may be harsh on organic materials.

### 3.2. Clothing and protection

- **Light layers you can adjust**  
  - It is warm but not hot.  
  - You need:
    - A base layer that wicks sweat,
    - A middle insulating layer you can remove or tie,
    - A light outer shell resistant to dust and abrasions.
- **Coverage**
  - Long sleeves and long trousers to protect skin from:
    - Sharp edges of metal plates,
    - Old metal rivets,
    - Dust irritation.
- **Head protection**
  - A helmet or strong hat with some padding:
    - Low inspection ports,
    - Overhead pipes,
    - Unexpected edges where your head height and the boiler’s geometry meet.
- **Eye and respiratory protection**
  - Full sealed goggles or high-quality safety glasses:
    - dust,
    - potentially fine particles from the boiler mat ecosystem if anything is opened,
    - any metal dust.
  - **Dust mask or respirator** capable of filtering very fine particulates.
- **Clean underlayers**
  - You should be able to sleep or rest inside clothing without chafing or becoming too sweaty in a constant temperature environment.

### 3.3. Illumination

- Bring **multiple sources**, assume:
  - Some devices may fail.
  - The environment may be mildly electromagnetic or strange in ways we don’t understand.
- **Red or low‑color light headlamp**
  - Preserves your night vision.
  - Reduces strain on your eyes in an already dark world.
- **Hand‑held torch**
  - For spot checks, attention signals, and longer range.
- **Glow sticks or small self‑contained lights**
  - For marking:
    - Shelter entrances,
    - Safe pathways,
    - Dangerous edges.
  - Avoids having to rely solely on batteries.

You **cannot** depend on the ambient light from the inspection ports. That’s reserved to the boiler residents and their ecosystem.

### 3.4. Tools and equipment

- **Rope and cordage**
  - For safety:
    - Leash into unknown areas,
    - Lowering or raising small packs,
    - Securing a temporary shelter.
- **Virtual ‘anchor’**
  - If your multiverse method uses reference anchors, be absolutely sure:
    - You have a clear object or token *that will leave with you*,
    - Ideally something you keep physically attached to your body.
- **Notebook and pencil**
  - Nothing electronic to lose battery.  
  - You will want to document:
    - The textures underfoot,
    - Your pathways,
    - The sounds of different parts of the floor.
- **Basic cutting tools and multi-tool**
  - For occasional cable, thread, or packaging, but:
    - Be aware this is a machine city where everything you see is already integrated into a very old system.
- **Reusable water containers**
  - Even if you don’t expect to find open water, have:
    - A collapsible vessel or canteen,
    - For storage if you are given any fluids.

### 3.5. Medical supplies and self-care

- **Basic first‑aid kit**
  - Bandages, antiseptic wipes, tape.
  - Pain relief.
  - Trauma supplies if you are trained to use them.
- **Skin care**
  - Dry body environment, plus dust and heat:
    - Moisturizer,
    - Lip balm,
    - Eye drops.
- **Hydration and nutrition**
  - Bring:
    - High-energy, compact food bars.
    - Rehydration salts.
- Given the **closed microbial ecosystem**, assume:
  - You are not biologically compatible with this world.
  - Avoid contact:
    - Cover your wounds.
    - Do not eat anything local unless explicitly advised by a trusted inhabitant.

### 3.6. Navigation and orientation

- **Compasses may be unreliable**
  - There is no magnetic field you can trust; the world is sealed and industrial.
- **Bring**
  - Small, durable physical tokens or color-coded markers:
    - To mark paths you take,
    - To avoid confusing loops of your own trail.
  - A notebook for:
    - Sketching boiler positions,
    - Writing pattern descriptions of tile vs powder vs plate underfoot.
- **Time discipline**
  - Set a regular watch:
    - Wake and sleep at times that you decide.
    - Resist the temptation of spending indefinite time wandering the open spaces just because there is no external day-night.

## 4. Hazards and how to handle them

### 4.1. The floor

- **Tile vs. dust**
  - Tile: solid, polished, black.
    - Safe to walk on when you confirm it by sound and feel.
    - Beware delamination and cracks that indicate hidden voids.
  - Dust: deep fine powder, ash-like.
    - May be concealing deep pits or broken steps.
- **Practice**
  - Use your staff to test in front of you.
  - Listen for:
    - The hollow echo of air under thin plates.
    - The change in sound when you transition from tile to dust to metal.
- **Never**
  - Run or rush blindly,
  - Trust that “flat looks” safe.
- **Always**
  - Maintain two points of contact (feet + staff or feet + hand).
  - Observe lateral edges: the floor is slowly failing downslope toward the inner core.

### 4.2. The edges

- The inner edge of the Shelf drops into the mountain core.
- Signs of edge failure:
  - Tile feels hollow underfoot,
  - Fine cracks visible,
  - Powder dust constantly moving downward,
  - “Give” in the surface when you weight it.
- **Rules**
  - Never step closer than one staff length to the edge if you can avoid it.
  - If you must go near:
    - Stay low,
    - Crawl or distribute weight over a large area,
    - Probe the ground ahead of you.
  - Never walk the same edge “level” two days in a row;  
    the floor is slowly grinding down and shifting.

### 4.3. The boiler interiors and microbial ecosystems

- The boiler interiors are:
  - Closed ecosystems,
  - Maintained by inhabitants who rely on them.
- The microbial mat:
  - Is their floor,
  - Is alive,
  - May be sensitive to your external biology.
- **Assumptions**
  - You are not adapted to this environment.
  - Your microbes are not theirs.
- **Rules**
  - Do not enter a boiler unless:
    - You are invited,
    - You understand basic protocol,
    - You are prepared to obey strict rules about:
      - What you touch,
      - Whether you wear gloves or shoes,
      - Whether you are allowed at all.
  - Expect:
    - To be washed or cleaned before entering their core ecosystem,
    - To be asked to wear specific garments or coverings,
    - To be politely refused entry if they are caretakers of a particularly fragile system.

### 4.4. The heat and the air

- The air is warm, close, and still.
- You will not bake to death, but you may be:
  - Constantly damp from sweat,
  - Uncomfortable in your clothing layers,
  - Easily dehydrated.
- Practice:
  - Rehydration discipline,
  - Resting even when not exhausted,
  - Noticing subtle temperature differences between boiler areas and open spaces.

## 5. Social and cultural protocol

### 5.1. How you might be seen

You are a stranger from a world that is not under a ceiling of rock. This means:

- You might be treated:
  - as a guest,
  - as a specimen of a kind of environment they thought no longer existed,
  - or as a threat to the closed ecosystem, depending on their history.
- Your presence:
  - Could tempt curiosity,
  - Could provoke distrust,
  - Will require patience from you.

### 5.2. Attitudes and customs

They likely:

- Think in terms of **closed systems**, **engineering**, and **balance**.
- Are deeply concerned with:
  - maintaining the boiler ecosystems,
  - preserving the thermal and biological stability of Sheol‑on‑the‑Shelf,
  - not altering the world more than absolutely necessary.

### 5.3. How to behave

- **Show respect**
  - Do not touch:
    - Unfamiliar surfaces,
    - The inspection ports,
    - Any visible biological mat (unless explicitly instructed).
- **Ask before**
  - Entering boiler buildings,
  - Taking samples,
  - Recording images,
  - Even shining lights into certain areas.
- **Communicate**
  - Explain where you are from in terms of contrast:
    - “A world with open sky and periodic rain.”
    - “A place where machines are not the main structure.”
- **Understand the weight**
  - You are visiting a place where they have engineered a world:
    - They are descendants of those who built the last boilers,
    - You may be seen as someone from the earlier time of the sky that they have sealed away.

### 5.4. What you can give

- Offer:
  - **Stories of your world**
    - Light of stars,
    - The sound of rain,
    - Reflections of sunlight on water.
  - They might be treasured like you would treasure a rare artifact.
- **Guides and techniques**
  - From your world:
    - Concepts of agriculture,
    - Atmospheric engineering,
    - Architecture of open spaces.
- **Do not**
  - Promise technology you cannot bring,
  - Introduce external organisms or liquids,
  - Pressure them to risk their stability for your curiosity.

## 6. Departure and self-care after the trip

### 6.1. Exit considerations

- Ensure:
  - Your anchor token for departure is physically tied to you,
  - You have not given it away or lost it,
  - Your instruments are still functional (if you rely on them).
- Plan:
  - The minimum time you need to recover physically,
  - The minimum time you need to re‑equilibrate psychologically once back home.

### 6.2. Reintegration

- Sheol‑on‑the‑Shelf is:
  - Deeply enclosed,
  - Very still,
  - Very machine‑like,
  - Largely devoid of the external “nature” you are used to.
- When you return:
  - You may feel overstimulated by open sky and wind,
  - Or you might feel terrified of open sky, having stayed in a closed world.
- Prepare:
  - Reintroduction plans:
    - Time to rest,
    - Time to write/tell what happened,
    - Time to re‑connect with trees, oceans, or sunlight of your world.
- Emotional care:
  - You have been in a place that is essentially:
    - A machine city,
    - A fossilized engine.
- Talk:
  - With someone who understands:
    - that you walked on the roof of a city,
    - on the last of the boilers,
    - in a world that has been closed like a box.

## 7. In summary: what you need to remember

- Dress for:
  - warm, dust‑covered, machine‑architecture world.
- Walk on:
  - tile, metal plates, and deep powder dust,
  - testing each step with staff and sound.
- Fear:
  - edges of the Shelf,
  - fragile tile near the inner drop,
  - disturbing the boiler ecosystems.
- Respect:
  - the adult inhabitants of Sheol‑on‑the‑Shelf,
  - their closed systems,
  - their last boilers and the microbial mats that carpet their interiors.
- Prepare yourself emotionally:
  - for dark enclosed warm,
  - for silence echoing,
  - for the absence of sky.

If you approach with preparation, respect, and caution, Sheol‑on‑the‑Shelf will be a place you can visit safely. Just understand that you are not walking into a machine museum; you are walking into a machine that has become home.
