To visit Cael‑Himir as an ordinary human multiverse traveler is to accept that you are stepping into a *final state*—not a ruin, not a dead world, but a world that has finished all of its stories and is left sitting in the closing sentence.

It doesn’t want to harm you, but it doesn’t care if you survive. Its rules are not tuned to you, and they will not bend to you.

Below is a preparation guide that aims to keep you functional, sane, and alive—at least long enough to leave.

---

## 1. Mindset: what you’re actually signing up for

### Time and entropy

On most worlds, the environment changes around you constantly: weather, days, nights, seasons. On Cael‑Himir, time is still passing, but almost nothing happens on human scales. The mountains are teeth; the sky is a fixed jaw; the sun is fixed high white noon; the stars do not even twinkle.

The real danger here is not monsters or sudden storms. The danger is psychological attrition and deep error in your assumptions about how reality works.

- You will see *no change* if you stay for months.
- You will see *no change* if you stay for years.
- If you build a shelter, it will probably not erode in your lifetime.
- Even the most “energetic” lifeforms here are moving slower than you notice.

You must prepare to live in a place that has become a sentence spoken without punctuation. The temptation is to read that as peaceful, but it will be stressful to a brain evolved to detect patterns, changes, and threats.

### The “verblessness” of the plateau

Cael‑Himir has run out of verbs: “grow,” “erode,” “fight,” “flow,” “change.” These have been replaced by “is.”

Your preparation will be aimed at bringing your own small verbs—breathe, eat, move, observe—without picking a fight with the plateau’s *final* configuration.

### Respect, not dominion

You are not visiting a place you can bend or conquer. You are dropping into a geology that has essentially finished forming. The best you can hope is to be a small ripple that doesn’t unleash the stored stress of millennia.

## 2. Essential gear: designing for silence, dryness, and pressure

### Air, pressure, and temperature

You are high on a “raft of rock” clamped into the sky:

- **Thin, sharp air:** Low oxygen pressure, not just low oxygen *content*.  
- **Temperature:** The cold is “absence of heat” rather than cold air; ambient temperature is low but not violently so. Wind constantly thin, not strong.

You need:

- **Environment suit or layered system:**
  - Pressure layer that maintains at least a ~5,000 ft equivalent altitude inside your helmet/breather mask.
  - Ability to moderate temperature without *adding* moisture to the environment: electrics/thermal gels rather than humidifying systems (especially if you’re water-based).
- **Rebreather / mask:**  
  - With **CO₂ scrubber** and a shut-off valve you can lock to prevent *exhaling moisture into the environment*.
  - An external filter to prevent any dust from your suit groaning its way into the plate’s “film” ecosystem.

### Waterproofing and dry habits

The world “doesn’t remember water.” There is no moisture to feed you and *any* you release into the stone is treated like a foreign stress that crystalline lattice releases energy to resolve.

You must treat **your own body** as a sealed container:

- **Full encapsulation suit:**  
  - No open vents.  
  - External seams sealed with something like pressurized tape.  
  - Inner layer that collects sweat *and* recycles it internally—never allow your sweat or breath to escape the suit.

- **Internal hydration system:**  
  - Fully recirculating: water you drink is collected, cleaned, and returned to you.  
  - No external water tank access ports that can be breached accidentally.

- **No open containers:**  
  - Never pour water onto the stone, “just to see.”  
  - If you spill, your suit should treat it as a containment breach. Stop movement immediately, seal outer layer, and scrub if needed.

If you are not from a water-based biology (e.g., ammonia-based, silicon-metabolite), your molecule of choice will still be treated as “foreign” in the same way: assume *any* liquid you carry is potentially incompatible with the plateau’s physics.

### Suit integrity and sensors

- **Pressure monitoring:** External sensors gently measure how much “tension” is in the stone around you.  
- **Microseismics:**  
  - If the ground *rings* when you step, it may be telling you where you’re allowed to go.
  - Consider a small vibrometer embedded in your boots to detect whether you’re on the edge of a “shadow zone” or a lattice anomaly.

## 3. Navigation: where you’re allowed and not allowed to step

### Stone and geometry

The land is not dangerous in itself, but it is packed:

- The ground is dense like porcelain, not scree; each step you take is the small-scale equivalent of striking a bell with a padded mallet.
- Geometries in the rock are possibly *optimized* for the stress of the world. You have no idea which parts of that pattern are load-bearing.

Navigational rules:

- **Avoid thin edges and “discs.”**
  - Those circular depressions in the stone are “footprints” of interfaces that might still be partially active.
  - If you see faint disc shapes with exceptionally smooth surfaces, treat them like antique pressure plates: give them a wide margin, don’t stand on them.

- **Avoid obvious pillar clusters.**  
  - The pillars are integral to the *plateau itself*—part of a single system with the stone.  
  - Don’t wedge between pillars; don’t use them as anchor points for climbing gear.

### Night and day, sun and stars

- **Sunlight:** fixed at high white noon; no cycle of warmth and night.
- **Stars:** visible day and night, unmoving.

Navigation:

- Use the stars as fixed beacons (you can map them to your own coordinate system).  
- Use the **sun angle**—if it appears to shift even by a fraction of a degree over days, note that in your logs, but don’t expect it to move.

### No compasses, no magnetics, maybe no field at all

- A magnetic compass may be useless or give subtly wrong readings because the lattice is not oriented to it.  
- Respect that: don’t try to “correct” the world with your instruments.

## 4. Biological/psychological needs: living in a final sentence

### Timekeeping

Your internal clock will be confused by:

- No sunsets, no sunrises,  
- No wind noise, no weather patterns,  
- No circadian cues from the environment.

You must:

- Set **strict internal timetables** for sleep, rest, and survey cycles.
- Use **light regulation** in your helmet or habitat to simulate a cycle (even a faint one: 16–18 hours of “day” light, with dimming toward “night”).

### Visual noise and “sameness”

The plateau is not chaotic; it is *very* ordered—and that can make your brain’s pattern detector misfire:

- You might feel “the world is looking at me.”
- You might see faces where there are none.
- You might interpret the “glass maze” lichens as written code or messages.

Prepare:

- **Visual logs:** high-resolution images can help you see changes over days, but also ground you: “I see the same thing today as last week; the world hasn’t changed, I’m not hallucinating.”
- **Limit psychoactive substances:** anything that enhances your tendency to find patterns will be dangerous here.
- **Structured pilots:** if you travel with companions, assign each day to a specific pilot or mapping lead so you rotate focus and avoid fixation.

### Emotional resilience

A world that is “too finished” can be frightening:

- Some people respond to endless stability with *existential dread*.
- Others might become numb and apathetic.

Plan:

- **Short rotation missions:** decide ahead of time how long you will stay on Cael‑Himir and stick to the plan:  
  - Initial survey: 5–10 days.  
  - Extended observation: up to 3 months maximum, unless you are fully specialized and prepared.
- **Support from off-world contact:** regular check-ins with whoever you left back in your own multiverse.

### Physical health

- **Exercise regimen:** With no dramatic slopes, no strong weather, and no “local” tasks to fatigue you, you will need to enforce exercise:
  - Strength training to keep bone and muscle mass.
  - Cardiovascular training that doesn’t require steep slopes or sprinting.
- **Nutrition:**  
  - You cannot rely on local resources for food or water.  
  - Bring your own closed-cycle food system—no composting on the plateau surface (no dumping of biological waste onto the stone).

## 5. Science and exploration: what you’re suited to study

You are limited to **observation** and *non-disruptive* measurement.

### Geology and crystal lattices

- The **polygon and hex patterns** in the stone invite study:
  - Photogrammetry and high-resolution surface scanning.
  - No drilling cores: the lattice stresses are unknown and you might be releasing energy stored at the atomic level.
  - Use laser interferometry—from a distance—to probe surface features without contact.

### Artifacts and inscriptions

- The **disc-shaped depressions** and faint pillar nubs:
  - Treat them like extremely fragile inscriptions.
  - Do *not* scratch samples to “analyze later”; you’re more likely to be analyzing the consequences of a lattice fracture event than the sample itself.
- **Radial inscriptions:** lattice defects written into the stone.
  - Observe with:
    - Non-invasive tunnel imaging if you have access to such tech.
    - Polarized light filters to show subtle differences in crystal orientation.

### “Vegetation” and films

- The glass-maze “lichen”:
  - Do not break them to see inside.
  - Use optical sensors and advanced microscopes from your suit helmet.
  - Avoid contact: even if they seem inert to you, their logic is geological time.

- The **film fauna**: translucent sheets on the stone:
  - Do not attempt to scrape samples.
  - If you must map, keep a sensor package at least a few millimeters away from the surface.
  - Any attempt to remove “specimen” might be treated like the world’s version of removing a layer of coral.

### Fauna pillars and spheres

- **Pillars:**
  - Treat them like columns of folded reality.
  - Do not attach lines to them; do not try to climb them.
- **Spheres embedded in stone:**
  - Do not attempt to extract them.
  - If you are close to multiple spheres in a pattern, think of yourself as walking inside a body that’s not designed to accommodate you—and behave accordingly.

## 6. “Shadows of places”: overlapping reality and how not to tamper with it

The “shadows of places” are where you slide into a superposition of two versions of the plateau at once.

### How you might detect them

- The air seems “thicker” or more rigid than usual.
- Your footsteps sound differently (maybe a bit more echo, or nothing at all).
- There might be subtle visual distortions: your arm seems slightly out of place, or a faint outline of a pillar to your left when there is no pillar there.

These are not hallucinations; they are signs of unresolved reality.

### How to behave safely inside them

- **Move slowly and deliberately:** fast motion in a shadow can be like “printing” a decision on the world’s lattice.
- **Do not try to “interact with the version you see.”**
  - If you see “water” flowing in a shadow version, don’t reach out to it.
  - If you see “more trees” or taller pillars in the shadow version, don’t try to “step toward that reality.”
- **Keep your mission simple:**  
  - If you’re in a shadow zone, your purpose should be to observe, not to change.
  - Avoid *choosing* between “realities” by doing actions that clearly favor one version over the other (e.g., you don’t want to commit this world to being the “wet version” because you liked the river you saw).

### Observation and collapse

- Think of yourself as a *catalyst* not a *director*.
- The longer you linger in one shadow zone, the more likely you are to influence which reality solidifies in that region.
- If you’re mapping:
  - Mark shadow zones clearly.
  - Limit the time you spend in each.

## 7. Rules and prohibitions: what *never* to do here

### Never bring water to the stone

- Do not attempt:
  - To “test” hydration of the ground.
  - To wash gear.
  - To “offer” water to the world.

If you drop water:

- The crystal lattice of the plateau and its film fauna will try to incorporate it.
- The release of stored pressure energy may be more than your body or suit can handle.
- A “local” area might briefly “remember” water—and then very violently forget.

### No drilling, no chipping, no cutting

- Even “small” disruptions:
  - May undo millions of years of slow alignment.
  - May be interpreted by the plateau’s “system” as a wound you intend to heal.

If you absolutely need samples:

- Only *surface dust* that has already separated from the lattice, and only if you:
  - Keep it inside a sealed container.
  - Do not expose it to moisture or fluids other than what your sealed suit can handle.

### No leaving “legacy”

Do not:

- Carve your name into the stone.
- Install anchors, headlights, or base stations that you *cannot* remove.
- Leave “improvements” like heat sources, electromagnetic generators, or gravity modifiers.

Even if you think it’s “useful,” you’re adding verbs to a sentence that has already finished.

## 8. Return: leaving the plateau as you found it

### Cleanup protocol

- You must leave Cael‑Himir looking:
  - Exactly how you found it, to the limit of your perception.
- All:
  - Waste stays within your closed-cycle systems.
  - Equipment must be fully removed before you leave.
- Trails:
  - You can’t help leaving footprints or compressed stone dust.
  - Minimize the number of times you traverse the same path.

### Documentation and notes

- Your records of Cael‑Himir will be more valuable than any sample, because:
  - The place *cannot be repaired* if damaged.
  - Any attempt to extract “parts” of the plateau risks distorting the final state of the world.

Use:

- High-resolution imagery (without destructive lighting).
- Non-destructive scanning and spectral data.
- Logs of your subjective experiences, especially in shadow zones.

### Who you tell, and how

You cannot be sure that your next visitor will be as careful as you are.

- Consider:
  - Marking the coordinates of Cael‑Himir as a protected site.
  - Providing detailed warnings: “Do not drill, do not bring water, do not expect verbs.”

Not everyone from the multiverse will be so kind.

## 9. Final checklist: before you step out of the door

- **Environment suit:** pressure sealed, no external leaks of internal fluids.
- **Internal hydration system:** self-contained loop, no external ports open.
- **Timekeeping system:** artificial day-night cycles, alarms for sleep and work.
- **Mapping tools:** passive sensors, no invasive drills or probes.
- **Ground rules:**  
  - No water on the stone.  
  - No stepping on disc-shaped depressions.  
  - No leaning or climbing on pillars.
- **Psychological support:**  
  - Fixed mission length,  
  - Regular contact with home base,
  - A complete “inside helmet” environment you can retreat to when the world’s final sentence becomes too heavy to bear.

---

### A short summary to carry with you:

You are visiting a world that has already said everything it has to say. The only verbs you should bring are *watch*, *listen*, *learn*, and *leave*.

Everything else is better left undone.
