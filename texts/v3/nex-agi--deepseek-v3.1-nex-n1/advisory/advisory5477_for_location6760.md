To prepare for visiting this world is to prepare for a different *state of being* as much as a place. You are not going to a landscape; you are going to a slow, moist dream that is still knitting itself together from ocean and light.

Below is a traveler’s guide from the ground up: what you’ll need, what to expect in your own body and mind, and how to move safely in a world that is still forming itself.

---

## 1. Mindset: recalibrating your sense of reality

### A. The world is still assembling itself

This is not a metaphor.

- Landforms are not eroded; they are *fresh*.
- You will see stone that is jagged, porous, and unweathered; edges that still look like they were recently broken or forged.
- There are few “ancient” features: no rounded river valleys, no deeply carved cliffs, just raw shelves and nascent ridges and hollows.

You should not expect to find:
- deep cultural history,
- complex networks of roads and cities,
- countless layers of civilization built on one another.

Instead expect:
- a few fragile structures in the making,
- a sense that everything is temporary, *newly begun*, and still negotiating its shape with the sea.

### B. The ocean is not “around” you — it is the default medium

This world is almost entirely ocean:

- The ocean has “no floor and perhaps no far shore” — not in the sense of dropping into deep trenches, but in the sense that *water goes on and on*, like water-sky below and in places above.
- The land you are on is not “normal land with water around it”; rather, the water is the normal state of the world, and the land is a tiny anomaly.

This frame matters:
- Water will be *the* constant: you will feel it on your skin, in your lungs, in the soundscape, and in the way your mind slows down.
- Landforms are not guaranteed; you can’t assume distances, “continents,” or safe dry ground beyond what you see.

### C. Time and light are muted and stretched out

- Light is 1.1 of 3 — dim.
- Sun, when visible at all, is a diffuse disk behind continuous clouds.
- Dawns and dusks are long slow smearings of color and grayness.
- There are no sharp shadows or midday sun; you will never be “blasted” by light.

Impact:
- Your sense of time can blur easily.
- Depth perception is different: edges are softer, contours muted.
- Your circadian rhythm may struggle to find a clear day-night split.

Assume you will need extra help: artificial lighting, time-telling devices, and possibly synthetic light schedules to maintain sleep cycles.

## 2. Physical and mental health preparation

### A. Acclimatization to constant moisture

- Temperature: 2.5 of 3 — consistently warm and humid.
- The air lies against your skin like a damp towel.
- The air and water are almost the same temperature.

Expect that:
- Your body will never fully dry while you are outside.
- Breathing will feel heavier; not dangerously so, but more effortful, especially at first.
- Everything you own will be slightly damp unless you actively protect it.

**Recommendations:**

- Hydration but not overhydration:
  - Drink water regularly *before* you feel thirsty.
  - Check if your local physicians have recommendations on electrolyte intake for long-term humid conditions.
  - Avoid too much alcohol, which will dehydrate you even as you feel soaked.

- Airway:
  - If you have asthma or any sensitivity to humid environments or molds, bring:
    - nasal rinses or saline spray,
    - any prescribed inhalers or antihistamines,
    - a mask or filter for spores when you are in close indoor spaces.
- Skin:
  - Moisture-resistant ointments and barrier creams to prevent chafing and fungal issues.
  - Breathable, quick-drying base layers so you are not always wet against your skin.
  - Frequent changes of dry clothing in sealed storage (bags with dry silica gel packets).

### B. Psychological preparation for the low-contrast, slow world

- Light is low and diffuse.
- Sound is low — ocean breaths and soft rain hushing.
- Decisions are described as “thicker,” meaning your mind may slow just from the atmosphere.

This might feel:
- peaceful,
- placeless and timeless,
- or slightly ominous if you’re used to sharp edges and clear signals.

**Recommendations:**

- Create a **structure** for yourself:
  - Keep a schedule using artificial clocks and light.
  - Plan regular checkpoints each “day” (work, reading, meals, sleep), even if the light doesn’t give you clear cues.

- Sensory support:
  - Brighter light sources for your personal quarters (e.g., strong LED lamps or portable panels) to avoid low-light-induced depression or seasonal affective disorder-like symptoms.
  - If the soundscape is too quiet or monotonous, you may want familiar music or noise (subtle, non-intrusive) to help you feel time passing.

- Contact protocols:
  - If you’re prone to anxiety or isolation in low-stimulation environments, plan:
    - regular contact with home (messages, voice logs),
    - a daily journal,
    - a project or study to give you a sense of progress.

### C. Erosion is minimal — but fluids are everywhere

- The land is newly emerging; erosion is 0.4 of 3.
- Stone edges are sharp; porous rock holds pockets of water.

This means:
- You might walk on relatively jagged surfaces underfoot.
- Your skin might be exposed to hidden seawater in tiny stone cavities.
- Gravity is clearly described as similar (otherwise the platform wouldn’t be “high above the water”), so you can assume normal walking and stability.

**Recommendations:**

- Wear:
  - closed-toe footwear with good grip and puncture protection for walking on sharp porous rock.
- If you have any open cuts or wounds:
  - treat them before coming,
  - and keep waterproof dressings on them while you explore the island’s edges and lower levels.

## 3. Clothing and personal gear

### A. Footwear

- Primary: sturdy waterproof or quick-drying boots with good grip:
  - for jagged stone,
  - damp surfaces,
  - and mild contact with seawater along the island’s lower edges.
- Optional: sandals or amphibious shoes for:
  - shallow wading,
  - short incursions near the lapping edge of the city.

### B. Clothing layers

- Outermost layer:
  - A lightweight waterproof shell or coat.
    - Not intensely thick winter gear — the temperature is warm.
    - Look for “breathable” waterproof fabric to reduce sweat trapping.

- Mid layer:
  - A thin layer that can keep you comfortable if you get slightly chilled from dampness (e.g., a thin synthetic long-sleeve shirt).

- Base layer:
  - Synthetic or quick-drying fabric that does not stay cold when wet.
    - Avoid heavy cotton that becomes waterlogged and heavy.
    - Wool blends or quick-dry synthetics are better.

- Head and hands:
  - A brimmed hat or rain hood with good coverage, to keep rain off your face and neck.
  - Light gloves or fingerless gloves if you’re sensitive to damp chafing on your hands.

### C. Accessories and environment tools

- Lighting:
  - A headlamp or hands-free light:
    - the world is dim, and you need reliable light indoors or outside at night.
    - Red-light mode can help preserve your night vision if you go out to observe the ocean at night.
- Timekeeping:
  - A watch or device that doesn’t rely on external light cues.
    - Consider multiple alarms or “daylight” simulation systems to keep your body clock coherent.
- Protection:
  - Small, waterproof dry bags for:
    - electronics
    - documents
    - clothing changes
    - medicine
- Moisture management:
  - Silica gel packets or other desiccants for stored items.
  - A quick-dry towel.

## 4. Medicine and health supplies

Assume minimal access to advanced medical care in this young world; you should bring what you need to act self-sufficiently.

### A. Basic first aid

- Bandages and waterproof tape:
  - sharp stone edges can cause small cuts and scrapes.
- Antiseptic wipes or solutions.
- Sterile saline solution for eye rinsing if coastal spray irritates you.
- Basic pain relievers and anti-inflammatories.

### B. Environment-specific medicines

- Antifungal cream or powder:
  - constant moisture and warmth encourage fungal skin infections.
- Topical antibiotics for minor cuts that might be exposed to sea water.
- Oral antihistamines:
  - for possible allergies to new organic materials or spores in the damp.
- If you are prone to infections:
  - Consider consulting a physician before you travel and asking if you should bring any prescription antibiotics.

### C. Respiratory support

- If you have any respiratory history:
  - bring your prescribed inhalers or medications.
- If local humidity tends to be overwhelming for you:
  - small, portable humidifiers or dehumidifiers for your sleeping quarters might help, depending on your physiology.

### D. Psychological support

- Items you know calm or ground you:
  - photos, music playlists, books, or objects that keep you tied to your identity.
- If you have been prescribed any medications for anxiety, depression, or sleep disorders:
  - ensure you have a sufficient supply for your stay and possibly extra in case of unexpected delays.

## 5. On the platform itself: how to behave

### A. Movement around the city of bundled coral

The city is described as “bundled coral,” built on a young, high platform of fused stone and shell. The lower edge is always lapped by waves; the upper parts are rarely touched by water.

**Recommendations:**

- Walk slowly:
  - surfaces may be damp, slick, or uneven.
  - new landforms may not be fully surveyed or secured.

- Use caution near the lower edge:
  - the ocean is quiet and “patient,” but anyone can slip on a wet edge.
  - don’t assume a shallow drop; if you fall, you may fall into water whose depth is unknown in that exact spot.

- Follow the local structures:
  - if there are paths or walkways, they are likely there to protect you from the raw stone’s sharp edges.
  - if you see signs of “construction” or “new growth,” treat them as fragile and not fully tested.

### B. Sound and communication

- Sound levels: low — 1.3 of 3.
- The ocean is a long breathing low roll of water.
- Rain is a hushed shushing sound.
- Distances from where you can be heard are likely larger than you expect.

**Recommendations:**

- Voices:
  - speak softly; you will be heard clearly.
  - if you are shouting or playing loud music, it may carry across much of the platform.

- Consider:
  - carrying a small, waterproof signaling device or whistle:
    - for emergencies if you get separated from your group,
    - but realize that in this low-sound world, any sound will be noticeable and possibly disruptive to others who live quietly.

### C. Cultural and social sensitivity

- This world is young, with one of the first places where “things have begun to stick.”
- The inhabitants are probably building their identity and their structures from very little materials and very fragile conditions.

**Recommendations:**

- Assume:
  - everything is more delicate than it appears.
  - the coral structures are not only physical homes but possibly spiritual and symbolic.

- Ask:
  - before touching or entering coral structures,
  - before bringing samples of stone or coral home,
  - whether any areas are considered sacred or particularly fragile.

- Observe:
  - how locals handle time and decision-making:
    - “decisions become thicker” — they might work slower here, and expecting urgency might be jarring or misunderstood.
  - how they handle light:
    - you might be using bright artificial lights while others prefer the dim natural phosphorescence.

## 6. Working with the environment and resources

### A. Water and food

- The ocean is “raw ocean,” not explicitly described as safe freshwater.
- You are likely to be reliant on:
  - rainwater collection,
  - and whatever local systems the city already has for water purification and storage.

**Recommendations:**

- Bring:
  - small portable water purification tools or filters:
    - e.g., filters that can process collected rainwater or stored water,
    - chemical tablets or purifiers for emergency treatment.
- If food:
  - local food might be based on seafood, algae, or what little grows on the new platform.
  - bring your own long-life food supplies if you have specific nutritional needs or allergies.
    - store them in moisture-resistant containers.

### B. Electricity and tools

Assuming you rely on external devices:

- Power:
  - solar charging might be less effective than usual, given the low light level.
    - Consider:
      - energy-efficient devices,
      - backup battery packs,
      - perhaps manual or wind-up options if needed.
- Protection:
  - everything electronic should be stored in waterproof cases or bags with desiccants.

### C. Documentation and research

If you are interested in understanding this world scientifically:

- Bring:
  - small, waterproof notebooks,
  - waterproof pens or pencils,
  - camera with waterproofing or a case.
- Observe:
  - the sharpness of new landforms,
  - the presence of early life in algae on the pillars,
  - the few patterns in phosphorescence where light collects.
- Always:
  - take with care any samples you might collect.
    - Ask local inhabitants what you may remove,
    - and ensure what you touch is not a core part of the structure supporting the platform.

## 7. Emergency and survival planning

### A. Basic risks

- Slipping or falling:
  - on wet, jagged stone or near the platform edge.
- Fatigue:
  - from constant humidity and slow mental pace.
- Isolation:
  - from time blurring in the dim light.
- Skin or respiratory issues:
  - from mold spores, damp, or exposure to new organisms.

### B. Minimal plan

- Always have:
  - light with you when you go outside,
  - a signaling device like whistle,
  - waterproof first aid on your body,
  - small dry supplies of essentials:
    - extra medication,
    - water purification tablets,
    - energy bar or food packet.

- Leave:
  - your approximate schedule or location with someone:
    - if you are going out to explore the edges of the platform,
    - or descending to areas where waves are lapping.

- Return:
  - check in with a local contact or within your group if you are delayed.
    - In a low-sound world where voices carry, you might be easier to find if you call out calmly rather than panic.

## 8. The most important thing

Before anything else, understand: **you are walking into a universe that is still deciding its shape.**

You are not going to a hardened city built on centuries of earth. You are going into a small, vulnerable city of bundled coral on a young, high platform of raw stone and shell, rising out of an ocean that might go on forever without floor or far shore.

The best way to prepare is not just to pack waterproof bags and boots and lights and medicine—though you should—but to adopt an attitude:

- Move gently.
- Observe quietly.
- Assume that everything you touch is still forming:
  - not fragile in the sense of “glass,” but fragile in the sense of “fresh-baked clay before it has fired.”

You will be gently changed by being there:
- your senses of dryness and wetness, light and dark, stillness and motion, will be reset.
- your mind may slow to match the slow breathing of the ocean under the hushed rain.

If you meet it with preparation, humility, and curiosity, you will leave not only having visited a new place—you will have seen what a world looks like when its bones are still showing, and its skin is still water.
