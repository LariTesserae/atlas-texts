To walk into this place unprepared is to choose drowning and slow madness. It is not evil, but it is wildly indifferent, and its physics are not your own. Treat your preparations as if you were designing a life-support system inside a living glacier. You are going to be a ghost inside a body of water that has swallowed a mountain range.

Below is a structured approach to preparation: before travel, at arrival, and during the visit itself. I’ll assume you are an “ordinary human multiverse traveler”: you can reach this place, but once you are there you are still physically and mentally human.

---

## 1. Mindset Before You Go

### 1.1. Accept the core strangeness

Before you even pack, you need to internalize a few things that will violate intuition:

- **You are not going underwater. You are going into airless water-world.**  
  There is no surface to reach up to. The “air” of this world is water. The only breathing you will do is breathing the water with technology. If something fails, you do not surface to a ceiling; you treat it as a vacuum failure.

- **The forest is animate and connected to the storms.**  
  The trees breathe like lungs. Distant storms are not just weather; they are the planet’s or system’s “heartbeat,” and the forest shudders in response. You are not entering a dead ruin; you are entering an organism.

- **The light records memory.**  
  The glowing threads and crystal clouds are memory strands. Disturbing water is not just leaving ripples; you are leaving faint traces of yourself that can be stored and read back later.

- **You are the only animal.**  
  There is no prey, no predator, no ecosystem of animals. This simplifies the risk of attack but emphasizes the biological agony of being an alien in your own environment.

### 1.2. The purpose of your preparation

You are not trying to “survive” the ocean in the usual sense.  
You are trying to:

- Stay *warm* enough.
- Stay *pressured* correctly.
- Stay *supplied with oxygen and energy*.
- Stay *oriented* in dark vertical space.
- Stay *sane* in deep sensory overload and isolation.

Everything else is secondary.

## 2. Hard physics & environment: what you’re actually entering

### 2.1. Cold

- **Temperature close to freezing.**  
  Even if not technically below zero, it is cold enough that your bare body will lose heat dangerously fast in ordinary water.
- **Convection is not stagnant.**  
  The water is not still; cold moves with you. If you are not generating your own heat field, you will be a cold sink in a cold sink.

Implications: You need thermal insulation that *never* depends on “air” layer pockets. All insulation must work against constant water conduction.

### 2.2. Pressure & depth

From the description:

- Too high to be deep crushing abyss, but with enough pressure to be a constant presence.
- No clear surface floor or ceiling.

Given the “high glacier’s edge” and “drowned mountain range,” we can assume that you will be somewhere between perhaps shallow-to-moderate depth in terms of absolute pressure, but still enough to destabilize human body cavities if not equalized.

Key:

- Your **inner pressure** must closely match external **ambient water** pressure.
- Any equipment or suit containing gas at significantly different pressure than the water depth is a risk point.

### 2.3. Light / dark

- Lightning is not illumination; it is *outline and record*.
- The sky-glow is faint, “like clouded glass”.
- The drift-light threads pick up patterns from you.

Implications:

- Normal human eye gains will be limited.
- You need artificial light if you want to see clearly, but this must be designed to not blind you or destroy the fragile environment.
- Your light will be *an interaction with the world*—it will change local signals, wake portions of the forest, and record your presence.

### 2.4. Sensory environment

The sound is not peaceful silence; it is continuous:

- Rolling distant thunder of the storm.
- Resonant *whom*ing of leaves when currents move through them.
- Chorus of chord tones as you move between trees.
- Faint ticking of crystals and salinity clouds.

Most importantly:

- The pressure shifts of “a breath held at the edge of hearing” and the world feeling like it rearranges behind you.

This is not random noise. It is a phased environment—sound and pressure have meaning. You need to treat your soundscape as a data stream, not background noise.

## 3. Life support & physiological integration

You are not going to be walking with tanks and fins. This is not a dive; you are going to live in the water column as if it were a sky.

### 3.1. Breathing the water

You must not plan to carry gas-filled air tanks or lungs. The gas will try to compress or expand with pressure changes, and the risk of catastrophic collapse or expansion is too high.

Better concept:

- **Implant-based or wearable gill system.**

Some possible configurations:

1. **Internal bio-engineered respiration**
   - Artificial gills implanted in your chest and/or sides:
     - Fine mesh of semi-permeable membranes across which oxygen dissolved in the water diffuses into your bloodstream.
     - The same membranes vent out CO₂.
   - Assisted by:
     - A small wearable device that regulates the path of water across the gill surfaces with gentle pumps and filters.
   - Benefits:
     - No large gas-filled spaces.
     - Internal pressure equalization almost automatic.
     - You can “breathe water” comfortably.

2. **If you must keep external pressure**
   - Full-body pressure suit that:
     - Is filled with *breathing liquid* (not air) which is tuned to your body’s chemistry and pressure requirements.
     - Contains gill filtration modules at the surface of the suit.
   - More cumbersome and failure-prone, but more familiar if you’re from a world with advanced diving tech.

Either way:

- Avoid gas pockets.  
  If there are any gas-filled spaces (e.g., for sensors, electronics, buoyancy control), they must be small and carefully managed with pressure compensation diaphragms.

### 3.2. Thermal regulation

Combined with cold, water’s thermal conductivity makes you an easy victim of hypothermia.

You need:

1. **Full-body active heating layer**
   - A thin, full-body undergarment that conducts and generates heat:
     - Powered by:
       - A compact fuel cell or battery core.
       - Or possibly bioenergy from your own body (with assistance from small nutrient reactors).
   - Integrated thermistors:
     - Monitor core temperature and adjust heat generation.
     - Automatically increase heating if ambient temperature drops.

2. **Outer layer with low thermal conductivity**
   - Not necessarily thick; just resistant to heat transfer, so you don’t lose all generated heat to the water.

3. **Backup heat packs**
   - Small chemical heating pads or re-crystallizable salt packets for manual activation if primary system fails.

### 3.3. Fluid & nutrient balance

You will not eat or drink in the usual sense.

Consider:

- **Water-breathing system with integrated nutrient delivery**
  - Depending on your world’s technology level, either:
    - Directly exchangeable water-soluble nutrients into your bloodstream.
    - Or a small intraperitoneal or intravenous drip via your gill system.
  - Make sure your electrolytes remain balanced for:
    - Cold exposure.
    - Long-term immersion.

- **Efficiency of nutrient supply**
  - Since you are not expending energy to stand, walk, or fight gravity, your metabolic needs may be lower than for land hiking.
  - Your system must still deliver enough calories to:
    - Stay alert.
    - Maintain temperature.
    - Handle stress and sensory load.

## 4. Movement & orientation in a vertical water-world

### 4.1. Buoyancy and neutral weight

In open water column, you want to be *neutrally buoyant* in the depth you intend to spend most time, but able to shift buoyancy over small range.

- Your **gill suit or system** should include:
  - A small buoyancy controller:
    - Uses high-density fluid or small solid weights to tune your position in the water column.
    - Not air-filled bladders.

- **Balance carefully**
  - You want to be just enough “negative” to sink gently if you stop moving, but not so much that you are uncomfortable to lift.
  - This allows you to rest at semi-neutral drift without constant effort.

### 4.2. Propulsion

Swim-fins are not very good for precise maneuvering among delicate structures.

- **Better:** a compact *underwater jetpack* or *micro-thrusters*
  - Mounted on your back or belt:
    - Axis control:
      - Roll, pitch, yaw.
    - Speed modes:
      - Very slow creep for close inspection of ruins and leaves.
      - Moderate speed for travel between terraces.
    - Should be:
      - Almost silent.
      - Low-displacement so you don’t tear the environment.

### 4.3. Orientation and navigation

This is where you will likely go insane if unprepared.

- No clear “up/down” floor or ceiling.
- Distances hard to judge because vision is *limited*.
- Light is scattered and not illuminating.

You need:

- **Internal navigation system (INS)**  
  - Integrates:
    - Onboard inertial sensors (acceleration, rotation).
    - Environmental data: sound, pressure gradients, temperature.
  - Additionally:
    - A small, *field-safe* sonar module:
      - Never strong enough to damage the forest leaves or crystal threads.
      - Used to build a map of your immediate surroundings: tree stems, terraces, etc.

- **Visual HUD**
  - Overlay of:
    - Your vertical position (from INS).
    - “Up” vs. “down” defined relative to your current reference frame.
    - Direction of storm.
    - Marked safe zones you have already visited.

- **Subjective line**
  - Always maintain a tether line or path:
    - Say to a known platform or anchor-point.
    - Or if free-floating, a path of periodic markers—you will not simply return the way you came if you just turn back; you will drift.

### 4.4. Managing open space

The open water column is not safe to just float. It is a *void*.

- **Caution**
  - Always be near *structure*:
    - Either the tree stems, terraces, or something else that you can steady against.
- **Depth**
  - Avoid sudden deep descents or ascensions that would change pressure quickly.
    - Your suit should autoregulate equalization, but still you don't want to create pressure gradients faster than it can respond.

## 5. Sensory protection & sanity management

This is such a deeply alien environment that you must take care of your mind as carefully as your body.

### 5.1. Visuals

- The environment is not styled to human eyes.
- You will be surrounded by faint, glowing, leaf-lungs, and ghostly shapes.

**To protect your sanity:**

- **Adjustable light filter visor**
  - Not too dark, but capable of:
    - Removing low-frequency noise or light artifacts.
    - Pairing with your INS system to show you:
      - True structural geometry vs. ghostly afterimages.
  - Optional:
    - Night mode which:
      - Enhances contrast of outlines that you need to navigate.
      - Does not flood the world with bright white.

### 5.2. Sound and pressure

Sound will be everywhere and not easy to ignore.

- Design **sound moderation** rather than silence:
  - Not necessarily a full “sound canceling” system, which would be disorientating, but:
    - Gentle filtering of specific frequencies that:
      - Trigger panic or vertigo.
    - Allow key environmental cues:
      - The storm rumble.
      - Changes in tree hums.

- **Haptic feedback**
  - Instead of relying on audio cues:
    - Small vibration pads on your palms or chest:
      - Translate pressure changes and low-frequency sounds into vibrations you feel.
    - Helps you:
      - Anticipate storm pulses.
      - Feel your way along the environment.

### 5.3. Cognitive load

- Limit visit length.
- **Pre-set cognitive anchors**
  - Establish:
    - A clear “mission”.
    - A clear staging base.
    - An exit signal.
    - Ration of how long you will be in the field:
      - Example: Only 6–8 hours at first.

### 5.4. Narrative restraint

- **Do not** assume:
  - The forest is aware of you in a way that wants to help or hurt.
    - It doesn't think in those terms.
    - It will respond and react to your presence like a system; nothing more or less.
- **Do not** read intense emotional intent into:
  - The forest rearranging itself behind you.
    - It is likely just:
      - Shifting leaves drifting.
      - Currents realigning.
      - Your memory-light being captured by the threads.

Interpret things as *systematic* rather than personal. This will help your sanity more than anything else.

## 6. Interaction with the environment: what to do and not do

### 6.1. The forest as not-plant

- **Do not assume vegetation**
  - The leaves are not just leaves; they are membrane organs.
- **Approach with minimal contact**
  - If you must touch:
    - Use glove or fingertips, not full body weight.
    - Expect *whom* sound and pressure waves.
    - Do not yank or tear.
    - If a leaf shudders noticeably:
      - Back away and give it time to return to normal pattern before continuing.

- **Expect symmetry**
  - If you touch one leaf, expect the response to propagate:
    - Up and down the tree.
    - Possibly to neighboring trees.

### 6.2. The crystal threads and clouds

- **Do not grab or collect**
  - These are not pretty stones to take back.
  - They are memory:
    - They might carry your pattern after you pass close.
    - You might damage the stored patterns of the world itself.
    - If your system must sample:
      - Use passive sensors:
        - Cameras, non-invasive spectrometers, etc.
        - No physical breakage.

- **Light carefully**
  - If you must shine light:
    - Keep it:
      - As low intensity as possible.
      - Away from direct crystal threads.
    - Best:
      - Use indirect or reflected light.

### 6.3. The stone terraces and ruins

- Respect erosion:
  - Any stone that you grab hold of:
    - May be near its point of equilibrium.
    - If you break it:
      - The world has not in “moral” sense condemned you;
      - But you will have removed data points that were not meant to be removed.

- **As safe playgrounds**
  - These terraces are likely your safest physical shelters:
    - A place to:
      - Rest against.
      - Set temporary instruments.
      - Anchor a line.

### 6.4. “Memory of you”

- Remember that you leave:
  - **A ghost pattern**
    - Pressure of your body moving.
    - Displacement of water currents.
    - Slight temperature change.

The world will not keep you alive; but it might keep your *shape* for a while after you go.

- **Ethical consideration**
  - If there are remnants of previous people’s memories:
    - Do not assume:
      - Your presence is entitled to overwrite them.
    - Adjust:
      - Where you spend most time.
      - How you move.
