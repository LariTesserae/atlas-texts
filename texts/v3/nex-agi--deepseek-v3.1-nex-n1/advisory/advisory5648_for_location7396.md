To walk into the basin is like stepping into the body of a convalescent planet: still brittle from fever, only beginning to sweat a new kind of life. You will not need armor or spells here so much as an unusual kind of discipline—slowness, precision, and a very cautious respect for edges.

Below is one way to prepare, if you are an ordinary human traveler crossing between worlds.

---

## 1. Before You Jump: Physical Prep

### 1.1. Feet and shoes

This is the most important piece of gear.

The ground is not like sand, snow, or rock: it is a field of broken glass plates with tiny edges and micro fractures.

- **Boots**
  - Use sturdy, high-top boots with:
    - Thick soles (preferably rubber compound with high puncture resistance).
    - No air channels or mesh cutting down where you step.
    - No soft seams or exposed stitching on the bottom.
  - Lace them tightly. The ideal is full support with no slip inside the boot.
- **Insoles**
  - Add hard, rigid insoles. If you can get custom orthotics or even thin metal inserts, do it.
  - Thin insoles or none at all = bending pressure on small glass points under the sole of your foot.
- **Protection**
  - Consider wearing thin metal plates or shanks over the toes and under the arch area of your boot, if they are securely attached.
  - If you don’t mind looking a bit unusual, wrap your lower legs with hard leather or plastic guards in case you slip and a sharp edge rises up your shin.

You will be stepping on something like billions of tiny knife-edges covered by a thin film of water or a fine layer of dust. Proper boots can be the difference between a difficult but walkable world and a place of constant wounds.

### 1.2. Clothing and protection

- **Base layer**
  - Moisture-wicking: long underwear and sleeves you can roll up if needed.
  - Non-cotton. The basin is damp with mist and condensation. You want to be dry, not cold and wet.
- **Outer layer**
  - Waterproof shell (top and pants) with sealed seams.
  - The mist carries fine mineral particles. If they get into your clothing layer, they will be gritty and persistent.
- **Gloves**
  - Thick but dexterous gloves:
    - If you fall, you may instinctively catch yourself on the ground.
    - If you need to move a sharp plate of glass to avoid a fissure, you do not want bare skin.
  - Consider gloves with reinforced palms.
- **Eye protection**
  - Essential.
  - Light mist plus fine mineral dust plus a reflective dark surface = bad for your eyes.
  - Wear:
    - Close-fitting goggles or a mask with wide lenses, if possible.
    - Something anti-fog: if your goggles fog up while you are crossing a basin where every step is potentially razor sharp, you will be in trouble.
- **Head covering**
  - A hood or wide brim hat to protect your head from:
    - Prolonged mist exposure.
    - Very subtle but relentless condensation on the scalp.
    - The occasional falling shard or brittle plate dislodging somewhere above you.

### 1.3. Body and health

- **General conditioning**
  - This place is not about high-altitude endurance or sprint speed.
  - It is about slow, precise walking over unstable surfaces for long hours.
  - Practicing:
    - Balance work on unstable surfaces.
    - Low-intensity walking for long stretches.
- **Foot care before**
  - Trim toenails carefully.
  - Any minor cuts or blisters should be healed before you arrive.
    - The ground is not clean rock in the usual sense; there are faint residues, minerals, and possibly unknown chemistry in the moisture.
- **Hydration and warmup**
  - Even though you’ll be surrounded by water in the air and on the ground, you’ll still lose liquid through exertion and breathing.
    - Start very hydrated.
  - Do some basic joint warmups before and after each passage of walking:
    - Hips, knees, ankles.
    - Falling on all that broken glass is one of the worst outcomes you can have.

## 2. Navigation and Timekeeping

### 2.1. No night or day; you will need your own time markers

The sun never fully appears. The basin is in a perpetual twilight, with muted light conditions.

- **Lights**
  - Bring:
    - Headlamp with adjustable brightness and red mode (to avoid spoiling your night vision).
    - Backup battery or light source.
    - Ideally waterproof.
- **Time instruments**
  - A reliable watch, preferably a simple rugged mechanical or digital one with no digital screens you can’t lose if broken.
  - Consider marking passage of time in your notebook:
    - “Left ridge pillar at tick mark 1, region of first table, 04:00 internal ship/body clock.”
    - This helps later reconstruct where you were when your sense of time blurs.

Keep in mind that in this world, **your own habits** define the structure of your day. You need sleep and rest breaks whether you can see the sun or not.

### 2.2. Mental and situational mapping

Because of the low ridges and low berms, visibility is not far in any direction:

- **Markers**
  - Bring durable colored markers or tape (if tape is allowed; you shouldn’t leave plastic residue in the environment).
  - Alternatively:
    - Construct temporary markers from small stones that you retrieve from your own pack (not likely local; stone is not abundant here).
    - Use small metal tags or non-permanent signs you can pick up later.
- **Orientation**
  - Use the faint line of the “mountains” (the ring of ridges) as a background:
    - You are inside the crater basin; they are the outer wall.
    - If the world is truly a basin, you should always be able to infer where center and edge are.
  - Rule:
    - In any moment of confusion, walk to higher ground (if the slope of glass is safe).
    - From the top of any low rise, you may see one pillar or stone slab table or a more noticeable landmark you can use to triangulate.

Static GPS and compasses may be unreliable or meaningless here depending on how alien the world’s magnetic and gravitational fields are. You should bring:

- A simple magnetic compass and a spare.
- Also note:
  - The direction of the wind tends to be fairly steady (long, slow breaths of the basin).
  - The prevailing mist movement (from which direction moisture tends to arrive).
    - These can be local “indicators” of direction after you watch them long enough.

### 2.3. Distance and fatigue

The ground is not steep, but it is **constant**. You will tiptoe over brittle surfaces, avoiding gaps:

- **Expect short days**
  - You may not travel more than a few kilometers in a day of walking.
  - Do not plan your camp-to-camp distances based on flat road standards.
- **Factor**
  - Surface instability + constant fear of falling on glass edges + low visibility + the mental pressure of the endless sound = early fatigue.

You will feel more spent than your usual walking distance would suggest.

## 3. Gear: What to Bring (and What Not to Bring)

### 3.1. Essentials pack list

- **Shelter**
  - A durable tent with a waterproof floor:
    - Preferably not a superlight ultra thin fabric; you may be placing it on sharp edges or very fine granules of his glass.
    - Use a footprint; expect minor punctures anyway.
  - Or:
    - A lightweight bivy and an insulated sleeping pad.
    - The pad protects you from the glass while you sleep.
- **Sleeping system**
  - Even if the air temperature is not freezing, the glass surface and the mist will be **cold**.
  - Use:
    - A temperature-rated sleeping bag.
    - Waterproof compression bag to keep your sleeping gear dry if you pack falls into water.
    - Bedding:
      - Heavy wool blanket or emergency layer:
        - The kind of thing that keeps warm even if damp.
- **Water filters**
  - You will be surrounded by water:
    - Films on the glass.
    - Small pools in depressions.
    - Possibly spring-like seepages from deeper cracks.
  - But:
    - The water is saturated with minerals from the glass and the ground.
  - You will need:
    - A high-quality filter system that can handle **fine mineral sediment**.
    - Possibly:
      - Chemical treatment tablets + filter.
      - Testable method:
        - A simple test kit or sensor strip (if available) to check mineral content before relying on it long term.
  - **Caution**:  
    - The water here is not likely to be pure “pure” H₂O. It is likely a slightly mineral-rich solution.
    - Even if you control biological contamination, high mineral content can cause long-term health risks if used as your only source of drinking and cooking water.
    - Consider:
      - Limiting how long you stay in this world.
      - Switching to another world if your water supplies are low and you have not thoroughly tested the basin’s water.

- **Food**
  - Calorie dense, dehydrated and vacuum sealed meals:
    - Low preparation time.
    - Less water usage.
  - Lots of nuts, seeds, chocolate or other high-energy snacks.
  - Pack:
    - Strong re-sealable containers.
    - The environment is damp and misty; food packaging must be moisture-proof.

- **Basic tools**
  - A fixed-blade knife with a solid full-tang handle:
    - You can use it as a pry or as a sort of probe, but mainly be aware of blade stress if you are prying on hard glass.
    - Treat it as a tool but not an infinite lever.
  - Sturdy multi-tool.
  - First-aid kit:
    - Comprehensive:
      - Antiseptics.
      - Sterile gauze and pads.
      - Adhesive bandages.
      - Splints and wrap for sprains or cracks.
      - Analgesics for pain.
      - Eye wash solution (because of the mist and mineral dust).
    - Assume wounds:
      - Glass cuts.
      - Abrasions.
      - Possibly falls from slipping on wet surfaces.

### 3.2. Recording and observation gear

Because you cannot be sure what is “normal” in this basin, you should document:

- **Notebooks and pencils**
  - Pencil, not pen:
    - It still writes under damp conditions.
    - No ink to run if the paper gets wet.
  - Waterproof notebook if possible.
- **Simple instrument set**
  - If you have access to basic standardized measurement:
    - Thermometer.
    - Small hygrometer (to measure humidity; this basin is very moist).
    - Optional: basic barometer or watch with barometric function:
      - The pressure swell of that “breathing” sound may be trackable.
  - Even if you don’t have high-tech tools:
    - A notebook plus a pencil plus a ruler or string can be used to:
      - Track the position of the wind whistles.
      - Track the size of a new crack in the glass.
      - Track the faint lattice of mineral crystals that you may observe day over day.

### 3.3. What not to bring (or what to be very careful with)

- Anything fragile that:
  - Cannot take being dropped on glass.
  - Can be cracked by a sudden vibration of a plate under you.
- Electronics:
  - If you must bring:
    - Protect them in waterproof and padded cases.
    - Assume the mist environment is not ideal.
    - Assume any accidental submerging in water will be immediately mineral-rich and conductive; corrosion may be rapid.
    - Back up data to other devices or world-based storage if possible.

## 4. Surviving the Environment: Sensory Adaptation

### 4.1. Light and the “twilight”

The dim, diffuse light will affect your mood:

- **Expect**
  - Long gray “days” and no clear night break.
  - Eyesight fatigue:
    - Objects are not sharply illuminated by direct sun, but by the dimly reflected light from the cloud cover.
  - Depth perception:
    - The uniformly black surface of wet glass can be deceptive:
      - A small puddle of wet film can look like deep water.
      - A deep crack can be hidden by the reflections of the sky.

Mitigation:

- Use your own light thoughtfully:
  - Keep your headlamp on low (not bright full beam).
  - Use the red light or dim light:
    - To preserve your night vision.
    - To keep your eyes from adjusting too drastically to full artificial brightness.
  - Make sure you:
    - Stop periodically and scan the horizon and near ground with your own eyes:
      - The pattern of reflected light on the water surface is actually one of your better navigation tools.

### 4.2. Sound and the “breathing” basin

The world has a deep continuous tone that feels like bone vibration rather than sound. You will feel it before you hear it clearly.

- **Expect**
  - Long-cycle breaths of sound (minutes).
    - You may not notice consciously at first, but your body might:
      - More relaxed at some moments of its cycle.
      - Tense at others.
    - Over time, this can contribute to fatigue or even disorientation.
- **Protection**
  - Ear protection:
    - Soft earplugs or more than noise canceling headphones:
      - These are less to block noise than to give you a baseline:
        - You can remove them temporarily to listen fully to the environment.
  - Be aware:
    - The constant sound background will be like:
      - A low hum in a machine room.
    - You may feel:
      - Slightly dizzy if you listen for too long without a break.

On especially active days—when more plates crack or the wind is stronger—you might hear:

- Metallic ringing.
- High-pitched cracks.
- These will be somewhat louder than you expect from such distance.

Important:

- **Sound** is not just a background; it can be a warning:
  - If the tone suddenly rises or drops.
  - If you hear a series of close cracks:
    - If may mean:
      - A plate is under sudden stress somewhere nearby.
    - You should:
      - Move away from the direction of that sound.
      - Watch for surface fractures that are new and unstable underfoot.

## 5. Walking on Glass: How Not to Die

### 5.1. Basic step discipline

- **Foot placement**
  - Always test:
    - Put your foot lightly and then transfer weight slowly:
      - Listen for subtle vibrations or flexing of the plate under you.
      - Watch for visible movement of the surface.
  - Aim:
    - For thicker-looking plates and:
      - More stable areas where:
        - The surface is not fragmented into tiny blades.
  - Avoid:
    - Walking directly on the very edge of:
      - Large plates near be fractures.
      - The “rim” of a glass table or artifact structure.

- **Balance**
  - Keep your center of gravity low:
    - Slightly bent knees.
    - Not walking fully upright as if on a sidewalk.
  - Use:
    - A trekking pole or walking staff:
      - Useful:
        - To test the ground in front of you.
        - To provide extra support in case of a slip.
  - But:
    - Do not rely solely on the pole:
      - It could be that:
        - If you push strongly with a pole on an edge, it suddenly breaks loose.

### 5.2. Dangerous features

- **Deep fractures**
  - Some cracks:
    - Look like gentle lines on the surface.
    - But actually:
      - Dropped deeply inside where water seeps up.
  - How to detect:
    - Look for:
      - Persistent mist or vapor rising from a line in the glass.
      - A darker band than its surroundings.
    - Also:
      - Pay attention to:
        - Sound of running water,
        - Faint echoes of your own voice.
  - Always:
    - Avoid stepping directly on or near the very edge of such lines.
    - Always give a wide berth.

- **Pools and low depressions**
  - Water can:
    - Conceal:
      - Sharp surfaces below.
    - Or:
      - Show that:
        - A plate there is very thin if you can see through the glass to a void below.
  - Approach cautiously:
    - Test:
      - How thick the glass is under the water.
      - If you must step into:
        - Shallow pools:
          - Step:
            - As lightly as possible.
    - Avoid:
      - Walking through:
        - Pool of unknown depth.
    - If you:
      - Absolutely need to cross:
        - Look:
          - If there is:
            - A thicker rim of glass around the edge.
            - Water over:
              - A thicker plate rather than:
                - A thin shiny film on glass above a void.

### 5.3. Heat and temperature

Though it feels like the world has cooled, there are still warm springs hidden beneath:

- Watch for:
  - Areas with:
    - Slightly warmer surface near the ground.
    - More mist rising.
    - Possibly:
      - Slightly melted ice if you placed any small object there.
- Use:
  - Your hands:
    - Feel gently on the surface:
      - If you need to test whether the ground is warm.
    - But:
      - Never:
        - Put your hand directly into deep cracks or under water in such areas.
    - There may be:
      - Powerful steam.
    - Or:
      - Water temperature high enough to scald.

### 5.4. Use of artifacts and pillars

The pillars and table-like slabs are stable enough but don’t assume they are floors:

- Don’t:
  - Stand directly on top of:
    - The black table slabs or the pillars.
    - They are:
      - Old structures not designed to be your sidewalk.
  - Touch:
    - The surface with:
      - Bare skin:
        - Consider:
          - The surface may be:
            - Slightly corrosive or:
            - Reactive with human body moisture.
          - Or:
            - Extremely smooth but:
              - Cold and wet.

Instead:

- Think:
  - They are landmarks.
    - Use them:
      - For:
        - Orientation.
    - But not:
      - As benches or seats or:
      - Secure anchors to hold you.
