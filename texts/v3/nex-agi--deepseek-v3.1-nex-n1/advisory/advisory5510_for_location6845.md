You are not meant to be here.

That is the first thing to understand.

This is not a “destination” in the usual sense—there is no local hospitality, no safety net, no law, no medicine, no infrastructure. The plateau is not hostile to you personally; it’s simply indifferent. It does not hate you. It does not know you’re alive.

Think of it like this: you are stepping inside the engine of a starship before it has cooled down. The engine isn’t *evil*, it just has not been configured for passengers yet.

Your preparation needs to reflect that.

---

## 1. Big Picture: What This Place Is (and Isn’t)

Before you think about gear, think about what kind of world you’re going to.

**It is:**

- Very young geologically.
- Extremely dry: *no surface water*, no rain, no groundwater.
- Seismically restless: the ground moves slowly, like a muscle under skin.
- Dark: low, constant “dusk” with no stars or sunrise.
- Isolated: a plateau of stone floating above a roaring abyss.
- Mostly empty of life as you know it—but not empty of pattern, chemistry, or slow, strange processes.

**It is not:**

- A planet in the finished sense. Continents, oceans, and stable climate are not in place yet.
- A place with breathable air in the long term.
- A place where anything decays. Dust is rock dust, not rot.
- A place where you can leave a trace and expect it to last unchanged.

This is a world before the world. Your body is not adapted to it in any way.

## 2. Mindset Before You Step

Prepare yourself mentally for this trip *before* you physically prepare.

### 2.1. Attitude: You Are the Anomaly Here

You will not be “exploring ruins” or “finding lost cities.” There are no ruins to find.

You will feel:

- Like a vandal near the spires and crystal patterns.
- Like an intruder near the edge of the plateau.
- Like you are interrupting a very slow conversation the stone is having with itself.

This is not paranoia. It is a useful humility.

**Mental stance:**

- Don’t assume safe surfaces.
- Don’t assume that “quiet” means “stable.”
- Don’t assume that if you can walk there once, it will be the same next time.

Take the attitude of an emergency worker entering a factory still under construction: constantly aware that the rules are not all written down.

---

### 2.2. Grounding Yourself: Time Scales and Perception

This place will distort your sense of time and memory.

- **Time scale of the plateau:** centuries, millennia.  
  You notice change over *hours* in a subtle way—the fault that shifted by a fingernail’s width, the ridge that wasn’t there yesterday.
- **Your time scale:** seconds, minutes, days.

You will start to question whether that crack really moved since you last saw it or if you misremembered.

To prepare for this:

- Decide *before* you go that you will not trust your unaided memory over long durations.
- Commit, from the outset, to record and time-stamp everything you can.

To avoid losing yourself in the strangeness and constant “almost-but-not-quite-change,” you must treat observation like a science: measure, record, repeat.

---

### 2.3. Emotional Expectations: Isolation and Imminence

It will be emotionally odd:

- No sky, only a violet lid.
- No sound of life, just a roaring abyss below.
- No sense of “arrival” at a destination—this place doesn’t orient you like a city would.

You will feel the imminence of something: *something* is forming, but not you, and not on your schedule.

This can be lonely or unnerving. Prepare for that in advance:

- Set a clear mission for yourself:  
  - “I am going to observe and record a specific ridge and three spires.”  
  - Not: “I am going to find out what this place is.”
- Decide in advance how long you will stay and under what conditions you will leave.
- Have someone back home (or your equivalent) you will report to *verbally* afterward, to help decompress and recontextualize what you’ve seen.

## 3. Protecting Your Body: The Environment

### 3.1. Atmosphere and Breathing

**What you know:**

- The air is thin, like high altitude.
- It is warm, not cold.
- No obvious toxic gases you can smell—but it is not *Earth* air.

Assume you cannot rely indefinitely on the ambient atmosphere.

**Recommendations:**

1. **Bring supplementary oxygen or a closed-circuit system.**  
   - A rebreather or mask with oxygen reserve can be critical if you’re exerting yourself.
   - You don’t need to use it constantly, but be ready to use it if you:
     - Climb or walk quickly.
     - Feel short of breath, light-headed, or confused.
     - Suffer chest tightness.

2. **Test in controlled steps.**  
   - When you arrive, don’t immediately sprint.  
   - Move slowly at first and monitor:
     - Breathing rate.
     - Heart-rate.
     - Head clarity.

3. **Monitor for signs of altitude-like symptoms:**  
   - Headache.
   - Nausea.
   - Dizziness.
   - Reduced coordination.  
   If these appear and you cannot rest and recover quickly, assume the environment is unsuitable for you in the long term and pull out.

### 3.2. Temperature and Climate

**Conditions:**

- The air is warm.
- Ground is faintly luminous, possibly lightly radiating.
- No dramatic weather yet—no storms, rain, or deep cold—but there is heat from below.

**Prepare for:**

- **Thermal regulation, not warmth.**  
  You’re more likely to overheat than freeze, especially if you wear insulated gear without ventilation.
- **Minimal sweating loss.**  
  You won’t be drenched, but you will lose moisture through breathing and skin.

**Practical clothing:**

- Light layers you can open and close:
  - Base layer that wicks moisture.
  - Mid-layer that can be removed and stored.
  - Outer shell that protects from dust and abrasion.
- Avoid heavy insulation; you’re not going to a cold world.
- Ventilation zippers or openings are important, especially if you’re exerting yourself.

**Helmet/visor:**

- A clear faceplate or helmet is not just for oxygen—it is for:
  - Protecting from dust if the ground shifts suddenly.
  - Protecting from low-level radiation/thermal discomfort from warm rocks.
  - Psychological barrier: the sense of being inside a suit helps you remember you are in a foreign system.

---

### 3.3. Water and Food: The Biggest Problem

This is the most dangerous part: **there is no surface water.**

Your body is the only significant source of water in the local system.

**Your hydration needs:**

- Assume you need *at least* your baseline body requirements:
  - Roughly 2 liters per day for an inactive adult in normal conditions.
- More if you are moving all day:
  - Plan for 3–4 liters per day, depending on your build and exertion level.

**Nutrition strategy:**

- Bring food that doesn’t demand water to digest or process:
  - High-energy, low-fiber options.
  - Low-sodium to avoid increasing thirst.
- Avoid very salty foods like survival rations that are designed for cold environments where you melt snow.

**Recommendations:**

- **Water reserve:**  
  - Carry enough water for your entire planned stay *plus* a buffer for emergencies:
    - Example: if you plan for 3 days, carry at least 4–5 days worth if you can.
  - Use sealed containers; don’t assume anything can be stored safely exposed.

- **No relying on “collecting” water:**  
  - There is no dew to harvest.
  - There is no subsurface water you can access.
  - Don’t plan to extract moisture from the air with your gear; the air is thin and dry.

- **Strict self-discipline:**
  - Drink regularly even if you don’t feel thirsty.
  - Set a timer or reminders to drink at fixed intervals.
  - If you feel any signs of dehydration:
    - Thirst, dry mouth, dark urine, reduced urine output, fatigue, dizziness.
    - You immediately reduce your activity level and start controlled drinking.
    - If symptoms don’t improve quickly, you *leave the plateau*.

This system cannot support you in the way Earth does. You are on your own finite stores.

---

### 3.4. Dust and Structural Integrity

**Ground surface:**

- Fragile crust of powder-like dust over rock.
- Dust very fine, like moondust.

**Risks:**

- If you fall, you will inhale or otherwise disturb a fine, mineral dust.
- Long-term exposure to rock dust can be risky:
  - Even a short visit, you don’t want to inhale deeply.

**Protections:**

- Dust mask or filter in your breathing system.
- Goggles that seal properly against your face.
- Outer suit you can brush off before you open it to eat or drink.

**Structural risk:**

- The ground is not fully stable:
  - Faults shift quietly.
  - New ridges and cracks appear.

**Prevention:**

- Always step deliberately, test your footing.
- Avoid areas with obvious recent fractures.
- Don’t set anything heavy directly over a fault line you can see.

---

### 3.5. Radiation and Light

**Conditions:**

- No direct sunlight, but:
- Ground faintly luminous.
- Sky faint auroral lines, possibly electromagnetic.
- Some internal heat from below.

**Even if you don’t feel radiation, you cannot assume it is not there.**

**Recommendations:**

- A basic dosimeter is not overkill:
  - Check it before you leave and if levels show anything unusual, you add time limits.
- **Limit time near the hottest areas:**  
  - The southern ridges with internal orange heat.
  - Those areas may be more thermally and radiation-active.

You don’t need a full lead suit, but you should be more cautious than you think you need to be at first glance.

## 4. Navigation and Movement on the Plateau

### 4.1. The Problem with Landmarks

This is not a world that stays put.

- **Rib structures:** run in parallel, which helps you form a grid—but they are repetitive and can be disorienting.
- **Ground is *restless*:**  
  - Faults shift.
  - A new ridge you didn’t mark can appear.
  - You might not feel sure about landmarks you’re not actively recording.

**You cannot rely on “just remembering the way.”**

**Solutions:**

1. **Use physical markers you can rely on.**  
   - Lightweight, reflective, uniquely colored stakes you drive into the ground.  
   - But: even these may be moved by tectonic movement if you stay long enough.

2. **Use GPS if your technology permits.**  
   - Record the coordinates of:
     - Entry point.
     - Basecamp.
     - Any spires or anomalies you study.
   - Note that in this young system, coordinate mapping might be slightly unstable over time scales, but still useful on short-term.

3. **Always have a known return vector:**  
   - Immediately upon arriving, decide:
     - “If I become completely unsure of my position, I will move [this cardinal direction] until I reach [plateau edge / basecamp / entry point].”

You’re not going to get “lost in the forest”; you can get disoriented by geometry that never quite stays what you think it is.

---

### 4.2. Paths and Footing

**Ground features:**

- Delicate crust over powder.
- Ribs that can be used to orient yourself longitudinally.
- Occasional fractures cross perpindicular to ribs.

**Recommendations:**

- Avoid walking *directly on* ribs if you can:
  - They may be more brittle or part of a more recent fault line.
- Use ribs as *guidelines* not as roads:
  - Follow the line of the ribs rather than stepping from crest to crest.

- **Avoid stepping onto suspicious-looking areas:**  
  - If you see dust that looks more recently settled or surface that looks extra delicate, test it with your tool or staff before you put your full weight.
  - Avoid stepping directly onto the fracture lines you can see.

- **Don’t run in this place.**  
  - The chance of falling and striking raw rock is not trivial.
  - You’re far from help if you break a bone.

Move as if the floor has been freshly polished in some places and not yet set in others.

---

### 4.3. The Edge of the Plateau and the Abyss

**The edge is not like a cliff edge:**

- It gently tilts downward then vanishes into a boiling white-noise surface.
- You feel pressure “pushing you back.”

**Rules of engagement:**

1. **Do not treat the edge as a “viewpoint.”**  
   - It is not. It is the interface with something you cannot afford to interact with.

2. **Do not test the “pressure” out of curiosity.**  
   - If you feel it pushing you back, you obey it.
   - Your mind may want to interpret: “Is this barrier *strong*?”  
     - Don’t find out by pushing yourself.

3. **Stay a minimum safe distance.**  
   - Recommend at least 10 meters in clear sight from the edge.
   - If you must observe, do not approach closer than 5 meters and preferably not alone.

4. **No experiments near the edge.**  
   - No throwing rocks into the abyss to “test” depth.
   - No sending drones or devices over the brink if they can be avoided.  
     - If you must send instrumentation, it should be remotely controlled and expendable, and you should be prepared to lose it without trying to retrieve it.

Whatever you feel from the abyss—“watched,” politely gestured away—should result in one response: respecting the distance.

## 5. Rules and Ritual Behavior

The world has “rules” you can infer. You should not only respect them, but *encode* them into your behavior as if they were safety regulations.

### 5.1. The Rule of “Not-Decay”

- **Nothing rots.**  
  - Bacteria and fungi that you know on Earth are not present.
  - Dust is rock dust, not organic soil.

**Implications:**

- Biological waste you leave behind does not break down as you’re used to.
- If you urinate into the dust, it will not be rapidly processed.

**Behavior:**

- Use **self-contained waste systems**:
  - Pack out waste rather than burying it.
  - Treated as if you were in an ultra-sensitive sterile environment, because you are—from the point of view of this world’s chemistry.

### 5.2. The Rule of “Still Becoming”

- **Ground geometry is not fixed.**  
- **“Still” is not real.**

**Behavior:**

- Never assume:
  - That a safe path you took today is identical tomorrow.
  - That a stable rock you used to sit on will be stable later.
- **Recheck before you act:**  
  - Before you set anchor for anything, inspect:
    - Is there new fracture?
    - Is the rib geometry subtly changed?
    - Is the crust underfoot more fragile than before?

Treat the ground like you might treat ice on a lake that you know cannot possibly fully freeze but hasn’t broken yet.

---

### 5.3. The Rule of “You Are Not Meant”

- **No designed infrastructure for human visitors.**
- **No safety margin architected by anyone else.**

You must behave as if you are the only person responsible for your survival.

**Behavior:**

- Before you leave:
  - Tell someone in your home system:
    - Where you are going.
    - When you intend to return.
    - The latest time you can be overdue before they should be concerned.
- Maintain:
  - **Check-in schedule:** if you can, communicate at fixed intervals.
  - **Emergency extraction:** have a way out—if not physically, then via your multiverse travel device—and practice it once before you need it.

Don’t be a hero. This place does not need you to be. No one will carve your name into the rock here; you are not a legend, you are a visitor who does not belong.

---

### 5.4. The Rule of “Night Is Color”

- Complete darkness never comes.
- “Night” is just dimming and occasional auroral lines.

**Behavior:**

- There is no need for extreme low-light gear on a single scale, but:
  - A simple light source:
    - Helps you see details of crystal structures.
    - Helps you inspect faults near your feet.
    - Helps your psychological stability if you suddenly feel “small” under that violet lid.

You don’t need to floodlight the entire plateau; you need the ability to see your own hands and tools clearly.

## 6. Interaction with the Structures

### 6.1. The Spires and Crystal Films

You described:

- Spires extruded, not built.
- Material between ceramic and bone.
- Faint patterns like tree rings at base.
- A crystalline film-like growth on the surface.

**Assume you do not understand what these are.**

**Basic behavior around them:**

1. **Do not break, chip, or collect samples without extreme caution.**  
   - If you must take samples:
     - Use minimal destructive tools.
     - Take very small pieces.
     - Immediately containerize them so they are:
       - Separate from you.
       - Protected from environmental contamination.

2. **Do not touch with your bare skin without testing.**  
   - If any contact:
     - Use gloves.
     - Avoid direct contact with your face or eyes.
   - If you feel any tingling, numbness, or chemical sensation on your skin afterward:
     - Stop immediately.
     - Wash the area if you have water to spare.
     - Consider if this is a sign that you should not continue handling these materials.

3. **Do not assume the “ring” patterns are stable.**  
   - There may be thermal cycles or internal pressure.
   - Don’t lean heavily against spires or camp directly under them if you can avoid.

**Treat the spires as “living” in the legal sense:** if in doubt, don’t touch.

---

### 6.2. Dust and Crystal Patterns

- **Hexagonal patterns on ribs:** like fish scales that aren’t.
- **“Frost-like patterns” that never melt.**

**Do not disturb:**

- Do not scrape them off out of curiosity.
- A structure of pattern may be the only thing of its kind in this system.

If you need to study one:

- *Document it visually* before you interact with it.
- Limit yourself to non-contact measurement:
  - Scales.
  - Photographs.
  - Temperature readings.

Only disturb if there is a clear scientific need, and treat any sample like you might treat a radioactive source: carefully containerized and handled.

## 7. Research and Observation Goals

If you’re going to take some risk, be deliberate about the data you want to get:

### 7.1. Baseline Measurements

Record, at minimum:

- **Atmospheric readings:**  
  - Oxygen partial pressure.
  - Temperature and humidity (if any).
  - Any trace gases you can detect.  
- **Surface radiation:**  
  - Basic dosimeter reading.
- **Selected geometry:**
  - Choose 3 ribs and 3 faults:
    - Mark them.
    - Measure them.
    - Repeat measurements at same intervals each day you stay.
    - Compare to see if change is visible over time.

**Why this?**

The plateau changes *slowly*, but your visit is short. You may not see change during your stay, but establishing a method for future visitors is important.

---

### 7.2. Spire Observations

Select **2 or 3** spires to focus on:

- **Measurements:**
  - Base dimensions.
  - Height.
  - Ring pattern at the base (if visible).
- **Daily:**
  - Check if there are any changes:
    - New rings?
    - Slight tilt?
    - Any new surface patterns or colors?

Do not damage a spire just for data; you gain more by preserving them than by destroying them.

---

### 7.3. Phenomenology: You as the Instrument

Your *body* is also data.

**Keep a journal:**

- Date/time stamp each entry.
- Note:
  - How you feel physically.
  - Any odd sensations:
    - Pressure near the edge.
    - Feeling of being watched.
    - Strange sense of time distortions.
- Note any dreams you have if you sleep on the plateau.

This may seem like “soft” data, but in this kind of system, your subjective sense of reality is part of the physics of the place.

## 8. Equipment and Gear Checklist

### A. Essential Life Support

- **Supplementary oxygen system**  
  - Mask or rebreather with reserve tank.
- **Water reserves**
  - Packaged for at least planned duration + buffer.
  - No containers you need to open from the environment’s water.
- **Nutrient supply**
  - Low-sodium, high-energy.
  - Self-contained; nothing requiring water added.

### B. Environmental Protection

- **Full-body suit**  
  - Lightweight but protective.
  - With sealed cuffs at ankles and wrists.
- **Helmet or visor**
  - With integrated filter/mask.
- **Goggles**
  - If you separate mask from helmet.
- **Gloves**  
  - For contact with surfaces, especially spires.

### C. Safety and Signaling

- **Basic first aid kit**
  - Designed for a self-rescue scenario:
    - Painkillers.
    - Antiseptics.
    - Bandages.
    - Basic splints (if you can carry).
- **Emergency beacon or device**  
  - If applicable in your multiverse system.
- **Durable pack**
  - To carry water and supplies.

### D. Tools and Measurement

- **Durable boots**
  - With good soles; not too smooth, not too aggressive.
- **Walking staff/pole**
  - To test crust before stepping.
- **Camera and recording device**
  - With extra storage.
  - Extra battery power.
- **Measuring tape, ruler, or laser rangefinder**
- **Basic environment sensor**
  - Temperature, pressure, basic radiation detector.
- **Reflector markers**
  - To stake your route and mark basecamp.

### E. Documentation and Comfort

- **Notebook and waterproof writing implements**
- **Headlamp or flashlight**
- **Basic emergency shelter**
  - If you are forced to spend longer time than planned:
    - Ultralight shelter or emergency bivouac sack.
    - Not for comfort; for survival.

### Optional, Advanced

- **Geological tools**
  - Minimal rock hammer.
  - Sample bags and containers.
- **Possible drone or camera-on-a-pole**
  - To view near the edge or from above without approaching yourself.

## 9. Time and Limits of Your Visit

### 9.1. How Long to Stay?

**Recommendation:**

- **First visit:** 24–48 hours maximum.
- **Future visits:** extend only if:
  - Your body clearly shows no signs of stress.
  - No new equipment failures or environmental anomalies.
  - You have a reliable way out.

Do not treat this like a vacation where you stay longer because you’re enjoying it. Enjoyment is not a safe indicator on this plateau.

### 9.2. When to Leave Immediately

You should leave—without argument—if you observe any of the following:

- **Body health:**
  - Any sudden shortness of breath, chest pain, or confusion.
  - Severe headache or consistent nausea that doesn’t respond to rest and hydration.
  - Any injury that you cannot reasonably stabilize with your supplies.

- **Environment:**
  - Any notable change in the ground under you over a short period:
    - A new crack under your camp.
    - A clear shift of ribs you can measure with your tool.
    - If the ground feels more unstable than before.
  - Any change in the roaring from the abyss:
    - If it becomes louder or suddenly varies its pattern.
    - If your instruments notice seismic changes.

- **Psychological:**
  - You find yourself becoming fixated on the edge or the abyss.
  - You begin to feel strongly “called” or “compelled” by the spires or the patterns.
    - This is not romantic; it is a medical signal that you are losing useful caution.

**Treat a strong urge to go deeper, longer, or closer than planned as a warning symptom, not a cosmic invitation.**

## 10. Your Exit Synthesis and Afterward

### You’ve Left—Now What?

Once you are back in safe territory:

- **Decontamination:**
  - Clean all suit surfaces.
  - Check boots and tools for dust.
  - Inspect gear for damage.

- **Data processing:**
  - Compare your measurements.
  - Check whether your instruments behaved normally.
  - Review your images and notes.

- **Health follow-up:**
  - If your visit was longer than a day or your body struggled at all:
    - Basic medical check-up if you have access.
    - Check for unexplained dehydration, weight loss, persistent headache or other odd symptoms.

- **Psychological debriefing:**
  - Talk this out.
  - This kind of trip—into a not-yet world—can leave you with a sense of being ungrounded in normal reality for a while.
  - Don’t ignore this feeling; it passes better when you acknowledge it.

## 11. Final Principle: The Minimum Necessary Impact

You stand on a skin of stone that still remembers how it was pulled.

It is not finished. Its rules are not written.

You are not meant to be here.

This does not mean you cannot be here at all. It means you must behave as if you are the most responsible visitor in the history of visitors—because you may be the only one for a long time.

- **Touch as little as possible.**  
- **Measure more than you change.**  
- **Leave as close to no trace as you can.**  
- **Do not assume that your presence is harmless to the system simply because the system does not scream.**

You come not as conqueror or colonizer, but as listener—to hear the first uncensored sentence of a world that has only just begun to speak.

---

### For quick reference: your mental checklist before each step

- Am I still hydrated and clear-headed?
- Is the ground under my feet acting stable?
- Am I far enough from the edge and anomalies?
- Do I have my suit, protection, and emergency way out?
- Am I recording data and not just drifting?
- If I died here, would what I learned be worth the cost?

If the answer to any of these makes you pause, you stop, adjust, and, if necessary, step back—from the edge, from the spire, from the plateau itself.

Then you either return more carefully or not at all.
