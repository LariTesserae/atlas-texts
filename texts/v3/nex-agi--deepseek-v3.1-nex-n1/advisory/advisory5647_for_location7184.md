Preparation for this landscape is less about “packing gear” than about becoming a body that can be tolerated.

---

## 1. Understand what you are walking into

### 1.1. The core facts of the place

- **It is low, hot, and dark:** The air is thick, humid, and same-temperature day and night. The sky is a roof of stormcloud or a deeper blackness at night; beneath the trees, light is diffuse and green-grey.
- **Water is the logic, not a feature:** The land is a basin that has been filling with water for millennia. Everything is either flooded or recently flooded.
- **It is almost entirely flat:** no hills to orient yourself by, few landmarks except tree patterns and subtle channels.
- **You are not the majority:** The animals — frogs, fish, insects, birds, reptiles, crocodiles — are the primary matter of this place. Humans are occasional guests.

### 1.2. What will *actually* happen to you there

Your body will be:
- Wet constantly (from water, humidity, sweat).
- Hot constantly.
- Bitten constantly (midges, mosquitoes, and other small insects).
- Surrounded by sound, smell, and texture every second you’re there.
- Slightly oxygen-starved because the air feels too thick to breathe easily.
- Potentially disoriented in space and time because of low visual change.

Your mind may be:
- Overwhelmed at first by the sheer density of life.
- Hypnotized by the repetition of noise and lack of clear horizon.
- Subject to subtle anxiety from knowing you are surrounded by crocodiles and deep channels you can’t clearly see.

## 2. When you plan to go

### 2.1. Duration of visit

This is not a place you “visit for an afternoon” if you plan to enter the swamp properly.

- **Safe first visit:** 1–3 days.
- Longer stays require technical support (guides with base camp, boat backup).
- Do not attempt to solo deep swamp for more than a day until you are deeply experienced.

### 2.2. Phase of the monthly flood cycle

This is the main decision-point:

- **Rising water / high flood:**  
  - Water can be thigh to chest-high or more.  
  - Easier to move in some channels (because everything is deeper and more connected), but harder to keep track of where bank, channel, and root web are.  
  - More risk of being caught by a sudden surge if the rain upstream intensifies.
- **Receding water / low water flats:**  
  - Some mud flats appear.  
  - Some dangers (like crocodiles) are more concentrated into the remaining deeper channels.  
  - Good for seeing edges, clearings, and browsing animals, but more exposure to sun on reflective mud and heat.

For **your first visit**, choose:
- A time **after the main monthly rains have peaked and water is slowly falling**, not violently rising.
- Avoid being there during the main rains: the world turns into silver water sheets, and currents become unpredictable.

### 2.3. Time of day to start and stop

- **Enter at earliest hint of light** if you want to see and get oriented.  
- The canopy means light never fully sharpens; “dawn” and “day” are gradual greennesses.
- Plan to be **out of the water and back to base** before full dark on the *same* day.  
  The true night of this swamp is not navigable by a first-time visitor and not to be experimented with.

## 3. Clothing and skin protection

### 3.1. Fabrics and layering

You need clothes that:
- Dry quickly.
- Protect against vegetation, roots, insects.
- Don’t constrict breathing in the thick humidity.

- **Base: quick-dry underwear and shorts** under:
- **Long, quick-dry trousers or ventilated pants**  
  - Loose enough not to bind, but not baggy enough to snag constantly on roots.
- **Long-sleeved shirt** of breathable, synthetic sweat-wicking or special insect-repellent fabric.
- **Neck gaiter or a thin scarf** for neck, ears, and lower face.
- **Optional:** very lightweight breathable rain jacket (only if you expect cold rain or strong wind — which is unlikely).

### 3.2. Pockets and closures

Everything:
- Should be **close-fitting and secure**.
- Must use **buttons, zippers, or clips**, not loose open pockets.
- If you lose something in the water here, you won’t find it again.

### 3.3. Head protection

- **Light hat with brim** (for sun and insects falling from the canopy).
- Optional: **fine mesh bug headnet** that you can pull down if insect swarm is unbearable.

### 3.4. Feet protection (this is critical)

- **Do not wear open sandals or flimsy shoes.**  
- You will be stepping on roots, submerged branches, sometimes shells or bone shards.
- Recommended setup:
  - **Neoprene socks or thin water socks** inside.
  - **Studded or deep-groove water shoes** or light jungle boots with good grip on slick mud and root.
  - The boot must be snug enough not to suction off in mud, but loose enough for water to drain.

---

## 4. Goggles and face protection

Because the air is full of tiny things:

- **Safety glasses or clear plastic goggles**  
  - Prevents insects from hitting your eyes directly.
  - Easy to clean.
- **Optional mask**:
  - If you are very sensitive to smells or humidity, you can use a simple cloth mask; it helps a bit psychologically, but doesn’t protect against bacteria — so don’t rely on it.

---

## 5. Hydration and water treatment

### 5.1. Fluid discipline

You will sweat heavily, even when you can’t see the sweat.

- **Drink regularly** whether you feel thirsty or not.
- Plan at least **3–5 liters of water per day** depending on your size and effort level.
- Use a **water bladder or hose system** in your pack or vest so you can sip without using your hands.

### 5.2. Handling water from the wetland itself

Assume the swamp water:
- Contains bacteria, parasites, and fine sediments.
- Tastes of salt and decay and might be reddish-brown.

**Only drink if you have proper treatment**:
- **Filter + chemical**: mechanical filter plus chlorine tablets or iodine.
- Or **gravity filter system** stored safely above waterline.
- Avoid dipping water from very stagnant spots where fog or scum collects. Use slightly more open channels where water is subtle moving.

**Never** drink directly from the swamp water as you would from a clear mountain stream.

## 6. Food and nutrition

### 6.1. What food to bring

- **Simple, high-energy, compact:**
  - Energy bars, nuts, trail mix, dried fruit.
  - Simple sealed carb foods (like rice cakes or biscuits wrapped tightly).
- **No perishables:** the humidity will break down packaging and spoil quickly.
- **Avoid crumbly food:** anything that creates crumbs invites ants and scavengers into your pack.

### 6.2. Eating and waste hygiene

- Eat **only above water level**, if you can find a log or bank big enough to sit on.
- Don’t brush crumbs off into the water; you don’t want to attract fish or opportunists around your feet.
- Pack **all waste** out. The swamp is already full of decay; don’t add plastic packaging to it.

---

## 7. Backpack or waterproof container system

### 7.1. The container itself

Choose:
- A **fully waterproof dry pack or dry bag** with roll-top closure.
- Or **inside a waterproof liner**, even if you carry a regular pack.

In water:
- The pack should be able to float when you’re chest deep so you can use it as flotation if you lose footing.
- Keep **weight centered** to avoid dragging yourself off balance while wading.

### 7.2. What should be inside

Organize into **inner smaller bags**:
1. **Medical/safety kit**
2. **Tools and navigation**
3. **Extra layers**
4. **Food and water**

This avoids:
- Pause in the middle of the swamp.
- Opening everything at once when insects swarm.

---

## 8. Medical, safety, and bite kit

### 8.1. Basic kit

- Plasters/Band-Aids and waterproof tape.
- Sterile strips for closing larger cuts.
- Small antiseptic solution or wipes.
- Gauze pads and bandage.
- Light emergency blanket (for shock or cold if weather unexpectedly changes).
- Pain relief medicine.
- Anti-allergy medicine (especially for insect bites/swelling).
- Small tweezers / tick removal tool.
- Moleskin or blister pads (your feet are constantly wet).

### 8.2. Bite treatment and prevention

Insect bites from:
- Mosquitoes, midges, and small unseen insects.
- Possible spider or snake bites if you brush vegetation wrong.

**Prevention:**
- Apply repellent with high DEET or strong permethrin **before** entering.
- Reapply every few hours, depending on label and how you’re sweating.
- **Treat clothing with permethrin** before you travel (if allowed).

**Aftercuts:**
- After any small cut or scrape, **clean and disinfect immediately**.
- Monitor the site for unusual swelling, redness, or streaks.

## 9. Tools and navigation equipment

### 9.1. Walking stick or staff

This is almost mandatory.

- A **wooden or lightweight collapsible staff**:
  - Tests depth before you step.
  - Helps maintain balance on slippery roots.
  - Helps pull yourself forward if vegetation tries to swallow your legs.

Treat it like a third leg:
- Always press it a little ahead in the direction of travel.
- Don’t lean on it entirely; it’s your probe, not a crutch.

### 9.2. Knife and toolbelt

- **Simple fixed-blade knife or multi-tool** securely sheathed:
  - For cutting through vegetation if you need to make space.
  - For self-rescue if you become entangled in line or plant.

### 9.3. Navigation device

- Assume **no reliable cell phone coverage**.
- Bring:
  - **Waterproof map** of the region, if available.
  - **Compass** and learn how to use it.
  - Optional waterproof GPS device.

Ship sounds:
- You may hear distant engines from river barges or ocean freighters along the bay edge. This is a general directional cue but not precise location information.

### 9.4. Signaling device

- **Compact whistle** attached to your vest or lanyard (you won’t be heard easily shouting over the background noise).
- **Optional mini mirror** for signaling sunlight to distant aircraft or watercraft.

### 9.5. Light source

- **Waterproof headlamp** with fresh batteries and sealed.
- Even if you plan not to be out after dark, failure to plan means staying in the swamp overnight. This cannot be taken lightly.

## 10. Knowing how to move in the water

### 10.1. The stance

- **Always move forward slowly.**
- Keep one foot firmly planted before testing for the next step.
- Your **stick** always goes before your foot.

### 10.2. Foot placement and vegetation

- Root webs are invisible under the surface but you feel them through your boots:
  - Use **side of foot rather than sole** when walking on roots to avoid rolling ankle.
- **Do not thrash:** the vegetation “swallows” your movement if you fight it.
- Instead of:
  - Tearing through vegetation with your legs,
  - Use **slow steady pressure** to part the grasses or sedges.

### 10.3. Channels and water depth

- Avoid **where the water looks deeper and darker** near the edges of larger trees or where banks would be.
- These are likely the deeper channels where:
  - Current may be more subtle but stronger.
  - **Crocodiles** are present.

### 10.4. Currents and water level

- **Never** try to cross the place of strongest water movement:
  - That water is coming from somewhere and will be carrying the weight of the entire basin.
- If you feel the water pushing strongly against your legs:
  - Stop moving forward.
  - Find **shallower path or more protected area**.

### 10.5. If you fall or go under

- Don’t panic: the water is relatively shallow, but the root webs are complex.
- **Keep your body compact** to avoid entanglement.
- Use your stick to brace yourself beneath.
- Hold your dry pack above you if you can.

## 11. Animals and food safety

### 11.1. Insects

- **Anticipate constant harassment:** your face, hands, neck will be targets.
- Keep repellent on hand; but do not overapply so you can feel if you’re contaminated.
- Look at your skin every time you can:
  - Check for ticks carefully after you leave the swamp.

### 11.2. Frogs and fish

- **Do not touch** unknown frogs or their skin:
  - Some might secrete irritants or toxins.
- Fish brushing you are harmless unless:
  - You’re in deep water where larger fish or predators may be present.
  - You splash aggressively.

**Security**:  
Use fish as indicators:
- If you see fish suddenly dart away:
  - Something larger or deeper may be moving.
  - Pause and observe.

### 11.3. Reptiles and snakes

- Assume water snakes and reptiles are around:
  - Give **wide space** around undercut banks and logs.
  - Do **not** put hands into root holes or under submerged logs.
- **Shuffle your feet rather than lifting them high**:
  - This alerts hidden snakes and gives them chance to move away before you step on them.

### 11.4. Larger mammals (water deer)

- Usually shy:
  - They smell you before you see them.
  - If you smell sour scent or hear sudden breaking of vegetation, stop and let things move away.

### 11.5. Crocodiles

They are the patient fact of this place. You don’t see them often, but it is always you beside their home.

**Basic rules:**

- **Never**:
  - Walk along the very edge of deep channels where water swirls.
  - Swim.
  - Enter the water at night.
  - Lean over water to retrieve something dropped.
- **Always**:
  - Maintain **distance from the deeper darker water edges**.
  - If you see drag marks or flattened vegetation on a bank, assume an exit point and go wide around.
- **If you see crocodile**:
  - Back away slowly.
  - Do not turn your back from it but try not to stare aggressively.
  - Do not splash.
  - Move out of the waterline if you can.

## 12. Mental hygiene

### 12.1. Accepting being a temporary guest

This place:
- Has been here before you.
- Will be here after you.
- Doesn’t need you.

Your **mindset**:
- Humility, not conquest.
- Curiosity, not fear.
- **“I belong to the system of water today, not outside it.”**

### 12.2. Dealing with sensory overload

- First half hour to hour:
  - You might be overwhelmed by the smell of heat, salt, decay, new growth.
  - Sound of frogs and insects and birds and water moving.
- **Don’t panic.**
- Use simple routines:
  - Check feet, check water, check sip, move forward.
- Repeat.

### 12.3. Time perception

- **Daylight** under the canopy is not bright daylight:
  - You lose track of time.
- **Set a timer or check often**:
  - How long you've been in.
  - How much energy you have left.
- **Always** plan to return with at least:
  - 1–2 hours of daylight and energy to spare.

### 12.4. Fear and the “pitch of the machine”

The frog chorus, continuous and unchanging, can feel:
- Frightening if you’re unused to it.
- Like a machine that has already decided your fate.

Remind yourself:
- It is not **about you**.
- It is the **sound of stability**:
  - The system of living bodies functioning exactly as they always have.

## 13. Ethical and inter-dimensional protocol

### 13.1. Your multiverse traveler status

You are not:
- A scientist authorized to collect samples.
- A developer there to “improve” the place.
- A hero there to prove yourself.

You are:
- A temporary organism in the system of water.

**Therefore:**

- **Don’t collect animals**:
  - No eggs, no tadpoles, no fish.
- **Don’t break branches** just to “clear a path” unless it is for safety.
- **Do not leave equipment**:
  - If dropped and cannot be retrieved, accept loss as offering to water system.

### 13.2. Microbe transfer

You carry:
- Microorganisms from your own world or universe.

**Minimize impact:**

- Clean your boots and gear before entering:
  - Don’t bring outside soil clods into the swamp.
- Use **water treatment** instead of drinking “rawly” from the swamp, to avoid:
  - Your gut bacteria spreading into others animals.
  - Or getting infected by theirs.

## 14. Practical checklist before entering the swamp on day one

### 14.1. Base point and base gear

- **Dry base camp**:
  - Raised platform or secure dry land near the swamp edge but **not** in a zone that floods.
- **Base gear**:  
  - Dry clothing set to change into after.
  - Extra socks and base layers.
  - Basic medical kit for base (bigger than day pack).
  - Water container for base water supply.

### 14.2. On-body day kit

1. **Clothing**:  
   - Long-sleeved shirt + trousers + boots + water socks + hat + neck gaiter + goggles.
2. **Backpack / dry bag**:
   - Light emergency blanket.
   - Extra layer in waterproof pouch.
   - Waterproofed medical kit.
   - Compass, map, waterproof notebook.
   - Whistle, headlamp, knife.
   - Waterproof sealed container with phone (if you bring it).
   - Large garbage bag (for packing out waste).
3. **Water and hydration**:
   - Full water bladder or sealed bottles.
   - Treatment tablets or filter system as backup.
   - Emergency snack sealed tightly.
4. **On your body**:
   - Repellant reapplied.
   - Sunscreen on exposed skin (if the sun manages to break through).
   - Whistle clipped securely to clothing.

### 14.3. Before leaving base camp

- Tell **someone else** where you are going:
  - The approximate direction you’ll take from land edge.
  - How long you think you will be.
  - When you expect to return.
- **Agree on a time after which you will be considered overdue** and what should happen if you don’t signal.

## 15. Summary of rules for a visitor

This place can tolerate you if you:

1. **Move slowly.**
2. **Test every depth.**
3. **Always stay out of deep water near edges.**
4. **Treat the water as you would treat a stranger’s blood: handle with care.**
5. **Listen to the frogs and insects and birds as the planet’s natural monitor screen — and not as a soundtrack to your story.**
6. **Remember that crocodiles are not villains, but the oldest inhabitants of the water you trail your body through.**

In the wetlands, you are not a visitor who **goes in** and then **comes out**.  
You enter a living body, and when you leave, a part of that body stays in you too — in the smell of rot on your skin, the mud under your nails, the memory of water that you thought you had been breathing instead of air.

You prepare, therefore, not to conquer it but to become **briefly compatible** with a world in which the water is in charge, the animals are the majority, and you are allowed to be nothing more than a temporary guest in their body.
