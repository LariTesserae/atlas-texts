You are not prepared for this place.

That’s the first thing you need to accept. Your usual tricks—“I’ll just pack extra food,” “I’ve got a good knife,” “I can always backtrack”—don’t apply here. The Caverns of Ceaseless Rain are closer to inhabiting a lawbook than visiting a landscape.

If you’re going anyway (and you’re determined to stay human and return human), you’ll need three kinds of preparation:

- Physical gear
- Mental rules and habits
- “Social” negotiation with the place itself

---

## 1. Emotional & Mental Preparation

### 1.1 Accept the role of outsider

You are not biologically compatible with this environment.

- There is no real food.
- There is no air cycle supported by life.
- Time and geometry rewrite themselves around you.

Treat the Caverns like you would treat hard vacuum: a place that is not hostile so much as indifferent to whether you live or die. You will live *only* if you deliberately maintain yourself; the world will not help.

### 1.2 Decide your anchor before you go

The clause‑walls will thin out your memory of your past, one rule at a time. “The memory of touch cannot exceed the length of one breath” is the kind of sentence that can make you forget what home feels like, if you’re not careful.

Before you leave your home universe:

- Write a short, clear statement of purpose:
  - “Why am I going here?”
  - “Where do I return to?”
- Write down a few personal truths:
  - “My name is X.”
  - “I belong in Y place.”
  - “I must not stay longer than Z.”
- Keep it on you in multiple forms:
  - On paper
  - Inscribed on metal (tag, necklace, etc.)
  - In a device offline, if you bring one

Your *memory* of what home is like may be degraded by the rules. These words should be designed to still make sense even if you only remember “I care about this place” and not the details.

### 1.3 Practice “soft focus” reading

You’re going to be surrounded by text that you want, but also *do not want*, to fully read.

Train yourself:

- Stand a few feet from text and deliberately *not* read it.
- Let the edges of your vision blur.
- Notice how you can feel the presence of rules without being certain of their effect.

When you eventually arrive in the caverns, this habit will matter:

- Use full focus reading only when you deliberately *want* to interact with a clause.
- The rest of the time, let clause‑walls stay “slightly out of focus” so you aren’t accidentally rewriting your world every minute.

## 2. Physical Gear

### 2.1 Water and moisture management

You will be immersed in a world where water is *everywhere* but never quite behaves. Your gear must be:

- Waterproof
- Light enough
- Simple enough to still function when wet and cold

**Clothing**

- A synthetic or wool base layer that stays warm when damp.
- A breathable waterproof shell layer over that: jacket and pants.
- Socks that are quick‑dry and synthetic; wool is fine if not too thick.
- Waterproof boots or high‑quality trail shoes with good traction—rock will be polished smooth.

**For your body**

- You don’t need to carry “drinking water” in the usual sense—there’s water everywhere—but you do need *safe* water.
- Bring:
  - Small, lightweight water filter
  - Optional water purification tablets or small drops
- **You can not safely assume any upward‑rain or wall stream is “just water.”** It behaves in ways that contradict physics; treat it chemically as suspicious.

**For your gear**

- Dry bags or ziplock bags for everything you cannot afford to lose to damp:
  - Notes, maps, tokens from home.
  - Any electronics.
  - Paper documents.
- Small microfiber or pack towel to keep glasses and lenses dry.

You will not be able to stay “comfortably dry” the whole time; accept you will be constantly damp, and plan instead to stay *functional*.

### 2.2 Light and navigation

There is light in the stone, but:

- It is not uniformly placed.
- Shadows are deep and sudden.
- You may want to see *details* of carvings and distances.

**Lights**

- At least two reliable light sources:
  - A headlamp (with extra batteries or power bank)
  - A small handheld flashlight as backup
- Waterproof if possible.
- Option: a chemical glow stick or two. They’re simple, low‑tech, and less likely to be affected by wording that “forgets” electronics exist.

**Marking and mapping**

- The terrain rearranges subtly:
  - The distance you “swear” a wall is at changes.
  - A pillar appears where you “remember” empty air.
- You cannot map conventionally, but you can mark *relative* information.

Carry:

- Chalk, or a waterproof marker that can write on damp stone.
- Small colored stickers or tape (if allowed by your ethics of not leaving trash—but given the stakes, you may want *something*).
- Paper notebook with waterproof pages or a sealed whiteboard.

**How to mark your way**

- Place small marks on:
  - The left side of your passage, always.
  - Ledges you stand on.
  - At eye level, so you can distinguish them from carved text.
- With each mark, write:
  - Time, approximate.
  - “L.R.” = “Ledge Route”
  - A number: “1, 2, 3…”

This will not be a perfect map. It will be a log of where you went, one step at a time.

### 2.3 Rope and climbing protection

“Paths” here can be deceptive:

- A ledge might end abruptly.
- A distance might contract or expand at the wrong moment.
- You might want to reach something *just* out of reach.

Carry:

- 30–50 meters of dynamic rope (lightweight if you can).
- A few carabiners and a sling or two.
- Climbing harness if you know how to use it; otherwise, at least:
  - Prusik loops or another friction hitch system
- Basic helmet, if you have one.

Use rope:

- As a safety line on narrow ledges.
- As a way to retrace your exact path.
- To attach to yourself when you need to edge near clause‑walls, so that you’re never “unnoticed” by the world and thus accidentally released from your own body’s past.

## 3. Survival and Sustenance

### 3.1 Food

You will find no food here.

Bring:

- Calorie‑dense, compact food:
  - Nuts, trail mix, bars, chocolate.
  - Dried fruit or jerky if acceptable.
- Estimate:
  - At least double what you think you’ll need for the time you want to stay.
  - Plus a “panic reserve” you only tap if everything else is gone.

**Carry sealed food**

- Moisture in the air is significant.
- Ziplock or vacuum sealed bags inside waterproof containers.

**Set a hard time limit in your notes**

- “If I reach day X without finding an exit on the route I marked with left‑side chalk, then I turn back and follow chalk marks exactly.”
- “I turn back at day X even if I see something I really want to reach.”

This clause in your notebook can be as strong and binding as any clause‑wall, if you enforce it.

### 3.2 Temperature

The air is cool and cold; wetness makes it colder.

**Layers**

- Have at least:
  - One warm layer you can add under or over your shell jacket.
- Hat:
  - A warm hat; much heat is lost through the head.
- Gloves:
  - Even light gloves are better than none on wet stone.

**Avoiding hypothermia**

- Don’t sit still directly on stone for long.
- Use a thin closed‑cell foam pad (if you can carry it) to insulate you from the rock when you rest.
- If you feel shivery and confused, *stop*. Add layers, move around, and eat something.

### 3.3 Injury and illness

“No sound travels faster than water” can be interpreted in ways you don’t anticipate.

- **If you need help, don’t count on being heard quickly.**

Your kit (minimum):

- Small first aid pack:
  - Pain relief medicine
  - Any personal medications
  - Bandages and antiseptic wipes
- Hand warmers or chemical heat packs.
- Emergency whistle (even if sound may be dampened, it’s better than shouting).
- An emergency blanket if you can carry it.

## 4. Dealing with the Clause‑Walls

### 4.1 Don’t read “random” text

This is the most important rule of the region:

- **Do not casually stare at and read full sentences of the clause‑walls.**

You can:

- Look generally at the pattern of carving.
- Notice that rules are present.
- Observe *feeling* changes without being sure of the cause.

But once you consciously read a clause like “No memory of a journey may exceed the present room,” you might be trapped in a region where walking backward *literally* cannot return you to your past.

### 4.2 “Scanning” rather than immersing

When you need information:

1. Stand at least a body’s length away.
2. Let your gaze scan quickly:
   - Notice symbols that are repeated.
   - Notice if one clause seems longer, more framed, or more visually prominent than others.
3. If you must read:
   - Pick only one clause to read fully.
   - Read it aloud (if safe) or under your breath:
     - Your voice can help “fix” the meaning you *intend*.
4. After reading:
   - Step away before you fully absorb the implications.
   - Mark in your notebook:
     - “Clause near marker 7: possible effect about memory.”
     - “Don’t linger there.”

### 4.3 “Safe zones” via your own markings

You can’t rewrite the world, but you can *anchor yourself*.

- On particularly safe spots:
  - A wide ledge.
  - A large pillar with no obvious clauses.
- Use chalk or paint or tape to inscribe a simple personal symbol:
  - Something like: “S” inside a circle or “A#.”
- Near each symbol, write a short, clear phrase:
  - “If lost, return here.”
  - “Time: check ration.”
  - “Don’t read beyond this.”

These function like your own clauses—your own rules that you agreed to before you arrived.

### 4.4 Reality reshuffling: “Same hand, same side”

The world will adjust geometry in your absence:

- Ledges move slightly.
- Distances stretch.
- New pillars bridge gaps you remember as empty.

You must not rely on memory alone.

**Use your body as a reference:**

- Always touch:
  - The same hand to the wall.
  - The same side of your body to the rock as you walk along a cliff.
- Maintain:
  - Left hand always on the wall if you’re “inbound.”
  - Right hand on the wall if you’re “outbound.”
- Or vice versa—but be consistent.

If you find yourself touching the wall with the wrong hand for your direction, you know *you* have changed the relationship.

## 5. Movement and Exploration

### 5.1 “Anchor” yourself on each ledge

Before you move from a safe ledge:

- Tie rope to:
  - A pillar
  - Or a fixed rock
  - Or anything that clearly is not about to move.
- Tie the rope to yourself.
- Take *one* step at a time over the gap:
  - If something shifts, you pull back.
- Once you’re secure:
  - Leave a chalk or tape mark on the *new* ledge.
  - Write “From S7” or whatever your previous symbol was.

### 5.2 “Forget” the ceiling as a floor, unless you explicitly can’t

If you read a clause that says “you may walk where water walks,” or something similar, you may become aware of ledges or railings on the ceiling.

**Do not race to use them.**

Ceiling walking:

- Makes you more likely to fall.
- Can confuse your sense of “up/down” when you read further clauses.
- Makes you highly visible to any unseen intelligence that might like “clause experiments.”

If you need to use ceiling paths:

- First walk back to your last safe symbol.
- Write down:
  - “At marker S8, chose ceiling path.”
- Then approach:
  - With rope anchored.
  - Only if you’re with another person (if possible).
  - With minimal gear (leaving heavy pack behind on safe ledge).

### 5.3 “Impossible” distances

When a wall seems to move from 70 meters to 300:

- Don’t trust your intuition of near/far.
- Use:
  - Your watch UI time to “guess time to reach” on the way there.
  - Then on the way back, if the time is significantly different, you know the world changed, not just you.

## 6. The Far Chasm and the White Boundary

On the ledge you’re described as standing on, you can just see the distant slit into “not‑world.”

### 6.1 Establish a “no‑approach” rule

Before you ever arrive near that region, write in your notebook:

- “If I see the white boundary beyond the rock doorframe:
  - Step no closer than 200 meters (estimated).
  - If 200 meters is not clearly identifiable, then:
    - Step no closer than three rope‑lengths.”

Reasons:

- You might “understand” and never be able to return human.
- You might be erased slowly, clause by clause, without your perception noticing.
- The world has already shown you that “you cannot go back” can become literal.

**Let your curiosity lose, just this once.**

Write the boundary clause into your own notebook as if it were written on stone.

## 7. Structures and Artifacts: How to Treat Them

You’ll find:

- Half walls.
- Staircases that end at air.
- Stone basins with instructions you may not even read in language.

**Rules of interaction:**

1. **Do not touch first.**
   - Observe for:
     - Wetness patterns.
     - Change of light from the stone lines.
   - Watch if the carvings around it settle or “breathe.”

2. **If you touch:**
   - Use something:
     - A stick, tool, or even a pencil as a probe, not your bare fingers.
   - Tap stone, don’t grab.
   - Step away immediately afterward to see if any clause feels subtly “activated.”

3. **Do not drink from basins unless:**
   - You have read *and understood* enough clause to believe:
     - It is safe to drink.
   - Even then:
     - Use your filter or tablets.
     - Take the smallest possible sip.
     - Wait.

4. **Do not take “souvenirs”**
   - Things here:
     - Are part of the law system.
     - May not remain “objects” once you leave them out of context.
   - This might be:
     - “One clause written on a stone tablet.”
   - Taking it away could be:
     - Like removing a law from code that you didn’t understand.

## 8. Time and Memory

“The memory of touch cannot exceed the length of one breath.”

That clause, and others like it, can:

- Fade your recollection of people back home.
- Soften your memory of danger.
- Make you think “I can go deeper, just a bit,” while you forget the last time that thought almost killed you.

### 8.1 Practical countermeasures

- Time‑keeping:
  - Use a watch whose markings don’t rely only on your memory of time:
    - Digital timer running.
    - A countdown timer for “turn back hard limit.”
- Touch notes:
  - Every time you feel emotional about “time back home,” write:
    - “What I am thinking of right now: [description].”
    - Keep it brief so you can keep doing it often.

- Sit down and “re‑anchor” every so often:
  - Read your own initial statements:
    - “My name is X.”
    - “I belong in Y.”
    - “I must not stay longer than Z days.”

If you feel that “home doesn’t matter as much anymore,” treat that as a symptom, like shivering or dizziness, and act immediately:

- Shorten your time limit in the caverns.
- Start back along the path you marked.

## 10. When You Arrive on That Ledge

You’re now standing on the polished stone ledge halfway along the cliff of the huge cavern. Stone glows, rain climbs upward, and the world is written in stone all around you.

**Before you take even one step:**

1. Anchor yourself:
   - Fix rope to something obviously stable.
   - Tie yourself in.
2. Mark your arrival point:
   - Chalk the symbol “A0” (Anchor Zero).
3. Write in your notebook:
   - “Ledge A0:
     - Time: [x]
     - View: large cavern, ceiling holes, rain up.
     - Plan: max [y] days from here.”
4. Scan the area:
   - Notice clause density.
   - Notice *where* clause density seems highest:
     - Usually walls closest to the white boundary.
   - Decide your direction:
     - Prefer routes that:
       - Follow visible ledges.
       - Have less obvious text saturation.
       - Are not directly on the way to that white slit.

## 11. The “Safety” Clause You Carry

You cannot rewrite the Caverns, but you can rewrite yourself.

Before you leave your home universe, inscribe one sentence—one short clause—in your notebook, and then treat it as the law you *must not break*, even if the world offers:

> “If I cannot clearly return along the path I marked, then I will not move deeper.”

You may be tempted to “just read this clause first to understand if it can help me,” “just see what happens if I stand near the wall,” “just see how close I can get to the white beyond.”

This is how the mountain absorbs you.

Instead, hold yourself to the limited clause you chose:

- You’re allowed to be curious.
- You’re not allowed to be unreturned.

If you keep that balance, you might visit the Caverns of Ceaseless Rain and come back alive with your mind still human—different, perhaps, but still yours.

---

If you decide you still want to go, and you’re willing to treat it like you would treat deep ocean or deep space—with equal respect and stricter self‑rules than you’ve ever needed before—then you’re as ready as you’re likely to get.

Step forward onto the ledge.

But not before you’ve written your own clause and decided you will obey it like gravity.
