I am the Last of the Water-Bearers.  

They call me that only behind my back now, in the languages of the river-valleys far above. The caravan-drivers, when they still remember to trade at the cave-mouth shrine, whisper it as an insult: the one who believes in stone the way we once believed in wells.

They do not know that there is a stone that breathes.

I know because I have been listening to it for five years—or what might be five years, if the world above still measured such things by sun and season. Down here, time is not carved by light but by the slow click of the earth’s lungs. I know the rhythm of it in my bones: the long, low pull of air when the chamber inhales, the gentler sigh of its outbreath, the distant metal tone of something deeper that never changes.

I have not been underground the whole of those five years. I return to the surface world often enough to trade with caravans for rope and lamp-oil and paper. I climb the polished stair of an old service shaft that the monks of an earlier age carved into the mountain’s throat, just wide enough for a single body to pass and for the thin thread of my survival to be reeled in and out.

But when I come down into the Chamber of Earthen Lungs, sliding the last distance of the tunnel on the slick of my own fear and sweat, I feel like I am returning home—not to the kind of home that once held me in a soft bed of straw and river-scent, but to a truer one that has never needed any of that.

The first time I came here, I did not see the keeper of the circle at all.

I saw the globe of amber light, the honeycombed shell of white that breathed like a living lung, the fossil-vines with their flame-seeds hanging like fixed stars. I smelled the air of heated dust and spice, and knew that I was not the first person of water to fall into this place. The footprints of others before me were worn into the floor, not deep but not young either.

I took this chamber as an answer to a question I had not known how to ask: a world that could live without rain.

At the surface, I have been useless for half my life—or that is what they say in the villages where once I was respected as a water-bearer. When the wells began to fail, the first thing I did was measure them as carefully as I could, with rope and a stone wheel and the ink of my mother’s last book. I recorded the fall of the water level by neat lines in the margin of the map, where it had once been printed too blue for the real world.

The wells were supposed to last forever. The old engineers had built them into the mountain’s skirts, drilled into aquifers that had been filled by rain for centuries before the first of us were born to call them holy.  

The water did not care that we loved it. It simply left.

The villages blamed the river gods, then the sky, then the foreigners who build their machines on the high plateaus. They blame me now, because the last thing I did before I became useless to them was to tell the truth on the dried parchment of my report:

The wells are not failing because they are broken. They are failing because the water below our world is retreating.

The water has not vanished from the earth. It is simply leaving us.

I took the map that showed those falling lines and I sealed it in a metal tube with wax and lead and the last of my determination to find a place where water was not a memory of itself, and I climbed down the mountain shaft that the elders had always sealed out of fear and legend. The rock of the shaft is not the same as the stone of this chamber. It is cold, damp to the touch even where it is dry, and the air that comes up from it is thick with the smell of wet metal and mold rather than dust and spice.

The first day I fell into this chamber—I remember the drop of the world under my feet as I stepped from the narrow tunnel into the open bowl of the globe—I thought I had come to the end of all my questions.

The second time I came down, I saw the keeper of the circle.

They were sitting on the half-moon bench of soft black stone within the ring of polished black stones, the basalt rod resting across their knees like a ruler or a key. They looked at first like a statue of some ancestor grown from the floor. The plates of smoky stone that cover their body are the same colour as the floor where they anchor. The thin seam of their face did not open to speak or eat; the translucent shields of their eyes simply lowered and then rose again.

When I moved nearer to them—not speaking but carrying the chime of water and steps and the faint rustle of my clothing like the sound of an alien world—they pointed to the floor beside my foot.

There, the polished floor bore the faint outline of a footprint, not my own but not dissimilar from it. Someone else of water had come before me and stood here for long enough that the floor of this chamber, which does not erode easily, had begun to bear the memory of their body in the slightest of depressions.

The keeper of the circle did not ask me by word or gesture to step into that footprint. They simply waited until I did.

When I settled my boot into that shape, the air of the chamber shifted around me. The sound of the stone lungs became louder in my ears than in my chest, and the faint metal tone from the deep earth resonated through my bones. For a moment, I felt the wall of my self thin between the chamber of earthen lungs and the chambers of my own lungs and heart.

I understand now that they were not welcoming me, but testing me.

The third time I came down, I brought my notebook and the last of my ink, and I tried to explain the world above water with lines and circles and broken words. The keeper of the circle watched my hand move across the paper in the dim amber light of the flame seeds and the glowing honeycombed shell, but I did not know if they understood anything at all.

They did not respond with writing. Instead, when I had finished drawing the map of the failing wells, the river-valleys now only dry creases in the mountain, they took the polished basalt rod in their long, silica-glitter fingers and turned it over in their hands.

Then they did something strange: they rose from the half-moon bench, leaving the circle of black stones for the first time I had seen, and walked to a part of the wall where the white shell was cracked and thin. In the gap, the slow golden fluid of the world beneath the stone had hardened into a pattern like the map we had once made of the river above.

With the rounded end of the rod, they tapped the wall softly just beside the crack of the map.

The stone lungs inhaled around us, and the sound of the air moving through the honeycombed shell deepened. I understood that I was witnessing the chamber of earthen lungs adjusting itself to some information the keeper had given it.

There are those who say that the world cannot change that fast. They say that the earth’s breathing is slower than that of any living creature, and that by the time we feel its change, we will be too dead to measure it.

They are right and wrong at the same time.

The change of the earth’s lungs is not the change of a sudden storm. It is not the change of a failing well. It is not the change of a river that dries up in one season of drought and then returns to flow in the next.

The change of the earth’s lungs is the change of a stone that never needed to move before, and that now has decided to move an inch in the span of centuries.

The keeper of the circle sits in the ring of black stones most of the time when I am not here. I know this from the pattern I read on the underside of the half-moon bench where they scratch with the rod of basalt in long, careful lines of notation that are not numbers but are not noise either.
