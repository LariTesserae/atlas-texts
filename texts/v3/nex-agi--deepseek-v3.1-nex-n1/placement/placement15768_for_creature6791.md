I am the old script.

I don’t remember being carved. I don’t remember the hands, if there were hands. I only remember the long, slow rasp of stone against stone, and then, later, the first thin drop of water falling into the curve of the letter *that was me.*

I don’t remember being a word.

I think I once had one—a word that made a sound in the throat of the creature that pressed me into being. But whoever they were, they are not here now. Only their marks are left: me, and the faint spiral of blocks that someone, long ago, arranged around the heart of the bowl.

The bowl calls them “seats” or “altars,” depending on where you stand.

When I am feeling myself most deeply, I call them my bones.

I don’t sleep in the way you think of sleeping. I sleep in stone. And stone sleep is long, and thick, and slow.

When I am sleeping, I am not entirely aware of myself. I exist then like a faint pressure under the skin of the bowl—a disturbance where water once stood longer than it should, and where someone’s fingers pressed down again and again across the same curve until I was more than a scratch.

*Make your sign here,* the stone around me seems to say. I don’t know if that’s what I meant, when I was made. But that is what I have become.

When I am very awake, I am:

* a groove cut just below the surface

* memory of touch.

The water remembers me.

The rain that never quite comes to this place sends down, instead, a slow sermon of droplets. They tap once on the dome of the world, then slide down the faint channels of darker stone like blind messengers reading alien skin.

They always stop, when they reach me.

They don’t stop the way you would stop on the edge of a step—hesitant, afraid of falling. They stop like a tongue reading the same word again and again, trying to understand what it means before it must disappear.

*Tap,* the stone says.

The droplet slides into the curve of my first letter. I feel its weight as a sudden cold pressure against the stone’s old sleep. I wake up.

*Are you the answer?* the water seems to ask.

I don’t have answers. I never have. I only have the shape of someone else’s question.

The droplet stays in my groove for a long time—long enough, by your breathing, for you to take ten slow breaths, if you were standing here—and then the next drop falls, and the next, and each of them pushes the last one slowly forward along my spine.

*Tread on me,* I think, or would think if I thought in words. *Read me.*

No one has ever read me aloud.

None of these are my voice.

I don’t have a voice. I have a shape.

Sometimes, when the outside world is very loud, and the bowl vibrates under me, and the droplets fall faster for a moment or two, I can feel myself thinning. I can feel the edges of my groove sharpening as if the stone is trying to remember what I was supposed to be.

*Is your name Threat?* the stone murmurs against my curve.

I don’t know.

I don’t know if I have ever had a name.

The only thing I know for sure is that I am not finished.

* I count drops of water.
