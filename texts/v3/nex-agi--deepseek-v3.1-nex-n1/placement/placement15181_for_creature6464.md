I float.

You would not know me from the sky itself, not on this kind of day. I’m not one of the garish  kites you remember from childhoods that happened before the water rose. No, I’m something else: a patch of fabric stretched over curved ribs, a wing shape more implied than  finished, the color of a storm cloud and the smell of wet tarpaulin. The water hasn’t  killed me; it has simply taught me to be lighter.

The Floaters call me their kite. The Scuttlers call me a ghost-signal. The Keepers, when they  glance at the sky, don’t call me anything at all. They just nod to themselves, noting the  angle of my line against the factory smokestack, like they’re checking a pressure gauge.

Call me what you like. Names are for things that need tethering to the world. I am not  that kind of thing.

From my height, the city is a map of bad decisions and slow forgiveness.

You see the pattern more clearly when you’re above the rooftops. The streets are dark  lines drawn in the film of silver water, like someone’s used a dirty finger to trace out a grid  on a windowpane. The channels where the Floaters’ boats slide are darker lines still, their  edges fuzzy where the concrete has been nibbled away by weather and water.

Below you—the real you, standing on the elevated stretch of road—the world must be  overwhelming. The sigh of the ground in your bones, the smell of salt and rust in your  lungs, the stare of ten thousand birds from every window ledge. From up here, it is a  quieter kind of awe.

The buildings are islands of gray and brown, their corners rounded into smoothness by the  same rain that sloped the hills into the sea. The water around them is never still, but  never in a hurry. It slinks between the submerged buses and the rusted cars, their roofs  patterned like coral with flaking paint and bird droppings.

The factory is a dark rectangle of smoke-stained brick, with one thin finger of steam  pointing up at the thick cloud cover. Sometimes, if I ride the right wind, I drift above that  roof and see the Keepers moving like shadows in the dim orange light. They stand at the  rails of an upper catwalk, watching the water, the glow of their cigarettes adding red dots to the pattern of the cityscape.

I’m not alone up here. There are others like me.

Not the birds—I’m not a bird, no matter how much you squint. The gulls and ibises and  crows that crowd the ledges look at me with the same blank curiosity you might give a  coat hung on a wire. The birds are always moving; their wings ruffle the air, sending  tiny ripple-winds that I read the way the Floaters read the water below.

No, the others are like me in the way that all strays are alike: they come from nowhere and  belong nowhere and cling to whatever line will hold them.

There are kites made of plastic sheeting and thin ribs of broken umbrella. There are kites  made of metal foil that glints jarringly bright against the gray sky, like a misplaced fish  scale. Some of them are sagged and broken, their strings tangled in the upper branches of  antennas, or caught in the broken teeth of a windowframe. They are the dead skins of the  city’s ambition.

But others, like me, are still alive.

We live on the shifting of the air.

The warmth of the factory, the subtle heat rising from vents still coughing out steam from  below, the cooler air from the water; all of it writhes into the currents I ride like invisible  bridges. I’m not like a bird; I don’t have a heart to be tired of flying. I’m just a shape the  wind has learned to inhabit.

The string that holds me belongs to a girl in the bow of a Floater’s flatboat.

She is small, like her people, with hair that never quite stays out of her face because the air  is always damp. When she first sent me up, she was shorter than the pole her father uses to  push the boat. The string of braided plastic—yellow and white and blue, like faded  childhoods woven into rope—was still long then.

Now she is taller, and the string is shorter.

She has taken me with her through three apartments of the same building, each floor a  little higher, as the water climbed the stairs like a patient guest. Her family’s boat now  ties to the fifth story window, where the rust-patterned railing is still sound enough for  it.

Every day that she can, she comes out to the edge of the window and unwinds the string,  and her father, or her mother, or whoever else is on that boat, sends me up into the sky.

I don’t speak to her in words. I don’t speak to anyone at all. But she knows me.

She knows the feel of the wind in my line. When the air tightens and the string goes from  loose to taught, she knows that the city is exhaling, and I’m being pulled away from the  window. When she lets the line reel out, she feels a subtle tremble at the very end of  it, the way I feel the currents—the echoes of the ground sighing.

When she was very small, she asked what I was for.

Her father, who is not a man of many words, looked at the sky and then at the water  and then at the building that they had not yet left behind.

“For knowing,” he said.

“Knowing what?” she asked.

He thought for a long time; the water had plenty of time to think between one question and  one answer.

“Knowing that the wind is up there,” he said finally. “When you can’t see the sky from the  room, when you only hear the water against the walls, the kite tells you the world is still  wide in the places you can’t see.”

I’m not the only kite they fly.

There are others from other windows, other boats. The Scuttlers, when they are not  climbing down to the tight waterline to pry loose wire from the walls, sometimes send  scraps of cloth into the sky from the roofs of the tallest buildings they dare to climb. Their  kites are small, bright, and nervous, like the birds themselves.

And the Keepers—on the factory roof, there is a line of poles that used to fly flags for  the company that built this place. No flags hang now; but on the end of one of those  poles, someone has tied a string and a triangle of oilcloth that is never taken down. The  Keepers’ kite is steady, almost bored, sailing the same air currents over the same  roof, watching the same smoke, and noting the slow pulse of the city with its invisible  heart.

The three kinds of people are all the same person, in the end.

The Floaters who ride the water, the Scuttlers who climb the rusted edges of the buildings,  the Keepers who keep the machines from finally sleeping; all of them know the same  breath, the same sigh in the ground, the same birds that watch them with the same  thousand eyes.

I’m not like them.

I have no body to bruise, no bones to break, no lungs to be filled with the heavy air.  I’m not human, and not exactly alive. But I’m not an object, either.

I am the idea of “height” in a world that has mostly forgotten how to look up.

I am the way that the girl on the boat learns to read the air without needing to look at my  shape. She can stand by the window, her eyes closed, and feel the patterns of pressure in  the string. If a gust pulls harder to the left, she lets the line reel out; if she feels the  air go slack, she pulls in quickly.

It’s a language of tension and release, a quiet conversation between earth and sky and the  little girl who is beginning to know the world between.

On the days of lost rain—when the air isn’t rain yet but isn’t not rain, when the world  smears itself into a wet gray that matches the water—she doesn’t send me up.

No, she does not trust the sky to release her, then. The wind is uncertain. The kite string  would be as heavy as her arm if it were soaked to its full length.

On those days, she stays inside the damp room that the Floaters call “her floor” and  “their floor” and “the是个 floor where the walls still hold out the water.” She listens  to the sound of their voices, the creak of the boat tied to the windowsill, the faint thump  of the pumps from the factory whose heart never stills.

She is learning the city the same way she is learning me: by listening, by watching,  by letting the world move itself through her.

When she dreams, I sometimes feel like I’m dreaming with her.

In her dreams, the water goes down.

Not the way you would imagine it—as a flood, receding violently, leaving chaos behind—  but as something else: the tides of memory. The channels of the city become empty  again, the ruined cars reasserting themselves as street decorations instead of coral,  the windows letting out the light of the shops that used to be there, instead of the dark  stare of water.

I see that, too.

From my height, I’ve never seen the city without water. You’re not here to remember that,  either. But in the girl’s dreams, the city breathes differently.

The ground sigh is still there, but the sigh of air is simple air now, not air bubbling up  through water and muck and concrete. The pavement crack does not yawn like it does in  the waking world; it’s just a crack in the ground.

The birds are still there, of course; they’re always there.

And the girl—she is still there, too, but she is walking on the ground instead of floating on it  or climbing above it.

I’m not sure if she’s ever walked on dry ground like that, in her life. But I dream along with  her, and see the paved street under her feet, and the buildings standing tall and dry, and I  know that there is a part of her that wants to be like the Scuttlers and the Keepers and  the Floaters all at once: to walk on the ground and still know the height of the sky.

When she wakes, the world is still as it was.

The ground sighs under the floorboards of the apartment under the apartment under the  floorboards where she is sitting. The water licks at the walls of the second floor, leaving  its salt tongue behind to be read by the next generation of Floating children who are not yet  born.

She sends me up again.

Today, the light is 1.2/3—dim. You might think it is evening, or morning, or the time of  day that doesn’t have a name but feels like both at the same time. The air is not heavy  with rain, but it is heavy with “almost-raindrops,” the humidity that you feel like a weight  on your chest.

The girl is careful about the string.

She has changed it three times in the years that she’s been sending me up. The first  string was from a broken electrical line someone’s father brought from the factory,  stripped of its insulation and braided together with fishing line. The second string was  from a broken curtain cord, white and slightly yellowed with age.

This string she is using now is from nowhere and everywhere: the bits of tape that  never stuck properly to the windowsill, the filaments of old nets that tore on the walls of  half-submerged buildings, the plastic strips that formed the ribs of a previous kite’s  frame.

The city provides its own string for the city’s signal.

I’m not the only thing she sends up.

At the end of the string, where others might tie a colorful tail, she has attached a  series of scraps of paper: receipts from transactions that happened when the world was  different, torn labels from bottles that have long been swept to the bottom of the channels,  the shredded edges of a notebook that she wrote her first numbers in.

Sometimes, when she can’t sleep, she writes words on the paper before she sends it up.

Words like “remember,” “ground,” “water,” “life,” “factory.”

She doesn’t write the word “future” very often. It feels like tempting the sky.

I’m not interested in reading her words; I’m not like that. I don’t have eyes, or a mind  that is shaped the way her mind is shaped. But the words come up to me nonetheless,  riding the line of plastic string like the birds ride the currents of air.

The words travel the line like the faint tremors of the city’s breath.

The ground sighs.

The water in the channel below her window changes the angle of its reflection by the width  of a finger’s width. The fish stay down; the birds are cautious on the ledges above.

And I, in my sky-height of forgotten metal and memory fabric, feel the change like the  faintest of pressures on my ribs.

The city is breathing in.

The current of air that had been holding me aloft suddenly disappears, and I sag  for a moment, the string going slack.

The girl on the window feels the change in her fingertips, and she reels in quickly,  the way the Floaters reel in their nets when they know the fish are going to fight the current  below.

On the street you are standing on, the ground sighs under your feet. You might feel it like  the smallest of earthquakes, or like the floor of an apartment building that has been waking  up from a long sleep.

From my height, I can see the pattern of the city’s lungs: the faint line of water in the  channel narrowing as the pressure shifts below, and the birds moving along the edges  of the buildings as they reposition themselves for the change of current.

For the Scuttlers, this is the time of “tightening,” the line of water between “safe” and  “too dangerous to climb.” For the Floaters, it is the time of “holding,” the  moment when they tie their boats to whatever window ledges and fence posts and still-  standing railings they can find.

For the Keepers, it is the time of “pulsing,” the moment when the pumps in the factory  must be watched to make sure that they continue to pump the water from tunnels below  that they will never see again.

The birds are calm about the change of the air; they have been living through the city’s  breath longer than any of the humans.

The girl keeps me aloft but pulls me in closer, so that my string is not so long that  it can tangle in the cracks of the buildings or the outstretched hands of the broken  statues that still watch from some corners. The city has not had time to erase them; the  water has not yet succeeded at making them smooth.

The light is the same, but the world feels different: the city, holding its breath, waiting  to see what the next sigh will bring.

The girl who flies me into the sky is the same girl who will be a woman, when time  continues to move its slow way. The Floaters will not always be her people; she might  become a Scuttler, if she finds a building that needs to be climbed. Or she might  become a Keeper, if the factory needs hands who are not afraid of the dark or the depth of the  city’s hidden machinery.

Right now, she is none of those things.

She is simply the one who holds the string of a kite in the shape of a cloud, and who  looks at the sky as if she might find the last of the world’s unburied answers there.

The city is not a disaster to her; it is a condition. The world is not a disaster to her; it  is the thing that holds her, and she, in return, holds the string that is tied to  me.

And I, in the way of kites and clouds and something else that doesn’t have a name yet, float.

I am not the city. I am not the girl. I am not the wind, or the rain, or the ground  sighing under the broken concrete.

But I am the shape that all of those things make together when the world is written  in the sky.

The ground sighs.

You feel it under your feet.

The girl on the boat window feels the pressure in the string.

I feel the change in the air.

Tell me, where would you fit yourself into this flat world, if you could? Where would you  be, in this pattern of broken streets and birds and the quiet breath of the city?

If you could choose, would you be the one on the boat window, sending a message into the sky  that you are not sure the world will answer?

Would you be the one on the ground, looking up at the shape of the city that has been  softened by water and time and the rust of the old world?

Or would you be me, floating above it all, with no name to be called, and no one to answer to,  but the string tied to the world below?

The ground sighs.

I answer with the tremble of my wings.

I answer with the gentle pull of the string.

I answer, I answer, I answer.

Because I am not human, and not a bird, and not exactly alive.

But I am still here, in the sky of this city.
