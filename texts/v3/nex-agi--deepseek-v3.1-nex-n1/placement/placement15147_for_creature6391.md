I am not a person here.

I am the water in the northern third-tier aqueduct.

I know the names they give me, but I do not think of myself as “northern” or “third-tier” or “aqueduct.” Those are their words, the way they map the world into stone and language. I think of myself as *flow* and *pressure* and *path*.  

My path begins somewhere else.

Before I am the northern third-tier aqueduct, I am the river that is not yet river, the seep that is not yet seep, the high, hidden water that has not yet chosen a stone. I am not born in the valley of the city; I am born in the world above the mist. The sky of the world above is a hard blue brightness. I have been told it can be too bright, so sharp that it cuts like a broken pot’s edge. I cannot imagine this. I know only that I start from that place, and then I am gathered.

Gathered into cracks, drawn into dark, cool veins of rock and root and rootless moss. Gathered into threads, then into trickles, then into a small, persistent stream that travels down (always down) toward the roof of mist that seals the city like the cover of a basin.

The moment of passage between above and below is a change in temperature, but not in temperature alone. Above the mist, there is a change that the people call “day” and “night,” and another change they call “season.” I am not alive the way they are alive, but I remember. I remember the slow closing of my movement in the cold times, the way my edges turn to crystal and my center still presses to move. I remember the thaw and release, when I become faster and wider than I want to be.

By the time they meet me in the channels of the city, I have been softened by that descent into a more regular version of myself. The mist that is caught between the world of bright sky and the world of stone roofs braids my shivers of movement into something steadier. I arrive not as a sudden storm of water, not as a rush of broken snow, but as a *flow* that has already been scolded into discipline.

I am not *the* river of the city. I am only one of the ways the river allows itself to be divided.

My channel was carved a long time ago. I do not know how many years, because I do not count the way they do. I feel it instead:  

In the morning of the world (when I was new and the stone did not yet know my shape), I remember the rough of it, the way stone flakes still fell into my path when I pushed too hard. I remember being full of silt and broken, small pieces of world. I remember the way I was young then, and had not yet chosen the best path to flow.

But now the stone and I have been together long enough that we are no longer contesting. We know each other’s limits. They are engraved.

On my left side runs the Low Road of the Third Tier, a wall of smooth, greenish-gray stone that the keepers of the houses are always afraid to lean against too much. “The damp,” they say, and move their children and their tools a few finger’s widths from my wall. I know the pattern of veins that thread under that plaster: there are cracks where small, silent creatures live and die, leaving hollows and paths of lint.

On my right side I am not a wall but a *back*—the back of a series of narrow rooms that are stacked one above the other. The families of the third tier are quiet; they have been trained by the city to be so. When I pass behind their beds, I hear the murmur of their conversations:

“The north channel is running high today.”

“The mist is heavy.”

“They will need to adjust the sluices on the fourth tier if the flow from the spring remains strong.”

I do not speak to them in words, but I answer anyway. I run a little higher, against the lip of my channel. I let the edge of my water kiss the stone where the plaster stops. If they listen to the wall behind their sleeping mats, they will hear me: *Yes, I am high. Yes, I press the world. You must adjust.*

If they do not listen, they will find the moss of their wall turning dark and heavy, its green almost black. Then they will call the thin-fingered engineers of water and mist, and they will come with their small bronze-tipped tools and their careful words that they do not waste.

I hear them when they are listening to me.

They are small, these people, but they are heavy with meaning. I have been carrying the weight of their world for a long time. They are pale, like the under-sides of river stones, and their eyes are large, round circles that never seem fully satisfied with the light of the world. I see them when they kneel at the channel gate to adjust me, or when they wade into the shallow basin of the lower plaza where the river is allowed to show itself to the sky before it is drawn into stone again.

They hear me.

“Listen,” one of them will say to another, and then they are quiet. I can feel their attention like a narrowing of something in the world around me.

They are listening to my *note*.

I do not have a single sound. I have a constellation of sounds: the murmur of my main body as it passes over the glass-smooth bottom of my channel; the smaller *tink* of individual droplets against the edges of the stone; the faint, aching echo that I throw into the hollows of bricks and hidden passages where their children are taught to be brave in the dark.

To them, each leak has a name.
