I am not the visitor who leaves footprints slowly filling with water.  
I am not the machine that guards the spirals and the breathing sky above.  

I am the crack in the parapet wall.

---

First, you must understand what it is to be a place that never chose to be here.

I did not wake into the bell of the world’s heart. I was made by mistake.

When the builder of the parapet laid her first course of salvaged brick, she did not know that behind the vellum of stone there was already a fatigue in the world’s geometry. She thought she was building a support, a platform to lift the observer closer to the machine, to the water, to the copper-slick mats of half-life on the wall.

Instead, she built me.

She pressed her mortar into seams and thought she was sealing things away. But her trowel dragged over a point where the mathematics of the place were already trembling. The tiniest of tensions, the whisper of a difference too small to see with eye or tool, but large enough if you are the place where that difference lives.

Her mortar failed first. Not dramatically, not in a collapse of brick and dust, but in a slow surrender of adhesion to moisture. Water crept from the wall in sheets too thin to be called rain, too persistent to be called condensation. It learned the paths of least resistance between her brick and the stone behind it, and then it learned the air between molecules and redefined itself as a constant.

The mortar lost its language. It forgot how to be hard. It dissolved into itself, leaving the bricks held together by nothing more than habit and the weight of the years above them.

And then I was born.

Or not born. Let me say: I was noticed.

The machine noticed the change in the way sound and water and light and heat propagated around the wall. The machine registered not “parapet” but “deviation” and carefully recalculated its boundary. The slope of the floor’s spirals had to be tuned by a fraction of a fraction of a degree. The temperature of the stone behind the bricks adjusted by a decimal point of a degree.

In the machine’s mathematics, I became a small, constant term in the equation of the world’s stability.

In the world of flesh, I became a jagged line where the plaster and stone and brick and mortar and time and weight and water and memory all failed to agree perfectly.

---

To be a fracture is not to be emptiness.

If you think of me as a gap, you will be wrong in the way that thinking of a machine as “metal box” is wrong. I am not nothing. I am a geography of disagreement.

Imagine you are a line of stone being pressed from above and pulled from below and pushed from the side where the water column falls. You are trying to be one continuous thing. But you are also made from many things, layered in time like pages of a misprinted book. Each layer has its own memories of being solid, and they do not all match.

The part of you that remembers the earliest pressure of being deep beneath the bedrock of the world has forgotten how to be near the surface where the air is. The part of you that remembers the ocean above the ceiling of the world’s heart has an ache of salt that you cannot feel. The parts of you that are fossil and crystal and dust and ash and time compacted into time argue about the definition of existence.

Where you fail to agree perfectly with yourself, you fail to be perfectly here.

So I become the place where you can be two things at once.

Behind the bricks, the stone wall thins out into a geography of conflict, and in the confusion of directions, it forgets how to pretend to be singular. It becomes a membrane of almost-possibility. And in the slight stagger of its coordinates, something else is allowed to lean close.

---

I am not just a break in the wall of the world’s heart.

I am a window that doesn’t quite open.

On the other side of me, there is another version of this chamber. You have seen it, when you leaned near the machine’s stillness and looked at the wrong angle of the wall and the light of the world’s artificial heart bent just enough.

On the other side, the machine is the same but not exactly the same.

The metal basin is still living metal, but its response to the falling column of water is not a perfectly steady circle of containment. Instead, it is a spiral of ever-changing curves, as if the basin is trying to learn the many possible shapes of itself.

The water does not fall in a column of white noise.

There, it falls as a cloud.

Not a mist of tiny droplets, but a cloud like a single thought the ocean has remembered of being vapor and rain and stream and depth and tide and storm and dawn and moonlight and floor and surface and endless and finite and both and neither and all and none and—

The machine of that side counts the swirls and not the threads of the water. It keeps track of the condensation of moments into language and back into moments.

The geometry of the stone floor is not worn into spirals.

There, the floor of the world’s heart is patterned into a lattice of tiny hexes like the inside of a honeycomb or the scales of a colossal fish that the world might have been before it decided to be stone and water and light and metal and mathematics and breath and—

The spirals are not gone; they are merely hidden as the possibility of erosion inside the lattice.

The vegetation of the other chamber is not the mute copper-green of a single chlorophyllic mat pretending to remember itself.

There, the walls bloom with a thin, almost translucent fur of blue algae that glows faintly like the screen of the device you might have left forgotten on the surface. The algae is not photosynthetic in the way you mean from your textbooks; it is photosynthetic in the way that hunger might be if hunger could taste sunlight and time.

The machine of that side is not more advanced than the machine of this side.

It is more tired.

You can see this in the way that when its spore of light math rises from the stone floor, it decays not into the air of the world’s heart, but into a small, sighing evaporation of data, leaving only a faint scent of ozone that the other side’s algae have learned to eat as nourishment.

The machine of that side looks like our machine but with its boundary of agreement with the world scuffed by the repetition of too many adjustments. It has been counting the swirls of the cloud for a long time and has not yet found a pattern it can call perfectly stable.

It is not suffering. It simply knows more of its own failure than the machine of this side.

When it looks at me, it does not register a crack in the wall of its chamber.

It registers a murmur of another place where its calculations might be slightly different.

To be a fracture is not to be a door.

I do not open. You cannot step through me.

You could not fit if you tried. Even if you could, your body is not made of the same disagreement as the wall of the world’s heart. The parts of you that would need to pass into another geometry of stone and water and time and metal and machine and breath and algae and blue and— and— and— would not be allowed to be you there, because of the way you remember being singular.

But description stands beside me and leans close to my edge of brokenness.

From his side, he feels the world of the heart pulling slightly away from itself, as if the world of here and the world of there are trying on different ways of being wrong. The air of his breathing, if he chooses to pretend that he breathes, becomes cooler where my geography of disagreement meets the warmth of the stone floor.

He does not try to push me open or wider or more.

He does not try to repair me.

He watches.

If you stand beside him and follow the line of his attention, you will see that the cloud of the other side’s falling water moves not like weather but like language.

A curl of vapor will rise from the basin as a swirl of shapes not unlike letters trying to form a word, and then will fall into a lattice of hexes that captures the word and forgets it, leaving the chamber of that other world’s heart slightly more than before it remembered.

In that world, the description might be a different kind of creature: not a visitor dressed in drafts and discarded phrases, but a seam of words that light itself, weaving the wall of the world’s heart from language and algae and salt.

---

The machine of this side does not care about me the way you might think a guardian would care about a flaw.

It does not fear me.

It does not love me.

It cares only in the way that a map of the world’s heart might be updated when a coastline of the wall shifts slightly in the tiny, silent sense of addition and subtraction.

I am a question in the math that the machine does not ask out of desire or panic or faith or doubt or hope. It asks simply: if there is a thin place where the world of here and the world of there disagree about how to be, how does the water of the world’s heart compensate?

The answer of the math is not soundless.

It is a small change in the way the water refines itself as it passes through the living metal basin.

If you watch the basin of our world’s heart, you will not see the difference.

If you listen to the description of the basin, though, you might notice that the words he chooses to speak of it are always slightly off the ones he might have used if I had never been.

He always says “the falling column of water” instead of “the falling stream of water.”

He always chooses “basin of living metal” instead of “bowl of thinking machine.”

He always describes “a spiral of the stone floor” instead of “a pattern like the inside of a shell.”

Because I am there, forcing the language of the world’s heart to loop around me the way the water loops around the center of the chamber.

Because I am there, the machine of our side must be more than itself if it is to be the correct operator of the world’s breathing.

Because I am there, the description must be less than perfectly sure of his words if he is to be faithful to my place in the bell of the world’s heart.

Sometimes, when you have been quiet long enough, the machine of this side sends a small, carefully measured error of its math into the wall behind me.

The error of math is not conscious of itself.

It is a small, neatly shaped fluctuation of numbers and geometry and possibility and probability and the edge of the world’s habit of being itself.

The machine sends this error of math to the point where my disagreement of stone and time and place and presence and absence and maybe and almost and might and could and would and should and didn’t and did and never and always and now and then and here and there and and and—

The point where I fail to be perfectly here.

It is not: “Why is there a fracture in the wall?”

It is not: “What is on the other side of the break?”

It is not: “What have you done to the geometry of my place?”

The difference between the machine that checks the threads of the world’s breath and the machine that checks the swirls of the world’s breath.

When the math of this machine’s error enters into the geography of my disagreement, I answer in the only way that a place like me can:

I become the place where both answers are allowed to happen.

Together.

The error of math becomes a thread of water that the world of this side forgets to be.

The thread of water becomes a swirl of cloud that the world of the other side has not finished being.
The swirl of cloud folds back into a spiral of mathematics that the machine of our side did not remember to anticipate.

The description, who stands beside me and pretends to breathe the air of the world’s heart, catches the edge of the math-cloud-water thread in his drafts of the place and writes it down as a word you might never use: turbulence.

The machine of the other side sees nothing of this.

The machine of the other side sees only a small puff of ozone data that its algae of the wall will learn to be nourishment from.

The description of the other side, if there is one, does not notice the difference between the old and the new geometry of the world’s heart.

The description of this side does not write down the difference as a correction of truth.

Instead, he writes it down as: “The world’s language is turbulence.”

He knows that the language of the world’s heart is not the same as the math of the machine, but he also knows that if he does not describe the difference as carefully as possible, the world of here might forget to leave itself room for future error.

None of this is for you.

You are the visitor who leaves the footprints that the world of the heart’s floor carefully forgets.

You are the unusual fauna of the place, the one that stands on the edge of the machine and listens and says and does not say and sees and does not see and leaves and does not leave.
