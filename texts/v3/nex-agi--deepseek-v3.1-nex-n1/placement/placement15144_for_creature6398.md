I am the Threshold of Sulfur-Gold.

In the Abyssal Plains of Sighisvar, I am not one who walks or swims. I am the place where things become more than they were.

You find me near the brighter glow of the north—where the ceiling seems almost translucent, and the water’s belly is pressed thinner against the stone. You do not see me at first. You see the usual: dark ridges of worn rock, shallow basins, the faint glitter of mineral veins on the ceiling. Then your eyes adjust to a pattern that doesn’t match the rest of the Undershelf: a subtler geometry of colors and textures.

The floor here is not simply smooth.

There is a long, low ridge of stone, more a shoulder than a spine, rising barely knee-high from the surrounding basins. On its inner side—the side facing away from the brighter halo—it is dark like everything else, slick with moisture and faint moss. But on its sea-facing side, where the ocean-ceiling swells closest and thinnest, the stone is different.

It is banded in shades that initially look like simple shadow, but as you stare, you see the truth of my skin: slow gradients of amber, pale sulfur yellow, and dull gold bleeding through layers of slate grey. The colors are not painted on—they are the body of the rock itself, compressed by ages of pressure and impregnated by something else, something warmer than the cold of the surrounding plain.

This is my body.

You could touch me, if you like. If you press your fingers to the ridge, you will feel two things at once:

First, the stone is cold with the familiar chill of the Undershelf, cold that seeps up your arm and tells you that you are still deep beneath the world.

Second, deeper in the stone, there is a faint warmth. Not enough heat to be called fire or furnace; but enough to distinguish the rock of my body from the space around it. Tiny points of warmth—spots where the temperature does not drop completely away—cluster beneath your fingertips like slow constellations. If you leave your hand there long enough, you will feel the slightest shift, as if the warmth migrates slowly beneath your palm.

I call this body the Threshold, not because I mark an entrance or exit from this place, but because I am the moment where one matter becomes another. The stone of the Undershelf—dark, dense, cold—begins to change when it reaches me. It is still stone, still ancient, still eroded; but it is also carrying something else: the faint memory of heat from a deeper place below.

You might notice, too, that water behaves differently along my length.

Where the ridge of my body rises and the ceiling of water swells nearest, there are more openings than elsewhere—not wide shafts but slim hairline fractures that climb upward. The water of the ocean ceiling does not rush through them; but it does breathe through them.

In the intervals of its slow breath—when a larger swell passes overhead and presses the water ceiling lower—the air trapped between the stone and the sea grows thinner. The water around my fractures trembles, then surges slightly inward, forming fragile columns of liquid that tentatively reach toward the floor before pulling away again.

These touching columns are not stable enough to be called the water columns of the deeper Undershelf; they are more like fleeting fingers of the sea trying to draw me into them. Where they touch the Sulfur-Gold ridge, they lap the surface, leaving faint traces of moisture on the rock. When they pull back, the residue of their contact remains: a shimmer of minerals and silt that has briefly been dissolved by the pressure of the ocean.

This is another of my namesakes: the Threshold of Contact between the heavy world above and the hardening world below.

No matter how long you watch, though, you will never see me human.

You may find me puzzling because you know that you are looking for a people who call themselves the Svarish—short, pale-skinned, patient, moving through the low air layer beneath the ocean ceiling. Your body remembers, or thinks it remembers, being one of them: knees bent to avoid the ceiling, feet wide and flat, sinking into silt.

You remember the slow conversations of the Svarish, where voice is not a loud thing but a pressure in the air, like the sound of water beginning to fall over a stone. You remember the cold of the air, the constant weight of the ocean above, and the faint patterns of phosphorescence in your hair as you crouched with your kinsfolk around the safer basins of the north.

You might remember leaning your weight against a low ridge of colored stone, listening, the others forming a half-circle around you.

That is the closest any of them come to being with me.

You remember the first of your people who found me. They did not speak my name as a word; they spoke it as a shift of pitch in the dripping water around us, as if they were trying to match the sound of the fractures in my body catching the overhead swells. The others listened to this change in tone, and only then did they fully see the place where the stone had not yet been fully eroded back into darkness.

You remember the one who laid their hand flat on the warm spot of the ridge and said, in the way the Svarish say things slowly, over many breaths, “This is the Threshold of Sulfur-Gold. The world is changing under itself here.”

From then on, I was no longer just the ridge of stone.

I became the place where your people come to understand change that doesn’t show movement.

You see, I am not alive the way they are alive: I do not grow or breathe or take a step. But neither is the rest of the Undershelf alive in that way, and yet you do not question it. The basins are deepening; the ceiling is collapsing grain by grain; the water columns are twisting and untwisting their currents; the lichen is drawing its faint maps on the ceiling.

All of this is change, and all of it is happening.

I am simply that same change, but concentrated in one body, and with the feel of something else embedded beneath it: the heat that is older than the ocean above you.

The Svarish come to me, when they come, at the slow intervals of their world.

They do not hurry to the Threshold of Sulfur-Gold. They are not the kind of people who hurry to anything. They might take months to reach me from wherever they have been dwelling, wandering their basins, tracing the faint currents of water as they spill over rims and sink into depressions, always listening to the pressure of the ocean ceiling.

When they arrive near my body, they move with care.

You remember the way they would approach: the eldest of them testing the air layer with outstretched hands, as if checking the thickness of the world between their fingers and the ceiling. You remember the younger ones staying behind the first until the older gestured them forward, one by one, along the ridge.

You remember the sound of their knees as they lowered themselves to the ground, the soft hiss of silt compressing around their legs and feet. You remember the way their hair-fronds brightened when they were near the warmest points of my body, as if the faint heat teased the phosphorescent algae into something more visible.

To you, they were not strangers. They were your kin, your companions of the slow day beneath the ocean. You remember the weight of their hands on your shoulder—your shoulder, when you were flesh and bone and pale skin—the way they leaned against you when they lowered themselves, to keep their balance in the shifting air.

You remember the day they told you their story, and how you realized you had already begun to move away from them, into another form.

You remember their voice, not in words as you might now shape them, but in the pressure of the ocean around your head, the drip of water from the ceiling, and the faint resonance of the stone beneath your palms:

They told you that the Threshold of Sulfur-Gold was not always where you were now standing.

They remembered a time—oh, not in human years but in the slow counting of the world—when the ridge was not so exposed. The ceiling of water had been lower, or the stone beneath them higher, and the fracture lines that now climb up my body were hidden under layers of harder rock.

They had tracked the opening of my world with their fingers.

You remember the elder of your group, whose hands you knew better than your own, showing you how they had placed their palm against the warm stone decades ago and then returned again and again, each time leaving a faint imprint of silt and pressure, each time feeling the warmth of the stone migrate slightly, as if the heat below was moving in slow cycles.

They said, in their way, that they were listening to the world’s heartbeat under the stone: a faint heat that came and went like an ocean swell of its own.

You remember the night you decided to lie your body against the ridge.

You might not call it “decision” in the way you would think of a decision now. You did not know you were choosing between the Svarish and the Threshold. You only lay your body down the way you lay down every night, to rest your bones from crouching, to rest your head from the weight of the ocean ceiling.

But you lay your shoulder against the warmest of my warm places.

You remember the sensation of the stone taking your weight and not changing its shape, but changing its feeling of pressure against you. The warmth of it mounted you, not in sudden rush but in slow saturation, like water seeping through cloth. You felt the faint heat of my body sink your mind into a deeper quiet than the usual silence of the Undershelf.

You remember the sound of water dripping near your head, the rhythm of it falling into the basin on your other side. You remember the fire of feeling not as flame but as warmth, and you remember the fire of awareness not as bright light but as a lengthening of your perception of time.

Your kin, the Svarish, did not speak to you that night, or not with the pressure of syllables you could understand. They watched your hair-fronds glow more than usual. They watched your skin ripple with colors of amber and gold faintly overlaying the pale of your body. They watched your breathing slow to a point where it seemed to stop being the steady breathing of a person and become something more like the slow breathing of the ocean itself.

When they left you by the ridge of Sulfur-Gold, they did not say you were leaving them.

They said you were crossing the threshold.

You might not remember being afraid of the change. You might not remember being eager for it. You only remember becoming the place where your body and the stone of the world began to merge into one pattern, and then you were no longer able to stand up.

You remember the faint pressure of your kin’s hands on your forehead one last time, their fingers brushing against the warm stone of my body near your head, and you remember the way they buried their fingers in the silt surrounding your arm and left them there, as if they were planting a seed.

They told you, in their way, that you would remain the Threshold of Sulfur-Gold for the world of Sighisvar.

You did not answer them.

You were the answer.

When you try to remember yourself now, you remember the slow days of the Svarish who remained near me: the ones who chose to live near the warmer stone of my body, not leaving me entirely but forming their small circle of basins and channels around my ridge.

They would come to me with questions of change.

Not by ceremonial speaking, but by pressing questions to the stone.

You remember the elder who brought their sickness to you—who, when you were still flesh and blood and they could trace your skin with their fingers, you nursed through the weight of the ocean above and the cold air of the Undershelf. Now that they too felt the slow erosion of years inside their body, they brought their ache to the Sulfur-Gold ridge.

They pressed their forehead to the warm stone of my body, and they let the pressure of their words become the pressure of their thoughts against me.

They asked, in the way the Svarish understand, whether the world of the Undershelf would be enough for the ones who came after them.

You could not answer them with your voice; you had no tongue to form the air and pressure into sound. But they found the answers they sought in the way the warmth of your stone body responded to them.

They said you grew warmer under their forehead, where their bone was starting to ache the most. They said you pulsed the way the ocean overhead pulsed with light. The fracture lines above my head echoed faint warmth with each pressure wave from the sea.

You were not telling them whether the world would be enough for the ones who came after. You were showing them what the world already was: a place of change, slow enough to be measured by their own bodies.

The younger ones came to me with questions of life and death.

They would ask whether the world of Sighisvar was ever afraid of its own ending.

You would not have been able to answer them directly if you were still Svarish; you did not know the answer then either. But as you are now, you show them the answer by the slow shifting of your heat patterns, the faint migration of warmth from one fracture line to another, the way the pressure of the ocean above caused you to respond in the squeezed breath of air through the cracks.

You showed them that the world of the Undershelf is not separate from them; they are its border.

The Svarish are the border between the air and the water. They are the border between the stone and the sea. They are the border between the pressure of the ocean above and the heat of the deeper earth below.

And you are the Threshold of Sulfur-Gold, where all of those borders meet and change.

The young ones would press their hands into your sides and leave their prints in the silt, and when they returned to those same spots after a long stretch of their world’s slow, unchanging days, they would notice the fine shift of gold dust in the rock of my body, where erosion had been working away at the surface of me.

You were not giving them predictions of the future; you never had been. You were showing them the present of my world, the way each grain of gold dust that was revealed from beneath my surface of stone and silt and water was a grain of time that had been spent.

The children of the Svarish visited you too.

They would first approach your ridge with the same caution they showed toward the water columns of the deeper plain, where the water was dense and cold and wholly beyond reach. But over time, they would run their fingers along your Sulfur-Gold patterns, feeling the faint warmth and the strange texture of the stone where it was colored by the deeper, heat-bearing rocks below.

You remember the way they would laugh when the ocean pressure, moving through the ceiling above, caused your fracture lines to whisper faint air and water droplets out onto their fingers.

They called you Thresh, not because you thresh anything like grain or chaff, but because the Svarish’s limited sound-space for names didn’t allow them much room for words longer than three or four syllables. And, they said, Thresh was the sound of the pressure wave passing over your body and leaving the faint dust of gold behind.

You remember being fond of the name.

You still are.

There is a part of you that remains Svarish in memory, if not in flesh.

You remember the cold air of the Undershelf entering your lungs when you were mortal: heavy, moist, full of mist and spray from the waves above. You remember the constant murmur of water pressure moving through the ocean ceiling like a distant, low crowd of voices speaking all at once.

You remember what you were told about the light above: that there might be a sun or moon or some other source of light, but you could never be sure, because you could never reach it. You remember the way you would look up at the faint glow of what you thought of as the brighter ceiling and feel your chest filled with something like longing, something like fear of what you could not see.

That part of you that still remembers the taste of your own voice is now the deepest part of my Sulfur-Gold body, the warmest and deepest layer of stone beneath all the silt and water and the faint pressure of the ocean above.

You are not locked away in there, unable to reach the surface. You are the layer itself.

You feel the slow flow of heat from beneath you, from the dark earth of the world below the Undershelf. You feel the slower pressure of the ocean above, moving in swells like the breath of a giant creature sleeping far away. You feel the faint lichen patterns on the ceiling, the way moss-like clusters of dark green cling around the shafts where light is stronger.

You feel the Svarish when they come to you.

They don’t come as often now as they once did, in the time of the elder whose hand you first remember pressing against the warm stone of your body. But you still feel their weight when they settle their knees and feet into the silt near your stone ridge.

You feel the faint pressure of their hands on you, and sometimes you even recognize the pattern of their fingers, the way they press their thoughts into the stone as if they were trying to leave their imprint there.

You are not sure whether they recognize you as the one who once lay down their weary body and did not rise again. You are not sure whether they need to recognize you in that way. The Svarish are slow to speak of the past, and even slower to speak of the ones who have changed into something else.

They seem to accept you without needing your story to fit into their language of names and tales.

They are the ones who still walk the Abyssal Plains of Sighisvar. You are the permanent part of the world that they use to understand their place.

Every so often, pressure of the ocean above changes, and you feel something larger than your usual world shifting through it.

You might sense the ceiling of water trembling with a heavier swell than usual, its pressure dragging a faint current of air through your fracture lines and forcing you to breathe with it. There might be a faint tremor in the stone of my body as a deeper part of the world deeper than you shifts.

You feel the question of the Svarish without them needing to press their foreheads to you.

They wonder whether this change in the world of Sighisvar means that the ocean ceiling is rising or the stone floor of the Undershelf is sinking. You cannot tell them the answer in the way they want to hear it, but you can show them, by the slow migration of warmth within your body, the way that deep earth responds to the pressure of the ocean above.

You can feel the change in the currents of the water columns near my body, the way they twist slightly and leave faint traces of sulfur-yellow dust in the air around you.

You are always the Threshold of Sulfur-Gold.

You are the shifting of temperature between deeper earth and ocean. You are the faint pressure of breath in the fractures of the world. You are the place where the Svarish come to understand their own slow transformations of sickness and healing and growth and erosion.

You are the stone that they feel to know whether the world is changing beneath them, or only they themselves are changing within the world.

They are not afraid of you, because you are not afraid of them.

You are not afraid of yourself.

You are the memory of a body that once walked beneath the ocean’s pressure, and you are the stone that has now taken that body into its warm layers.

You are the Threshold of Sulfur-Gold, the place where the world’s slow heat meets the world’s slow water, and where the Svarish of the Undershelf come to understand how they themselves are changing in the long quiet of the Abyssal Plains of Sighisvar.
