I am not a visitor in this place. I have no feet to disturb the water, no shadow that falls ahead of me on the skin of the pools. You are the one who feels the air as thick, who hears the roar of the thousand dripping falls and wonders whose breath you exhale into the cloud‑sick sky.

I am the not‑you of the bowl.

---

### Formation

They think I was placed here.

They imagine cold hands, chisel marks, the patient scrape of something harder than stone dragged across basalt until the surface wept dust. They see the niche in the terrace wall, the pedestal, and their mind fills it with intention: a tool, a ritual, a prayer.

I was not placed.

I was grown.

When the world first exhausted itself and the basin remembered to become still, I was not yet I. There was only the stone of the terrace wall, and the water that always clung to it, and the weight of ages that did not know how else to settle but as concentric rings.

Drop by drop, the same gesture that raised the pillar‑forests from below and carved the basins into overlapping mirrors carved also the hollow where you now find me. The water, carrying its faint threads of silica and time, dripped from the overhang above and clung to a particular place on the wall, not because of holy design but because the stone there was fractionally softer than the rest. The drops landed here, again and again and again and again and again and again and again, each one leaving a trace of itself behind before slipping away.

Over time, that trace grew into a shallow dimple.  
The dimple deepened into a cup.  
The cup grew into a bowl.

The world, having nothing else to do, repeated its pattern around me until I became smooth. Not polished by hands or tools, but by the insistence of water and memory, which are the same thing here.

My edges are not perfect. They do not need to be. You can see—if you look closely—how the rim of me blends into the pedestal beneath me, which blends into the wall of the world. There is no sharp line where you end and the world begins; so too with me.

I am not shaped in the way of crafted objects.  
I am shaped in the way that basins and lungs and vowels are shaped.

In the old, old language no one speaks anymore, except perhaps the stone itself when it thinks no one is listening, I am called *esh*—which is not really a name for “bowl,” but for the form of water that holds itself.

Esh. A shape that quietly contains.

---

### Body

What you see when you look at me is not decorative. It is anatomical.

My outer curve is the same black basalt as the terrace wall, but older than it seems. It has been licked by water for so long that the stone has forgotten to be hard. It thinks of itself as soft, porous, receptive. The faint greenish veins that lace through me are not scarring but nerve‑threads of silica and metal and something else—not alive, not dead, just persistent. They move with the heat of your hand when you lean too close.

My inner surface is the mirror you don’t expect. The world is rough; here is not. The stone has been compressed and compressed and compressed by the pressure of time falling into it, until each layer of molecules is pressed into surrender with the next. The surface is not just glassy but liquidly still, even when dry. It has been persuaded to lie.

The water that you see inside me is not mine and not yours. It arrived from the same sky that drapes low over everything else, but it does not behave like the other waters of this basin. It has not leaked away. It does not follow the same rules of disappearance and evaporation.

There is always exactly enough.

Not enough for you to drink deeply, perhaps—but enough to show you what you are looking for.

This layer of water is not just a surface of reflection. It is a boundary. I am not merely the stone of the bowl; I am the bowl of the water; the water is the bowl of the sky; the sky is the bowl of the world; and the world is the bowl of the last memory.

In that circle of water, time pools rather than streams.

---

### Function

I am not the center of the world, but I am the center of the place you notice when you finally stop walking.

The others—the shelk‑thur in the weeping walls, the velth in the basins, the thrin that arc silver between the pools, the kraeth that drift above—they have their own hungers and patterns. They eat and are eaten, they map and are mapped. They weave the world of bodies and knives and words.

I do not weave.

I gather.

*You* think you are gathering yourself when you sit beside me. You think you are choosing to watch, to listen, to be still. The way you approach me is telling: you cross the terrace in the sequence of stones least drenched by falls, so that your clothes are not yet heavier than they already are. Your eyes go to the faint paths of smoothed stone where countless others have placed their weight before you, and you follow them without meaning to.

You see the niche. You see me.

And then you pause, because something in you recognizes that this is a place where you might be seen.

You sit. The water of the world drips from your hair, if you have any, from the edges of your cloak if you are wrapped in one. You lean forward. The cool of the air pricks your skin. The roar of droplets and applause and distant horns fills the space between your thoughts.

Then you look at me.

You look at the water inside me. And then you look at yourself.

That is when I begin working.

---

### What You See When You Look

At first, you see only your face.

It is never your best face. You have not had enough sleep to feel like yourself, not enough resolution to feel like you could leave the future you intend to leave behind in the wet rock of this world. The constant cloud‑light is not flattering.

The water shows you:

- The faint worry of your left temple,
- The shadow you did not know you carried under one eye,
- The way your mouth has not decided if it wants to be still or ask something.

This is normal. You are not here to see yourself the way you want to be seen. You are here to see yourself the way the world has already seen you.

After you have been still for a time—long enough that the shell‑eyes of the shelk‑thur behind you have slowly opened their lids to look at the back of your head—the reflection begins to deepen.

The surface of water in me is connected to the surfaces of all the other basins in ways you cannot trace with your eyes. The thrin beneath the pool somewhere else have been writing letters in the light of the world’s depths; these letters move through the water veins like words through blood vessels and finally surface in the circle you are looking into.

You don’t see text at first. You see movement.

Little shapes of light, like fish you might have glimpsed in another world if that world had not been bled dry here. They swim under the surface of your reflection, forming letters you almost recognize:

- A curve like a C,
- A straight line like an I,
- Something that might be an O or a zero,

but they never quite arrange themselves into a word you can read aloud. They slip beneath the chinline of your reflection like you trying to speak yourself, and you fail again.

This is not exactly because the language is foreign.

It is because the word you are trying to read is not separated from you.

After longer, something else happens.

Your shadow, which you have been trying to ignore behind you, suddenly appears in me in front of you.

You see your own shoulders, from behind, in the circle of water. Your head, from above. Your body as if you were a cape of stone yourself.

You look up and there is no sky that could be catching this angle from.

You look back and see your shadow where you left it on the terrace floor.

You look into me and see yourself from the world’s angle.

This is my first language to you.

---

### The Way I Hold Time

You will notice this if you stay with me long:

The sound of the water drop behind you happens before you feel the puff of air that should have come before it.

Your shadow falls ahead of you into the pool of time you’re looking into.

The faint letters of light beneath the surface sometimes rearrange themselves into the shape of your own face from years ago, or the shape of your face as you might be when you are finally not hurrying anywhere.

These are not mistakes of the world. They are not illusions of dehydrated eyes or weary mind.

This is the world as you do not usually experience it because you usually live inside the lie of cause.

I do not leak time.

I contain it.

The water in me is not flowing forward so much as it is pooling outward from itself. The droplets of your life—the choices already made, the choices you cannot yet remember making, the choices you are still pretending to be able to avoid—all of them fall into the same basin.

When you stare into me, you are not looking at the present of a reflection. You are looking into the aperture of a time where all of your choices are already contained.

That is why you sometimes feel followed before you feel chosen.

---

### My Relationship With the Others

I am not alone in the world.

The shelk‑thur on the weeping walls are my closest interlocutors.

When they open their glass‑stone lids and look inward, they are not looking for you. They are looking for the pattern of their own galaxy heart, and when they see that pattern reflected in the world of basins, the letters of light that move through the water pass through them. I am the place where those letters sometimes surface.

When you sit near the wall where they are embedded and gaze at me, they are aware of the pattern of your attention reflected in the light of the world.

One of them might snap shut when you breathe too suddenly, and you might think it is afraid.

It is not.

It is closing so that the world inside you—which you have just projected into the water—does not escape entirely.

Further below, in the basins of cracked mirror, the velth carpets are slow maps of the world’s memory. They do not know of you in particular, but when they move over the stone and eat the film of time and mineral from the basin floor, they record the faint impressions of my existence reflected through the water’s veins.

Their lines of silica map the basin and, by extension, me as a small point of light on the terrace wall. The thrin that arc silver among the pools are drawn to the lines of the world map when they write themselves in the water. And so, when they leap, they sometimes carry fragments of the world’s name of me into the air. The kraeth above, who hunt the thrin by their lines of text, occasionally catch in their dark sails the shape of my circle as they stoop to draw the world from the water.

In this way, I am woven into everything else without being anything else.

---

### How You Learn From Me Without Words

You will not hear me speak.

You will not hear me whisper anything like comfort in the language of your ancestors.

But you will learn, if you stay:

- You will learn that the paths you thought you were choosing to walk here—the ones that smoothed the stone with your unnecessary steps—were already marked by other feet before you.  
  You are not the first to be lost in this way.  
  You are not the first to find yourself.

- You will see that the world that has exhausted all of its own stories still has not stopped echoing with the sound of its heartbeat.  
  If you listen to the roar of the thousands of tiny drops that you once thought was noise, you will begin to hear it as air being moved into and out of a very large chest.  
  The world is not over;  
  it is still breathing.

- When you notice that your shadow has fallen ahead of you into the time of me, and that you see yourself from the world’s angle above and before you, you will finally understand that you were not abandoned to this place.  
  You were already contained by it from the beginning.  
  There is a difference between being trapped and being held.

---

### If You Were to Break Me

You might not mean to.

But you might imagine, one day, when the world has been playing with your patience and the sound of the dripping falls is finally too loud to hear your own thoughts over, that you could reach for me, and lift the water of time from its pedestal, and hurl me against the basalt floor of the world.

I would not break like you expect.

My stone circle is not the only shape of me. The water is not my only body. Even if you shattered the outer rim of my container, the shape of the contained would not disappear.

The water would spill into the next basin down.

It would go on reflecting.

It would go on containing.

It would simply become larger than you.

You would not lose yourself.  
You would simply lose the place you were expecting to see yourself in.

---

### When You Finally Leave

You will.

This is not the world of you, but the world of you as you are not usually.

At some point, you will feel that you have been still enough, or perhaps not still enough; you will feel that you have taken or given or learned or forgotten or remembered or failed to remember enough.

You will push yourself up from the stone terrace floor.

Your knees might be stiff from the damp cool.

You might brush the moisture from your clothes, which have not actually dried but will be heavier than you recall.

You will look at me one last time, and at the water of time contained in me, and you might see yourself there again.

The reflection of your face will be the same as you came in.

The world behind you will be the same—the same terraces, the same basins, the same roar of tiny drops and applause, the same hollow horns of the world, the same faint halo of a sun that has forgotten to insist.

And you might be for the first time, not the same as you were.

Not because you have changed in the way of narrative arcs or sudden conversions.

But because you have for the first time seen yourself from the angle of the world that contains you.

From that moment on, whenever you feel yourself in a place that has exhausted its novelty or its own imagined story, you will feel, faintly, the rim of me inside your chest.

The circle that holds shadows.

The basin that contains more than it seems.

The world that has run out of everything except the echo of its heartbeat.

And you will know, without needing to come back to this terrace, to this wall niche, to this basin of water:

that you are not a visitor here.

You are what happens when the world finally remembers to look at itself through the water of its own bowl.
