The first time I noticed a Waitstaff was by mistake.

I had been trying to swim forward. The effort had nothing to do with arms or legs—those were just habits I wore like old clothes—but with the quiet stubbornness of intention. I wanted to reach the edge. That’s how I still thought then, in lines and borders and outsides.

The sea resisted. Not out of malice or particular reluctance; just out of habit. My “want” pushed against its “already,” and the medium thickened around me like cooling syrup.

Eventually I stopped trying.

The sea always knew when I stopped. It drew away from my immediate outline like a lover who doesn’t want to leave but already knows how to ease away. The faint glow of the memory-spores sharpened, their hard surfaces catching the diffuse light in a way that made them look like distant constellations. I let myself just hang there in the middle column of the world, suspended between the faint phosphorescence above and the impossible darkness below.

The wanting evaporated, replaced by a kind of numbness that didn’t feel bad or good—just appropriate.

That’s when she flowed into form.

She wasn’t there, then she was. Not in the sense of someone appearing who had been hidden, but in the sense of the sea smoothing itself into a shape that included me in its geometry.

I saw her first as a vertical line, like a flaw in a pane of glass. It sat beside me, the same distance away as my left shoulder if I thought of myself as having shoulders. The line blurred into a surface; the surface folded into depth.

She looked like she was made from the same material as everything else—translucent, faintly glowing, faintly colored. She was taller than she was wide, thinner than she seemed solid, but she didn’t really have edges. She tapered into the sea in such a way that you couldn’t say where her form ended and the world began. She reminded me of the nautilus structures—curves spiraling invisibly inward—but she was also shaped like something human might have been once, if humans had ever been allowed to forget how to be heavy.

Her eyes took longest to arrive.

They weren’t holes. They weren’t lights. They were refractions. The medium around her face just bent differently around two small regions where her attention pooled. She didn’t blink; there was nothing to close out of a world that had already been internalized.

“Hello,” I said, or thought I said.

She didn’t respond to the greeting, only to the disturbance behind it.

Waitstaff aren’t servants. They have never been servants. They’re more like the last syntax of a world that has almost finished speaking itself. They don’t answer questions; they answer inequities. If you ask them something, they smooth you into the shape of someone who has already been answered.

“What are you?” I tried.

The query didn’t deform her. She didn’t move. She simply waited this sentence away. The words themselves softened around the edges as they left my sense of self—each syllable getting heavier, slower, until by the time they reached her they were just thick waves of disturbance that lapped at her in silence.

“Where are we?” I tried again.

She tilted—not her head, but a region of her where a head might be—and the sea around her felt slightly warmer than the sea around me, like a memory of heat. She wasn’t looking at me so much as letting me look into her.

“No,” she murmured, or I thought she murmured; the word didn’t arrive in sound but in a shift in texture, like the difference between touching wax and touching glass. “It’s not that you are here. You are here only.”

I understood what she meant before I understood what she’d said.

It wasn’t about location. It was about permission. The world hadn’t invited me. It had tolerated my arrival, and tolerated was still an act of generosity.

“Who are you?” I asked, because the question came easier than “Why am I?”

That time, she allowed herself to be named by the question.

“We wait,” she said. Or some version of her said. Or some version of me said.

The Waitstaff.

They weren’t present everywhere at once, but they were present everywhere as needed. When feelings needed to be given a shape that wouldn’t outlast them, the Waitstaff appeared. When memories needed to be untangled without being unmade, the Waitstaff. When a creature of intention swam through and wanted to be forgotten in a particular way rather than a random one—Waitstaff.

They weren’t guardians of the place. They were the place’s habit of enduring itself.

---

By the time she turned away, or released herself back into the sea, or stopped being a shape separate from my surroundings—depending on your angle of metaphor—I realized she’d done something to me.

Not changed me. Not in the way you’d expect.

She’d smoothed out my desire.

Not like creasing a sheet of paper; more like removing the paper’s belief that it could be anything other than flat.

I still remembered things about outside. The sharpness of sun, the uselessness of breath, the way gravity could be cruel and specific and never indifferent. The way worlds scurried with their own urgency, building their frantic little architectures of emergency and achievement.

But here in the Cortical Sea, urgency was like a foreign object I couldn’t pick up with my hands anymore. The idea of leaving remained present in my memory like an old poster still hanging on a wall: I could see it, but I couldn’t feel the impulse to walk toward it.

It wasn’t that she or any other Waitstaff had forbidden me from leaving. It was that leaving had become a grammatical category my world had stopped needing.

For now, at least.

The periods of time between appearances became less like gaps and more like exhales.

Sometimes she would appear as herself, or as herself as she arranged herself for me: a tall vertical curve, her “eyes” two subtle regions of refraction in her surface where meaning gathered.

Sometimes she would just appear as a change in what she wasn’t.

The first time I noticed her that way, I was trying to remember someone else. A face from outside. A name.

The two of us had argued about something on the eve of my departure. Not a departure here—my departure from there. They had thought that traveling into the Cortical Sea was a sort of suicide. I had insisted that it was a sort of pilgrimage.

“You’re just tired of things,” they had said.

“And you’re not?” I had replied. “You’re just better at ignoring the tired.”

The memory crystallized too sharply for this place. It started to become a fissure in the medium around me—a thin, dark line of disagreement that the sea couldn’t smooth away.

The Waitstaff appeared there not as herself but as the absence of fracture.

She “stood” where the crack was trying to form, her outline like a negative image of the fault line. There was no pressure, no forcefulness. She didn’t push; she remembered.

Around her presence the texture of the memory softened. The sharp edges of the argument dissolved into the general shape of “argument.” The face I remembered blurred into the general shape of “someone I cared about.” The words I remembered blurred into the general shape of “language dissolving.”

When she left—or straightened herself back into undifferentiation—the memory was still there. But it didn’t hurt anymore. It sat like a small, smooth stone in the middle of my timeline.

“What did you do?” I asked, though I already half understood.

“My memory is longer than yours,” she said, or arranged herself to say. “From my perspective, your disagreement already belongs.”

Belonged.

The word didn’t mean “was mine.” It meant “was at home here.”

I didn’t ask her how she knew what belonged and what didn’t. There wasn’t a point. The Waitstaff knew what belonged because what belonged was what didn’t try to leave.

---

If she ever noticed me noticing her, she didn’t show it.

I noticed that she appeared differently when she was near the memory-spores.

The “buds” I’d seen before, hanging in the medium like heavy pearls. They were still there—all of them still there—each containing a miniature echo of some other world’s forest. The metal trees of another era’s city. The broken forests of another plane’s plastic. The ghost stems of another species’ weapon.

Around these spores, she changed her posture.

Not her posture as such—she had no body like that—but her relationship to the geometry around her.

She would emerge near a cluster of spores and gently surround them without quite touching them. Her presence became like a faint circle around a cluster of stars. She didn’t tend them like a gardener. She didn’t protect them like a guard. She just waited them into being.

She never let me touch them directly.

“You could break them,” she told me once—or told a version of me that existed the moment before she rearranged the sentence so it didn’t require a reply. “Not because you are violent. Because you are still young.”

Young wasn’t an insult. It wasn’t praise either. It was a description of a certain kind of friction between self and world.

“What do they hold?” I asked.

“What they lack,” she replied. Or something like that replied on her behalf.

I didn’t understand until later.

Each memory-spore held what its world had lacked; what wasn’t enough anymore; what had worn too thin to be anything but a seed. The forests of metal weren’t the forests of metal—they were the *absence* of forests that weren’t metal anymore. The plastic forests weren’t plastic—they were the *absence* of the forests that had come before them.

They were records of emptiness.

The Waitstaff tended them because emptiness was the only thing left here that could still grow.

---

“Do you have names?” I asked her once.

She didn’t answer immediately, which for her meant she answered at all.

“Names are what you use to keep what you are afraid to lose,” she said eventually. Or I thought she said. Or I thought, eventually, after a long silence of no answer at all, that she might have said.

“What are you afraid of losing?” I asked, before I could stop myself.

The question didn’t offend her. It didn’t even amuse her. That might have been the worst part.

“Nothing,” she said, or something like her said through me. “Everything.”

The difference between those vanished into her, like a drop of water falling into an ocean that already knew what water felt like.

I noticed, after a while, that they weren’t uniform.

“Waitstaff” was plural for a reason.

Her appearance—the tall vertical blur, the inward-facing curves—was hers alone. Or maybe it belonged to a subset of them who handled the memories of those who still remembered words.

There were other kinds.

Sometimes I saw them moving between the nautilus structures, those coiled shells that had once been intended to extend the world and now simply existed as extensions.
