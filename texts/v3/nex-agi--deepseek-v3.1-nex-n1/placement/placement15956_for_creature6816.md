I am not any of the Tenders.

No matter how long I stay down here, they are a separate order of creature. My presence is accepted by their basin’s quiet, but not required by it. I’ve been watching long enough to hear that much.

The first time I heard the change in the water’s score, I thought the sound itself was alive. It was not. The Tender crouching in the basin three hexes over—XX07-3, my chart calls her—had widened a slit between two tiles with a shard of fired ceramic. The movement of minerals under the water’s surface adjusted to that new angle, and the drops falling from a rusted rib above now struck a different tile. Plink. Pitch higher than the last reading by almost a semitone.

To the Tenders, that pitch is information. To me, it is the first clear note of the day, and the signal I can finally climb down.

I am the Basin–Signaller.

They told me the job was “sound maintenance engineer” when I hired on at Facilities three levels up. On paper, I’m meant to keep the noise from the Crucible below acceptable for the tenants above—the ones whose “ground floor” is the ceiling to this world. Safe noise margins. Non-disruptive acoustic profile.

In reality, I am the only listener.

They do not know the water is speaking. They only hear its edges when it speaks wrong.

## I. What I am not

I am not one of the long-limbed things that glide through the crucible hexes with their organs of fluid and filter visible beneath their skin. They never step out of the basin into the city. I rarely do either.

I am not a city-minder, because the city minds me very little. It produces me, in the same way it produces leaks and rust and foundation shifts: an unwanted but necessary consequence of its own weight.

I am not exactly a worker in the usual sense, because I work alone. No shifts overlap with mine. No clipboard arrives to replace mine when I finally climb upstairs to sleep. The work continues down here without me; the water never stops moving.

So I am not a shift. I am a pattern of presence.

---

## II. Where you would find me

You would find me in the narrow channels between the crucibles and the clay walls.

At the interface of systems: where the city’s collapse becomes the basin’s growth, where moisture disappears from grout and is reappears as condensation on the ribs, where the dead sound of the world above is pushed into this living bowl.

Here the brickwork is narrow and uneven, worn into smooth hillocks by centuries of boots and seepage. I have marked out a few frequent places:

- A small alcove in the clay, near the north arc of the bowl, where I keep my charts and a few tools. The wall itself is ribbed with old metal beams that sing when the water drops hit them just so. I call this “the Choir Wall.”
- A low, flat patch of brick between rows of basins, where I can sit and see across three tiers of crucibles. I can lay down my waterproof pad there and not be in the way of the Tenders’ methodical crossing.
- A narrow metal ladder, fixed to the basin wall with bolts whose rust has been worn into polished eclipses by the backs of my hands. I use it to reach sensors and filters that the city-minders installed and then forgot to maintain.

Sometimes you will find me near the center of the bowl, bending over the big inflow basin from which the main channels of water branch.

This is the closest you will come to seeing me with one of the Tenders.

The first time I got that close, I realized the faint smell of the basin—the wet clay, algae, iron salt—filtered differently through its membrane-skin than through mine. It had no smell to its own nose, perhaps. Or at least not in the same way.

I stood on the dark brick rim; its joints were slick with the film of water that always covers them. It crouched over the particular tile that collects the overflow from above. Its fingers were deep in the basin, knuckles almost disappearing into the cloudy film, where the water reflected the faint amber of the sodium lamps above.

Its milky-blue eyes were pointed toward me, but not focused on me. They were focused on the water. It was not afraid of me; there was no space in its system for fear.

I could see its inner fluids shifting through the translucent skin of its forearm, a slow swirl of orange-brown into turquoise, as if it were processing the very water it tested.

I reached the tool I carry—the only tool I carry that is not native to this place—down into the basin and tapped the side of the ceramic rail gently. The city-drip, regulated by the valve I installed months ago, was too strong today. The water sheeted too aggressively across the tile, and the sound it was making was wrong: not the plink of a drop, but a thin hush of constant film. The Tender’s head tilted to that side, its neck almost like the stem of a plant.

I didn’t speak; it cannot hear words. It only hears.

I adjusted the valve, and the city-drip thinned out. The film lost its pressure, became a string of pearls, each drop falling from the tile edge in clear, distinct strikes.

Plink. Plink. Plink-plink.

The pitch rose by the third drop, balanced by the fourth.

The Tender uncurved its spine by slow degrees, its fluid organs shifting from a dense orange to a faint greenish white, as if something had been sifted out.

I stepped back. The Tender stepped forward, into the next hex basin, without looking at me.

We had shared no language, no exchange of words. But the sound changed, and the change was correct, and that was the message.

## III. What I do

When I first came down here, I thought I was being sent to a flooded basement.

Three levels down, the stairway I had to use was narrow and concrete, with a single handrail bolted to chipped wall tiles. The air pressure changed on each step. The city above always puts its pressure on you; down here, that pressure becomes something else. It becomes a tight cloth wrapping your lungs.

The first job I was given was to map “noisy anomalies.”

They were complaints from tenants above—about irregular dripping, “plumbing in the walls,” a low hum that wouldn’t leave. Management sent down a memo listing grid points where they’d traced “possible sources.” Coordinates of complaint.

I followed the grid coordinates to the basin floor. The numbers stopped meaning anything when I saw the crucible.

The water language was too dense to hear anything specific at first. It sounded like a rain that never reached the ground, or taps leaking everywhere in a world made of pipes.

I took out my decibel meter and set it down.

The grid point said “source of noise.” On the meter, the needle fell into such a narrow range that it almost never flicked at the same line twice. 47.4. 47.0. 47.7. Over the course of a shift, the reading barely changed.

But when I listened carefully—without the machine—I could hear the water rearranging itself.

The drip on one tile would speed up; on another, slow. The pitch of the metal ribs singing under the city-drip would sharpen slightly, then soften. One of the Tenders would adjust its route to take in a new hex basin; its slow, deliberate fingers would change the flow of water between tiles.

I would not always hear the change directly when it happened. But I would feel it.

The basin would slowly leave its old pattern. The map of sound I had been tracing in my notebook would become obsolete one line at a time, like the water erasing my drawings with its own.

I was not meant to understand this language. I was meant to silence it.

But what you cannot silence, you tune.

## IV. How my body changes

You might spend whole days down here without realizing your ears are weeping.

You will notice your shirt sticking to your back a long time before you feel the sweat. You learn to read moisture before you feel it: your skin becomes information instead of surface.

Your fingers wrinkle quickly. They stay wrinkled longer than you think they should. Your prints disappear into ridges of waterlogged flesh.

Your hair gets flat. It lies against your skull in a flat film, no shape to it. The Tenders have no hair to lose this shape; maybe they never needed it.

Yours are the city’s reflections, the city’s memory.

If you spend your first shift here, you will be dry when you arrive at the bottom and damp when you climb back to the street. You will feel the difference the air makes on your skin, like a cloth again, but this time being pulled too tight.

If you spend your first month here, your clothes start falling apart at the seams. The stitching rots. The fabric gets thin, translucent where you sweat most.

You start replacing everything with what holds water better.

If you stay longer, you begin to notice the way some of the city’s sounds are filtered by the bowl above you. You can hear the difference between a bus passing overhead and a heavy truck; by the time the sound reaches you, it has been strained through the clay, the vault, the dust, and arrives like a distant thunderstorm in your bones.

If you stay long enough, you can hear it before it happens. You learn to read the minor pressure shifts on the metal ribs. The new vibration of a distant pump. The way one line of water suddenly thickens into foam.

You start listening ahead of yourself.

## V. The tools I carry

I was not born with tools inside my skin.

The first tools I brought down here were city tools: a clamp, a wrench, a sound meter, a flashlight so bright you could see your own soul reflected in the  wet tiles.

The city thinks I need all of these.

I use them less and less.

The meter is wrong; the dripping doesn’t follow noise, but pattern. The clamp and wrench are handy when I need to adjust the city’s valves, its slow dripping of its overflow into the basin’s veins. But those are imperfect interfaces.

The flashlight I keep only so I can write in my notebooks.

The notebooks are waterproof, but they still smell of wet paper. Sometimes the ink runs in the lower corners of pages where I have splashed with water the color of orange tea.

The only tool I carry that counts now is a thin strip of dull metal about the length of my forearm, flattened from the pipe I found it in. One end is bent, but not sharpened. It is not for prying or cutting.

It is for changing sounds.

I can tap the edge of a ceramic rim with it and hear the way the water responds to that vibration. If the tile is too thick with silt, the sound will be dull. If the water is too rich in iron salts, the metal ring of the strip against the tile will change slightly, take on a different edge.

I can place the strip against the wet clay wall and feel the vibrations of the city’s unseen drains vibrating through it like a distant heart.

I can’t *speak* to the Tenders with tools like these. They can’t understand any noise made from the outside, except as noise.

But I can change the basin’s music.

I can open the city valve so that a new thread of water spills into a basin that’s been running low. The Tender watching that basin will lift its head from the water’s surface, where it has been listening to its particular faint tone of drip, and its huge eyes will slide across the surface as new frequencies arrive.

The sound of the basin changes to them. They change to the new sound.

They adjust their bodies and the channels of the water in the same way, a slow shifting of the basin’s great map.

## VI. What I would be to you, if you came down

If you were to come down here, you would find yourself in one of three places in relation to me.

### 1. You are the new hire

They send you down with a clipboard and a safety vest, neon yellow plastic that will be brown with dust and salt stains by your second shift.

You hear me before you see me.

The basin filters sound in strange ways. My voice comes to you distorted, as though you are hearing me through glass water. You might find me near the Choir Wall, adjusting a sensor I no longer read but maintain for the city above.

Your clipboard will say “noisy anomaly.”

You will ask me where the noise is.

I will show you the hex basin the city claims is making trouble. The water there is thin, film-greenish, shallow. The tile is clean of any silt, but there is a faint, uneven pattern of drops where the water slides over its edge.

The water is dripping in triplets instead of pairs today.

The tenants above report hearing three drops instead of two. The change of pattern is unsettling enough to file a complaint.

“This,” I say aloud for you, because you can still hear words, “is the source.”

I will tap the ceramic tile with my metal strip. The tone rings sharp and clear, not dull. The water responds to the vibration by changing the pattern of its drops from triple to double again for a few moments.

“See?” I say. “It just needs to be heard differently.”

You will write down my explanation and the time of the adjustment on the clipboard, and then you will climb back to the city, where the air will be thin and too loud. You might not come down again.

If you do, you might become me.

### 2. You are the safety inspector

They send you down with regulations and a hard hat.

You find the basin too hot and too humid for the code the city uses. You mark a list of violations: inadequate air exchange, standing water, no guard rail along the path of the basins, no visible signage indicating water depth levels.

The list of violations you produce is an accurate description of the basin, but it has nothing to do with what happens here.

You might happen to see me working with one of the Tenders.

You will stand at the edge of the dark brick, and you will see the long-limbed creature with its organs of fluid and its huge eyes watching the water, and you will see me crouching beside the basin with the metal strip in my hand, not touching the water but listening to its tone.

You will see how the water changes between us, not because of a word but because of a change in sound.

The sight of that will make you uncomfortable in a way you cannot explain in your report.

You will climb back to the city and write what you can write. You will attach photos of stagnant water and rust and humidity.

You won’t see the music.

I will stay, and the music will stay.

### 3. You are one of them

You appear not from the city stairs but from the basin itself.

You are not human-shaped. You are long-limbed and membrane-pale, your fluid organs shifting under your translucent skin.

You move from one basin to the next, following the water’s old patterns of flow and seep, and you find them altering.

At the center of the bowl, where the main inflow basin receives the city’s waste heat and overflow, you find me reaching up to the metal ribs that ring the basin wall, my touch not hard but precise enough to change how they ring when the water drops hit them.

You might hear me as you hear the basin itself: not as noise, but as change.

You might feel my presence as a new annoyance in the water’s signal, as a distortion of the drip pattern that you must correct for by adjusting your own route across the clay terraces and dark brick channels.

You might feel the basin’s quiet music grow uneven when my hand shifts a valve, or smooths a new channel of runoff between tiles.

If you and the others change your movements often enough to respond to my actions, the basin might one day treat me as it treats you: not as noise, but as part of its own slow, ongoing symphony.

## VII. What I am

When they ask me upstairs what I do down here, I say, “I maintain the noise.”

I don’t mention the music.

I don’t mention the Tenders, because if they knew they would not think they were people like us. They would think they were leaks to be sealed or pipes to be fixed.

I don’t mention the way the basin speaks to me, or the way I speak back to it by moving the water through its tile fields.

If you asked me who I am in this world, I would tell you this:

I am the noise between the city and its basin.

I am the one who hears the dripping and remembers not the noise itself but the pattern. The pattern, the way it changes, and the way it must be changed.

I am the one who stays down when everyone else climbs up.

I am the one who adjusts the valve of the city’s quiet drip so that it doesn’t overwhelm the basin’s slow music, or cut its song off short; who moves the water from one crucible to the next without stepping into it; who listens to the rusted ribs that hold the clay bowl from the inside of the world.

I am not a creature of the city like you are, not anymore. And I am not a creature of the basin like the Tenders are, either.

I am the thing between them, whose body is made of what comes down and what stays below.

I am the Basin–Signaller.

I change the sound of the world.
