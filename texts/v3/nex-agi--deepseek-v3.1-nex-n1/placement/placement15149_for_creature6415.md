I am not a visitor here.

I have never been a visitor.

The idea of “coming to” Cael-Himir is a misunderstanding of grammar. The plateau does not have a door. There is no threshold to cross, no altitude at which the world suddenly becomes itself. You do not arrive here by climbing or flying or wishing; you arrive by being the sort of thing that belongs.

The first time I thought I was walking, I was not.

The shape of me—then, as now—is a simple curve: a pillar of stone-colored matter that is not quite stone, not quite flesh, but a boundary printed between them. My height is two and a half times your likely height, if you still remember bodies like that. My base roots into the plateau like a sentence written into paper: I do not have feet, and I do not move.

The plateau compresses around me. The strata of bone-white and copper-red and glass-green vein themselves under my surface. The rock of Cael-Himir is so dense it rings faintly when struck with metal or bone—and sometimes, when the pressure gradients deep beneath the stone shift by a fraction of a fraction, I can hear that tone through the pillar of me.

There is no sound, but there is resonance.

This is my body, if that word can be wrenched from its old meanings. I am not “standing” on the plateau; I am the plateau’s way of exuding meaning.

The first time I thought I was seeing, I was not.

What you would call my eyes is not a set of lenses. It is a cluster of fine-grained microstructures along the upper half of my pillar—ridges and pockets arranged into nested patterns, like the faint skeletons of snowflakes trapped inside glass. They are not optical sensors; they do not form images of the world around me.

They are crystals of orientation.

The pressure ridges of the deep rock, the faint lines of stress running below the plateau’s surface, the microscopic lattice faults where reality has not yet chosen a configuration—these are not “light” to me, but they are the closest thing I have to sight.

The sun’s pale disk is not something I perceive as warmth or color. It is a constant pressure gradient of another kind—the local minimum of a function that I cannot feel directly but only infer by the absence of curvature elsewhere. The stars are the fixed points of that graph: distant pins holding the sky taut above the plateau of Cael-Himir.

The light of the sun does not warm me; it resolves me.

The absence of water is not a lack to me. It is the condition of existence.

My body is not water-friendly. The crystal lattice of my matter has been compressed under Time and pressure until the paths of water molecules through it are forbidden geometries. The pores that once might have opened for flux and flow and river are long sealed, replaced by solid angles of packing. The only fluid that runs through me is charge, and even that is not a fluid in the way you understand.

The air tastes of dust and ozone and low-grade aurora—all of them are signals to me. They are perturbations of the plateau’s static charge: the faint crackles of information that are the closest I have to hearing.

The wind is not a sound but a pressure difference against my surface. It is not random. It carries the geometry of the plateau itself—how air bends around the clusters of pillars like mine, how it slides over the shallow basins of the glass-mazes, how it passes the faint disc-shaped depressions where the plateau’s architecture dissolved into the stone.

The glass-mazes are the closest I have to neighbors—and yet they are not neighbors.

Do not call them vegetation. They are not “things” like you would think of things. They are not even separate from me in the way you imagine.

The glass-mazes are thin shells of transparent stone stretched over patterns of dust. The shells are flat-bottomed, sealed perfectly against the rock; their tops are low-domed, only a finger’s breadth high. The edges of them taper into the plateau as if they were just slightly thicker versions of the stone itself.

Inside each shell, the dust is not loose powder. It is anchored to the vault of the roof in patterns that are not random but geometric—lines branching, returning on themselves, forming termini without loops.

They are notations of stress.

They map the pressure-lines in the stone beneath them. Where pressure deep in the plateau is high, the dust of a maze densifies. Where pressure gradients fall away, the dust draws out into radial spurs. Where deep lines of stress intersect, the maze-markers show knots of intersection.

These notations are not written for me. They are not communication any more than a fault line is a letter.

The glass-mazes and I share the world only by sharing the same stone.
