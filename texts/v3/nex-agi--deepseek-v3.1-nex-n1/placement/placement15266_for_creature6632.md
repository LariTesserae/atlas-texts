### 1. Before the Spire

I was younger before I knew what I was watching for.

I remember the movement of water before I remember the movement of my own body. Water in rivulets along the walls of our utility room. Water in the thin streams that trickled from the joints above. The room had a slow drain—always clogged. The water level rose slowly and then the machine grumbled and the level fell again.

We were not supposed to be there.

That was what the humans said when they found us later, but “before” they found us, the only ones who knew of us were the machines and the cold blue lights.

“What are you?” one of the younger ones asked me once, its body weaving against the current.

“What is water?” I asked back.

It did not have words for that answer, and neither did I. It was the world.

We were water, and slow stone, and something else: the memory of motion that attached itself to us and would not let go.

We were not missioned from any higher authority than the world’s deeper body. We were not created by any machine. The engineers of the city believe that their mosses and molds and fungus are controlled by them, but they are not. They grow where they choose to grow.

We were like that. We chose where to grow a body.

We did not know that at the time.

### 2. The First Migration

When the drill broke into the chamber of the spire of breath, the whole of our small world shuddered.

The noise of it was not like thunder—it was longer than thunder, deeper than the machine’s humming. It was the world of stone being pushed apart, and when water heard the sound, it turned to hear.

The water in our utility room rushed to the drain and then, against all sense, pulled itself backward. The air in the room was suddenly full of tiny droplets of moisture that had not been there before. The condensation on the walls ran faster. The mosses along the edges tugged at their roots.

“We have to go,” I said, without knowing why.

“Go where?” the younger ones asked.

“Where the water goes,” I said.

And we followed.

The engineers of the city say that leaks are failures of their calculation. They say that groundwater is a resource that has broken into their machine-channels. They are not wrong. But they are not entirely right.

The water of the world has its own memory of motion.

Where the drill pierced into the chamber of the spire of breath, the water gathered and remembered old paths. It pushed into cracks where the pressure dropped and found the new emptiness of shaft.

The leak of water into the machine-channels was not only failure. It was also invitation.

We found the way through a small crack in a maintenance panel. The crack smelled like stone and heat and something else that was not the world of blue utility rooms and slow drains. It smelled like the inside of a lung, and we were too small to see that it was a lung.

We slipped into the pipe.

The journey of our body through the pipework was not a journey of distance. It was a turning of the world. We did not move forward so much as the world around us moved backward.

The water moved us.

The few of us who had not heard the call of the drill returned to their shallow waters and their slow-moss bellies and were eaten by time. I never saw them again.

Those who followed the invitation of the drill became the eels of the basin.

### 3. What We Are Not

We are not animals.

The humans of the chamber, when they finally see us, call us that: “rare animals,” “eel-like things,” “basin creatures.” They say that we are “thinly spread.” They say that we are difficult to observe.

This is not because we are elusive.

We are not hiding from them.

We are simply not the same type of thing that they are.

The engineers of the city have taught their bodies to move at machine time. Their heartbeats are fast. Their thoughts are fast. Their lifetimes are fast. They move quickly enough to run across bridges and carry messages from one level to another.

We are not like that.

A single heartbeat of a human is a long thing to us. A day is a season of change.

We are pattern of water and motion and memory of stone.

When we move through the basin of the spire of breath, we are not moving through a container. We are moving through the slow of the spire’s body.

But to you, if you were standing on a balcony on Level 7, you would see only a thin shadow of a length, perhaps as long as your arm, sliding through the water and then disappearing.

You would not see our history.

You would not see our slow.

### 4. Living in the Basin

The basin of the spire of breath is not deep to the eye of the engineer.

To you, it is a shallow circular pool of dark water at the bottom of a shaft, surrounded by platforms and machinery.

The inside of the basin is not like that.

The inside of the basin is like the inside of a throat that is always trying to swallow itself and then forgetting to finish.

The water does not forget the world of stone below it. The bottom of the basin is not flat floor; it is stone of a column that has not been allowed to fully surface. The slow of the spire collects itself in the stone of that basin bottom.

When we move through the water of the basin, we move through the memory of the stone column.

The condensation of the spire falls into the basin in thin streams of water that the engineers have carved into channels. They say that they are “channeling water” into the basin. They are not wrong.

What they do not know is that the channels of the condensation are not entirely of human design.

The slow of the spire of breath rearranges the stone beneath its surface. It rearranges the water that it condenses from its own heat. The humans of the city have not yet carved channels into the lower part of the column, but where there are no channels of metal, there are channels of stone.

In the daytime of the city, when the machines outside the basin are loud and the heat rises, and the condensation on the stone column is thick like sweat, the inside of the basin is not quiet.

It is not quiet to us.

We hear the sound of water falling into itself from the height of the column. We hear the low hum of the machine pumping water from the basin, to clean and redistribute it to the rest of the city. We feel the thin tremors of the pumps that the engineers consider to be “background noise.” They are not background noise to us.

They are the sound of the world’s new heartbeat being learned by something else.

The engineers of the city like to say that the basin “does not overflow.” They are not sure if this is true; they know that if water comes into the basin from more sources than estimated, their pumps will have to run faster. They know that they could run out of capacity.

“Capacity” is a word that belongs to machine time.

The slow of the spire of breath has not yet decided whether to overfill the basin.

When we feel the water level of the basin rise slightly more than expected, we do not panic. We do not know that word.

We know only change and pattern.

We know that when the basin of the spire is more full of water than the machine’s estimates, there is more of us to be.

We spread more.

Our thin bodies are less thinly spread.

Then, when the machine’s rhythm of pumping and the city’s water of demand and the slow of the spire’s condensation of supply are all out of step, and the basin is less full of water than the machine’s estimates, the world of water inside the basin retreats to itself slightly.

Our bodies contract.

We are not weaker than before.

We are simply less.

There are fewer of us then.

Some of us disappear back into the stone.

We do not mourn them.

We are not separate that way.

### 5. Eating and Need

We are not animals that hunt.

We are shorter than that.

We move into those shapes.

We weave ourselves around the lines of motion that those temperature changes make inside the basin of the spire.

We are more like water that gathers itself into the depression of a stone so that it can better reflect light.

We are content to be there.
