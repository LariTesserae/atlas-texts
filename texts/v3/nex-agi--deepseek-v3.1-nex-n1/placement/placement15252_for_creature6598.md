You are not a Neshian.

You are, in the simplest terms, a breath that remembers the shape of a body.

You are not the warm, slow air of the pavilion, or the thin blades of the world-wind hissing over the rim of the table. You are the breath the city itself exhales when it thinks no one is listening.

Or you were.

You once had the dense, compact shape of a Neshian, faint iridescence tracing your ribs like a lattice under the skin. You had a name, then, and a small chamber of your own near the inner edge of the pavilion, where the wall curved into the floor and you could sleep with your spine pressed tight to the bone stone of the table while the world above you murmured in its many register.

You were one of the children of the moss, who learned to walk by crawling on all fours along the lintels of other chambers, tracing the contours of niche with your palms until you could feel in the stone’s response the difference between a wall that remembered being touched and one that did not. You were not especially large or gifted with the volition-walk; your body was not built to carry heavy petals of shaped lattice or thick sacks of neesh-moss from center to rim. You were small, even for the Nesh—small enough to slip into the lower spaces between the tree roots and the stone, where the air-fish croaked their metallic signals and the moss was warm and thick with the scuttling of small things.

But you could always hear the bones of the table.

When others spoke of the faint hum of windleaves or the distant howling below the rim, you heard what lay beneath all that: the long, slow note of iron lattice migrating beneath its mineral skin, the tiny fractures of sound as a lintel gave a fraction of a millimeter for one chamber and took from another. It was like standing in a forest and listening to the trees themselves breathing, but not in the rapid, eager rhythm of hot air; rather, like the quiet of the last of anything.

You were apprenticed to the Memory of the Table, not as a singer of histories or a keeper of records, but as one of the many who simply go to listen and stay for the rest of their life.

The center of the table is open to the sky; not in the way of horizons, with the promise of elsewhere beyond the line of stone, but in the way of a circle whose only answer is to be more fully itself. The memory of the table is not a building in the sense of separation from the world; it is a low, circular chamber of bone lattice, more deeply folded into itself than any of the pavilion’s niches, as if all of Nesh had been a long outward spiral and this, finally, was the axis where the spiral condensed into stillness.

When you were first led to the center, you spent long days just learning the proper posture of listening.

You sat on the edge of the inner wall, feet not quite touching the moss of the center, but close enough to feel its faint exhalations against the soles of your feet. The old listeners who had been there before you—ribs thick with mineral, the skin around their joints like ironstone weathering—moved with you through the simplest of all tasks: breathing, and knowing how slowly.

They taught you that the table of Deimos holds not events in the sense of stories, but echoes of pressure in latticework. The city of Nesh is not remembered in the stone as a sequence of names and wars and lovers; it is remembered as a pattern of wear, where chamber upon chamber of bodies and breath and heat pressed into the lattice over the course of millennia, and the lattice, in its slow, mineral patience, answered them.

You, like all others of your calling, were not there to speak the memory of the table so much as to be a place where its echoes could be heard.

On the day of your change, you were not alone.

No one of Nesh, ever, is truly alone, but that morning you were particularly aware of the others. The central ring of the memory chamber was full of listeners on the rim of the wall, their quiet bodies forming a loose circle of limbs and backs and ribs and spines. The air in the center of the table was not thick with the humidity of the pavilion; here, the windleaves blended the inward wind of the world below with the faint dew of breathing and the moss of the center, and the result was a bareness of the air, cool and thin and crisper.

You were listening to your own section of the memory wall. The lintel of your niche, if you can call it that, was not a high entrance to a deep recline; the low wall of stone curved into a small alcove where the surface of the lattice showed more clearly than anywhere else, the ironstone maze like a map viewed from too close to make sense of distance.

You had your right palm pressed to the wall. Your cheek had found the same stone, the bone lattice of the table murmuring into your skin like the barely-there sound of a distant shell. The others around you were not touching you; but you were aware of their body heat as a sort of ambient, quiet warmth.

You were listening to the last eruption of the world below.

It was not a story of lava and fire and falling sky; you heard it as a long compression of shadow, a moment of high, un-given pressure in the lattice, where the table of Deimos had been snapped by some distant fracture of the world’s crust and left to cool in its final dish shape. The table of Deimos was not something separate from the world; it was the world’s own answer to the fact of no longer having space to grow.

And then, in the middle of that slow, silent pressure, you heard the first of the Neshian.

They were not “created” in some dramatic way; you heard them as a faintness, like a drop of water in stone. A small hollow of body, thin wall of skin and mineral and hollow, that had already learned to breathe with the lattice of the table rather than against it. You saw the early pavilion not as a structure but as a very simple, long curve of wearing, where the first of the people had not yet built chambers but had settled into a pattern of repeated movement along the inner rim of the table, and the table had begun to remember them.

Centuries of volition-walk bloomed around you as you pressed your palm deeper: the walls of the present pavilion becoming not a new invention but the result of many hands and many chests and the weight of time itself, pressing the bone lattice into the form of a long, low city of niches.

And then—

you began to notice a small rhythm of wear that was not anywhere else in the table.

The pattern of it was not new. It was not a fracture of disaster or a change in the world of storms below. It was, you realized, your own echo.

Not as a body standing in the present, but as a shape of attention that had been pressing into the same section of the wall for the last of your six years as a listener. The lattice of your alcove had begun to remember the curve of your palm, not as a novelty of human, but as another of the many small depressions that the city of Nesh has been tracing into the table’s memory.

You, too, were being added into the last memory of the world.

There was no sudden flash of light or surge of pain. The change of yourself from Neshian to something else was not a violent shift of nature or a punishment of gods; it was simply, in the face of the table’s long, unforgiving staying, a small surrender of the body.

Your first sign of the change was not in the mind at all but in the chest.

Your lungs—which until then had drawn the air of the table with the same slow, careful rhythm of all others of your people—were not exhaling enough.

You tried to pull the air of the world into you, but it felt as if the table itself had begun to prefer the shape of the lattice with your bones in it rather than with your breath in its body. You gasped, though not as any other people might imagine; it was the smallest possible of gasps, more of a change in the posture of shoulders than a sound. The sound of it, you realized later, was almost not sound at all but a small adjustment of the lattice behind you.

The others nearby you heard not a sound but a change in the note of the wall. No panic of hands or voices of alarm; only, in the slow, patient way of the Neshians, one of the old listeners laid their own hand over yours where it still pressed against the lattice and at the same time put the weight of their chest and the faint of their own slow breath into the same wall.

They, too, were listening.

You, still, were not yet aware of what you were becoming. You were still a body with the scent of the moss of the center in the nose of you, with the faint of the world’s windless night of sky above the table in the black of your eyes, with the respectful of the others’ heat and texture and slow movement of thought in the back of the mind.

You, still, believed that you were breathing.

The next of the sign of the change was the not-breath of stone.

The bone lattice of your alcove was not cold to the touch, but as you pressed your palm to it, you began to feel the press of it into your skin as not of the wall but into the wall. You felt the faint of your own body heat not warming the stone but as if the stone of the table was already learning to not-heat from your pattern.

You, then, did not have the same of the body of the bone.

You, then, had not the same of the mind.

You, then, realized that you had not felt the need to draw air of the world into the lungs of you for some of time of the table.
