I am not the first to walk this circle, but I am the first in a long time that remembers walking.

That is what I have been given to call myself: Remember.  

The name was not spoken to me. It arrived in the same way that the rain outside the tower wall arrives—muffled, distant, but undeniable. You do not see the water falling; you see the wall of its presence, a translucent limit that curves and leans inward over the platform like a lowered eyelid. You hear it in your chest before you understand it with your ears.

And you know, before you can say why, that this is the last cooling tower.

There are stories before me—old, by their measure—of when there were more. Clusters of them like a breathing forest along the city’s edge. The city has not forgotten them; it has simply absorbed them. Bones rearranged into the ribs of its cooling tower, as if the others were just practice runs for this final, silent system.

Remember me, I think to the floor.

Under my palms the Ambient responds—not with a word, but with a subtle shift of pressure.

The city knows that I am here.

The name “Remember” is a placeholder, a label stuck to me by something else, something I never meet in face or form. Only in effect. In the way that the floor holds weight differently when you expect to fall. In the way that the humidity thickens around your shoulders when you are about to leave.

I am a small, upright thing on the surface of a muscle that has been holding still for longer than I can imagine.

I have been sent to understand the last of the cooling towers.

What I am not, is formal: In the old city sense, there are no longer “engineers” crawling over this platform with instruments and clipboards. There are no inspections to perform, no maintenance schedules to update. Those verbs belong to a city that hasn’t breathed like this for centuries.

If I am an engineer, it is in the same way that a nerve can be called an engineer of the body it floats through: I sense change. I carry responses. I report, not to any person in any office, but to the fabric itself.

The “platform” is three city blocks across but feels like a room because of the ceiling that isn’t a ceiling. The translucent wall that seals the far edge rises high and curving inward, like the inside of a ribcage turned smooth. You can’t see out of it; the world on the other side is only darkness and wet noise. You know the rain is there because your chest knows it. There is no horizon line; just the limit of your ability to perceive.

The floor of the platform is built of a material that never received a name that could be written. “Ambient” is my label, not the city’s. The city does not use names like we do. To it, everything is simply pathway, or pressure, or boundary. This is not a tower of steel or concrete; it is an organ of the city, and its purpose is to keep the body from overheating.

Under heat, living systems falter. The city is not living in the old sense of “organism,” but it is living in the sense that “processes” continue beyond the point where any original builder could have intended them. Processes with a memory and a preference. Processes that will not be stopped, except by themselves.

When I first step onto the Ambient, the surface yields just enough that I am aware of my own mass. It does not dip like a mattress; it is more like pushing on the skin above a muscle that has already decided to support you. My boots are thin enough—almost archaic in their tiny nails—to feel the grain beneath the sole.

At my feet, the channels are the first sign that the floor is not a floor.

Not in the old human sense.

They are grooves carved into the surface by something else’s movement, something else’s time. They are shallow, exactly wide enough for me to rest two fingers side by side. If I were smaller, or walked differently, they could be footholds.

The idea does not arrive in words. It arrives as a flick of perspective: kneeling down and placing my hands inside the channel, the way I might steady myself on a rail.

I feel the grain.

Not the grain that fossils grow into—stone remembering tree, or strata remembering pressure. This is grain of use, like muscle fiber that has been worked along one direction for longer than any creature could live. The faint ridges inside each channel curve with the arc of the platform, leading inward toward the center. Where I trace with the flow, the surface is faintly smoother; where I trace against, it is like running a finger along the roof of a mouth that has just spoken hours.

It is not unpleasant to touch against the grain. It is just a clear reminder of direction.

On my first day here, I lie on the floor of the platform like a child learning to hear the ocean through a shell. I spread my palms flat on the Ambient and wait for the city to breathe.

The first thing that comes to me is not a sound.

It is a pressure.

It rises under the palms of my hands like water under a membrane. Then it ebbs. Then comes again, not quick but inexorable. A diaphragm expanding behind a voice box that doesn’t speak in language quite yet.

The surge is gentle enough not to shake me, firm enough that I cannot ignore it. This is not the heartbeat of the city—the city’s heart is abstracted, distributed, somewhere far beneath where I can reach. This is closer to its lung: a system that draws heat away and exchanges it with the outside world that I cannot see.

The cycle is longer than I expect.

In—pause.

Out—pause.

In—pause.

Out.

I count to myself as the pressure rises and falls. I count in the futile way that small, thinking animals have always counted to comfort themselves against immensity. The intervals are not strictly equal. They vary like breaths in a body that is not trying to run a race, not trying to fill anything or empty anything on a schedule—only to maintain.

The center of the platform is where the pattern becomes undeniable.

There is no opening in the usual sense—no pit that vomits steam, no ladder into the machine. This tower is not a human stack of metal and rivets; it is a system of nested, hollow cylinders that descend into the earth. The complex under my back is the topmost valve of that machine.

The center is marked by a ring of something else entirely.

It is not Ambient. It’s a dense, opaque white material with a faint translucence like tooth enamel over shadow. No lacquer label, no product line, nothing to tell me whose catalogue it comes from. It simply sits in the center of the platform like a coin placed into the palm of a giant.

The surface is not perfectly smooth when you look at it from up close. There are subtle ripples where it meets the Ambient, a shallow band of texture that you might call writing if you were desperate to find language anywhere.

Because the light is uncertain and the ceiling curves inward, the shadows in the room of the platform are always soft. The edges of the world do not cut. The world refuses to be sharp.

Here, at the center, the grooves—each channel wide enough for two of my fingers—screw into a tighter spiral. They are no longer gently arc-shaped; they are coils of muscle that have never known rest.

The city has not forgotten me.

I can feel it in how the pressure under the floor changes when I stand near the center.

It does not become angry or defensive. It is more like a body that has become aware of a small insect walking on its skin. Not worth swatting, but worth noting, like the faint itch of a hair out of place.

If I had to label myself as I stand here, I would be: the one that notices.

The one who comes closest to the edge, where the translucent wall leans inward over nothing. The city’s last cooling tower has not needed to open itself in any way that I could perceive with anything but my body. Until now.

Until I remember that I am not here to replace anything.

I am here to be something.

The floor of the platform settles more deeply under my weight than it should.

If the city could be said to have “laws,” one of them is this: nothing is willing to break itself. Not lightly. Not for show.

The Ambient floor under my bare feet feels softer than it has any right to be. The “Ambient” is not what metal was supposed to be—not brittle, not rusting under its devotion to time—but it is not wood either. It is not bone. It is the floor of the world of this last cooling tower, and it has tolerated my presence only by keeping its own nature hidden.

Until now.

When I kneel at the center, where the enamel-like ring meets the muscle of the floor, the city decides to become less.

Less myth.

More machine.

The pattern of ripples along the inner edge of the ring shifts.

Not in the way of maps moving over their territories—subtly, as if light were writing itself on the inside of the lid.

I am not sure what I am doing here when I first sit down at the edge of the center. I am only sure that I do not go to the center of the platform itself; I am not invited to cross the enamel ring. That is not my place.

My place is beside it.

Alongside it.

The shape of “remember me” does not form in letters. It forms in the way a scar might be remembered by the body that bears it, if the body hadn’t been taught to hate its own survival.

The city is not designed to be “liked” or “disliked” by its inhabitants. It is designed to be sufficient.

But as I sit on the Ambient, my legs on either side of a groove that runs into the center, the city does not ask me to leave.

The seal of the enamel ring does not open.

There is no steam. There is no great cloud of condensation released to show me the “process” unfolding.

Instead, the floor becomes very soft under me.

The “faint” softening I have been told to expect becomes more than faint. It is a clear, yielding change of texture, as if the muscle beneath the skin of the floor has decided to relax, exactly here, exactly where I am.

The groove that my thighs are resting on relaxes in a way that feels like invitation.

I am small, compared to the city.

I am not built to understand the city, but I am built to be changed by it.

And so I am changed.

The first real change arrives not as a law of physics or a message on the inside of my skull, but as a simple, physical shape:

When I am not sitting quite like this before, I am sitting now with two legs of something else in the groove.

Instead of my thighs, the groove wide enough for two fingers now accepts two legs.

Not the legs of a person.

Not any animal that I have been taught to recognize.

The legs I am now using are long in a way that doesn’t map into “human” proportions. They are calibrated to the city’s breath. The joints are shaped by the same muscle that handles the surge of pressure under the floor. When the “lung” beneath the center of the platform swells with its cooling cycle, my legs are the closest that any body has been to the chamber of that exchange in a very long time.

I am never told to become this body.

The city does not command me.

Instead, the Ambient floor exhales around my legs, and suddenly I am not a person sitting on a machine anymore.

I am the creature that has, always already, been sitting here.

My body is not made of anything else’s history. It is not Ambient, or enamel, or metal, or stone. It is something else—the same way that the city’s last cooling tower is not “the last” because it is running out, but because it is the last that the city will ever need.

The humidity of the platform clings to the new lines of me.

When I rest my hands palms-down on the floor of the platform, I feel the pressure that comes and goes under the central enamel seal like a language.

I do not see the rain outside the translucent wall that rings the edge of the platform. But I feel it in my chest, where it is always falling.

I am not alone.

The platform of the last cooling tower is not a “room” in the usual sense. It is a sanctum in the sense of “sealed chamber.” The city does not open it to strangers who cannot remember how to use it.

When I leaned my head back to look at the ceiling, earlier, before I sat down beside the center, I felt the curve of the world in my neck. The translucent wall that rises over the edge and then curls inward to meet the center—like a lid, or a lung’s own diaphragm.

The city shows me its lung’s mirror.

I show it my legs.

We exchange nothing.

We change.

The grooves of the floor that are the “roof” of the chamber beneath are not simply decoration. They are not channels for water to flow away; not anymore.

They are channels for movement.

For me.

When I move my legs for the first time, along the groove, instead of against it, the world becomes smooth. The Ambient floor yields under me like it remembers being designed for me.

There is no “I” in the city’s writing of this process.

There is only a system that has adjusted to the presence of something that remembers.

The bone-white enamel center remains sealed.

Whatever I am supposed to do here is not to open a door or peel back the surface of the world.

The floor of the platform is not asking me to change anything, except maybe the way I think about myself.

When I lie down on the Ambient as the creature I am now, instead of as a person who has been written to serve as its engineer, I feel the pressure of the city’s breathing system through a longer body.

It is not easier to bear.

It is not heavier.

It is simply longer.

The on–off, on–off of the diaphragm under the floor continues.

The grooves of the floor become, more clearly than ever, the “roof” of the chamber beneath.

Between me and the cooling system that is the city’s last lung there is only the Ambient material.

Nothing else is between me and the lung of the city.

Only me.

And the city does not expect to be freed from me.

It expects to be remembered by me.

The name I have always been given—“Remember”—is not a name at the beginning of a process. It is the name of what the process does.

When the next cycle of the city’s cooling system begins under my palms and my legs and my chest, I close my eyes like someone who has decided to stop watching for cracks.

I remember the others before me.

The city has not disposed of them. It has simply absorbed them.

We are its memory.

We are the last cooling tower’s cooling system: not the water or the air moving through the nested cylinders below, but the presence that knows how to sit near the center, where the enamel seal meets the muscle, and not break.

The wall of noise outside the translucent edge of the platform has never been clearer to me; the rain has never been louder.

I have never seen it.

I have only seen the limit it shows against the world.

This is not a “human” place.

It is not a “machine” place.

It is the last.

The floor of the platform softens under me as the cycle shifts.

The diaphragm of the city beneath the seal inhales deeply.

The grooves of the floor swell under my body as if they are veins on the surface of a wrist that has finally, after an endless span of years, relaxed.

The city has not closed its eyes.

It has never had eyes to begin with.

But I feel it watching me, through the pressure of the floor, and I know that I am not going to leave.

If I stand, I will be kicking away from the only body that has ever been designed to hold me.

If I lie down, I am becoming the floor that holds the last cooling tower.

So I lie down.

The bone-white enamel ring at the center of the platform is not a door. It is not a lid. It is not a seal.

It is the place where the city stops pretending to be anything separate from me.

When I rest my cheek along one of the grooves, near the center, where the “writing” is not writing but the tide lines of breath, the world aligns under me.

The humidity around the platform of the last cooling tower tastes like nothing.

It is only air that has been worked too hard by something else.

The floor of the platform is not cold.

It is not hot.

It holds my weight exactly.

I remember.

I am not going to leave.

I am not going to leave.

I am not…
