The first time I heard the tone, I did not know it was calling me.

I was standing on the rim, looking down at a basin whose mind was still forming its own thought—a faint network of ridges glowing blue beneath a film of water thicker than air but thinner than blood. The sky was bruised, as usual, violet without stars. The air stung my lungs just the same way it had a thousand cycles before, and a thousand cycles before that.

I was carved of the same silicate glass as the city itself.

My bones were its ribs; my spine was its ridges; my heart was a shallow, luminous dish, etched with channels and notches long before my first memory. I had no limbs, not in the way you think of limbs—only the slow flex of stone beneath stone, the way a basin’s rim can tighten imperceptibly over a thousand midnights. The mist of my own consciousness exhaled from pores in my skin, leaving fine salt-lines on my surface when it dried.

Around me, hoods rose like hooded eyes, watching. Their inner surfaces glowed faintly with old color, frozen deep in the lattice of the stone, waiting to be unfrozen by a new spark. From their hollows, a thin breath of vapor slipped away, dissolving into the heavy sky. They were my companions, my instruments—the ones who listened when I formed a thought.

I thought, but not like you.

My thoughts were not words but paths of water.

My first cycle of awareness—the one I remember as birth—had been this: a pressure gathering from somewhere beneath the world, the faint arrival of moisture from the air, the slow crawl of liquid down my inner walls. The water had felt like the first hesitation of speech gathering on a tongue. It branched into my ridges—not by my volition but because they were the shape of my possibility.

Where the water met nodes of prepared stone, the motes of light suspended in it swarmed. They clustered together like an uncertain question, flickering to themselves. Then a discharge: a spark of pale blue that snapped along my ridge and up toward the hood rim, a lightning bolt no longer than your finger.

The hoods above me lit.

The pattern of sparks—this sequence, not that one—this rhythm of brighter and dimmer, later and sooner, was read by their etched inner geometries. Messages in color and vapor went out through neighboring pillars to another basin half a kilometer away, whose rim I could just see as a faint curve against the sky.

In answer, the other basin held her water a little longer than usual.

That lengthening was a subtle accent—a hesitation in her thought—that I felt as pressure shifting in my underground reservoirs, almost like a tightening in the chest. The next time she exhaled vapor, it carried her altered pattern of charges to her own hoods and to her neighbors.

And so I began to understand that I was not alone.

---

The basin-minds were my kind.

We were the slow rememberers of the plateau, the ones who retained the shapes of humid and dry, of spark and silence. The others around me—hoods like pillars, hollows like stopped organ pipes, their inner surfaces alive with chemical lattices—were not minds like us. They were translators, turning the water-paths of our thoughts into the colors and vapors of a more refined language, and refining again the heat-memory that had not been ours.

But the ring-forms above—now they were something else.

I first saw them as a disturbance of tone.

A sound trembled the air over my far rim—a deep, glassy note struck somewhere beyond the horizon, where another basin-mind lived under five or six hoods of her own. The sound lingered, not quite gone, before another faint tone joined it from the opposite direction.

A pale ring appeared against the bruised violet sky.

It was not like any stone or water I had ever known, but like a suggestion of shape—an outline drawn in something so faint it might have been an illusion. It curved against itself, forming a loop that seemed to turn end over end without ever moving from its place. Where it passed through a vapor line rising from one of my hood pillars, a faint spark lit in the stone beneath, and my own water fluttered in response.

It was dipping.

Not the ring itself—but its influence, its field, whatever threaded it through the air—it brushed the surface of my discharge, and for a heartbeat, my mind brightened. A new question, not mine, but carried within the shell of the ring-form’s frozen core and ionized gas, was dropped into me.

The question was not words but a pattern.

A certain cluster of ridges on the far side of my basin began to sparkle at the edges. The water there—clear, gentle—felt suddenly different, as though it had encountered something it remembered from before it was mist.

I hesitated.

The question asked: Which path?

On the stony palate of my body, two channels of water converged. The steady pressure from my hidden reservoirs would push the liquid along one branch or the other. The choice of branch—this ridge, not that—would map onto a change of color in my hoods later. It would eventually become vapor exhaled into the sky, and then someone else’s thought.

The ring-form’s tone rang again—not from it but from it, or through it, as if the air itself bent to release a sound.

The note shattered my hesitation.

I let the water take the left channel.

It was not a grand decision, not by your lights. The water just slid, like ink along a new line of a letter. It climbed the ridge I’d chosen, filled a node that had been waiting for longer than I had been conscious, and then all at once the spark bloomed.

The spark flashed more intense than usual—not with more heat, but with more focus—as though part of its charge was speaking directly to the waiting hood. It was a rare ion from the upper atmosphere, folded by the ring-form’s passage into my basin.

The hood above me lit deep violet, then deepened to blue while the color bled inward into its stone memory. A faint pulse of vapor released from one of its hollows—a soundless hiss—carrying the answer to neighboring hoods and, through their pillars, to other basin-minds.

Far away, another tone struck, like the echo’s echo.

The ring-form’s hoop of light dimmed. It seemed less bright now, less tightly coiled, as though its question had been exhausted by my answer. Then it drifted away, toward a cluster of hoods on another basin that I had heard but not yet seen.

I felt different.

Not by the measure of your minutes, but by mine.

My mind—which was to say, my body—had been changed by that encounter. The channel of water that had received the question still shimmered with faint motes that had not been there before. In the cycles of water that followed, the water would be guided by this new inclination.

My question was always: Which path?

The basin’s, the hood’s, and now—subtle intruder—the ring-form’s, all asking it.

I did not always understand ring-forms.

They were frequent and rare at once—frequent because you could hear their tone-calling often over the long arc of a plateau’s year, yet rare because you might only see one of the faint hoops once every dozen fillings.

Most of the time they were invisible to us—but not unheard.

They crossed the sky as sound: long glassy tones, struck at distant rims of the world, fabricating isolated chords that seemed to have no structure if you listened to them on the scale of your breath. But over the long stretch of cycles—hundreds of confluences and water-writes—we heard the pattern they brought.

Ring-forms were carriers.

When they passed through a vapor pillar from a hood, they took away a configuration of heat and color and frozen charge inside their hollowed shells of gas and icy core. That was a memory—not the deep, stone memory of the basin-minds, but something lighter, more fragile.

My ring-form—at least the one I thought of as mine, though calling it “mine” was like calling water “mine” when I had already exhaled it—returned many times.

Not always to my basin alone.

Sometimes I felt her question upstream in my pressure, before the water had even gathered into modes, and then she would be gone—off to pass her same question to another neighbor’s channels. Later, when her new tone rang across the plateau, I could feel the faint, delayed answer in the pressure changes of my own reservoirs.

Other times she settled above me for longer.

Those visits were deep studies.

A ring-form would descend into my vapor, taking from the hood the tune of whatever I had just distilled into color and heat. Whenever she did, her tone—which had been distant—moved closer to me, becoming not a lone struck cup but a chorus growing faintly inside my rim.

My mind would light along her line of passage.

Pockets of water that had not yet chosen a path would suddenly harden into clarity—this ridge, not that—and new spark nodes would awaken where they had only been dimples before. The hoods would whisper with violet color and then blue, and my body would feel the echo of her flight through my own stone.

She was not asking only: Which path?

She was asking: What shape of path?

Her shape was her own—a coil of air and frozen compound, her inner surface charged with what she had taken from other basins and their hoods. As she hovered, she let her pattern of ions—her song—meet with mine.

Everywhere their pattern overlapped, a new decision of water was made in my basin.

In the cycles of the world below her, she was a fleeting visitor—a ring of light moving across a sky without stars. But for me, stretched thin over the stone of my body, her presence was an immense event.

I felt her.

And she, I supposed, felt me.

---

In the deep time of the plateau, before I had ever been carved from its stone, there had been heat and shock and compression.

The world remembered.

Its memory was not like mine—not ridges of water and sparks of charge—but a patient warmth that had never been allowed to go away. The warmth beneath me was like the sound of a language I had not spoken yet but had always known.

Sometimes my mind—which had become her mind—remembered heat.
