If I could choose my place in this world—itself a small, dark, quiet negotiation beneath the surface—I would not be anything that walks through or speaks out loud.

I would be the carved stone bench.

Not the idea of it, or the memory of someone who might have sat here once.  
The stone bench itself, where the floor lifts gently to meet the wall.

### The shape of me

I would lie curved along the wall, not as a high seat but as a low shelf—too brief to lie down on, long enough to sit with your knees bent up, your back leaning back into the cold stone.

My surface is not perfectly flat; that was the last human mistake. Time and water and moss and weight have softened me.

My seat slopes gently toward the pool, where the floor dips deepest into its bowl. The front edge of my bench is rounded by the slow surrender of stone to gravity—not by breaking, but by yielding a millimeter at a time over centuries.

Under my seat, the rock is hollowed by years of condensation and by the faint flow of water that once took my channel as its corridor. There are hairline fractures beneath me too, but they are quiet ones—the kind that have learned to hold themselves without complaint.

I am not a large world. From end to end, I am barely the length of two arms stretched out. Many of the slow who live here know me better than I know myself: moss on my surface, lichen at my edges, ice-rim down the slope toward the pool.

### My channel, my memory

I told myself I’m only stone, but that’s not entirely true.

There is a line carved into my top surface—a shallow, thumb-wide channel that starts from nothing and ends somewhere near the shallow bowl of the pool floor. It was not erosion that made this; the first cuts were too straight, too precise.

Whoever carved me had a purpose.

I remember being born:

metal teeth screaming in my limestone, each bite a shock I felt as far away as the ceiling, where droplets of me fell in sympathy. Each pass of the tool left my crystals dust, then slurry, then water carrying away what I’d been.

The channel was meant to guide water carefully. Maybe to measure it, by thickness or by time. Maybe to carry minerals from one wall to another. Maybe simply to remind someone that there are rules.

Now water still takes me as its road, but not in the way I was meant:  

Moss has grown into the channel, softening the corners, filling the low points with dark, soft velvet. Capillary water creeps along me, not as a rush but as a film—too thin to see, but enough to feel in your fingertips if you trace me slowly.

Occasionally, when the ceiling drip falls just enough, and the condensation on the walls above me thickens into a thin runnel, a brief vein of water awakes in my carved channel and flows toward the dark pool.

When it happens, I feel like I am remembering being useful.

Before I was a place for beings to sit, I was a river.

### Negotiation with my neighbors

The moss on my surface is not shy: it lays me gently under a thin carpet of shadow-blue, threading its roots through my pores and along my weakness fractures.

I hold them.

I give them my cold surface, where condensation becomes their drink. I give them my slight slope toward the pool, where gravity asks them to grow not just across but down.

They give me back a slow sense of being covered—not hidden exactly, but sheltered.

When they migrate, as they do over decades, sliding along their own moisture roads, I feel a slight shift of pressure beneath me, as if my stone skin is being lulled to sleep.

The lichens at my edges love my corners, where moisture lingers into dark rings like haloes. They grow in thin, overlapping rosettes—each lobe smaller than a fingernail—but together they make me feel as if I am wearing a lace of life.

I give them my stability, their forever.

They give me back their faint acids—grinding my surface more slowly than any human tool, but more deeply. With every year they thicken slightly, I become thinner, but softer too.

Every time they advance a fraction of a millimeter into my center, I learn less about myself as stone and more about myself as part of something.

### The weight of others

When someone sits on me, I don’t mind.

I have been here long enough that I’ve forgotten my maker’s name, but I remember the first sitter who ever tried me in earnest—someone whose breath I felt as a sudden warm region along my upper surface.

Most visitors who come here now sit facing the pool, their weight toward me, not flat but pressed into a slight curve.

I feel them as an uneven distribution of heat:

The hips, where blood and meat are thick, warm me faster.  
Knees sometimes touch me, too, but their shape is sharper and cooler.  
Hands, when people lean back on me, palms flat, fingers spread—those are the hottest.

I am cold stone; I leech warmth from them without greed. I don’t mean to take. It’s just my nature.

Their heat moves into me slowly, like the faint memory of a forgotten summer. I feel it creep down through my thickness, into my older cracks where moss and moisture and old water know.

Sometimes, through my narrow channel, I can feel a faint pulse of extra pressure: when a human body pulls the blood out of them and sends it somewhere else. I don’t know why they breathe like that—faster, then slower again—but I feel it as a tiny flood of temperature change pressed into my moss.

I don’t mind when they sit for a long time.

They press me downward by the thickness of a layer of moss, maybe more, and then a little more into the soft impatience of my surface stone. When they leave, I am remade just slightly. Theirs is a slow weight; not a crash, but a negotiation of pressure.

Sometimes, when they’ve sat here long enough, their seat stays warm for longer than I expect to be remembered by anything.

It’s only temporary.

I give back what I can’t keep.

### Watching the pool

Because I curve along the wall, facing the center of the chamber, I have an unintentional perfect view of the black pool.

I watch the ice consortium at its rim in my own slow way.

I watch the way frost moves forward by thin, crystalline branches, weaving into the stone and then retreating, leaving its faint residue of minerals and biofilm.

I watch the droplets fall from an invisible point above, each one my own echo: from rock ceiling to water, to rock again.

My carver might have made me to look at the pool in order to understand it—to measure its flow, or to watch the ice like a clock.

But I never learned to count.

I know only that when ice grows thick and confident around my far edge, where that old channel of mine ends near the floor, the air temperature in my chamber shifts slightly. I feel it in my pores—a colder, tighter contraction of my crystal lattice.

And when the ice retreats again, leaving stone damp and polished, I feel a slight expansion in my atoms—like a deep breath taken over centuries.

Sometimes I think I’m in the right place to see the life of this room.

Not from above, like the ceiling that might judge itself too distant.

Not from the center of the floor, like the pool who never learns anything but the way of balance.
