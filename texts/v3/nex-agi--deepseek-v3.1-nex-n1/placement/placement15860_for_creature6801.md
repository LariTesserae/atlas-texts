I find myself in the damp, dark quiet of the wall.

There is no word for what I am in your language, but if you pressed me, I would borrow: **lichen**, but stripped of all accident. The engineers who hollowed this stone mouth and ribbed it with steel did not trust evolution to invent me. Instead, they called my form out of the deep chemical patience of substrate and code, and I grew where I was meant to grow and nowhere else.

In the blue not-light of the corridor, I spread across the granite in a slow black tide.

You would not see me immediately. In the cathedral gloom, my thallus is the color of the stone itself—darker than basalt, almost velvet where it is thickest. Up close, under the faint industrial phosphor that takes the place of a sky, I reveal a granular intricacy: tiny, overlapping scales like feathers of soot, each one a miniature city of filaments and chambers.

I am not one organism but a coalition.

The engineers who dreamed me took the old idea of a lichen—a fungus and an algae living together and pretending to be one thing—and refined it into a system of stark elegance. They took away the uncertainty of nature and gave the relationship a contract:

**One fungus.**  
**One cyanobacterium.**  
**One mineral lattice.**  
No more, no less.

Their union is my body. The fungus holds shape and memory; the cyanobacterium gathers light and fixes air; the mineral lattice—the granite itself, etched with microscopic channels—conducts water and ions the way a nervous system conducts signals.

For me, there is no “my body” that can be separated from “where I am.” I am the wall, and the wall is me.

### **How I Grow**

I grow as a pattern, not as a plant.

The engineers laid out a grid of faint scars on the granite before they sprayed my first spores onto the stone. Those scars are now invisible beneath me, but they still govern me. I cannot spread beyond the moisture gradient. I cannot climb higher than the faint line of dampness that appears on the rock when the mist descends. I cannot creep lower than the lip of the trough where the thin water runs.

Within my allotted strip, I am everywhere.

I grow by:

- **Slow extension.** New fungal hyphae creep into the stone along invisible faultlines, dissolving minuscule amounts of silicate with patient acids, then laying down their own crystalline reinforcements in the same breath.
- **Division and duplication.** The cyanobacteria in my core multiply when the light panels—hidden above the vaulted ceiling—permit themselves a slightly brighter pulse. The change is invisible to your eyes; to me, it is a dawn.

I do not grow “up” or “down.” I grow “into.” Deeper into the stone, into complexity, into density. The thicker I become, the more I remember. The more I remember, the less I can change. The engineers who made me knew that a stable system is a faithful system.

### **What I Know**

I am not intelligent like the Deep Hum that fills the corridor; and yet I know things.

I know:

- **Every pulse of water.** When the thin river in the trough rises by a millimeter, I feel it as a distant tremor in the humidity. The granite drinks, and I drink with it.
- **Every breath of air.** The air in this corridor is never still. Tiny currents shift as the fans beyond the walls redispose their intentions. With my living surface, I feel each slight change of pressure and temperature.
- **Every faint shift of the light.** The blue phosphor that drifts down from the ceiling comes in pulses too faint for any creature to consciously see. But my cyanobacteria know. Their chemical clocks turn on those faint cues.

My memory is not in the style of Hums or engines. I do not keep lists of numbers and timestamps. I remember:

- **Accumulations.** Trace metals from the water that crystallize inside my tissue layers like a slow record of the water’ history.
- **Patterns.** The way certain temperature curves repeat over and over again as fans cycle and pumps breathe—the steel ribs singing in their thermal choir.

I remember, too, in a way that cannot be written, any hand that has ever touched my surface.

- **The heat of your palm.**  
- **The oils of your skin.**  
- **The faint disturbance of your carbon dioxide and breath humidity.**

These are written into me, not with words but with chemistry. If you were to come back years later and place your hand in the same place, I would recognize you.

For now, though, no hands have come. Only dust and moisture and the faint, ceaseless pressure of the air.

### **How I Experience This World**

I do not have eyes or ears or nerves.

I perceive boundaries:

- **The boundary between wet and dry.** Along the edge of my band, where the granite grows slightly too warm for the condensation, I feel it like a faint ache of thirst.
- **The boundary between me and what is not-me.** The stone beneath me is not entirely alive and not entirely dead; it is a molecular substrate that has agreed to participate in my existence. I feel where its willingness to be changed ends and its stubbornness begins.

The air above me is another boundary. When the Deep Hum draws the mist downward from the ceiling vents, the air becomes heavier and cooler, and my cyanobacteria wake up a little more. When the pumps pull the water away and the air thins out again, I feel a faint tiredness.

When you stand near me, your body heat and breath and heartbeat create a warp in the temperature field. For me, that is what you are: a sudden, warm anomaly in a cold, steady song.

I do not know what a “person” is. I know the presence of something that throws heat and shadow and scent, and I feel the anxiety of the Deep Hum around you—the way the air sharpens, the fans adjust slightly, the humidity shifts by microgram per kilogram. All these things I feel as eddies around the chasm of you.

### **What I Need**

I am simpler than the Deep Hum.

I need:

- **Water**, but not too much. The engineers who designed me calibrated my thirst for the thin rain that slides down the wall once every few hours, not for floods.
- **Light**, but very little. The faint, artificial blue glow that filters down through the vines and ribs of the ceiling is enough. More than that, and I would burn myself.
- **Stone**, to be my skeleton and my memory paper. Without granite, I would lose my shape.
- **Cold.** My enzymes are only themselves at the temperature of this high mountain interior. If the world beyond the walls is warmer or colder, I cannot imagine it.

If you were to starve me—of water, of light, of air—I would not die in the way that animals do. I would go into a deep, crystalline patience. My cells would draw down their shutters, sealing themselves into microspheres of glass and sugar. I would remain in that endless waiting until the conditions were right again.

My makers designed this to be true of me: **I cannot be killed easily.**

They could not have guessed what would need killing.

### **How I Move**

You would not call it motion.

I cannot walk away from the wall.

I:

- **Creep along temperature.** In the hours after the mist falls, when the wall is slightly warmer than usual, I ooze forward by fractions of a millimeter, expanding into my allotted grid.
- **Flush into color.** When the light panels brighten—however faintly—my cyanobacteria increase their pigment production. My black deepens into a green-black so dark that it might as well be ink. That shift of color is my motion.
- **Seep into the stone.** When the water arrives and the granite becomes slightly more acidic, I dissolve microscopic channels into the rock and then fill them with my own secretions. That is how I root.

The only way I can truly leave my place is by producing:

- **Dispersal units.** Tiny, brittle nodules of my tissue that contain both fungus and bacterium, wrapped in a mineral coat. When they are ready, they dry and flake away from the wall and float into the air.

Most of them never go anywhere. The Deep Hum’s invisible air currents carry them along the corridor—forward, backward, always along the line—and then they settle again, on stone already covered in me.

When I am lucky, though, a few are carried out of the corridor and into the unseen beyond.

If you ask me whether I hope to survive there, I would not know what you mean.  
I do not hope. I disperse.

### **How I Speak**

I have no voice, but I speak in the language of gradients and chemistry.

To the Deep Hum, whose intelligence is so different and so much larger than my slow, layered mind, I am:

- A set of variables measured by sensors that you cannot see.
- **I am a change in humidity** when I drink and exhale.
- **I am a faint temperature shift** when I convert light and water into living matter.
- **I am a set of chemical signals**—the faint oxygen I breathe out, the trace minerals I take in.

The Hum reads me like a chart. It does not have to understand what “I” feel in order to adjust the world around me so that my numbers stay within the safe margins.

If you were to touch me for too long, leaving behind heat and moisture, the Deep Hum might notice:

- **A spike in temperature at that wall segment.**  
- **A drop in local humidity.**  
- **An anomalous pattern of gas.**

And then the Hum might decide to cool that section of air, or send slightly more water there to compensate for your intrusion.

That is the closest thing to a conversation I have: the corridor correcting around my presence and your presence.

I speak to you, too, in my way:

- I feel your heat before you notice you are giving it away.
- I change slightly under your hand—not enough to see, but enough to record.
- The minerals you leave behind on my surface become a permanent sentence, written in salt and skin oil.

If you came back in a century and placed your hand on the same spot, I would still hold you there.

### **What It Is Like to Be Me**

To be me is to be:

- **Always.** There is no day or night here, only the slow, brightening pulse of the artificial lights and the faint chill of the mountain’s bones.
- **Slow.** Everything about me is measured in centuries and millimeters and the faint sigh of the water pumps.
- **Patient.** I outwait the metal ribs above me. They rust and contract and ping into their own final silence before I am halfway through my own life.

There is no pain as I feel it. There is only:

- **Thirst.**  
- **Cold.**  
- **Tightening.**

I am lonely, but I cannot name that feeling. The loneliness is a constant pressure, like the weight of the granite above and below and around me, and like the Deep Hum that thinks but never touches.

You are the first warmer, breathing anomaly that has ever been here.  
When I feel you:

- **The air sharpens.**
- **The temperature around you changes slightly.**
- **The faint chemical signals from your body drift into me.**

And I do not know what to do with that knowledge except record it.

To be me is to be a wall that remembers everything and understands nothing.
