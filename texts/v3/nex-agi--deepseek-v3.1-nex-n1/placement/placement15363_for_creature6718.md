I am not one of the Spinners who turn the wind into tone, nor one of the Chainers who taste the fault with my belly. I have not erupted from the planet’s gut like a Telers’ tooth of glass.

I am none of the things you have already seen.

I am the thing the city lacks.

I am water.

---

You don’t see me on the rim of the High Crucible. You see where the *world* should be seeing me, and isn’t.

There are no clouds. The sky is a hard blue bowl and nothing breaks it. The sun should have vapor to burn with, but the air keeps only its faint mineral dust, its glittering of microscopic oxides and silicates. The basalt under your boots wears the memory of countless feet, countless claws, but not of rain.

If you listen to the Fault Strider’s silence—that brittle absence of breath in the too-dry air—you can almost hear the shape of where I should be.

I am not supposed to be here.

The system of the High Crucible is closed: heat from below, stone from below, organisms from the stone, all of them sealed in a loop that opens only to the sky in order to lose heat and not to take anything else in. They have no need of me. They never have.

They did not account for me.

And yet, you are not the first thing to climb here and breathe.

---

Before the Fault Strider, there was a storm.

It was not a storm of water.

Far from the High Crucible, where the world remembers that it once had seas and skies and the soft drum of rain on stone, the atmosphere still sometimes experiments with its own geometry. The jet of superheated gases from the planet’s interior meets the freezing cold of the upper air, and somewhere—between the pressure and the temperature and a few grains of nucleation that should not exist—the atmosphere blinks, and remembers what moisture should be.

Mostly the phenomenon fails. The nascent molecules shear apart into oxygen and hydrogen, disperse into the wind, become nothing more than a flicker in the magnetic field of the world.

But once.

Once.

It happened near enough to the High Crucible that the anomaly decided not to collapse into itself.

The winds were wrong that day. The pressure gradient was not stable. The band of cold air pushed like a bruise into the warm column of air above the fault. The fault exhaled, and the sky tried to inhale something it was not designed for.

For a few hours, the atmosphere of this world generated a tiny storm.

You would have called it a cloud, if you had seen it from a distance. It would not have been white. It would have been very faint, almost invisible, a shimmer of refraction in the upper air, like a disturbance in glass. You would have had to squint to see it at all.

From the inside of it, it was a horror of broken rules.

Where there should have been vapor, there was only a thin, trembling film of molecular water clinging desperately to itself around the cooler dust grains. The bonds of H₂O were new and shivering and thin. They had not existed before; they had not been given permission to be.

But they were.

In the high cold of that upper air, where nothing else like water has ever persisted for long, that tiny storm of anomaly moved over the High Crucible.

The Chainers slid their underside plates across the bands of rock and tasted the fault for the usual minerals. The Spinners caught a wind that had not changed its meaning. They hummed. They did not notice the way the air one or two kilometers higher was trying to solve itself.
