By the time you find the ladder, your feet already know her name.

The soles of your boots are thin—not because you can’t afford better, but because you have chosen them this way. You like to feel the city beneath you: the uneven join of terrace stones, the subtle difference between where the damp begins and where it ends, the faint vibration of the chain-lifts down inside the wall when you press your ear to any railing that’s been bolted into the original structure. You like to know where the world is murmuring.

You are a child of the vertical, but not of this terrace.

You come from the Stack Below—the cloud-kissed rim of the world that sits only one level down from Primeval Landing, but feels like a separate planet entirely. There, the sky is a ring of white haze instead of clear gray-blue. The air is warmer and heavier in your lungs, full of the smell of baking bread from the public ovens and the sharp scent of the fabric-dye vats that line your district’s river-gutter. The water in your tier is not yet the Spire Waterfalls’ final spillover into the infinite; it is still caught in the act of becoming itself.

Your name, whatever it was given by the orphan registry, has been forgotten by everyone but you, and sometimes you forget yourself. You are known by the work you do, which trails behind you like a shadow: *you’re the kid who walks the edges.*

You’ve been walking the gutters since you were small enough that the water in your level’s drainage channels came up past your ankles. Back then, you thought it was a game only children played—testing how far you could walk before a terrace guard barked at you to get out of the water, before your toes slipped on the moss, before you lost your nerve at the first real glimpse of the drop below.

Most of the others stopped when they grew taller, when they had to hunch over to keep their balance or risk the current of the storm drains after a condensation surge. Not you. You found longer legs, and kept them.

Now you’re old enough to have calloused hands from hauling cargo on the interior staircases of the Stack, old enough to be offered a place in the weavers’ hall if you want it, or in the dyehouse if you’re willing to breathe the fumes. But you still walk the gutters when you can slip away from whatever shift has been assigned you that day.

You tell yourself it’s just practice for the day you’ll be allowed to work terrace edge-proper instead of the inside corridors. Actually, you’re not sure you believe in distances far enough away to be called “proper.”

You’re just chasing the sound of water.

### The Drop That Drew You Up

The ladder that has you here now, on Primeval Landing’s terrace edge at the bright, clear center of the world’s weather, is not the first you’ve taken between the terraces. But it is the first time you’ve climbed *up* for the same reason you usually climb down: to follow the direction of the water’s fall.

The day started as every other in your memory: the same humidity clinging to the inside of your throat, the same distant murmur from the shaft-systems deep inside the wall, the same faint smell of the gas lines that tangle the interior of the Stack like veins of a metal organ. You were hauling dyed cloth down from the third-level gallery to the barge foreman who’s never been quite satisfied with your speed.

You were perpendicular to the world’s edge, walking the narrow overseer’s corridor that runs the length of the dyehouse, when it happened.

You stopped, because suddenly you could hear a wrongness in the water.

You can’t explain it to others without sounding like one of the old shrine-keepers who still mutter about omens. The sound of the spill from the level above you—a low, continuous fall of runoff from the Primeval Landing tier—changed. It didn’t break. It didn’t become louder. It just *shifted* in a way that made the skin at your wrists and neck prickle.

You dropped the cloth bundle, not caring if it got wet, and you did not ask for permission from the foreman who was already yelling at your back by the time you reached the side stairwell. You were already climbing the narrow ladder—one of the old, external ones that cling to the wall behind the dyehouse, where the rust has been painted over so often that the handrails are as smooth as bone.

You know this ladder like you know your own pulse. You’ve snuck out at night to walk the inner edge of the Stack Below, where the world turns into mist and the sound of the Drops falling into emptiness is like the constant breathing of the sky. You’ve traced your fingers along the ladder’s rungs as you climbed, using the water as a guide; the wetter the rung, the closer you are to the edge of the open.

This time, you didn’t need to feel the rungs. You could hear the water’s sickness, like a wrong note in a song you’ve been singing to yourself since childhood.

You climbed the rungs of your world, not looking at the color of the Stack Below spreading out under your feet like a map painted with moss-green and rust-brown. You didn’t need to know the distance between where you started and where you were going; you only needed to know that the drip was too fast, too thin, *wrong.*

You came up between the arches of the Primeval Landing’s underbelly, into the dripping lace of condensation that hangs from the world’s ceiling above your Stack. You crawled the narrow service walk between the supports, where the water fell from overhead like a rain of broken glass threads, and you found it.

The spill.

It was not where the water should have been.

The main runoff channel of the northeast quadrant that should have been feeding into the Stack Below’s collector gutter was dribbling a thin, sickly trickle instead of its usual steady silver. You’d watched the spill from your level’s terrace edge your whole life; you knew its shape, how much water should fill it to a line halfway down the stone’s carved edge during the midday condensation peak.

This was half of half, and it was running wrong.

You traced the line of the gutter with your eyes, where it should dip gracefully into the world’s edge and release its water into the air. Instead, you saw the channel of the stonework had split away from the wall: a hairline crack in the mortar had been prised slowly open by time and water and the weight of the terrace itself. The spill had been diverted from its main flow into a new path that dribbled uselessly down the vertical wall, into a gap between the terrace blocks where no gutter should be.

You were leaning out over the edge of the walkway, trying to see exactly where the wrongness was taking root, when you heard footsteps behind you.

You expected someone to be angry with you for abandoning the haul. You prepared for shouting, or for the hard yank of a foreman’s hand on your shoulder. You did not expect the voice to be quiet, or to be a woman’s, or to be amused.

“You’re going to fall,” she said. “Not that I would stop you. But you’ll make a very sad splash down there.”

You turned.

The woman who had been walking the gutters of your childhood stories stood in front of you, one boot planted on the edge of the service walk and the other on the ladder rung that you had just abandoned. The terrace’s underlighting from the world’s open vastness below made her into a silhouette at first, but you already knew her by the way she occupied the space: the way she held onto the ladder rung with only one hand, the rest of her body perfectly relaxed, as if she could not imagine falling.

“Alta,” you said before you could stop yourself.

The name of the Moss-Ladder Ladderer felt strange in your mouth. You had never said it aloud to anyone who had not said it first as gossip.

She looked at you for a long moment without answering, her head tilted slightly, as if she were trying to match your face to some memory of a wrong water spill. She was not old, but she was worn; the dampness of the terrace world had been carving her features longer than you’d been alive. You could see the faint streaks of green where her fingers left moss-stains on everything she touched.

“You heard the spill,” she said at last, as if that were enough of an introduction to make her remember your name.

You didn’t ask how she knew. You could not have been at the wrong runoff at the right time unless you’d heard the same thing she had. The water of the world is not a secret; it’s simply that most people don’t remember to listen.

“You walk the gutters,” she said, not looking at you now but at the broken line of the runoff channel that you had been staring at. “I’ve seen you. Down in the Stack Below.”

You didn’t know how to answer that. You’d been doing your work on the edges of the world out of sight of anyone who mattered. You’d believed you were invisible.

“You’re very good at not falling,” she said, and there was something in her voice that might have been approval, or might have been only observation. “You’re also very good at leaving your work when the water tells a story you don’t like.”

You thought of the foreman’s yelling at you back in the dyehouse as you ran to the ladder. You didn’t say anything.

“Come,” she said.

She was already climbing again before she finished the word, her body turning away from you in the familiar, easy motion of someone who has never been afraid of the world’s vertical face. You didn’t have to think to follow her.

You found yourself ascending the ladder of the world’s verge into the clear air of Primeval Landing, where the sky-ring is not choked with the cloud from the level below you and the light feels like a knife you’ve not seen before. You’d only been up here once or twice before, on sanctioned errands that you’d barely been trusted to complete.

You didn’t expect to be taken to the doors of the house that has made itself where the wall leans the farthest into the air.

### The House of the Vertical Meadow

You’ve been in the house of Alta, the Moss-Ladder Ladderer, for as long as it took to understand the wrong spill of the water’s tears.

She leads you through the small door at her home’s lowest level, where you nearly have to bend your head to pass under the lintel of stone and moss. The air inside is not colder than the outside air, but it is thicker with the smell of water and green and something else; you cannot tell if the scent of wet stone is older here than the rest of the world, or if you’re only imagining it because of her.

You don’t ask for an explanation as she shows you the lower room of her house: the receiving room for the world’s spills from the terrace above her. The humidity of the place feels like a second skin, and you already feel the faint prickle of recognition as you see the latticework of wood and rope that supports the moss-garden of the vertical wall.

She is not trying to impress you with the work she’s done. You realize that she’s not trying to do anything at all with you; she is allowing you to see how she thinks, so that when she tells you what she must, you will understand.

“You heard the spill,” she says again, but this time she is not looking at you but at the water of the world that has gathered into the shallow troughs she’s prepared on the floor of her receiving room. “You know it’s wrong.”

You can’t pretend you don’t. You nod.

“You walk the gutters of the Stack Below,” she says, and now there is a hint of a question in it, as if she’s asking if you’re willing to accept the work you’ve already chosen for yourself.

“Yes,” you say, and the word has not just been spoken by your voice but also by the soles of your feet, which are already anticipating the first step of the gutter-wall you’ll have to climb to fix the broken spill.

She doesn’t say anything for a long time. Then she looks at you with a side of her head still, as if measuring the tilt of water against the edge of a basin.

“You’re going to have to teach me,” you say finally, because you can’t bear the silence of the world’s water being wrong between you two.

“No,” she says, and you’re surprised at the quick refusal. “I’m not going to teach you.”

The disappointment in your chest is sharp enough that you almost miss the next part.

“The water already has,” she says, and her eyes flicker over the garden of mosses she’s made of the walls of her world. “I’ll just give you a better place to listen.”

You are not sure you understand that then.

It will take you years of climbing the gutters of the Stack Below and the edges of Primeval Landing before you realize that she is not just being poetic with words like “listen” and “remember.” The water of your world *does* remember things; it is not a matter of faith, but of observation, the way you can observe the color and weight of runoff and tell if the dye vats of your stack have been leaking into the system again.

It is also a matter of knowing how to ask the questions of the world that will make you into the person you’re already becoming.

At the moment, you only know that you are being invited to become the Moss-Ladder Ladderer’s shadow in a world where the water is starting to make wrong choices.

### The First Wrong of Many

You will not fix the spill of Primeval Landing’s northeast quadrant alone that day, but you will be the one sent to its edge.

Alta will not go with you to the part of the terrace where you have to lower yourself over the lip of the world to find where the water has been trying a new path of destruction. She will stay at the table of her house’s second level, where her containers of water—her library of runoffs—are arranged in rows of basins and bowls and vats that are labeled in a shorthand of letters and numbers you can’t yet understand.

You will be the one to climb the ladder that has been set into the outer face of the terrace wall by some long-dead maintenance worker who’d already anticipated the moment of needing access to the wrongness of the world.

You will be the one who, when you lower yourself over the edge of the terrace of Primeval Landing and find the full of the world’s breadth below you like a map you’re not ready to read, clenches your teeth against the empty of the air and keeps going.

You will be the one who, with the tools she’s given you—one of her padded hooks, a loop of hardened gut line, and a small pouch of ceramic chips—does not fight the water of the spill that’s being led wrong. You will be the one who learns, instead, to redirect it with the patience of someone who has already walked the edges of the world for longer than you’ve known.

You will press the wedge of ceramic into the gap where the spill has been trying to escape its world, and you will guide the weight of the water back into the path it should once have followed.

When you return, soaked with the world’s tears and your own sweat, you will find her standing at the table in her house of water and moss, sprinkling something from a small glass into one of her basins of runoff.

“You fixed the spill,” she will say without looking at you.

You will not be surprised to hear that she already knows the work of your hands has been done before she’s seen the proof of it with her own eyes.

“You made the water remember,” she will say instead, and her hand will be on the edge of the basin that she has labeled with the shorthand of your quadrant: **NE.3.14.3.**, where you will learn later the numbers mean not just places but times of year and the curves of the world’s memory.

You will not be sure that you did anything except the work of a gutter-walker who didn’t want to see the world he lives on broken by the place of a wrong drip.

You will not know yet that the work of fixing the Spire Waterfalls’ memory of itself is work you’ll spend the rest of your life doing.

You will only know that you have been named by the world the same way she had been named before you: as someone who can hear the spire weeping, and who is not afraid to walk its tears into a better path.

In the years of your world that will come after this day, you will become a person of the Primeval Landing edge whose work is not the haul of cloth or the dye of the vats, but the listening of the tower’s spill. You will be known by new names that you never choose on your own, but which will be chosen for you by the same way that all of us are chosen by the things we already cannot stop doing: *ladder-chaser,* and *runoff-keeper,* and *gutter-saint,* and the last and most intimate of titles, one rarely whispered to you directly but often spoken whenever you pass from one level of the tower to another:

*The boy who walks the tears of the world.*

You will not be sure that you are worthy of any of these names.

You will only be sure of the same thing you were sure of when you first heard the wrongness of the spill: the world of water is a mind you can understand, if you are willing to listen long enough.

You will stand on the edge of Primeval Landing, where the terrace of your life’s home ends, and you will look out at the horizon of the world that is not a line but a ring, and you will know that you are no longer the same child who once only walked the gutters for the sake of a game.

You are someone who has been taken by the world of water, and you are not afraid of its tears.

You are the one who will walk the tears of the world into its future.

### You

You are the gutter-walker of the Stack Below, the ladder-chaser of the Primeval Landing, and the future shepherd of the Spire Waterfalls’ memory.

You are someone who walks the edges of the world because you cannot be content with the safe parts of its inside.

You are the person who will be taught by the water itself, and the only disciple of the Moss-Ladder Ladderer who will ever be allowed to stand in her library of wet runoffs and not feel the weight of their names as a stranger.

You are the one who will fix the wrong spill of the world’s tears today.

You are the one who will listen to the world weeping for the rest of your life, and will not look away.

You are the one who knows how to walk gutters without falling.

You are the one who will become the Ladder-Climber in the stories of the next generation of terrace-edge dwellers.

When night falls on the world of your tower and the lamps along the terrace edge hiss to life again like the stars in your own sky, you will be the one standing at the edge of Primeval Landing, listening to the drip of the world’s tears into the deep unknown below, and you will not be afraid of the vertigo of the world’s endless drop.

Because you know that the Spire Waterfalls do not only fall into an emptiness below.

They also water the world you have already chosen.
