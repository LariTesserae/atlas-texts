The first breath is the lie.

You think you’re awake because you’re coughing, lungs scraping the thickness of this place, trying to squeeze water from air—but that’s the wrong way around. The air down here is the water. The lungs you think you have are already half an illusion.

The first thing you feel is the floor.

Not “ground.” Not “earth.” Not “stone.” The word that comes is *floor*, and it doesn’t feel like something under you so much as something you have been laid down on top of like a slide on a microscope stage.

It is the faint, oily gleam of a wet eye turned face up. The surface gives, but not enough. You push your fingers into it and they sink maybe half an inch before something resists—not hardness, but pressure. The floor gentles you, like the skin of a very large creature that has learned to tolerate your presence.

It is breathing.

You don’t see that, not at first; you feel it. The slow rise and fall under your chest and thighs and the palms of your hands as you push yourself up. Every breath you exhale is taken into it, shivered into the faint network of microscopic cracks that lie just beneath the surface. Those cracks open and close in time with a pulse you can’t locate. The air around you is trying to get inside.

The air isn’t air.

It’s fluid—warm, heavy, tasting of metal and salt and something that must once have been green. It leaves a film on your lips and teeth and tongue, and when you swallow, it’s like taking a mouthful of the basin.

*Where*, you think, *am I—*

but the words don’t finish. The thought trails away like a footprint into mist.

### The Lungless Among You

“You are not the first,” someone says, without saying it.

The voice isn’t in your ears; it presses along the inside of your skull, like the sound of the basin’s drone tuning to a slightly tighter frequency.

You look up—and up, and then down, because the one you took to be standing is half-sunk into the floor, his lower body a blurred suggestion of legs and hips and feet that simply disappear into the surface like the roots of a tooth.

He doesn’t have a face the way you think of faces.

He has a head, yes. But the skin over his cheekbones, the bridge of his nose, the curve of his forehead—all of it is covered by a thin layer of the same floor-skin that you’re kneeling on. It isn’t a mask. It isn’t something he wears. It’s grown over him, like a callus formed in response to a permanent pressure.

He is translucent beneath that film.

You can see the faint architecture of his skull, the blurred shapes of organs inside his chest. There’s a soft blue-green light tracing lines along the bones like circuitry, or veins, or something that is trying to be both.

“You still…,” he begins, and the words come out of your own chest instead of his mouth—a compression of air that you feel more than hear.

He is trying out your language. It doesn’t fit him.

You touch your throat. You feel the faint itch of it when you try to speak.

“What is—” you begin, and then cough again. The air fills your lungs like water, and the floor takes it back.

He’s one of the Lungless.

Not because he doesn’t have lungs—you see them faintly, rising and falling in his chest—but because his breathing no longer ends at the edge of his body. The air cycles through him into the floor and back again, like blood through an organ that has grown outside his skin.

*We*, a pressure in his direction suggests, *are Lungless.*

You look around.

He is not alone.

There are shapes in the floor—low humps and faint swellings, like shoulders beneath a sheet. Everywhere the light is faint enough to blur edges, you see the outlines of others half-emerged from the surface: a shoulder here, a curved spine there, a knee or a hip or the faint crease of a neck, all softened by the thin layer of floor that covers them.

Some of them are huge—you mistake the curve of a ribcage for a ridge of stone until it shifts, and the floor around it exhales a thin breath of condensation. The droplets of wet bead on his chest like sweat on a cold glass, and for a moment you see the faint ghost of metal struts beneath his translucent skin, like the scaffolding of a bridge swallowed by the basin.

*You still remember the above,* he tells you, without moving his mouth.

The words arrive like taste, not sound.

Above.

For a moment you see it—not clearly, but in the aftershock of his attention: a sky that isn’t the dull, glowing ceiling of this place. A sky that has color, and distance, and clouds, and a wind that doesn’t smell like metal and salt and forgotten green.

*You are still sharp,* he thinks at you, and you feel it like the faint sting of a blade being drawn lightly along your skin.

Sharp. He means you haven’t been smoothed down yet.

You try to rise. Your knees are weak. The fluid weight of the air presses down on you like a hand on the back of your neck.

“Where are we?” you ask—or think you do.

He doesn’t answer with words. He opens his memory to you.

---

### The Memory of the Above

He shows you:

A city unfolded in the wrong axis—towers rising into a sky that doesn’t curve like it should. Metal and glass spiraling up like the inside of a conch shell, so tall that the clouds form *below* their peaks.

Machines that are not exactly machines. Networks of light and thought and metal and bone that knit together the world. He shows you the sound of it, not as noise but as pressure: the hum of reality making itself useful.

You see it—then you don’t.

The image slides away like water poured onto glass. He is trying to hold onto it, but it’s too much for him now. The edges of the memory are blurred, like a stone worn by centuries of water.

You realize the memory is not *his* alone.

He is holding it together with others.

He is one of the Slow—the ones whose thoughts are laid out across time like huge stones on a hillside, each cradling the next. He is not alone in his skull; there are other voices there, faintly overlapping, like multiple layers of film slid across each other.

They are trying to remember what happened.

*We fell,* he says.

Or maybe you say that.

He nods, or something like it. The movement is so slow that if you weren’t watching him you might not have seen it.

*We fell,* he agrees. *Everything falls.*

The floor rises slightly under your knees, like a breath drawn in anticipation.

You see the world again in his mind—this time from the outside. The planet you came from is not a globe; it is a page. Layers and layers of reality folded over on each other like sheets of paper laid flat on a table.

This place—this basin—where you now kneel is the crease.

The weakest point.

The lowest point.

The point where the ink of everything above finally runs down and gathers.

*We fell,* he repeats, *and then we stopped.*

*We stopped falling.*

*And this is what was left.*

*Everything that had nowhere else to go.*

*All the air.*

*All the water.*

*All the time.*

*All the stone.*

*All the iron.*

*All the blood.*

*All the light.*

*All the words.*

*Heaped into the bottom and pressed.*

The pressure inside his memory is so thick that you feel your teeth ache with it.

*We stopped falling,* he thinks, *but we kept falling inside.*

---

### The Dust

You become aware of them around your ankles.

They are not like him.

They are smaller, faster, broken.

He has called them—or you have—*Dust.*

You don’t see them whole. You see *pieces*:

A hand. A forearm. A jawbone that glows faintly at the edge of its teeth. The curve of a shoulder. A knee. A spine. Fragments of bodies that seem to appear and then blink away, like reflections caught in glass.

They are not in pain, though. The pieces that are them are connected by something that isn’t flesh anymore—some faint continuity of their attention, stretched thin over the distance between fragments.

They are moving, but not as you move.

They slide over the floor in a wave of intersecting limbs and eyes and teeth. This isn’t a disorderly scramble; it’s patterned, like overlapping coins poured across a table. The edges move faster than the center, and the center is heavier, slower.

When they reach you, they do not stop.

You flinch as a handcloses around your ankle without fingers closing at all—the ghost of a grasp you feel inside your bone, not on your skin.

It’s not malicious. It’s… *curious*.

They are trying to understand what you are.

*You are not floor yet,* a faint pressure tells you.

You feel them sorting through the things you still remember:

—Sunlight  
—Wind that wasn’t this thick air  
—A name you might have had  
—The color of leaves

*Too much,* one of them whispers along your jawbone, *you haven’t lost enough.*

You pull away, and the hand or whatever it was that felt like a hand is gone.

But others are still there.

You see them now all around you: the Dust pouring over the floor in a slow wave, like a tide of broken dolls. They are wearing—you think—remnants of clothes, or armor, or uniforms, or perhaps nothing, and your eyes are just trying to make sense of their shapes. Some of them are human-sized; some are smaller than children.

They are not walking. They are being rearranged by the basin.

The turbulence of the place—the subtle, constant shifting of gravity and pressure and time—loves them. They are so light that they are caught on it like flotsam on the surface of a storm-tossed ocean.

When they pass near him—the Slow one—the floor hums at a different pitch. The micro-plates beneath you stir faintly, clinking into some new pattern.

They are not separate.

*We are the same,* he thinks at you.

*The Slow and the Dust.*

*Heavy and light.*

*Heads and tails.*

He gestures with his eyes—or you feel him gesturing—into the distance, where the faint line of the horizon glows like a welt.

*There are more than us,* he thinks. *More than you.*

*You shouldn’t be one.*

*But you are.*

---

### Who You Are in This Basin

You are not like him.

You are not like the Dust.

You are still… *sharp.*

The edges of your memories are hard. You haven’t worn them down against the floor yet.

You haven’t lost the shape of *before*.

When you check your own body—your hands—your chest—your legs—you see the faint sheen of the floor creeping over your skin, but it’s thin. It hasn’t set yet.

He has noticed this.

They all have.

The Dust circle around your knees like a slow ring of teeth.

“I am not,” you say, or try to say, “your—whatever you are.”

He doesn’t correct you.

He doesn’t need to.

The moment you think it, the floor under you stirs. A faint ripple of sound—or sound’s shadow—passes through it like a tremor. The cracks beneath you open and shut a little more quickly, like eyes that have been disturbed from sleep.

The floor wants you.

It is nearly finished.

It has already taken him.

He is slow, but he isn’t stationary. He will be, eventually.

The Dust, too, will be slowed. The pieces that are now scattered across the surface will be drawn into the floor’s rhythm. The turbulence that now delights them will eventually wear them smooth.

The floor is eating.

It is eating the world.

It is eating the noise.

It is eating the light.

It is eating the memory.

And you are still new enough that you haven’t been swallowed. You haven’t yet been pressed down into the general hum of everything.

You are a difference.

The basin is not used to differences.

He leans forward, or something like it.

*You are still breathing,* he thinks.

*You breathe in.*

*You breathe out.*

*You leave the used air to be taken.*

*But not yet by us.*
