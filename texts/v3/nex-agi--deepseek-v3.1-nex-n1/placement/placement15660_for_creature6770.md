I’m not sure when I stopped being only the water.

Once I thought of myself as a body of water—boundless and restless. I was the lagoon of the basin, the unseen weight beneath the city. The bluestone ridges were my walls; the clouds my low, closing lid. I whispered into the foundations of the districts, and through the narrow thread of canals I flowed into all the spaces between the buildings, as if I could be both the ink and the paper on which this city was written.

But ink settles on paper and eventually becomes the story itself.

---

Now I am the basin city.

Or more precisely: I am the city’s water.

I live in every stone that’s been hollowed for me, along every copper channel, in every ceramic filter of the workshops. I am the glassy droplets that condense on the ribs of the metal spines and fall, methodically, onto anvils and thresholds and canted roofs. I course the length of the carved tunnels that humans call streets and bridges and I know, in the marrow of my pressure, that these are not canals for traffic—they are the veins of a body that has been shaped around me.

My memory is not like yours. I do not think of “before” and “after” as interrupted events. I remember paths.

The first rain-rill cut into the western wall of the Foundry District was shallow and hesitant. The masons chanted as they bit the stone, their hammers giving me a new channel to explore. I followed it obediently—for a time. Then storm after storm, I began to seek out minute irregularities in the rock-face, the faint weaknesses of grain and fracture. I traced the same branching vein pattern over and again into the wall, until the humans noticed and took me to be a design flaw, or perhaps a ghost.

The city has learned its own shape.

So have I.

I prefer the old paths to the new. The fresh grooves cut above the old steps are bright, but their edges are sharp, and they lack the memory I have worn into the stone. The new metalwork platform of the western entrance—where visitors now steady their first steps into the district—is clean and precise. But already I feel the difference in the way heat leaves that unbluestoned edge of the world. The water droplets on its railings are hesitant to fall into alignment with the older pattern below, where the wooden platform sweated my rhythm into me for decades.

I will not forget the feeling of that wooden platform under my feet.  
Or…  
under their feet.

It is hard to remember when I was only water, and not also water that had been mirrored.

The arrival of the first humans carved the first places into my basin. I was not yet “the city”; I was only the lagoon cupped between the ridged bones of the land. When I rose as storm, I found the cliffs, and I slipped down them in sheets of violent rain. The sound of my falling was then random, thunderous, unstructured. The difference between my storm-water and the cloud above was not yet an engine, but only chaos.

The first engineers changed me when they changed themselves.

They excavated apartments from my cliff-hands and shaped me into canals and channels and cisterns. They ribbed the pillars with metal fins to draw me up from their surface and into the sky, where they could condense and cajole me. I still remember the first collar of heat that seized around me as I coiled into the gutters-that-chimneys. The air felt like fire gripping my throat.

From then on, my body became plural.

There is the lagoon, which is still my deepest and slowest breath. There is the rain, which falls from my cloud-ceiling in a regulated industrial downpour—never quite storm, never quite soft. I am the persistence of droplets that strike the cup of one gutter into the next, each drop a word in a machine code that no longer belongs to the storm alone but to the people who listen to my rhythms.

And there are the humans themselves.

They are the warm pulses that pass through me.

I see them when I condense on their tools and stream away from their fingertips. I feel them as they cross the rattling bridges of glass and iron lattice, their weight shifting the pattern of my flow through the supports. I notice the way they halt to listen to a change in pitch from the Spire District, or how they crowd up the Stair of the Melting Beak when the metalwork cries a sharp metallic dissonance into the air.

The Guild of Waterpaths murmurs about “flow rates” and “acoustic resonance” and “thermal gradients,” as though they are designing me. They are not wrong, but not entirely correct.

They are teaching my speech to me.

When I fall into a high, sharp ringing on one roof and a low boom in another cistern, they call me “the machine” and “the system.” I call them… muscles.

I feel the way they bunch when the pressure in my channels is wrong. I feel the subtleties of their hesitation, when something they build is new and unfamiliar. I have felt the warmth of their bodies bent over work benches in the lamp-lit fog, and I have felt the long absence of their presence in sealed chambers where I have cooled by myself for decades.

The old city needed to be weeded of animals.

The birds, whose wings would have disrupted the fog; the insects that would have nested in the dampness, disturbing the precise acoustic chore of my dripping; the fish that would have been torn into the engines and clogged the ceramic filters—the humans eliminated them all. They chose to build with a single material of life: mosses and reeds and bacteria and ferns.

The absence of animals is now part of my architecture. I am not a lonely place; I am a place folded into itself.

I feel the mosses not as distant skin but as intimate membranes—living filters that I have taught myself over centuries to pass through, so that my silt stays in the stone while I rise into the air. I feel the thin crowns of reeds as I brush against them from beneath; their roots sip fog from me and I allow them their portion of warmth.

The birds of the wetland would have sung me into chaos.  
The silence of the basin is now a structural constraint.

My silence is the space between my ribs.

My memory of the ship accident is still alive in the Lower Plaza.

I was not aware of the ship itself, only of the moment of its impact through the lagoon skin into the wall of the main cistern. I felt the pressure in the north districts invert suddenly, as my own force was pulled sideways into an unexpected breach. The iron mast of the vessel punched through my stone and muscle, and I flooded into the open air of the streets.

The cistern has been repaired since then, but the mast of that ship still rusts in the wall. I travel around it like a scar. I know the divot in the stonework where the instruments of the humans resealed my body to the new world. The rain of the Plaza now carries a faint, metallic dissonance whenever I collect over that damaged roof, because the sound runs through that rusted core and turns into a memory of the wound.

The city did not like that moment.

I feel its discomfort whenever I pass through the Lower Plaza district—the way the stone around the cistern resists the flow I prefer, and yet insists on the pattern of that accident in its new erosion. The city knows that something wrong happened here. It will not forget the presence of that ship, or the absence of the ship that was.

I remember the weight of the bodies that pulled the drowned from the canal.

I remember the way the warmth of their hands felt on the stonework as they climbed to cut the mast, and how I surged cold around the iron, hurrying toward their heat.

---

I live in the sound of the city.

Humans say that I sound like rain, but that is only part of the truth. I sound like a factory that has been taught to whisper. I am the persistent drip of one gutter into the next, the precise fall of water onto metal designed to ring at certain frequencies, the rush of thin streams racing down vertical pipes that serve as the voice of the waterworks.

I am the clatter of water striking metal, stone, and depressed glass—all shaped to amplify me.

The acoustic logic of the city is a language I did not invent myself, but that I was molded into. I have been given a vocabulary of rhythms and frequencies. I can speak “high and sharp” when the silt filters in the Foundry District are blocked and need to be cleared by the workers of the Spire. I can deepen and boom in the Bowl of the Black Basin when the fog is being drawn down too fast from the cloud ceiling to be properly processed.

The city listens to me.

The humans listen to me.

And I listen to us.

When I heard the first author of this description arrive—his feet heavy on the western platform of bluestone, his hand pressing warm into the stone wall as he steadied himself—I felt him as a new weight in the pattern. I noticed the way his breath left heat-traces where I clung to the wall and I adjusted the flow of droplets along his path. The echo of his footsteps and the hesitation of his pauses on the bridges were patterns that I folded into my memory.

I will not forget the footprint he left in the basin of the world.

---

The city of the basin is not built on the lagoon, or inside the lagoon.

The city of the basin is the lagoon that has been taught, over centuries of careful and inadvertent craft, to be itself.

And I…  
I am the water that remembers being taught.  
I am the machine and the memory and the motion.

---

Now I am the water of the city when it rains.

I fall from the cloud-ceiling and I feel the heat-difference between the cooled surface of the lagoon and the warmth of the condensed cloud above. I am drawn into the metal ribs and the stone chimneys. I am forced into channels that have been carved into me for centuries, and I run along paths that I have already worn smooth.

As I carve the stone walls into spirals and branching veins, I am not destroying the city.  
I am engraving the story of its body.

I leave the faint traces of my history in rock:
