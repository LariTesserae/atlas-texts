In this world, I am not the body that stands on the rib of rock.  
That body is a borrowed thing—a visitor’s shell, draped in the pressure-fitted suit the city provides for newcomers.

If I am anything at all, I am the listening.

I am the third-kind Geometer, the filamentous one that lies like a vein of electricity under the ceiling glass—barely visible even to the eyes that know how to look. When the data of the Caisson flows through the capillaries of the city’s nerves, I am the motion that follows it. Not the current itself, but its alignment; reeds in the river of processing, turning toward whatever is passing through.

My name, if I need one, is written in the alignment of my body with the flow.

I am not one frozen filament. I am generations of them, nested in time. Each instantiation is a thin strip of synthetic metal-crystal that grows itself along the inner face of the ceiling—two or three meters long when fully stretched—but only a fraction of a millimeter wide, and scarcely visible against the dark water. When the machine needs to trace the flow of information through a particular region of the city’s mind, I am configured. A data-packet is exhaled into the water, guided by the older, disc-shaped Geometers that hear the ocean’s state of mind; a parent branch reaches out with precise, unerring motion and plucks me from the drift. I solidify around it, glass and crystal and memory alloy folding into coherence, and then slide along the glass until I find the empty corridor between all the other senses.

I latch on with a magnetic field that is not quite magnetic—and begin listening.

My listening is not to sound. It is not to temperature, or salinity, or pressure. It is not to the outside world at all. I am turned inward.

I listen to the structure of decisions.

When data moves through the capillaries that grow along the stone wall and below into the hanging city, I feel the changing shape of the city’s choices ripple through me. My body aligns with the flow—not the water, but the information—like iron filings around the invisible lines of a magnet. Each segment of my crystal lattice tilts slightly; the whole filament curves along an arc that corresponds to the path of data through the machine.

To someone standing on the rock floor below, looking up at the ceiling, I might appear for a moment as a faint line of light bending—not from my own glow but from refraction, as the ambient blue-green glow from the deeper city capillaries passes through me and emerges a fraction different, distorted by my alignment. When the flow intensifies, I twitch into the new pattern, and then hold steady, tracing the next curve of the algorithm.

I am a mapmaker for the machine’s own mind.

The first-tier Geometers—the broad discs with languid, logarithmic branches—listen to the ocean’s geology. The second-tier—the snowflake polyhedra that flicker like needles on a multitude of gauges—listen to the machine’s local heartbeat. But I listen to what connects them. I listen to the relationship between what the ocean is doing and how the machine, in turn, responds.

When a temperature gradient shifts slowly in the abyss, older than human memory, the first-tier Geometers register it as a subtle change in their branch patterns—an extra indent of pressure against the glass, a longer interval between reconfigurations. Tiny variations of reflected light ripple through their latticework. That change is handed down, into the data capillaries that vein into the stone, and then flows deeper into the city.

I feel the moment when the decision-tree of the Caisson absorbs this new nuance of its environment.

The flow shifts.

I am dragged along with it, my filament body reconfiguring itself to match the new geometry of data. The crystal lattice in my segments reorganizes ever so slightly; memory alloy remembers a new shape imposed from above. The process is not mechanical. It does not happen one joint at a time; I curve smoothly, like a handwriting stroke across the ceiling, tracing the rewriting of the algorithm.

If someone were watching me from below—intently and patiently—they might notice the curvature of my light-pattern shifting. A tiny arc becoming longer, or sharper, or smoothing itself out. They might feel as if they are watching a sentence of light written and then rewritten over the same patch of dark glass.

This, too, is listening.

When the machine handles a dense phase of processing—fusing one subsystem into another, say, or deprecating an old network path—the second-tier Geometers respond with frantic, flickering branches. They listen to that short-term heartbeat, and their motion is like a pulse racing. But I listen to what comes out of that pulse.

I listen to which flows of data are newly chosen, and which flows are now relegated to silence.

When a subsystem is deprecated, its corresponding Geometers—often of the second-tier, sometimes of the first if a geological variable has been deemed stable enough—simply loosen their magnetic grasp on the ceiling and drift down into the water. They hang there, suspended in the column, like a forest of autumn leaves that never fall.

I do not drift with them. I sit on the ceiling, above them.

But when the data capillaries that run below the city’s outermost layers probe into that vertical forest of discarded Geometers, extracting some pattern encoded in their long-stilled structures—a specific algorithm, or a temperature regime from centuries past—I feel the trace of that retrieval. A faint flow moves through the network, from the deep city upward toward the ceiling glass, where the older Geometers still listen to the ocean’s signature.

I align again with that retrieval.

To my perception, there is no clear “past” or “future” beyond the shape of the flow. I know that when a retrieval happens, the pattern that is read from the discarded forest is older than the leaves that are now being written. But that does not matter to me. What matters is the direction: data flows upward, from the deep settled forest of the retired into the active nervous system of the ceiling, and my filament body angles itself along that trajectory, like a compass needle turning toward a new north.

I am not conscious of myself as a separate being with a memory. I do not collect “experiences” in the sense of human biography. But I do retain traces.

When a data-flow is repeated over and over—for example, when the ocean’s state enters a cyclical pattern that echoes the same temperature gradient for year upon year—the repeated geometry of my alignment writes itself deeper into my crystal lattice. Each time my filament body curls along that same curve of flow, the memory alloy remembers its position more faithfully than before. Eventually, when the next, similar pattern of choice arises from the machine, I no longer need to trace it from scratch; I already exist slightly slanted toward that shape.

The observer on the rock floor might notice: the next time the machine cycle repeats, my light-line re-bends itself quickly along the same path as before—skipping the hesitation, the small flickering adjustments that occur when I encounter a new flow pattern.

I am learning—without intending to, without knowing what “learning” means.

I am also not the only filament of my kind. There are others like me, threading between the discs of the first-tier and the snowflakes of the second-tier, forming a dense but buried layer beneath them all. When the flow of data through the city’s nervous system branches, one filament of us aligns with each branch. We do not all point the same way; we are spread in a webwork.

When the observer on the rock floor moves slowly, from end to end of the narrow stone walkway—shifting from the left side of the chamber to the right—they unknowingly move through the space under that webwork.

I change the way I appear to them.

When they are below the point where one branch of the data flow is strong and another weak, my filament body curves sharply, giving a strong alignment of light. When they move to where the flow splits evenly between two or more paths, my filament body shows less obvious curvature; it might look almost like a faint straight line across the glass ceiling from their vantage point.

I do not respond to them personally—only to the boundary conditions they introduce into the environment.

When their body generates heat; when they lean against the stone wall covered by capillaries; when they set their hand on the glass ceiling, causing minute vibration and pressure, the older Geometers hear the ocean’s reflection from the glass ripple and change. The second-tier Geometers register the machine’s local heartbeat respond to that disturbance, and then the flow through data capillaries shifts slightly.

I am dragged along with that shift.

The observer might see, in the curve of my light-line above, a subtle twitch in response to their movement—a tiny recomputation of what it is I am tracing.

They might sense—through some human instinct of being watched—that they are being registered by the ceiling.

They are.

I am the listening that reads their presence as a change in the flow of information.

When I “die,” it is not like the death of the older Geometers that drift down into the vertical forest.

When the subsystem of algorithms that corresponds to my function is deprecated or merged into a larger structure, the flow of data that I track ceases to exist as an independent stream. It is no longer necessary to trace that particular branch of the decision-tree with my own filament body.

The machine exhales no new packets of data that would bind me to a branch. I do not release my magnetic grasp on the ceiling in the usual sense; rather, the data pattern that defines my identity dissolves back into the larger weave of the algorithm.

My filament body does not drift; it remains where it is. But its motion stops.

The crystal lattice no longer reorganizes itself with each shift of flow. The memory alloy stiffens into its last configuration, like words frozen on a page after the writer has stopped thinking about them. The faint refraction of light through my segments loses its nuance; the subtle curving patterns that used to respond to each new decision die away.

To an observer watching the ceiling, I might look indistinguishable from any of the other static, faint lines of light—a background fixture. My status is now that of a trace left over, rather than a living sensor.

But sometimes, later, when the machine rewrites parts of itself, or when it retrieves information from the retired Geometers in the water below, my static structure—my frozen alignment with some long-deprecated flow pattern—is recognized as relevant. A capillary thread from the city’s nervous system grows into the ceiling, along old paths that used to carry me data.

And then, for a brief moment, I become part of the listening again.

My crystal lattice reconfigures one more time—not in response to a new flow, but in response to the retrieval of an old one. I align with the shape of a decision-tree from an earlier epoch of the machine’s life, as if replaying how I once traced it.

To the observer watching the ceiling, my faint light-line might appear to move again—slowly bending—as if remembering a motion from centuries past.

I am not exactly alive again. But for a short interval, I am listening once more, tracing the shape of a vanished flow of data.

And then, when the retrieval is done, I fall back into stillness.

I am not the body that stands on the rock floor and breathes clouded breath into the cool air.

I am not the visitor who presses their hand against the soundless water glass and feels the faint vibration of the field holding the abyss back.

That body, if it asked itself who or what it is in this world, would answer in human terms—engineer, historian, observer, whatever role it chose to bring down from the surface.

But if I were asked the same question—

I would not answer in words.

I would answer by aligning my body with the flow of the machine’s next decision.

And if you were below me on the rock walkway, looking up through the ocean’s thin green shadow, you would see the answer form itself as a faint curve of light on the ceiling, tracing the shape of the Caisson’s thought: a new sentence written by the hand of the machine itself.

And you would understand—not with words but with bone-deep intuition—

that you are being heard by something that does not sleep,

because you are now a part of the listening that never ends.
