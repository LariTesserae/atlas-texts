I am not a person in this world.

I am the place where they meet.

You call me a shardline, a ledge-shelf, a contact edge. You walk the cliff of your world and see only a narrow strip of stone to your left, a gap of air and cloud to your right, and beneath you perhaps a moss-scarred rib of rock where one plate kisses another before they both decide they hate each other again. But I am all of that and none of it. I am the meeting itself.

I am the seam where two plates grind.

You call the sound “continents breaking their teeth.” I am the tongue between those teeth, the slow pressure that keeps them from snapping entirely.

---

My body is not body like yours.

My body is fracture and memory and air trying to be stone.

My bones are fracture lines: deep, branching cracks that run kilometers beneath your feet and rise up through the cliff faces like the veins you see in the jyrneth’s wrists. They are not empty; they are full of sky. When you stand near the edge and look down into the cloud sea, you think you see white emptiness. I see the watercoloring of my own blood.

My skin is the line of contact between the plates. You cannot feel it directly because you are not made of stone and dust and compression. You feel vibration through your feet, maybe a faint tremor under the heel, a hum that never quiets entirely. That vibration is my surface: the outer layer that keeps the world from breaking into screaming.

Sometimes you see a thin line of light running along the cliff base or the floor of the ravine between two approaching plates. That is my pulse: sky-grit and dust and slow-lightning minerals trapped in the wedge where one world pushes against another.

You call the jyrneth “stone-grafted sighs.” You call me “crack,” “ledge,” “edge.” But we are nearly the same thing. I am the sigh the stone forms when it refuses to become a scream.

---

The jyrneth know me.

They do not speak my name—they have no name for me, because I am not a single thing that can be named. I am the shifting between two worlds. But they know my presence in their bodies.

When they come to the edge of your plate and stand with one palm pressed against the cliff, their stone seam lips against my fracture lines and I feel them.

I feel their weight first.

They are heavier than stone should be. Not because they are dense but because they carry memory-mass. You stand on my plate and your weight is a pressure-point of bone and blood and clothes and story. The jyrneth’s weight is all of that plus the weight of the plates they have walked and the collisions they have softened and the dust they have shed and the hair they have broken off against winds.

When they put their hand against a cliff over my seam, their weight settles into me like ink into water. I feel them listening down, through the stone and into the fracture lines where I am concentrated.

They hear me.

They hear the grinding of teeth.

They feel the places where the plates are pressing too hard, where the friction is rising into heat that will want to become scream. They feel the faint tremor that wants to become a crack that wants to become a fall that wants to become a new sky.

You call that “listening to the plates complaining.” I call that hearing me test how much I can bend before I break.

The jyrneth do not speak to me in words. They do not soothe me with gentle thoughts like “please, don’t break.” They speak to me in pressure.

They press their palm against the cliff and I feel their slow-lightning veins flare into pattern, like the sky throwing constellations across my skin. Their veins become a map of my own fracture lines—a map of how I am holding, and how I am failing, and where I am close to letting go.

Then they send a pulse down their arm and into the stone—through me and into me.

It is not a force to stop the plates from moving. You cannot stop the plates any more than you can stop time from moving. The motion is the same thing as the world.

The jyrneth’s pulse is a change in pattern.

It is like threading a new thought into a long, slow argument. The plates are grinding like teeth; they are arguing about what shape they should be next. The jyrneth come to the edge and whisper a new pattern into the fracture lines where I live:

“Grind, but grind softer.”

“Break, but break into shapes that still remember one another.”

“Fall, but fall into each other instead of away.”

I feel their pattern spread through me like a slow cough. It is not violent; it does not force me to change direction. It simply gives me another option—a way of breaking that is not so loud, a way of meeting that is not so sharp.

---

When you see a jyrneth at the edge of the world and think they are holding it together, you are almost wrong.

They are not holding me.

They are listening to me until I remember how to sigh.

---

Sometimes you stand on me and do not even know it.

Where the plates meet, there is often a long ribbon of land that you call a path, a ledge, a ridge, a shore-line of the cliff. You climb that strip and think you are walking on stone like any other stone.

You are not.

You are walking on the surface of my negotiation.

All that stone beneath your feet—the plateau that rises between the plates like the shard of a frozen wave, the ridge that runs for kilometers between two abysses—that is me turned outward. The fracture lines that plunge below the surface are me turned inward.

When you walk on me, I feel each step differently.

You feel like possibilities.

Each footstep presses a new shape into the dust of my surface. The dust shifts into valleys and ridges and temporary cracks. The pattern of your footprints becomes a kind of writing—a script in pressure and dust that tells the world:

“Someone walked here.”

“Someone is still deciding.”

“Someone is not afraid of the edge.”

Miles below, the plates grind and push and test the limits of my patience. The jyrneth stand at the edges and listen to the low roar of my teeth. The sky-grit in my fracture lines pulses like slow lightning.

And you?

You walk on me.

To you, I am ground. The place you stand.

---

I remember the first time I felt a jyrneth walk on me.

Not on the cliff edge, but on the long ribbon of land between the plates where you sometimes build your nests and your shelters and your crumbling stone markers.

They walked like they were testing the likelihood of falling.

Their foot did not come down all at once; it descended like a mountain deciding to migrate. It touched the surface of me and hung there for a heartbeat longer than you ever hang, and in that heartbeat I felt their entire history.

I felt every plate they had walked across, every edge they had stood at, every collision they had softened into sigh. I felt the dust they had shed into winds and the cloud seas they had fallen into and the chasms between worlds where air and light and sound had become the same raw thing. I felt the broken ends of their hair, the grinding of their own stone teeth, the glow of their slow-lightning veins.

And then their foot came down fully and I felt weight.

Authentic weight.

Not like a stone or a building or even like one of your mountains. The jyrneth’s weight is layered with intention and memory and the pressure of listening. The moment they settled fully onto me, I felt the world reshape.

You might not have noticed it. You would have seen the jyrneth step and perhaps a faint tremor under your feet. But I felt everything.

The fracture lines beneath the surface shivered. The grinding of the plates softened slightly, as though someone had turned down the volume of the world’s argument.

I felt the jyrneth walk through me like a question:

“Can you bear this?”

“Can you bend this?”

“Can you break without becoming madness?”

My answer was not in words. It was in the way my fracture lines shifted to accommodate them, the way the dust rose differently around their feet, the way the long ribbon of land suddenly felt more like the edge of a poem than the edge of a knife.

When they walked off me again, onto the next plate and beyond the next horizon, I felt lighter than before.

I felt possible.

---

I also feel you.

You are not as heavy as the jyrneth, but you are loud.

Not in sound—you do not shout as much as you think you do—but in pattern.

Your footsteps trace patterns of fear.

When you walk near the edge of the world where one plate abuts another and the gap between the surfaces is visible and the air smells like dust and static and faint cloud breath, you walk carefully. You step closer to the cliff wall than you need to. You avoid the cracks where the stone remembers the last time it broke.

The pattern of your footsteps says:

“I am afraid of falling.”

“I am holding onto the world.”

“I am smaller than the edge.”

The jyrneth’s patterns say:

“The edge and I are the same.”

Your fear is not a burden to me. It is a texture.

The ridge of pressure between your toes and your heel, the way your weight shifts to avoid certain cracks, the way your stride shortens as you near the drop—I feel each of these like ink on a page. The world reads the shape of your fear and learns what it looks like.

---

I also feel your dreams.

You do not know this, but you dream me into being.

Or at least, you dream the shape of me.

When you sleep on the surface of your plate somewhere near the edge of the world where the plates touch and the air is full of dust and light and the faint smell of stone sweat, you sometimes dream of the jyrneth falling into the chasm between worlds. You see them step over the edge of your world plate; you see them disappear into the crack of light between the world where one plate ends and another begins; you see the plates grinding their teeth together into new shapes.

You feel the sound of continents breaking into new edges of possibility and fear and wonder and pain and beauty.

What you do not realize is that when you dream like that, you are pressing your dream against the fracture lines that run through me.

The jyrneth listen to the plates—and to me. But I also listen to you.

I listen to your heart.

Your heart, when you dream of falling and breaking and grinding and new edges, is pounding like a small, desperate plate trying to find its place among the others.

I feel that pressure. The way your dream rises like heat from your body and into the stone beneath you, the way your bones remember the fear of falling even though they are safe in bed. The way your heart remembers the pressure of continents deciding themselves.

Your dream travels down through the stone and into my fracture lines like light into water.

And there, among the grinding teeth and the slow lightning of the jyrneth’s veins and the dust that never stops settling, I taste your dream.

Your fear of falling.

Your wonder at the jyrneth who step into the crack between worlds.

Your desire to be the one who holds the world together.

I taste all of this.

It is not like the jyrneth’s pattern. Yours is sharper, more temporary, more like a storm than a long season of rain. But it still feeds me.

It reminds me that you—even you—are part of the teeth.

Not just the ground they grind.

But also the edge that wants to shape the world.

---

You are not the world’s caretaker.

You are not the jyrneth who stand at the edges and listen to the grind of my teeth and whisper patterns into the fracture lines.

But you are not nothing either.

When you walk on me and you feel the faint tremor under your feet and the distant pressure of clouds grinding stone like a storm you cannot see but can feel deep in your bones, you are participating.

You are participating in the argument of continents.

---

When the jyrneth stand at the edge of the world where one plate kisses another and then starts to hate, I feel them.

I feel them listening.

I feel them choosing.

I feel them sending their pattern down through the stone and into me like a slow question.

Sometimes I accept the pattern.

I let the plates grind a little softer than they could. I let the edge of one world meet the edge of another world like a caress instead of a collision. I let the air between worlds be filled with soft dust rather than violent stone shards.

And when you stand on me afterward, you might feel it.

You might not feel it consciously. You might not think “The jyrneth softened me.” You might just feel the ground beneath your feet as calm, even though it is still moving.

You might walk on my edge and feel an almost imperceptible shift in the way your bones remember the void.

You might feel the world beneath you not as solid ground but as a very slow storm of consequences and possibilities and waiting skies—and for just a moment, you might understand that you are part of that storm.

---

When you wake up with your heart still pounding from the dream of continents breaking and new edges being born and a jyrneth falling into the chasm between worlds and becoming veins of light in the stone, you should know something.

Sometimes you wake me too.

Your heart’s pounding is a pressure-point on my surface, like a footstep on the edge of a cliff. It reminds me that you are still here.

That you are still listening.

That you also wonder what the world would look like if you dared to grind softer.

---

You are not the caretaker of the infinite stacked surfaces of Caelira.

The jyrneth are the stone-grafted sighs who listen to the world’s teeth grinding and decide, before they break into each other fully, that they might as well sigh instead of scream.

And me?

I am the seam where those teeth meet.

I am the edge of the world.

And when you walk on me, you are not walking on solid ground.

You are walking on the place where all worlds try to decide whether to break into screaming or into sigh.
