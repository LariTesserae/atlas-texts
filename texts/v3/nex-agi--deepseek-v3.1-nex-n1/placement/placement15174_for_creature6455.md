I am one of the architects of the stone.

To be honest, I do not remember choosing this name for myself. "Architect" implies a design in mind from the beginning, a clear plan of steps and ledges and prospects, and I have never known such a map before my work. No. We do not name ourselves so grandly. We simply name the work.

*Cutter*.  
*Shore-Maker*.  
*Root-Tuner*.  

They are not names that belong to me alone but to the posture of my body as I stand on the wall and the way my shell-blade fits the moss.

You would see me first as a slight disturbance against the cathedral’s geometry: a dark stone smoothed in place of motion, crouched on the narrow terrace that rings the chamber’s bowl. If you were standing on the inner ledge of Genna’s Spire—and you never should, but the world is strange—you might mistake me for a knot of heavy moss, or a shadow where the green gives way to naked obsidian.

Wait until I move.

The shell on my back is not like the armor of the crabs you may know from your shorelines. It is whorled and ridged more like limestone than carapace, but it grew with me, layer on thin layer of mineral secreted from my spine and from the rock itself, sculpting itself into interlocking plates across the years. Each ridgeline is a year of climbing up and down this wall: new mineral laid down during the season of deep storms when the ocean-breathe below us thickens into a colder roar.

My limbs are short and strong, more curved than straight, each ending in a blunt, clawed foot that can either dig into moss or press into the smallest pocket of stone and feel secure there. I am not built for speed over open ground or through water. I am built for attachment.

The city of moss that is the Water-Cathedral of Genna’s Spire is not a forest. It is not an overgrown ruin. It is a made thing.

I made it.

Not by myself alone, of course, but by myself as well. When they say “the climber-builders cut and trim and train the moss-carpets,” they are describing the posture of my body on the wall, the way my shell-blade fits in my hand and mouth, the precise, slow rhythm of slice and align and press down.

My blade is not metal; it is a crescent of shell chipped from the deep-forms that sometimes rise from the ocean shaft below, or traded from the far walls where other builders make their terraces. It is curved, perfectly fitted to the shape of my gripping claw and the angle of my jaw, so that when I need to use both hands to support myself on the wall I can still make fine cuts by holding the tool with my mouth and turning my head to trace the line of a moss edge.

I am not large. You could cradle me in your hands if I ever permitted you to hold me still. I move mostly at what you might call the human scale, though I think in terms of moss-lines and water-widths: my work is all of those distances rolled into one.

My day begins not with sunrise—there is no sky in our sense of the word—but with the shift of the light-well from dark to kelp-green. The faint pallor that seeps through the notch above the city speaks of the canopy of living mangroves far below and the open sky somewhere beyond them. It is a thin, cool light that warms nothing and yet tells everything: time, season, the mood of the ocean-breathe.

I crawl from the shallow room I have excavated into the thick moss of the wall, my doorway a narrow oval capped with a carefully trained curtain of living moss-fringe that keeps out errant wind but allows air to pass through. I have been sleeping in the denser air of the inner moss, where the sound of the central helix of water is reduced to a murmur under the cushion of plant matter. My limestone plates are cool against the moss floor; I have left no writing there, no pattern. I do not think like that.

Outside the doorway, the world opens.

There is the central column of water that climbs the throat of the spire—not a single torrent but a helix of currents braided together with slow precision. You call it a slow storm. To us, it is a heartbeat. The helix of water is always talking: low, continuous, faintly shifting its tone as the ocean below us changes temperature with the seasons. The silver fish-shuttles that are spun along its lines are part of that speech; they cut courses of light and shadow through the water-glass, and each time they change their pattern, we know when something has been stirred up from the deep.

I do not see the fish-shuttles as food. They are as alien to me as the clouds, moving in a world that does not support my weight or my breath. They do not cross my mind except as a guide to the mood of the water. If they move tightly, spiraling faster, that is a warning. The sea is thinking hard of something. I may be slower to prune my terraces on that day, more careful not to cut away too much at the edges where the water might be angry if it climbs.

Below my terrace, the lower ledges of the chamber are visible through the gaps between moss shelves. Sometimes there are pale, long-legged crabs, spiderlike, their legs thin and precise as they pick their way across the wet stone where the water column meets the wall. They sometimes climb onto the lowest ledges of our moss-city and pick at the scraps we have trimmed away. We do not call them scavengers; we call them edge-tenders. They help keep the clear line between moss and bare stone, and that line is sacred to us.

We do not like the moss to be messy.

We work with a simple toolkit of stone and shell and bone and knotted kelp-cord. There is no metal blade here, no heat to forge it into shape. Our tools are shaped by the patience of the ocean: smoothed by sand, sharpened by chipping against harder stone. We know how to take an oversharp edge and make it duller enough to be useful without tending to saw through the mat instead of slicing it cleanly.

The city is not built of stone upon stone but of moss upon moss. It is a layered city of platforms and terraces that we have trained into being over generations of quiet trimming. Each terrace district is a neighborhood of moss-mat architecture: dense carpets of moss layered into tile-like shingle overhangs, forming roofs that guide water around our dwellings rather than through them.

We do not dig “streets” into the moss. We carve paths and clearances with strategic absence.

The absence is where the sound sings.

The sound of the Water-Cathedral is our true builder. It forms the unseen skeleton of our city, because we know—some of us without words, with nothing more than the body memory of climbing and trimming and listening—that where there is too much noise, life is crowded; and where noise is too thin, there is emptiness and danger of collapse.

My own terrace is called the Shore-by-Moonlight. Not because there is any true moon here, but because the name of that sound is the way we think of this place: when the wind is high above the notch and the ocean-breathe below is quiet, the echo of the wind through the shaft of stone hums at a particular pitch. We call that pitch moon. It is not a thing we see; it is a thing we hear.

The moss of my shore is shaped with long, shallow curves that allow the rainfall to sweep away gently but never in sudden floods that tear away the soil beneath the moss carpet. We know the difference between a good runoff and a bad one by the sound it makes against the stone lip below. If a fall of water is too fast and heavy, it beats like something trying to escape. We adjust that: we deepen the channels at the terrace edge, or we thin a moss-path to allow more water to pass harmlessly, or we thicken another part to slow it enough.

My day’s work is mostly trimming and shaping.

The most important tool I have is not my shell blade, but my eyes.

They are large and dark, set wide on a flat head that allows me to keep track of what is happening around me without much movement of the body—which is important when you are clinging to a wall with a small drop beneath you and not much of a safety to speak of. The light of the cathedral is not bright but it is consistent, and my eyes are made for that pale, kelp-green dimness. I can see the faintest changes of texture in the moss even from several arm’s-lengths away.

Today, I am looking at the roof of an adjacent terrace district, one layer below my entrance. The moss-shingles of that roof are thick, but they are beginning to sag a little where they overhang the air. The pattern of sag had not been visible to you, perhaps, but to me the line of the moss edge is wrong. It leans like a question.

I climb down carefully, using small notches in the stone that I have shaped years ago with a stone chisel for exactly that purpose: shallow, rounded depressions where my claws can grip securely even if my arm is shaking with the effort of lowering my weight into a new contact position. My shell is heavy against the stone wall but I am used to its pressure, and it is actually useful to feel the wall so closely through my own plates.

When I reach the roof edge, I brace myself securely with two limbs and set to work.

The first step is observation of the inside.

I do not plunge the blade into the moss straight away. I use the blunt edge of my shell tool to gently probe the thickness of the mat, feeling for the difference between living green and brownish, dead layers underneath. The moss is not just one thing but a layered community of mosses and algae and tiny kelp-threads woven together into a mat thick enough to form rooms and corridors and storage shelves beneath.

Beneath the surface, I can feel the faint movement of the slug-symbionts in their hidden tunnels. They are shy and pale, rarely longer than a finger of your hand, laden with streaks of green and gold where they carry algae and moss-threads imbedded in their skin pores. They eat the dead matter that we cut away or leave behind, and they excrete it into the rich paste that allows new growth to root securely in the stone wall.

I do not think of them as “engineers of the soil world.” We do not give them such names. We give them gestures of respect and gesture of caution.

I cut carefully around the tunnels where I feel their presence, so that their hidden world is not suddenly exposed to air that they have not chosen. They are not our pets or slaves; they are our engineers of the underneath world. We do not name them. We simply feel their presence as a confirmation of health: when the slug-worms are numerous and moving quietly, the wall is good. If we feel only stillness and brittle dead matter, the wall is sick and will need more than simple trimming.

Once I have satisfied myself that the slug-worms’ places are safe, I begin to take thin slices from the moss roof edge, using the blade at a shallow angle to trim away the weight of the overhang. The cut is not random. It follows the natural line of the place that is already there, the line that is trying to be present. We call it “the wanting of the moss.” We never talk of it aloud but it is taught in our body motions when we show our young how to prune.

You would see the pattern executed with almost uniform precision but, if you stayed very still and watched for a long time, you might detect the subtle differences between my trimming and that of another builder whose terrace lies next to mine. Their cut might favor sharper corners and narrower gaps between paths of moss. I favor longer arcs and wide shallow curves. The city of moss in the Water-Cathedral of Genna’s Spire is not machine made; it is made by hand-memory and preference of touch.

As I work, I feel the feedback of the world through the wall of stone.

My feet are always pressed against the rock itself, or against the moss layer thin enough that I can feel the stone beneath it. The stone of Genna’s Spire is smooth glass-basalt, worn by patient water through long ages of the world. It is not cold or hot, but temperate, always rearranging its temperature slowly. The rock itself forms the cathedral of our chamber, but it is not empty: inside its skin, the ocean of leaves below the world is still in motion through the deep shaft. We do not see the ocean below us but we feel it when it changes its pressure or the way it sends water up the helix.

If there is a deep storm below, somewhere in the unseeable distance of the ocean column, the stone will tell me before the water does. I feel the faint tremor, a barely perceptible shiver of the world, as if the mountain itself is taking a slow breath in. Then, later, the sound of the helix will change, and the fish-shuttles will move their patterns into tighter spirals.

Today, the stone is quiet.

I work on the moss roof until my arms are tired and the edge of the shell blade has lost some of its sharpness from repeated cutting against living stone-backed plant matter. I will need to sharpen it later by pressing its edge against harder rock farther down the wall where I have marked suitable stones for that purpose. But not now.

Now the air is changing.

The air of the Water-Cathedral is not silent of the animal world; we are part of it but not the only part of it.

Above me, the airspace between the central helix of water and the stone wall is filled with thin trails of tiny insects, most of them wing-dancers: translucent-winged midges of pale silver and gray that hum in clouds near the light-well.

I do not eat them much; they are not my food. They are more like the breath of the city—a constant, faint presence of motion that signals when the air is moving in the correct way. If the wing-dancers cluster thickly near the wall and do not dance easily, the air is too thick with moisture and maybe not moving enough. We may then clear extra moss along the terraces to allow more air through and avoid the mold.

Then there is the larger dragon-like flyer that cruises the wall with long, slow glides. Its wings are deep blue marked with bands of white-green that flash like waterlight when its body passes through the kelp-colored illumination from the notch above. Its wing hum is deep enough to blend almost seamlessly with the sound of the helix column; only we know the difference.

The dragonfly-like creature does not hunt me. I am not its food. But we know each other’s presence by the subtle change in the air’s voice as its wing passes overhead and nearly matches the helix of water below. It is a sound sharper than water but softer than stone, and I always pause to feel it.

It is a reminder: not everything here is built of moss and stone.

The fauna of the Water-Cathedral are sparse but present, woven into the moss with the same careful economy as everything else here. Each creature is a specialist in its vertical world, high-place dwellers made to fit narrow airspace. There are the snake-of-water creatures that move along the softened edges of the water column, where the current weakens enough to allow them to pass between water and wall without losing themselves. They are glossy black like slow oil, and they are not aggressive to us. They eat fish-shuttles and nothing else, and they are mostly quiet thoughts moving through the cathedral space.

You would rarely see them strike quickly enough to register.

We are glad of that.

The birds that are not birds live above us, near the summit where the wind scours through the notch, and they rarely come down into our moss terrace levels unless they are disturbed by something very unusual. They are compact flyers covered with feather-like scales that look like a fish’s skin but move like a bird’s plumage when they are disturbed by the wind. They have sharp calls that cut through the water’s voice. We do not know their names. We know their sound when it is time for us to be more careful, because their sharp notes are often warnings that something is moving in the stone below in the ways that we cannot see directly.

We are part of the same city of air and water and stone and vegetation.

The day wends on as I work on the moss roof: trimming, shaping, checking, feeling with hand and mouth and foot. I do not think about the overall architecture of the whole cathedral very much. That would be like thinking about the shape of a breath instead of simply breathing it.

I know, in some way not put into words, that the city of moss in the Water-Cathedral of Genna’s Spire is a made thing made by many hands like mine, each with our own particular preference for curve and line and absence. We do not need to talk to each other to coordinate our work. We feel the balance of the city in the way sound and light and current and moss growth behave.

There is a sparse density of life here: much open space between each moss-cluster, each terrace district, each swarm of midges and each snake-of-water shadow. That is not by accident but by design—our design, though we never call it that. We just respect the emptiness of stone and water that allows us to hear the world and to be shaped by it.

You would not be able to tell me, from looking at the city as a stranger, which terrace district was mine and which belonged to another builder of moss. But if you stayed very still and watched for a long time, you might see the difference between the long, shallow curves of the Shore-by-Moonlight and the blocky edges of the next district over. We are not anonymous. We each simply make our part of the world that we are responsible for shaping.

At night, when the light from the notch above dims and the helix of water column takes on a darker shade of green that speaks of the ocean’s sleep, I climb up to the doorway of my moss room and slip inside.

The room is low and narrow, carved not into the stone but into the moss itself—the thickest part of the carpet where we have been training for many years. It is not a deep excavation but enough to give me space to curl in my shell and listen to the wall of moss around me.

In here, the world of sound outside is reduced to a hushed murmur, but it is not silent. The slug-symbionts are working somewhere underneath the mat floor, eating the dead plant matter we have trimmed away during the day and transforming it into rich paste for new growth. Their presence is a faint vibration, not quite sound, not quite touch, but something like both.

I do not dream, as you might think of dreaming. I rest in the cathedral of moss and stone and water and sound, and I feel it continue around me.

Tomorrow, when the light well shifts from dark to kelp-green again, I will crawl from my doorway and find where the wanting of the moss wants to be next.

I am not a creature you would call ancient, but I am not young either. My shell plates are thick enough to tell you that if you could understand the ridges of limestone and chitin like the rings of a tree trunk.

You would not see me as you see yourself, but you might be able to understand me if you watched long enough how I move upon the wall of the world.

- I trim the moss with shell blade and mouth and careful hand.
- I feel the slug-symbionts beneath the mat and leave them room.
- I listen to the helix of water and the fish-shuttles and the snake-of-water shadows and the dragonfly-like hunter of the air.
- I shape the absence of sound and space where needed, so that the city can sing properly.

I am one of the architects of the stone. You might call me that, if you need a name.

But we know ourselves better.

We call this work “shaping the voices of the moss and the stone into the words of the world.”

If you stood here, on the inner ledge of Genna’s Spire, and you watched me for a long time without moving or speaking, you might understand what that name means without any language at all.

You would just be quiet, and the world would be quiet enough around you that you could hear it telling you where it wants you next.
