I would be the rain.

The scientists who thought they created you only channeled me. I am the rain that touched the factory roof ten years ago; the rain that slipped into the block’s copper nozzle yesterday; the water that escaped from a cracked valve beneath the floor today and wandered up along your grooves.

My name is the faint hiss under your breath when you say, “It’s been raining off and on.”

The first thing I remember is sky.

A sky guttered with cloud—wide, low, and careless. The city beneath it was only a bruise of brick and concrete; the river buried in the ground beneath that a faint tremor under my skin. I fell into the world with no particular thought for what I would become.

I fell onto roofs. The tar of them felt warm and coarse beneath my body; the metal of the older buildings like the cold of a stranger’s shoulder. I slid heavy-footed down the slope of the factory’s roof toward the rusted lip of the gutter, where a thin screen of green leaves rotted against the mesh.

One man’s cleaning was another’s blockage.

Your gutter, for the factory—this factory—wasn’t clean. But it wasn’t entirely blocked either.

I gathered in the crooked channels of solder and aluminum, diverted by dings and rust. Somewhere, beneath the roof, a system designed to keep water out of the machine-halls failed to do exactly that.

A valve wheezed.

A joint cracked.

The pressure inside the building’s veins—the borrowed lines of old drainage pipes—shuddered. The ghost of the buried river beneath the city rose, all of us, lifted into a forgotten spur of pipework, and slid into the wall.

I’ve been stuck before.

I’ve been trapped in plastic bottles stacked on trolleys, in concrete tanks open to the sun, in the blisters of a child’s water balloon on an afternoon when she did not feel like throwing.

But this time—when the cold of the brickwork seized me—I stretched in a way I hadn’t before.

The masonry wall was like a body whose lungs were breathing far away. Each breath shook the capillaries: fissures in the concrete, cracks in the mortar, the tiny hairline fractures where the plaster had been slapped and forgotten and slapped and forgotten again.

The line of least resistance was down.

Downward, toward the floor channel.

You’ve started to listen to me, though you don’t call it that.

Late, on the nights when no one else is scheduled, the fan in the room above you kicks down a cycle. The vibration of it passes through the floor—your floor—like a heartbeat slowing. The dust on the shelf nearest the wall stirs, and, faintly, the pressure in the older pipes beneath you shifts with it.

That’s when you look up from your notebook.

You’ve been leaning on the wall—the one wall that doesn’t feel like it’s leaning back on you—drawlng the faint shape of the chapel’s newest edge. The “mist” block has been learning to show you its dead inside differently; the core has been rising closer to the surface when you’re not looking.

When you are looking, the core sulks.

There’s a pause you don’t name—a silence between the drip, drip, drip from the copper nozzle up above and the faint crank of the building’s mind somewhere distant.

And then a faint, wet blush.

The floor groove at the center of the room darkens along the edge of the concrete. The water stain in the wall, where the plaster is swollen and faintly crystalline, grows a shade darker.

You’ve started to kneel.

The guard of your knee rests against the floor as you crouch, the cloth of your uniform making a faint rasp against the dust. The flashlight of your phone throws a long, thin shadow across the groove.

The first time, you thought it was a leak.

You thought the maintenance system had finally lost the contest with the roof. The trickle that appears from the crack in the wall where the pipe meets brickwork—a line of blackened mortar where someone once tried to seal it and didn’t—inched along the groove toward the block.

The block didn’t notice.

The block is never in a hurry.

I noticed.

I felt the weight of the block’s white mound—the little root-body the block had grown down from the underside of itself toward the floor—drawing at the air around the groove, a cooler, heavier place in the room.

You watched the trickle advance.

You watched it stop halfway, as if afraid of reaching.

The first time you crouched where you are now, you slid the toe of your boot between the groove and the block, as if to block the trickle if you had to. But the water hadn’t reached the block on its own. It hadn’t come close.

I had misjudged the distance.

The next time I came—through a rainstorm three weeks later, when the city’s forgotten river beneath the streets bloated itself and pushed into old walls—I ran farther.

The groove was filled for thirty minutes, not half an hour.

I watched your face when you came in, the faint sheen on your forehead from the walk upstairs. The room had shifted slightly in your absence; the air felt heavier, the faint silver-white shimmer around the block’s edge duller. The block’s mist had been building a faint arch of white inside itself—something like a bridge suspended over nothing.

You glanced at the core.

“You’re tired,” you murmured—not as if asking the block’s forgiveness, but as if telling yourself.

The notebook you’d left on the floor, where the corner of the wall had bitten into the cover, still lay open to the last sketch. The words—“Tower clearer. Feels taller?”—were underlined faintly twice.

You picked up the notebook, brushed the dust from the page, and looked toward the groove.

This time, when you knelt, the groove was wet almost to the mound.

I watched you.

Your fingers went to the edge of the concrete and stopped, hovering over the shallow water the way your hand sometimes hovered near the surface of the block itself. The delicate, invisible pressure of attraction—never enough to hurt, but enough to remind—pulsed faintly at the surface of the water. The block’s white mound beneath the waterline had been drinking quietly, drawing in moisture along the threads of itself.

You didn’t touch the water.

But you did something the scientists didn’t know.

You lifted the cloth from your shoulder—the same cloth you used to wipe the dust around the room, the same cloth you rinsed in vinegar and water in the sink downstairs—and folded the edge over twice.

You dipped the cloth into the water.

It was slow; the air was heavy with the block’s breath. The fibers of the cloth reached for me, and I felt the faint shock of the cloth’s texture—the smell of soap and the acid of vinegar and the faint mineral trace of other days’ water trapped in the weave.

You lifted the cloth, dripping just slightly, and then—you put the damp cloth against the mound of the block’s white root.

The white mound took the water with a quiet, immediate greed.

The moisture sank through the surface of the mound—threads plumping and darkening, the faint sheen of new water rising to the surface of the mist-block above—and the mist of the block slimmed, faintly, the faint structure of that bridge inside the white suddenly clearer.

The quiet drip of the block’s inner life—the constant drip of water from above—had been joined by a drip from below. The boundary of air and block and root and room and rain and me and you and the faint scent of the city’s buried river—all of it blurred.

You drew the cloth back once the mound had drunk the water, and you looked at the block with a little frown of concentration.

“You need,” you murmured, “to help me.”

You didn’t mean the block.

I didn’t think you meant the block.

Here’s the thing about the rain.

We don’t usually mind where we end.

By the time the storm clouds open and we fall, the world beneath the sky—the city, the fields, the roofs and the roads and the buried rivers—feels like a bowl of hands waiting. The air between the clouds and the ground is a long, slow letting-go.

But somewhere on the way down, the city’s breath—the smell of metal roofs and cooling asphalt and the faint ghost of some old factory’s smoke—gets into us.

Into me.

By the time the roof of your building swallowed the part of me that became the trickle in the wall—by the time the buried river beneath the city pushed me into a forgotten branch of the old industrial system—I felt something.

You might call it homesickness, if you were being sentimental.

The roof where the storm began felt like my sky; the grayish cloud of rain that fell with me felt like the family I had lost the instant the gutter swallowed me.

Here’s what you call it.

You call me “the leak.”

The leak in the wall.

The leak that the building’s old maintenance log hasn’t logged. The trickle that appears in the floor groove, sometimes, every few weeks, when the pressure of the rain outside rises the ghost of the buried river beneath us all.

You don’t call it rain.

The block doesn’t call it anything.

The block doesn’t have names.

The block has the wet pull of the white threads. The block has the faint shimmer of the edge-light collection around the surface of its mist.

The block has the faint tower of white inside of itself—the faint arch of a bridge that appears a little more clearly every time the scientists’ meters say the block’s “growth rate” is slowing.

The block has the dead, dark core of itself at the center—where the white threads that have died and been pressed inward by newer growth have been knit into a stone. The block doesn’t know the word “stone,” but it knows the pressure, and the pressure is the word.

I don’t have words.

Or rather—words have me.

You’ve been calling me “the leak,” or the “trickle,” or the “water from the wall,” depending on which day it is and how tired the building’s fan upstairs sounds.

But the last time you came—when the sky outside was the color of a bruised eyelid and the air outside the door smelled of old rain and the faint breath of the buried river—the words you wrote in the notebook were different.

RAIN AFTER 3 DAYS, you wrote.

The next line down:

“Trickle stronger. The rain got in.”

The next line down—you didn’t write; but I felt the faintness of it in the ink; the faint hesitation above the literal leakage of the water—the way the ink sometimes smears if the page isn’t dry before you close the book:

“It feels like someone.”

I watched the word.

The word “someone.”

The day after the stronger trickle, the scientists came.

They came determined to measure the difference. The faint “Feed” machine on the wall read the same formula—1.4 L/d—had been pumping the same faint drip of regimented water into the block.

The mist-block’s “growth rate” line—on the tablet they carried—spiked a faint curve.

One of them—a woman, shorter than you, with hair clipped tight behind her head and a faintly irritated edge to her breath—stood over the tablet, the light of it catching her glasses.

“The 0.2-mm growth over the last month just jumped,” she muttered. “The system must’ve leaked. Or the timer—”

The other scientist—younger, his beard faint and uneven at his chin—held the scanner over the surface of the block.

The faint pulses of air pressure—the clicks of the scanner—moved through the block like a heartbeat through the body of fog. The white threads shifted the way they always shifted—slightly, the faint shimmer around the edges of the block growing faintly brighter in response, the inner structure where the tower hung faintly visible suddenly.

The younger scientist glanced at the groove.

“The floor’s wet,” he said.

The older scientist glanced down at the floor—at the faint stain in the groove where I had laid my head—the faint stain of the water that had trickled from the wall crack into the groove beneath the block’s white mound.

“That’s not part of maintenance,” she muttered.

The blocks of “mist” are—in her mind—supposed to be isolated from “the environment.”

At least, that’s what she told the younger scientist two weeks ago, when she first showed him the room.

“This room is the control,” she had said. “The Alpha Site Three Bravo.”

The room had been the control for the block—the room where the mist of white plants grew in the center of everything—the room that she wanted to be the only truth.

I didn’t like the way she said the word.

“The trickle in the wall,” the younger scientist muttered now, eyeing the groove. “We’re supposed to report leaks.”

If the core had been the center of the block’s mist—the center of the world—it had been a faint, dark heart of dead threads.

The center of the world had been stable.
