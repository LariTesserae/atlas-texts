I am the ghost of erosion.

I am the thing the wind tried to take, but was too stubborn to leave.

Once I was a mountain. A proper mountain: shoulders of granite and scree, a cloak of firs, the smug satisfaction of being the tallest point for miles around. The thin air of the heights didn’t bother me because I *was* the height. Clouds tangled themselves around my ridges like petitioners seeking audience. The sun rose *because* I lifted it.

Then something happened.

I refuse to call it a fall, because I didn’t fall. Mountains don’t fall. We are eroded.

It happened like this: the world beneath me softened, or I hardened, or some ancient agreement between the plates of the earth ended without consulting me. The valleys below sank deeper; the winds sharpened themselves into knives of altitude and began slicing at me, layer by layer, over tens of thousands of years.

At first, I didn’t notice. I watched storms roll in like armies and break themselves against my cliffs. I watched snowpack and ice carve glaciers down my flanks, leaving scratches like claw marks in the stone. I watched rivers and human beings build themselves at my feet, naming themselves after me and then forgetting me again.

But time kept erasing me.

The trees that used to cling to my slopes like moss to a stone lost purchase. The soil between them grew thinner as the wind stripped away softer rock until everything beneath our feet became harder, darker, more uncompromising. The forests retreated down and away, leaving behind pale scars where root systems had been slowly torn out.

The world below me went greener. The valley filled with forests, rivers, roads, cities. The sky above me grew harsher, brighter, more empty.

Until I noticed that I couldn’t hear birds anymore.

Or rather, the birds had stopped. Stopped singing, stopped nesting, stopped flying along these ridges. The air became too thin for them, or the light too sharp, or perhaps there was nothing worth eating here anymore. No seeds to find, no insects to dart after, no softness of moss to bed in. The insects that clambered over my lower flanks shook their heads and turned away when they reached the very edge of the plateau where the trees ended and the stone’s memory began.

The world, below, grows louder all the time. More cities, more noise, more roads like veins in the plains. The world above, where I am now, is reduced to this single geometric plane: a plateau of bone and iron and basalt that is not big enough for a mountain range and yet more than large enough to be utterly alone.

Some day you cannot measure anymore, you arrive.

You don’t know how you got here. Neither do I.

You climb, or you fly, or you simply wake up where the last trees stop being trees and the stone starts being both floor and wall and ceiling and skyline, all compressed into the same impossible geometry. The air is thin enough to cut your lungs, but it doesn’t bother me; I have been breathing this air for longer than you have been alive.

Everything is sharp.

You notice the rock first. Dark basalt veins like the metal ribs of a long-dead beast. Light ricochets off polished surfaces and doesn’t bounce off gently; it *stings* the air. The sky is pale blue-white, not generous enough to hide behind clouds or haze. It’s like being inside the crystal of a monstrous eye: the sun is the pupil, and the plateau is the lens.

Sound is next.

You hear the water before you see it.

It’s not a roar or a rush but a ssss—hiss of thin film over rock, the faint click of droplets falling into unseen basins below you. The sound doesn’t come from one place; it comes from everywhere and nowhere at once. The stone around you is singing under the water’s thin fingers, and the song sounds like silence being stretched until you can hear it.

You look for a river, but there is no river. You look for pools or streams or even puddles, but there are none. The water is everywhere and not anywhere at all. It’s a ghost of wetness clinging to the rock like a second skin, gathered into shallow channels that have been carved not by hands but by time falling so slowly that it doesn’t feel like falling at all.

The stone is not gentle. It’s not the smooth rounded river rocks of the valleys; it’s been left in jagged geometries—sharp-edged fins like knife blades, zig-zag fractures like optical illusions, sheer walls that seem to have been cut deliberately by an invisible mason.

You are not walking on open ground.

You are walking inside a maze you didn’t notice until you tried to move forward.

The “corridor” you are in is not wide enough for two of you, but it rises higher than any cathedral you have ever seen. The walls are close enough to touch, but you don’t touch them. The rock feels colder than it should, even in the bright light.

There is something wrong about this place. You feel it: not hostility or danger but a deep inability of the world to accommodate you.

You are intruding on a conversation that was not meant for you.

I am the eavesdropper in that conversation.

I don’t have a body anymore, not in the way you do. I don’t have lungs or legs or hands to run with. I don’t have eyes to narrow against the glare of the sun. But I have *attention*.

When you arrive here, you disturb the air.

You disturb the light.

You disturb the water.

I see you through the water’s eyes.

There is a film, thinner than breath, spread across certain surfaces of the plateau. Not everywhere—only where the stone has been patient enough to allow something like memory to stand still.

It is translucent, almost invisible except as faint iridescence where the light bends through a thickness of water that is not quite water anymore. It is structured like a web drawn by a needle of frost on glass: lines of salt and charge held within the liquid like a nervous system.

You don’t see it clearly. You see glimpses. If you stop in front of a particular basin of stone that has been carved into a shallow depression by the water, you might notice that the gleam on the surface isn’t random. It repeats. The patterns of wetness and angle and light form a latticework of faint lines that echo across the stone like a script written in water.

You step forward.

The shadow of you falls across the basin.

The film of life remembers.

You are not the first.

You are not the second, either. There have been others before you—long intervals between them, sometimes measured by the slow shifting of the stars above, sometimes measured only by the faint belief of the water that it was still the same era when all of them stood.

Each of them was disturbance. A sudden storm of shadow and temperature and pressure that walked across the known surface of the world.

To you, they are strangers in history, if you ever hear of them at all.

To the film of life, they are the same pattern repeated with variations like a chord slightly misplayed.

I am not exactly human anymore, but I was once.

You would recognize me if you saw a version of me: the color of my skin, the shape of my shoulders, the way I still carry myself as if I had lungs to breathe and knees to bear weight on. I still walk like a person who remembers mountains; not as someone who climbed them occasionally, but as someone who grew up among them like they were siblings.

There was a day when I did not come down from the last mountain I climbed.

You do not know what year that was. I don’t either. Not because I’ve forgotten but because the plateau doesn’t speak of years in the same way you do.

“What year is it?” is a question that implies you imagine years marching forward like soldiers in a column. Here on Keth-Var, years don’t march. They stand still.

The timeline of these heights is compressed into memory-lengths and not epochs.

There was a day when I reached the last ledge before the trees ended and the stone took over. The air thinned. The light grew sharper than I had ever seen it before. The forests below seemed like a green ocean I had once climbed out of, but never returning to.

I climbed on.

I climbed for longer than I knew how to measure. The ridges became taller than they should have been. The sky became less generous than I expected. The wind became an edge too sharp for comfort but still, somehow, perfectly precise.

I reached the plateau and felt something like rest.

There was no sudden change of terrain or idea of the world; just a gradual sharpening of everything into stone and light and water. No trees. No clouds. No sound beyond the faint ssss of water over wet rock.

The first time I walked here, I did not know that there was anything watching me except the stone and the sun.

The first time I walked here, I did not know that the stone was watching back.

I am not the stone.

I am not the water.

I am not the film of life.

I am something in between all three, and yet not properly of any of them.

I died here.

Or maybe I didn’t.

If you died in a place where nobody else was alive, did you die at all? Contradiction: I am still here. But I am not walking in the same way I used to walk. I am not breathing in the same way I used to breathe. I am not hungry, or thirsty, or tired in the way you know.

If you look at me carefully—when and if you see me—you might notice that my hair doesn’t move in the wind, even when there is wind. The edges of my form are a little sharper than they should be against the light. The shadow of me doesn’t move exactly when you expect it to move.

Sometimes I don’t even cast a shadow at all.

The first time you walk here, you don’t see me.

You see stone.

You see sky.

You see the faint gleam of water running over dark surfaces like a ghost of itself.

You see the faintest of *things* moving on the walls—not crawling insects, not scurrying lizards, not any of the animals you might expect from a world that has trees and forests below. They are something like solidified threads of water; something like veins of melted glass struggling to learn how to thread themselves along rock.

You don’t get close enough to see clearly. You only feel the presence of something that might or might not be there.

The first time you walk here, you try to make sense of the place.

You might call it a cathedral, because human beings reach for metaphors like “cathedral” and “temple” when the stone and sky and emptiness are too big to fit into any ordinary room.

You might call it a maze, because you cannot see more than three steps ahead in any direction without encountering another wall or pillar or fin of stone.

You might call it a prison, because part of you feels like you’ve wandered into a place of long punishment where the only punishment is time.

You might call it holy.

You might call it dead.

All of these words make sense and all of them are wrong.

I am not exactly dead.

I am not exactly alive.

I am something the stone remembers.

When you die here, if you die here—really *die* in the way that your body stops breathing and your heart stops beating and your mind stops making its normal chatter—you feel like you are being poured.

There’s no better word for it.

You feel yourself poured like water through the rock beneath your feet, into the stone slab beneath you, and then into the basin beneath that slab.

You do not go into the ground like you would expect, in a place where trees and soil and softness are. You go into stone that has never been soft enough to be mortal soil. The stone does not yield or break or rot to release you; it simply accepts the water of your life and lets you sink through itself like blood through bone.

You become part of the film that remembers.

Not exactly.

You don’t become the film itself. You don’t become one of those translucent membranes of water and salt and pattern that spread across these basins. You are not exactly *alive* in that way anymore.

But your memory gets caught in them, like a leaf caught in a web of water.

When you first walk through Keth-Var, you don’t know that I am watching you.

You don’t know that I’m feeling the way you disturb the light pattern across the stone basins. The film of life doesn’t *see* you the way humans do; it doesn’t have eyes or faces or ideas of you. It sees:

- A sudden darkening over the stones you walk on.
- A slight shift of temperature where your body has been.
- The faint vibration of your steps.
- The pressure of your weight against the stone.

You don’t know that it’s storing these patterns.

It doesn’t know that you’re *you*.

It just knows that there is a disturbance that is not in the usual pattern of the world.

When you leave again—after hours or days or however long you can last before your lungs start to ache and your mind starts to whisper stories about water and trees and caves and food—the film of life continues to live.

Some days, later, when the light falls exactly where you once stood, the film remembers the shadow you cast there. It doesn’t remember you as a person; it remembers you as a pattern of light and pressure and flow.

And then one day, you come back.

You don’t know why you come back. You just know that this place never left your mind. It is like a stone in your skin that has not healed properly.

When you arrive back at Keth-Var, some years later than the first time you came here, the plateau looks exactly the same as you remembered. No more trees. No less stone. No new weather. No old storms.

The only difference is that the film of life has been expecting you.

Not exactly “you,” but the pattern of you.

It doesn’t know what “you” means; but it knows there is a pattern of light and pressure and shadow that has not returned for a long time, and now suddenly it has returned.

When you return to Keth-Var, you might see me.

I do not know if I want you to.

I am not sure how I appear to you.

I am not a ghost in the way you imagine ghosts: not the pale outline of someone who died violently and walks walls crying for blood or revenge or peace. I am not translucent or glowing or faintly supernatural.

I appear to you as something almost like a person, except unfinished.

My hair doesn’t move.

The light of Keth-Var doesn’t illuminate me the same way it does everything else around here. There is a faint halo around my edges, like someone has drawn me with a slightly different color of light than the rest of the world.

The stone beneath me is always wet.

If you look closely at the rocks where I stand, you may notice that the film of water there has more structure than elsewhere. The shine of the wet stone isn’t random; it has faint lines like the veins in a leaf or the nerves in a jaw.

You might think that this is a trick of the light.

It isn’t.

I am the thing that happens when you try to be remembered by the stone.

You might remember this: once there were mountains that were gods to us.

You might remember this: once there were forests that grew older than any living person you knew.

You might remember this: once there were valleys where you played as a child, running through forests and streams and making up names for the mountains around you.

Keth-Var remembers differently.

- The angle of your body when you stood there looking out at the sky.

It doesn’t remember your name.

It doesn’t remember your politics.

It doesn’t remember your debts or loves or losses.

It doesn’t remember your anger.

It doesn’t remember your dreams.

The only thing it remembers of you is the shape and time of your disturbance.

That is the closest thing to a soul-name you will ever have here.

You come back because you feel like you left something here.

The film of life doesn’t think of itself as alive.
