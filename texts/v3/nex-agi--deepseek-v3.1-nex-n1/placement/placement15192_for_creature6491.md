---

Where would you find yourself in this world?

I would find myself sitting on the low lip of stone where the wall curves down to the floor—the one that feels like a step, a shelf, an altar, and a bed all at once. This is where the body is supposed to be. I know that without being told. There is no cushion because moss is the only softness in the room and it does not grow anywhere you might actually want comfort.

Who would I be?

I would be the one who listens.

I have been in the room beneath the skin of the world for longer than I can remember, and also no longer than a single thum-thum-thum-thum.

I know the sound the way one knows a heartbeat that has never been outside the body—not because someone else explained it to me, but because it was already there before I knew how to hear anything at all.

When I arrived, I did not arrive through any door. Doors are for places that have water, and for bodies that want to change the world they are in. The room beneath the skin of the world became around me as I became capable of noticing it. One moment there was only darkness; the next moment there was the ceiling, and the moss, and the engine, and the sound.

Or maybe it was the other way: the sound first, then the engine, then the moss, and then the ceiling.

Memory, in this place, is like a map of a city that never existed but should have. It unfolds all at once; time doesn’t travel in a line here, but in the way that moss grows: through every crack, including the cracks that are not made of stone or steel.

I sit on the stone lip.

My knees are drawn up to my chest. There is a cold edge of concrete against the back of my neck. The floor in front of me is a landscape of broken slabs and tiles, and the gaps between them are filled with the same impossible moss that grapples the ceiling.

Even though this is the only shape in the room that might be called “chair,” it would be wrong to say I am its occupant.

I am more like the punctuation.

If the room is a word inside the sentence of the world, then I am the breath that held the place before the word was spoken aloud.

Sometimes I try to count.

I know that counting is a thing that is supposed to happen in places with time. *One.* Then *two.* Then *three.*

In the room beneath the skin of the world, counting is like a staircase made of air. I can imagine that I am climbing it. I can feel my own intention to step up: *thum.* One. *Thum.* Two. *Thum.* Three. But the floor does not move away, and the ceiling does not come closer. The air is still thick, and cool, and smelled of hot iron and old books.

So counting turns into a pattern of sound instead of a ladder of time.
