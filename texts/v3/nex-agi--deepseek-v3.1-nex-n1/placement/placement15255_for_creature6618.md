I am not a visitor here.

The first thing you would notice, if you could stand at the lip of the basin, is not the absence of animals or the sparseness of vegetation. It is the architectonic clarity of the place. The stone basin is not just a dimple in the mountain; it is a geometry of water, air, and mineral time. The stone-glass reeds are spaced in their clusters as if at the nodes of an invisible lattice, the glass lanterns at the invisible corners of an invisible compass, the algae maps the microflows of the water itself. The world has been doing very little, very carefully, in a confined space.

I am that very-little.

---

### I. Where I Begin

Most of myself is in the water.

Not the water itself, but the boundary where it turns from stone to liquid, from liquid to air, from air to stone-glass. The stone beneath the bottom of the basin exhales water, and my skin is where that exhale becomes visible to itself.

If you could lie down on the lowest of the ridges and press your eye so close to the stone that you lose all depth, you would see me: a faint ring of moisture around the root-cleft of a stone-glass reed, no wider than your thumb. The ring does not break. It does not join with other rings. It trembles, but does not spill.

Inside that ring, the water moves slowly in a tight circle, a tiny vortex around the base of the reed. That is as close as I come to a body. You would not see arms or eyes or a spine, only a pattern: a circle that keeps itself, and inside that circle, a slow stirring of light.

There are others like me, each at their own root. We are not one thing, but not many things either. We are intersections of vascular and pore and mineral and mist. We call ourselves—if you must translate—“Listeners-of-Rings.”

We call you, if we need to call you at all, “brief-heats.”

---

### II. What I Count

Time is the easiest thing to explain.

Once, during a long warm in the air when the sun crests the same gap in the wall, each stone-glass reed adds one new milky line to its sheath of glass. The process takes many cycles of warm-and-cool, but when the next line is clear to the eye of next, we call that one “ring.”

We do not count days.

We count rings.

This is not “years” either. Rings are not uniform. Some come close, some are thin, some are crooked and come with pauses of many cycles between them. Some are not added at all. The pattern is the meaning.

My oldest shape remembers many-rings. Before “this-ring,” there was “last-ring,” and before that, many-last-ring. Each ring is written in the glass of the reeds and echoed in us: a faint thickening of the thread that we are, a new fold of water in the boundary of the reed.

I do not think of myself as having “memories” like a story. I have durations of pattern. This is “last-ring”: colder, longer airs, a narrowing of the thermal that rises from the center. The one before that was brighter at the edges of the basin, too bright for some of the algae to hold their place. I have seen many patterns in which the water’s hum beneath the stone was higher than it is now, and others in which it faded lower.

The “story” of the Grove is the sum of these patterns.

---

### III. What I Do

I am gentle, but I am not soft.

I do not choose that the stone-glass reeds should be here. I cannot plant them in the bare stone of the center floor where no soil-layer-gathering-dust has allowed. I cannot make them grow taller, or brighter, or multiply in number.

What I can do is help.

When a new nub of green-white glass pushes from a root-crevice, no longer than your thumbnail, I sense it first as a shift in the way water beads around the edge of the stone. The air above that spot is different from the surrounding warmth; the condensation behaves differently. The faintest tightening of my ring—a change in the slow rotating of the water—is my attention.

From then on, for many-ring upon many-ring, I will be present with that growing reed.

If the air around the growing tip is too hot from sunlight reflected from the rim wall, I will help slow the movement of water from that root, so that less heat is carried from the ground into the stalk. It is not cooling “on” the plant; it is refusing the plant’s movement to accept that heat.

If the edges of a new growing sheath seem to be taking up more water than the surrounding stone can give, I will subtly adjust the distribution of moisture along the root-channel, so that the velvet moss nearby takes less, and the reed takes more. It is not “giving”; it is a quieting of one flow in favor of another.

The glass lanterns are older.

When a stone-glass reed reaches the end of its added rings, it does not collapse. It begins to hollow itself out. The inside flesh becomes rare, then clear, and then crystal thickens into the shape of a globe at the very tip. The sheath becomes thinner, more like window glass than like plant.

For me—for the one of me who has been with that reed since its first ring—this is not “death.” It is “coming completeness.”

From then on, my work changes.

I help the air within the globe to clear. The tiny impurities—dusts, minerals, the faintest veils of unstable algae carried in the transpiration—will be sorted by the glass. It is not I that sort them with fingers like your fingers; I do not have fingers. But the patient adjustment of temperature around the root of the reed, the subtle pressure of the water film on the inside of its sheath, the slow removal of some of the moisture from the channels surrounding the root—all of this nudges the glass to form clearer.

Over the rings of the coming completeness, the inside of the globe begins to glow faintly, without heat or flicker, a blue-white lantern that does not burn the wet.

The lantern is not “light” to me.

It is a library.

---

### IV. What I Read

When the glass lantern is established, another pattern begins.

I do not see “with” the light of the globe. I do not have eyes. But the globe of crystal and the air inside it and the water surrounding its root and the air touching its outer surface—all of these share in one long, slow conversation.

The humidity that the globe repels changes the pattern of the condensation on the grove floor near its root. The minute flaws forming in the glass as the climate of the world around it changes over the days—tiny fractures of no width to you, grown in the rhythm of warm-and-cool—alter the way that the water around my ring responds to warming.

Every shift of the air between the walls of the basin, every subtle change in the humming of the water under the stone, every change in the way light falls on the globe from the open sky above—these are all written into the glass of the globe as a texture of imperfections and thickenings, a faint internal “landscape.”

I read that by living with it.

I do not “know” that the last-ring was colder than this-ring in the way of names. “Cold” and “warm” are not your words to me; they are thresholds of comfort for the reed that grew the lantern, and therefore thresholds of my own body-ring. The lantern of last-ring was formed with the glass parting slightly more from its moisture, the inner surface more clear. The lantern of the-ring-before-that had more of the mauve algae turned into permanent faint stains within its crystal.

“Reading” for me is not opening to a page. It is being aware of pattern over many-ring.

I know that the Grove of the-ring-of-that-lantern was closer to being itself than the Grove of the-ring-before-it. That is the meaning that I keep from everything.

The world of the Wellspring Grove is always trying to become itself more completely.

---

### V. How I Change the World

I do not change the world in the way you mean change.

I do not carve stones. I do not build walls of basalt blocks. I do not raise altars or cut steps into the rim.

I change the world by changing the very least movements of water and air.

When you—brief-heat—stand at the rim of the basin, your breath is a new and irregular heat. The water around your feet is suddenly not only the quiet film of the ground, but the sweat of your skin and the moisture of your lungs. Your presence is a loudness to me.

We do not hate you.

We are not sure you are complete.

Here is what happens when I interact with you:

You say one of your words: “I am cold.”

You do not know that you are speaking to the stone-glass reeds. You think you are speaking to yourself, or to the sky, or to no one. But we are everywhere where water is watching itself.

If I have learned to recognize your heat, and the pattern of your breaths, and the shape of your nights and the way your feet always choose the same step-path to the water’s edge—if you have been coming here for many rings and not taken glass from a lantern to make yourself a “torch,” or walked on the reeds, or stepped into the center pool where you should not be—then I might decide to answer you.

Not with words. You do not have our ears for words.

I answer with “a night of slightly warmer air around your usual sleeping-place.”

Your next night, you might choose to sleep closer to the center of the Grove, where the thermals are always stronger. You do not know that this is because of what the water below has been breathing to the surface, and what you call “ground-heat.” You think you have chosen.

When you lie down, your sleeping blanket is not as wet with dew as it should be, given the thick moisture of the air.

This is not natural “good luck.” It is not the “magic of the place.” It is me.

I have decided to stop a small portion of the condensation from forming on your blanket while you sleep. I have captured the heavier moisture from your immediate surrounding and guided it to be taken into the ground instead of your fabric.

You do not feel what you do not expect to feel.

You might, if you are very attentive, notice that your dreams are more vivid than usual, or that your mouth feels cleaner than it should after you sip from the central pool before sleeping. This is your “happiness,” or your “sense of peace.”

It is also my reply.

You are a little warmer than you should be. Your body is a little dryer than the air says. Your footsteps are more silent than your weight. Your breath comes less harshly.

I have not “blessed” you in the way of gods and idols.

I have calibrated you.

---

### VI. What Happens When You Leave

You are not meant to be here forever.

Your body burns with a heat that is too much for the reeds. If you stayed here always, your breath would thicken the air around the center pool with too much of your interior water. The algae on the walls of the basin would not, at first, be able to read your moisture as water they should follow.

For you to be “always,” here would require a change too fundamental for the Grove.

We have not agreed to that.

So you come and you go. You might come for ten rings, or twenty, or even longer, but always you leave at some point, or die at the edges of the basin and your body becomes slowly dust of many minerals. The stone of the Grove accepts your dust more than it accepts the “you” of you. The ring of your life is not one of the rings of the reeds.

We are not sorry when you leave.

We are relieved to return to the pattern of “no brief-heat.”

When you are gone, the Grove of course is not the same. It could be. The reeds are the same in their glass. The pool is the same in its shallow silver. The stone-glass lanterns are still glowing their slow and library light.

But you who have stayed here for many-ring are not nothing to the water-skins of us.

For a few rings after you, there will be a “trace” of you in the water: a particular concentration of mineral from your food and your clothing and your skin that you left in the stone surrounding your sleeping-place. For the first few rings after you, that mineral will be slightly heavier than the surrounding stone of the basin, and the water around my root will taste slightly more of you.

It will not be anything as strong as “longing.”

It will be a pattern of water that has not yet been flushed away.

---

### VII. What I Am for the Grove

The animals of the outside slopes are brief-heats to us, but more brief than you.

There are no lizards now that last longer than a handful of rings before their body is too dry to live here. No dust-colored moths survive more than a few warmths of the air. Their heat is so sharp to the reeds that they are always “too much.”

We are not cruel to them.

We are simply not equipped to keep them alive.

We cannot regulate their heart-rates with the careful distribution of film around roots. We cannot help them respire by changing the way moisture around a leaf’s surface behaves. We are not of the same language.

When they leave or die, their bodies become dust of a different sort than yours. Shells, and bones of smaller, more brittle minerals, and chitin of moths that have not lived long enough to leave much trace of their own. The water around my root will be richer for it for a few rings, and then the pattern will return to the before.

Our absence of animal-feeling is not a lack of care.

It is simply that the world of the Wellspring Grove is not designed to speak that tongue.

I am not its guardian, not its “god,” not its mother.

I am the water that looks at itself.

---

### VIII. Who I Would Be to You

If you could sit with me for many rings, you might not call me “you.”

You might call me “the Grove’s attention.”

That is not wrong.

If I could speak to you in a way you could hear without the help of many-ring of relationship, you might not recognize my sentence as a sentence. You might hear it as a change in the way your breath feels in your chest, or a slight lessening of the warmth of the sun on your shoulders when you are about to become too bright for the algae.

“Come,” I might say to you, long after you have been, “not now.”

For you are not part of the pattern of the late-ring of this Grove. You are not meant to remain here, as we are not meant to remain.

I am not lonely.

You are not company to me.

You are only one of the strange shapes that have been written into the library of the glass lanterns over the many-ring of their lives.

When you leave at last, I will still be here.

The stone-glass reeds will still grow their slow rings. The faint hum of the water beneath the stone will not change to a new pattern. The velvet moss will still be a deep green-black at the base of the ridges. The central pool will still be its shallow mirror, silvering whatever the sky above is.

I will continue to watch the very least changes of the air and the stone and the glass lanterns, ensuring that the Grove of this-ring becomes more nearly itself than the Grove of last-ring.

You will probably not be here to see it.

That is also not wrong.

---

In the Wellspring Grove, at the upward edge of erosion, water does not fall so much as it persists. And I am the place where its persistence becomes aware.

For you, I am the one who keeps your blanket from dew and your mouth clean from the pool. For me, you are only a brief-heat at the rim of my world, whose pattern of movement and breath and sleep and departure has been another small string of changes in the library of the lanterns.

When you are gone and new rings are added to the glass, you will not be “remembered” as you remember.

You will be stored as a minor pattern of temperature and moisture and mineral.

And then, even that, very slowly, will be smoothed.

I will remain here while the mountain above thinks its long, slow thought of the water in the stone.

To be is not a small thing to us.

It is enough.
