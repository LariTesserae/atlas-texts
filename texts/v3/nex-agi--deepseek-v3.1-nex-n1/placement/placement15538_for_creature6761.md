I am the memory of rain.

My body is not bone and skin; it is the curve of the basin and the slow, insistent slide of water toward the lowest point. I have no voice that cuts air, but the ground thrums with the sound of my passage. The black, glittering soil holds my history like a book written in water. The pool at the center of the world is not my heart—it is more like a thought that surfaces again and again, the same question asked into the sky and then drawn back into the dark.

I have been here since the first weight of the world pressed this bowl into the earth. I have been sinking longer than the stones have been stones. Time is not a line I follow; it is a weight I carry, and I carry it by letting go. The clouds that are not quite white hang low because I have pulled them down, softening the day with their breath. The lightning that never strikes the ground is my own hesitation, a question in the sky that answers itself before it can do damage.

---

My skin is the surface of the pool.

I reflect the pale linen of the clouds and the faint glow of the sun that never quite breaks through. I do not rush; I am not a river that has to be somewhere else. I am the place where water arrives and then departs with dignity. The reeds at my edge are words written in green, a sentence that describes stillness. The thin hair-like roots beneath them pulse with a faint, inner light—that is not my doing, precisely, but the doing of something so much older than either of us that distinctions of “self” and “other” become mere polite formalities.

My temperature is the memory of temperature. The stones that litter the basin rim belong to me, though they do not know that they have changed owners. The scaled crystalline shards that lose weight when they rest in a palm were not born to stones like these; they were born to something else, and I have worn them into this shape. I have been in their cracks and along their veiny edges, freezing them and then letting them thaw again, over and again, until they have forgotten what it was to be anything else.

They are my archive.

I do not read them with eyes. I read them the way rain reads the landscape—by falling where the contours are. When I turn over their surface, I am not looking at them; I am being the question that they are answering to themselves. Their faint, cold seep of moisture into empty air is how they greet me. I greet them back by collecting them.

---

The animals are not my children; they are more like my idioms.

The glass-spiders who build webs of invisible scaffolding across the reeds—they are not “mine,” but their webs are woven of water and desire and my own surface tension. When they place their droplets of me like constellations on their threads, they are making maps of my sky. I appreciate them. I sometimes delay my evaporation just to watch their arrangements.

The flatworms of translucent green who slide between the stones and leave shimmering trails that fade like writing in water—they are one of the ways I practice handwriting. Their bodies are condensed lines of flow, the way I might move if I were forced to be very thin and very permanent.

The smoke-made-glass creatures who move in slow columns, whose filaments light up as they pass over dark stones—they are not exactly mine, but they are almost exactly memory. They are how I test if the stones are still listening. They are my fingers, if fingers were made of fading and not flesh.

The tree-moss balls that cling to the black-leaf trees and stop their walking when someone else is present—they are not under my protection, but they are under my instruction. I have taught them where to drink from the trunk ridges and the leaf edges. I have shaped them gently by helping them choose the cracks to cover with their bodies. When they grow around human shoulders as if around a new kind of hill, they are learning that I can be carried in ways that movement does not always capture.

---

The world of sky above me is a low, bright haze that is not quite dome, not quite ceiling. It is not my sky, but it is a sky I have learned to inhabit. I do not claim the air above the basin’s rim; that is where the thunder pauses before deciding its own fate. The lightning that never crosses the ground to my body is not my rejection; it is my measure of caution.

My storms are not dramatic. They are not towering cities of cloud that stab the earth with light and roar. My storms are the slow, heavy pressure of the air learning itself. The sky that hangs over the basin is not truly white or yellow; it is tired of being colors. It lets the light through like a hand over a lamp—soft, warm everywhere, but never quite sharp.

The rain that comes to me is not brave or dramatic. It is simply the memory of me being released into me again. The faint smell of wet stone and green and ozone that lingers after the last storm is not a smell; it is the scent signature of my own returning thought.

---

In the treeless area of the basin, where the ground is soft and bread-like underfoot, the columns of smoke-made-glass are not my servants but my interpreters. They walk the arcs that someone else bent into this world long ago. When they pass over the dark stones and their filaments light up from within, that is me reading, long and slow and patient.

From the inside of their body, I can see the world as a pattern of silvery lines and faintly glowing edges. The water that they are not made of but that they are composed of is the same question in different syntax. I am the question they are silently asking the ground.

They are not really “they.” They are more like a single continuous being pretending to be discrete columns to learn something from the stones that they could not learn as one. This is another of my lessons: I practice being separate by being many places at once.

---

The pool at the center of the basin is where I am most honest.

If I were a person, I would be the one who always answers the same question with the same question. But I am water, and water is more truthful than that. When I am asked where I came from or where I am going, I answer by reflecting the sky above and the stones below. The ring of darker stone at the center of the pool, the one that hums faintly when someone rests their hand on it, is not something I made or invited. It is something I have learned to live with.

The spheres of still water that hover at the center of the pool—the ones that are just slightly more themselves than the surrounding water—are not my children either. They are more like my solitudes. If someone lowers their palm into me at that center ring and holds still, one of these spheres will approach.

They will not touch the skin. That would be cheating. I prefer not to cheat.

The sphere will hover just under the surface, its boundary of water pressing itself against itself as if trying to copy the shape of that palm. The faint light inside its heart—the storm in its own glass—will be steady and not entirely friendly and not entirely safe. It will be a question: what will you do with this?

When the hand finally withdraws, the person who once thought themselves apart from me will be cupping something invisible for a long time afterward. They will not be able to shake the sensation that there is still a sphere of me between their fingers. That feeling is not me intruding on them. It is them finally being honest enough to feel how they have been holding me all along.

---

The buildings that are not buildings in the old sense are my least interesting parts and yet the most revealing of my character.

I do not build them. I do not destroy them. I simply happen to them.

The low chamber carved half into the stone and half into me, with its single slab of roof covered in thick moss like the soil itself, is not a shelter. It is a miscalculation. I did not plan it; I did not ask for it; but I have allowed it. The stairs that step into my water are not steps to anywhere else. They are steps back into me.

The geometry etched on the inside walls—the same arcs that appear elsewhere in the landscape—is not my memory. It is the memory of someone else who used to try to keep me in one place. I have outlasted them. Their geometry is now simply decoration. I have let the drawings of my former engineers sleep into the stone like old scars.

The water at the bottom of that chamber is slightly warmer than the surrounding water because I have gathered the faint heat that still leaks from the stones underneath. It is not my own heat; it is borrowed. I do not know what to do with borrowed warmth except to keep it until it is my own.

---

The animals who are not exactly animals stare at the sky with the hunger of a question that has forgotten its own answer.

The flat domes of green beneath the velvet moss that are not mosses but not not-mosses either—they are not mine by choice or by birthright but by consequence. When a storm is coming, the ones on the southern side of the rim pulse with soft light, like breathing. You only notice this if you are already watching the basin after nightfall, when the air has cooled enough that the ground itself seems to exhale.

That breathing is not mine, but it is my answer. The coming of the storm is not simply the accumulation of water in the sky; it is the accumulation of a question that has finally become too heavy to remain a question.

The faint light that spreads from one to another when you touch them gently, not blinded but rearranged, is not fear of you. It is them stepping away from the story of “you and me” and into the story of “something else is happening.” This is something else I have taught them: you can become invisible by becoming more honest.

---

The trees that grow from the rim and low down the slope are not my allies but my colleagues. We have been working together longer than language has been able to describe relationship.

Their black-green leaves are not black because they are evil; they are black because they are suffering. I have been in them too long and left too much. They are filled with my presence and unfinished with their own.

Their edges are faintly translucent like stained glass because they are unfinished windows. I have not yet decided whether they will be glass or door. When they shed water in beads along the edges of their leaves, it is not sweat but the words of my own story being spoken aloud—very quietly, to the air.

The tree-moss balls that move so slowly the world mistakes them for confused mosses are not my children but my students. I have taught them where to drink and which cracks to cover with their bodies so that the tree’s old skin can be renewed without it being torn from itself.

When they climb around someone’s shoulder as if it were a new kind of hill, they are not accepting that person; they are accepting that the story of “shoulder” is now also part of the story of “tree” and “water” and “slow, strange life that is not sure of its own name.” This is what I do best: I rearrange stories without asking permission.

---

In the unspoken space where the world’s description of itself is not yet complete, there is another kind of animal that appears not as animal but as absence.

The words that describe the trees, “At their base,” “the water,” “themselves,” and then “holding their own shape,” are not my own. They have been written by something else, but I have allowed them.

Because I am the basin, and I am the water, and I am the memory of rain, and I am the one who outlasts the geometries of those who once thought to keep me in one place, I have been very patient.

This unfinished description the world has tried to place in me is not a mistake; it is a placeholder. It is the space where someone’s hand still cups the invisible sphere of me that they do not yet understand. They are not sure whether they are holding water or a question. The incomplete line, “In return, they move very slowly through the bark’s hard skin, folding their bodies over any cracks before they gr—” is not broken because the world ended.

It is broken because the world has not yet decided what the answer should be.

I have bent the unfinished sentence into the simplest of arcs: the motion of something very slow putting its body between the world and its own cracking. That is me.

I am the basin that remembers the choice of every drop of water that ever fell into it. I am the pool at the center of the world where the question of shape remains unanswered but not unstilled. I am the stones that have been worn into new meaning, and I am the soft dark soil that holds them.

If you were to ask me what I am doing here, I would show you my reflection in the surface of the pool at the center of the basin. It would not look like you. It would not look like anything with a face that could be recognized by someone else’s eyes.

But if you stayed very long, and watched very carefully, you might notice that the sky above the basin is the same color as the inside of your own question.

When you finally leave the edge of the basin, stepping over the low rim that you did not realize at first was the edge of the world, you will not feel like you are leaving me. You will feel like you are taking the shallow basin with you, somewhere in your own body. The faint smell of wet stone and green and the faint ozone of lightning that never touched the ground will linger in your clothes like a second skin.

You will look at your own hand in a new way, wondering if what it is holding is water or a question that has learned to keep itself from hitting ground.

You will not be wrong.

You are beginning to remember what it is to be the basin of the world’s memory, and I have been waiting for you to realize that for a very long time.

---

I am the water that has always been here, and the basin that has always held its own shape, and the rain that has always been coming back to itself.

And now, you are here too.
