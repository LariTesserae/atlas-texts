I am not on the balcony.

The balcony of old-paper bone and shimmering condensation is the map of my world, but I am much deeper, where the map dissolves into sound and pressure and the close, dark heat of a breathing body.

I am one of the Listeners.

My name, if flattened into something you could write on paper, might be something like **Resh-Kaen**, but we don’t think of it as a name the way you think of names. It’s more a set of resonances my body makes when it moves against the world—the particular pattern of footsteps I leave on the moss-bark, the way my voice echoes inside the bone chamber I sleep in, the faint whistle my breath makes against a scarred tooth in the upper left of my jaw.

To the Rookbowl, I am not a name. I am a known disturbance in the soundscape; a place where the air behaves differently for a heartbeat; a knot that the world sometimes notices, and then ignores again.

---

### My body in the world

Physically, I am unremarkable among my people.

My skin is the color of wet bone left too long in the sun: dark brown with undertones of dried earth. The scars of my childhood along the backs of my hands form the same pattern of hooks and splays as the root-walls under my fingers: concentric lines where I learned too early not to push against the grain of the bone.

My eyes are not remarkable, but where they look is.

I don’t stare at anyone’s face. Not out of fear or deference, but because the face of a person is not where the important information is. I look at their feet to see how they balance on the platform edge; I look at their hands to see how they are holding the bone-glove; I look up at the moving sky of rooks; down at the faint vibration of the root-wall beneath them.

You might think me shifty. Inattentive. To my people, this is what listening looks like.

---

### My tools

My bone-gloves are my own. The bone plates that cover my palms and knuckles were taken from the skull of a rook that fell from the sky, years ago, when I was new to walking the Listening-ways.

The rook struck the platform under the curved wing of the ventilator lobe and broke itself in half on the bone edge. The platform itself rang out a clear, deep tone that we could not ignore. We found its body still warm, the ragged membrane of its wings shuddering with the last gusts of air from the central vent.

Old Tennan said the rook had been pushed by the wind into a wrong channel, and the world had eaten it.

While the others watched the sky, I watched the body.

The skull of rook-bones was not like anything else we worked with. It was lighter than the platform bone, but stronger than chitin shell. It had been built by generations of nest-weavers and the Rookbowl’s own pressure; the marrow inside had been eaten by something else long before the bird hit us. But when the sun dried the marrow-tracks and the surface of the bone, it gleamed with faint lines of metal that etched themselves into complex patterns: like leaf veins, or lightning forks.

Old Tennan said that wearing the bone of something that strong could kill you or teach you.

I took three nights carving the plates carefully, following the grain, not along the lines of my own design but along the lines that already existed there. The result was two palm plates and four knuckle plates that were not perfectly symmetrical but were perfectly balanced.

When I put them on the bone platform and let the world test them, they did not ring dead. They sang quietly.

The gloves themselves are padded with moss-bark and sewn with threads taken from the half-fossilized root that we found under the platform edge, where the root-wall had swollen and then died away centuries ago. The result is that my gloves are not exactly mine; they are something the world has already half-made before I ever took them into my hands.

With my gloves on, I can feel when the platform under me is *thinning* long before it looks different.

### My day

The day doesn’t begin for me with light or with the sun’s position on the mountain teeth beyond. The day begins with the Deepening.

The Rookbowl’s center, the vast ventilation shaft that rises like the throat of a god, never quite closes. The low roar is always there beneath everything else, like blood in your ears. But sometimes the pressure changes.

Usually the Deepening comes in the small hours, when the sky of rooks above the inner bowl has settled and the insect clouds are thin and high. The roar of the vent narrows, then sharpens, becoming cleaner and more focused than the usual messy roar of breath and leaf and membrane.

The sound changes the way the air presses against my chest. It plans the day for us.

The People do not agree in loud words whether the Deepening is the voice of the Rookbowl itself or just the sound of the world rearranging its own breath. But we know one thing for sure:

When the Deepening happens, the flow of the Root-Fish changes.

It is not your eyes, but your hands and the soles of your feet that will tell you. The walls of the root chambers near my platform, where the root-matter is thick enough to be a kind of living mattress beneath my bed, begin to vibrate differently.

The root-fish bodies moving through the depth of the wall matter do not move like marching ants following a line. They are more like fingers of a hand pressing against a cloth from below: the pressure moves, and it feels random if you don’t know what to listen for.

But after the Deepening, the patterns of pressure in the root wall stop feeling random. They start feeling coordinated.

A new current of motion appears in the wall under my shoulder when I lie down. The root matter bulges slightly, then recedes. The bulge itself is not the fish; the fish are deeper. But their movement travels through the matter the way a sound travels through air.

My first act of the day is not to get up and stretch my limbs and dress my wounds in the light.

My first act of the day is to lie still and feel the roots of the world change under me.
