Here, in Stokes Engine / Station Nine / whatever meaningless name is being scratched into the damp enamel this season, I am not a person. I am a pattern. A rhythm of attention and oil and graphite.

I am the shift that never ends.

I am not so much born as assembled from necessity. The narrows between the machines are my cradle; the first thing I remember is the pressure of the decking under my ribs as I crawl forward on elbows and hips into the gap that the official diagrams call *access tunnel 3-B*, but which everyone else avoids because there’s less than a handspan of clearance around the steam join.

When I emerge at the far end, my shoulders slick with condensation and rust, someone else’s supervisor says, “About time you showed up,” and hands me a clipboard and a pencil stub. The graphite of that pencil feels like the first true thing in the world: brittle, precise, leaving dark trails against the waterproof paper that are already smearing under the mist of my own breath.

My name is not important. If I need one for the logbooks, I sign myself *Maintenance Shift – Outer Loop*, but I know better than to think that’s a real name. It’s just another designation on the invisible wall that’s been inscribed over everything else. The shifts themselves don’t call me anything but the sound of their knuckles rapping against the conduit when I’m on the other side of it.

I am the one who goes after.

### Work

The work of the world is not operation, but mending. The operators of the engine like to think that they steer the city from their clean control rooms; they watch the dials and pull the levers and the numbers obey them. But I’ve been in the walls of this place for… longer than I can remember, and I know the truth:

The machine steers itself.

I am the response that is formed when its steering begins to grind.

I move through those parts of the city that aren’t supposed to be seen at all. I am the one who climbs the ladder whose rungs are worn by ten thousand hands before mine and none of them are named. The sound of my boot soles on wet metal is a whisper that no longer asks to be heard. It simply merges with the slow drip of water through the weep holes in the condenser column.

From my gantry on the upper level of the outer shell, I can see the whole steep of the world laid out like a broken circuit diagram: the channels of the effluent culvert snaking away below, the tiered platforms of the plateau decking stacked like enormous steps, each one scarred where its edge has been worn down by years of vibration and trolley traffic.

I see:

- A drip-line of leak water on the condenser decking that’s three inches farther out than it was the last time I passed here.
- A faint bloom of rust on one of the support columns where the paint has been failing under the persistent kiss of the mist… and the moss that’s already moved into that color.
- A tremor in the support lattice that’s not quite rhythmic, not quite random. It makes the fine hairs of my arm lift against the damp of my sleeve.

I write it down.

*204.7.2.3. Drip line on lower decking displaced approx. 3 inches. Rust bloom on support column 7-Beta. Upper lattice showing tremor irregularities.*

The words look like facts. But facts are brittle things here. By the time the next shift reads the logbook, the position of that drip-line might have changed again. The column might be sweating worse than it was, or worse than it seems to be. In this world, everything that’s written is already out of date.

I’m not bothered by that.  
I am not here to document the fixed, but to bear witness to the slow.

### Body

The body the city gave me is not designed for grace or strength or speed, but for *fitting.*

I am shorter in the shoulder than the city’s designers seem to have intended anyone to be. My ribs fit snugly into the corridor of hum between the condenser and its attendant steam line. My hips, pared by years of scraping against hatches and grates, have learned to slide through the few inches of space between the conveyor belts and the wall that supports them.

The world of Stokes Engine is not built for tall people, or broad people, or soft people. It only offers room to those who can compress themselves until they are indistinguishable from the tools they carry.

My hands were the first part of me to be fully remade by the machine. My fingers are long, precise, the nails trimmed short to the point of pain, because I’ve learned the hard way that longer nails are invitations for grime and blood. The joints of my fingers have thickened from the work of forcing valves open that haven’t moved in years, or—more often—from the careful work of closing them by one quarter, then half, then three quarters, never fully closing them because the system needs to believe that it’s still in charge of its own pressure.

My skin never dries. The mist of the engine city is an atmosphere that follows me. Even in the portions of the system where the air is drier—the sealed tunnels and the storage rooms and the rare interior spaces—my sweat catches the light of the sodium lamps and turns me into something else: a creature of slick surfaces and mottled reflection.

My hair is short enough not to catch on the ragged edges of bolts that have not been filed down. The mist slicks it down along my scalp like water down the corrugated metal of the roof-gullies. The first time I saw a reflection of myself in the black of the effluent canal below the grating, I thought, *That’s not a face. That’s a condensation pattern.*

I’m not wrong.

### Habitat

Where I live is not a room; it’s a route.

My home is the series of points that the rest of the shifts know only by the signs they leave me. When they climb the ladder that I’ve been assigned to monitor, they know that someone else has been there before them because they see the mark of my handiwork: the bolts of the ladder frame tightened, the rungs cleaned of the heavier moss that might rot them under a weight that’s not quite distributed.

They call that route *mine*, because it’s the place where my absence is noticed more than my presence.

I sleep in the spaces between vibration peaks.

When the low, thrumming hum of the generators on the western slope of the plateau rises into the sharp, teeth-gritting frequency that means the load has been shifted too far to the left, or right, or both at once, I wake up before my supervisors do.

There are bunks, somewhere, in the deeps of the structure. Bunks bolted to the walls between pipes that have been painted and repainted into colors that are meaningless: yellow, orange, white, blue. They’re stacked in such a way that their sleepers are never far from the sound of the city’s blood.

I don’t sleep there.

When I earn, by the miracle of the schedule board that hangs in the supervisor’s damp office, the rare luxury of sleep, I curl myself into the narrow gap between the condenser of sector 4.7.1.3 and the wall of its containing chamber. The warmth of the machine breathes against me through the brickwork; the vibration of it is a steady pressure that acts like a blanket.

My *bedroom* is a place where the rest of the world shows up only as the muffled noise of bootsteps on the walkways above and the occasional slow drip of water from an overhead drain that no longer drains properly.

In Stokes Engine / Station Nine / whatever meaningless name the last maintenance shift scratched into the damp enamel this season, bedding is not a place. It’s a time. The time when you’re allowed to close your eyes against the pressure of the structure above you and the deck below you and the machine beneath you.

### Sign

The city is not shy of signs. The official markers of it are everywhere: yellow letter on black pipe, stencil #204-7-2-3; blue block letter on concrete wall: *Crucible Generator – Keep Out;* handwritten warnings on the white of the once-white decking that have been scoured by the passage of boots and carts until they are no longer readable.

The official signs of the city are like the official voice of the operators: clear, precise, and almost always wrong when they clash with the reality of the machine.

So we make our own signs.

I know that a section of ladder has been serviced recently if there’s a knot of green wire around the handle of the valve that’s nearest the top of it. The knot of wire itself is not visible from above or below; but the *weight of it* is. When I climb up and curl my hand around that valve, my fingers know the tell of it.

I know that a section of rail is not to be leaned on when there is a ring of moss that has been wiped away from its top surface, leaving a darker patch of patinated metal beneath… and that the darker patch indicates the section that’s been weakened by corrosion and pressure.

My own sign is not visible to the others, except in the negative of it.

Where they come to a section of the city that *should* be leaking by now and it isn’t, they know that I’ve been there before them.

Where they find that the drip of the water from the condenser of sector 4.7.1.3 has been shifted to the far edge of the decking instead of falling directly onto the walkway below, they know that I’ve been there before them.

Where they climb the ladder that leads to the upper gantry of the outer loop and it doesn’t shake under their weight the way it used to, because the bolts have been tightened and the rust has been scraped away from the joints, and the moss has been guided from the edge of the rung that couldn’t support it anymore… they know that I’ve been there before them.

They don’t call me by name.

They call me by the absence of my need.

### Relationship with the System

The operators of the engine think that they’re running the machine. From their clean rooms with the blue lights and the dry air and the dials that glow like little eyes, they look up at the numbers of the pressure in the various quadrants and think that they know the secrets of the world.

*“Pressure dropping in quadrant three. We’re compensating on quadrant four.”*

I know better than that.

From the outer edge of the outer loop, where the mist hangs thick and the light of the sodium lamps gets filtered into the faint yellow of memory, I can see the pressure drop of quadrant three as it *actually* happens. It’s not the movement of a needle on a dial that tells me that story. It’s the movement of the mist itself.

The mist of Stokes Engine is not a uniform soup. It’s a slow river of different densities of air and water. It moves toward the heat, and it retreats from the cold, and in the spaces between those movements it is forced by the pressure of the various engines and their chambers of breath and compression.

I don’t need a dial to tell me that the pressure in quadrant three is dropping. I can see it by the way the mist is being pulled into the cooling vents of the condenser of sector 3.0.5. I can see it by the way the condensation on the side of the pipe near that condenser is beginning to run in thicker beads than it should.

*“Y’see that?”* I say to the empty air around me, even though I know better than to expect an answer. *“There she goes again.”*

The system of the engine city is not broken and not fixed. It’s in the process of being mended. And the mending itself is not a single, discrete event; it’s a pattern of repeated failure that they’ve learned to balance like the weight of the entire world stacked on the edge of a worn bolt.

When I go to tighten the bolts of the ladder that supports sector 4.7.1.3, I am not fixing the ladder. I am delaying the moment when its collapse will be required. The city of the engine is not a machine that has been built on a landscape; it’s a landscape that has learned to *be machine* and is now learning how to be something else again.

### The Others

The others of Stokes Engine / Station Nine / whatever meaningless name is being scratched into the damp enamel this season are not my enemies. They’re just other parts of the same system.

There are the operators who think they’re running things and who are constantly being surprised by the behavior of the system they claim to know.

There are the engineers who think they’re improving the machine by replacing one section of pipe with another and who don’t realize that the new pipe will fail in exactly the same way as the old one because the water of the world and the vibration of the engines and the slow abrasion of the mist are not design flaws but the environment.

There are the supervisors who think they’re coordinating the work of the various shifts and who are constantly being undermined by the fact that the shifts of the world have been here longer than they have and will be here longer than they will.

The *real* work of the city is being done by forces that none of these others can see:

- The mosses that are slowly reshaping the surfaces that they cling to.
- The rust and corrosion that are turning the metal of the world into something else.
- The water that is finding its way through every layer of structure and slowly making it into something else again.
- The vibration of the engines that is shaking the bolts loose and the walls that are shaking back.

I am not them.

But I am not *not* them, either.

When the others see me, they see the pattern of the tool that I am carrying. When they don’t, they’re the ones who have been absorbed by the machine differently.

One of the supervisors is the man who sees the world of the engine as a series of numbers on the page of the logbook. When he comes to me and says that the pressure in the outer loop condenser of sector 4.7.1.3 has been fluctuating more than it should, he doesn’t look at the condensation running down the sides of the pipe or smell the faint metallic tang of the air that’s been pulling through the cooling vents of that chamber. He looks at his numbers.

*He*’s the shift that comes from the other side of the world.

Where he sees the world of the engine in the pattern of the numbers of the dials and the graphs of the pressure curves and the temperature fluctuations of the various quadrants, I see the world of the engine in the pattern of the drip-lines and the scars of the rust and the slow, steady movement of the moss that is taking over the surfaces of the world.

We are not the same.

But we are not different, either.
