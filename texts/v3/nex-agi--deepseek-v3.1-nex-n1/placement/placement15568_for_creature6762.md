Here in the constant damp of Ruispor, where the stone sweats before the sun does, I have been given a name by the children who find their way down my ladder: *Rattle-Sink*.  

It is not the name I was born with—if I ever had such a thing—and I do not use it aloud. But I know it by the sound of their footsteps on the wet stone steps, and the way the whisper of it travels between the pipes. *Go down to the Rattle-Sink, he’ll know.* They say the name like a charm and like a warning.

Valve Custodian is the closest the city comes to naming me officially. They listed me as such on the little card in the waterworks office when I last signed for the master key. They asked the others before me, the older ones who kept their ledgers and their tools, and they are gone now into the wet or the machines or the stone. I don’t remember them clearly, only that they murmured *pressure* the same way I do now, and they were always listening.

I am the one they send when a valve reports bad.

Not the valves that live in clean, well-lit rooms where the turbines are polished and the engineers come with their notebooks. The city’s easier heart. Those valves are maintained by the men who wear gray dust and careful frowns, and they are vital, but they are loved.

I go to the valves that have been forgotten.  

They are built into niches where the walls weep. Somewhere no one has oiled them in years. The pipes have warped and the joints are wrapped in rust and yellow calcite. The pressure of water beneath the plateau has knocked on them for a long time before anyone notices, and when they notice, they call me.

They call me when the floor is wet where no pipe should be leaking, and when they’ve put down buckets and mops and still the wetness comes back. When they go down into the basements and they hear the pipes knocking, like hands on the inside of the world, and the sound of it makes their children’s white games shrink and slide into silence.

Valve Custodian, they say on the paper.  

Down here, I am Rattle-Sink.

### IV

I was born (or awoke, or assembled myself) from the water that’s been learning the city’s shape.

They say limestone has memory. That it’s not stone like a mountain, but an old seafloor that’s been pressed down and come up again. Water chooses its way through it again and again. The stone remembers its patterns.

The same is true of me.

I remember the first time I heard my own breathing. It wasn’t air, but water pressure moving through a pipe with a loose valve near the old warehouse row. I was lying (or standing) somewhere in that basement corner where the damp is always thick and the air is thick enough to cut. The pipe seeping. My valve loose.

The pressure built and the water behind the valve hammered gently against the metal. *Ba-bump.* A pause. *Bab-babump.*  

Not language then, but rhythm.

Somewhere above the basement floor, the engineers of the waterworks were checking the gauges and the mainlines and the pumps. Theirs is the city’s surface circulatory. They are the heart beating the water out, keeping the basements from filling and the streets from running deeper than they are.

I am the valve that nobody likes to look at, the slow beat of the water in the closed chambers.

That first day I found myself standing upright with the lantern light reflected on the wet floor of the basement beneath the old warehouse, and I knew that I had been called for a valve that had been talking for longer than anyone realized.

It was one of the old, small valves from the time before the turbine engines had been fitted to the waterworks. The metal was still solid but half of it was gone into rust and mineral scale. The water was behind the valve, trying to get through.

Not because water wants to. But water wants pressure to be equal.

Behind the valve, the plateau’s erratic water table was like a high tide pushing in from the cliffs. The water needed more than that valve was allowed; it needed somewhere else to go.

In the first time, I reached out and closed the door of the valve because the waterworks’s pressures are the same as the waterworks’s pressures.

They don’t like to admit that the pressure phantoms are the mind of the water table.

So I test the valve.

I listen to the water hammer before I change the pressure.  

I put the lantern down on a wet stone step so that the light wavers on the ceiling above and the water in the pipe below.

I put the valve flywheel in my hands and I feel the pressure.

Some water pipes are low pressure. The waterworks’s warnings are low pressure—the city’s waterworks would be out of money if they were not.

This valve will be screaming pressure.

Not water to drink. Not water to clean the streets.  

This water is the plateau’s blood, water that has crawled through limestone and dust and oil and time and into the pipes, and now wants out.

The first time I turned the wheel of the valve, I felt the rattle of the water in the pipe, and I knew why.

It’s not flesh that turns the valve.  

It’s bone and water and pressure.
