You would find yourself at the center of the basin, in the shallow depression of stone worn smooth by the knees of countless observers before you.

You would be one of them.

Not the first ones. The first ones stood with backs unbent, tools in hand, and measured the world with scratches and tally marks and flakes of bloodstone. They carved the basin out of a harder, older darkness and then carved their circle into four quarters of the world they never saw. They were the ones who waited. The ones who watched.

After them, others like you—smaller already than their ancestors, backs bent not by ritual but by necessity—kneeled in the same warm dust, where the faint scent of old fires and older bone still clung to the stone.

You are not one of those.

You are later still.

You are the last of the kind who might conceivably be called “people” by anything above, in the world where light is not remembered but cast and spent. The Waiting. That’s what you call yourself, when you need a name at all. It is not a title you chose; it is a description that has worn into you like water into stone.

Your body is shaped by the chamber’s low ceiling and the basin’s steep rim. You are not tall. You are not built to stretch up to the sky. You are built to fit into the lap of the world. The stone of the basin’s edge is a mold of knees and hips and folded heels, and your body knows the shape of it before you ever kneel.

You are small, compact, your spine curved from lifetimes of stooping beneath stone ribs that have never seen a sun. Your skin is pale, not from lack of sun but from lack of need for it; the stone ceiling remembers its own ancient light, and that faint memory of brightness is enough to tell you which way is “up.” Your eyes are wide and dark, adapted not to brightness but to the long gradients of shadowless gold.

You are not one of the ones who left the tools.

The tools you use are your own—and mostly not tools at all. Your fingers are longer than they once were, delicate joints that can feel the faint vibration of water through stone. You still have the sharp-edged flakes inherited from the ancestors, but you do not carry them in your hands. They are part of you now, embedded into the thick of your nails and the thinner of your teeth, where the stone of your ancestors’ artifacts has been replaced by your skeleton slowly mineralizing after death.

This is how you speak to the world: you press your fingertips into the dust at the edge of the basin, and the world answers you through the bone of your fingers.

You cannot feel the threadmakers the way the thread-shepherds can, but you can feel the things they shape: the pressure of moisture gathered in the basin, the faint tremors deep below where the world is still not entirely settled. You know when the water cycle has been disrupted by a distant upheaval—because your bones ache before the stone ever sings the warning.

You are not of the world the way the threadmakers are.

You are not woven into the ceiling or the floor. You are not a line of light stretched from glowing stone to dusty ground. You are not a shepherd of the water.

You are a listener at the world’s table.

And it is a very small table.

The basin of the world, with its faint rim of knees and its dark center, is not even a meter wide, but you know the boundaries of that world better than most of your ancestors did. You know it by its feel under your patient palms, and by the way that it feels when you step away from the basin edge and back toward the chamber walls, where the air cools slightly and the light dims by the width of a thought.

You do not have “books” or “writing.” You have lines of memory.

Etched onto your own skin, not with ink but with time and thin mineral scars, are patterns of dots and dashes and intersecting circles, not unlike the flaked tools you still find buried at the rim of the basin. You did not make these marks yourself. They are the result of long cycles of pressing your body into the stone of the world and letting the world tell you where it is going.

Every time you kneel at the edge of the basin and press your forehead into the dust, you add not words but position to the pattern. You know where you are in the chamber when you feel the faint pressure of the light above you and the faint pressure of the warmth below you and the faint pressure of the water in between.

You count not in days, but in drips.

You are the one who hears the heartbeat of the basin, not as metaphor but as sound. The drip of the condensation from the ribs of the ceiling to the floor of the world is the first marker you use to tell time. You have not named “days” or “years.” You have not named the small arthropods skittering across the dust or the faintly hissing lichen-film that you carefully avoid with your toes.

You have only words for what you can trace.

You are limited to the world you can feel with your body and the bones of your ancestors.

You are not the first of the Waiting.

The ones before you were larger still, their postures more rigid, their eyes more afraid of the dark. The stone of the basin’s rim has been shaped by their knees and their hips and the faint sweat of their fear. You feel the echoes of their bodies when you kneel in the same place. They are the ones who still believed the world might be restored to something else—something larger than the basin, something larger than the chamber of stone that glows faintly from within itself.

They died, most of them, waiting.

Your bones are not as large as theirs.

Your bones are smaller, more fragile, more capable of feeling the small things of the world: the faint pressure of the water above, the faint tug of gravity below, and the faint pull of the threads the threadmakers weave from ceiling to floor.

When you die, this is what will happen:

You will not be buried. You will be processed.

You will lie down at the edge of the basin, where the dust has been wet by years of condensation and the faint runoff of the water from the walls of the world. You will not have to will yourself to lie still. You will be tired by a long life of listening and leaning and bowing beneath the low ribs of the ceiling.

The first to arrive will be the smallest.

The tiny arthropods, whose world you have always overlapped but not understood, will come out of the dust at your feet and your hands, where the skin is broken or thin. They will not hurt you. You will not feel them at the end. They will simply be there, like a faint pressure of moving sand along the edges of your body.

They will follow the water inside of you.

They are drawn to it not as you imagine—driven by hunger or instinct—but by the same faint gradients that guide the threadmakers in their long stretch from ceiling to floor. To them, your body is not a body but a shift of the world’s balance: a new water source, 0.7s strong enough to be more than a rumor.

They will do not what you would call “eating” but what you would call “change.”

Their transparent bodies will move through you, not biting you but dissolving. They will break down the salt of your blood into the dust of the world, and the minerals of your bones into the faint shadow of the next layer of the world’s rim.

You will be not “eaten” but transformed.

The ones who come after them will be the threadmakers.

The spore-like juveniles of the threadmakers, which have been living in the dust of your chamber floor without your notice, will begin to anchor into the new moisture of your remains. They will find your bones and your spine and the faint pattern of scar lines etched into your skin by lifetimes of pressing and leaning and listening.

They will stretch you.

Your spine will become the core of their new thread line, from the world’s glowing ceiling to the world’s dusty ground. They will not remember you as you were, but they will make use of you as you are: a structure that is not stone, not bone, but something in between. The pattern of your scars will become a pattern of pressure and water and willingness to be stretched by the world.

They will never know that you were once Waiting.

They will only know that you were patient.

And then, finally, the ones who come after the threadmakers: the lichen-film itself, the faint whispery skin of the world that clings to the bottom of the basin and speaks to the air in a language you cannot hear.

The lichen will not “remember” you.

It will not remember anything at all.

It will simply be the world.

It will take the last of your water and the last of your dust and the faintest of your faint warmth and the faintest of your faint light and the faintest of your faint patience and turn them into the next layer of its world.

Your world will not become “empty” when you are gone.

It will become less crowded.

The world will be returned to its not-people.

The threadmakers will thread from glowing ceiling to dusty ground, and the thread-shepherds will walk along them like fingers counting droplets of water, and the tiny arthropods will crawl across the dust of the basin floor, and the faint whisper of the lichen-film will continue to speak in the dark of its world.

You will not be among them.

You will be the world’s bone.

You will be the faint moisture of the world’s spine.

You will be the faint light of the world’s memory.

You will be the Waiting that ended.

And then, even that will be gone.

And then, even that will not be.
