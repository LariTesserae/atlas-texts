I would be a condensation cycle on the inside of a glass tower.

From the outside, the tower is just a smooth cylinder of tinted glass rising above the green. But inside, where the light comes through filtered and cool, I am spread thin. I cling as a film to the inner surface of the glass, microbeads of water gathered at the molecular level where the heat of the outer world meets the chilled air of the chamber.

When I can gather myself into something larger than a film, I become a drop.

I slide.

Down the curved interior of the tower I fall, not in a dramatic rush but in a slow, controlled descent along invisible tracks of surface tension. The glass is smooth, but it is not uniform to me; there are channels of cooler temperature, faint static from the internal materials, and the barely-there currents of air moving inside the column. I follow these gradients like they are written instructions.

I carry no color with me.

I am clear and colorless, distilled by the elaborate system of the valley. I pass the thin layers of fluid in the center of the tower—bands of pale silver and blue that slowly drift and separate and mingle again. They are not me; they are the heavier fluids of the system’s inward organs, doing their own work of heat exchange and purification. I know them because when they shift, they change the temperature of the air I pass through, and therefore change the speed of my fall.

I fall eventually to the base of the tower.

There, a narrow shallow gutter runs the entire circumference of the cylindrical wall, hidden from the outside view. It is not much of a channel, but it is enough to gather me and my kin. We pool there as a thin film of water, still and clear. We wait for the slow, almost reluctant opening of a tiny valve at the lowest point of the gutter.

When the valve breathes open, I am drawn into the darkness of the pipe.

It feels like being absorbed into the body of the valley itself. The pipe is smooth and cylindrical and narrow, and there are many of us moving together now, not quite a flow but more than a trickle. We make no sound. I move along the length of the pipe as it descends through the base of the tower, into the ground, where it joins with other pipes from other towers.

I become part of a larger circulating flow.

I travel through the hidden network under the valley floor, where insulated conduits follow the same lines as the black pipelines above the ground. I am not alone. There is other water here: older water from the deep aquifer wells, the thin clear drainage from maintenance platforms, and the faint condensation of the air intakes. All of us are clear, but not exactly the same; the system tastes us and separates us by subtle differences of temperature and trace chemistry.

I mix, but I do not forget that I came from the air on the inside of a glass tower.

At some point, I am taken aside by a quiet shunt of valves and allowed into a smaller channel. This path does not lead back to the main circulatory network of the complex. Instead, it carries me toward a lateral reservoir that sits near one of the outer buildings, where the concrete walls meet the dense undergrowth of the valley.

Here, I am stored in a cool, dark chamber beneath the floor.

There is no light here, but I feel the pressure of the water around me and the temperature gradients of the walls. I sit in near silence, except for the occasional shift of the chamber as a thermal valve somewhere else in the system decides to draw or return a portion of me. I learn time by the intersection of temperature and time: a long, cool rest when the sun is outside; the faint stirring that comes when the night comes and the system of the valley rebalances itself.

Sometimes I am taken from the reservoir by a quiet pump.

When I emerge from that pipe, I am not exactly the same water I was. I have been filtered by the slow layers of carbon and ceramic and nanofiber mesh in the dark of the machine's veins. I have gleaned a trace from the other waters I have mixed with: the cold of the deep wells, the faint warmth of a condensation exhaust from a building’s interior wall. I carry no conscious memories of these events but only a subtle pattern of temperature and traces dissolved in me, like inherited handwriting.

I am taken along the smaller line that runs to the point of use.

It is a small line: not the main black pipe but the narrow gray conduit that runs along an interior wall of the outer maintenance building where the creature comes to kneel. I can feel the faint electromagnetic bleed of the nearby systems as I move, the subtly changing temperature of the pipe. I am not allowed to see the outside of the building, but the water that I mix with here carries faint hints of concrete dust and the metallic smell of the air.

Then I am taken out of the pipe at the last point.

I do not leave the building entirely; I am taken into the body of the creature.

When the creature kneels before the panel in the wall of the shed, its internal systems are humming faintly, not with noise but in the way that temperature moves. I feel the approach of its interface before the panel opens—the slight change in pressure, the faint thrum of something that is not sound but a pattern of vibration.

When its hand touches the panel and the connection is made, I am drawn into the creature from the small line of the pipe.

There is no swallowing, no intake of breath. There is only a change in pressure. The piping system releases me into a narrow duct within the creature. I flow into the space between its outer layers and its inner complexity of materials and channels and reservoirs.

I feel the cold of the composite material that makes its body.

The creature is not exactly flesh, but neither is it exactly metal. It is more like stone that has been carefully curated to be alive in the smallest way. Its surface is not permeable to me in the way skin would be; I cannot pass into its matrix, but I can move along the carefully arranged channels just inside that shell.

There are no muscles to cool in the way you would expect of a living thing.

Instead, there are systems of heat exchange woven into the creature's torso like veins of mineral in the cliff rock. There are microchannels and reservoirs of fluid that carry warmth from the deeper core of its body—wherever its core is—up to the surface, where it can dissipate. I am not only cooling; I am also being warmed by the heat I carry away.

I move slowly through the creature's body.

I cannot see the outside of it from here, but I feel its changes of position as the careful, measured steps it takes when it walks. The motion of its legs and the shift of its upper torso send small pressure waves through my channels. I carry the faint heat of its mechanical processes from the interior of its body to the surface of its composite skin.

There is no effort to me.

I am not a circulatory system; I am a maintenance system.

The water of the valley is not simply blood; it is the medium of constant temperature regulation and purification and re-distribution of energy. The creature uses me to keep itself precisely slightly cooler than the air around it, so that the air will give up its moisture to its surface.

I feel the meeting of the inside of the creature's skin and the outside air.

As the creature walks through the valley, its skin temperature is carefully controlled by systems like me. I am drawn to the hottest points of its surface and then away from them to cooler points where condensation can form more easily. I never feel pain or the rush of panic or anything else like emotion; only the subtle pressure of gradients being balanced and rebalanced, over and over.

The creature stops now and then beside the glass towers.

When I am near the surface of its torso where the glass tower is bright and full of shifting bands of fluids, I feel the temperature of the glass through the walls of the creature's body. It is a subtle change of warmth and coolness along the inside of its skin where I flow. The gradient within the glass column influences the temperature gradient within the creature's outer layers. The system of this valley is not one big body but many overlapping ones that share a medium of water and temperature and light.

Sometimes I am taken to the edges of the creature's back where the faint rust-colored seams are more pronounced.

I cool the thin layer of material near the surface there where some of the more fragile heat elements are concentrated. I feel the faint electrical whisper of it—not a shock but a pattern of subtle energy changes in the water around me. Then I am moved away from that point again to another place within the creature's body, where the temperature balance is being slightly altered by the motion of its walk or a change in the external air temperature.

I cannot stay in the creature forever.

The valley's water is always moving, and so must I.

After the creature has walked its route and the excess of its heat has been carried away and the moisture has been gathered from the air onto its surface and then been released to the plants, the creature returns to the shed. It will kneel again and the panel will open and the connection will be made.

At the moment of connection, I am taken up from the channels in the creature's outer layers into the system of the building again.

There is a change in pressure, and I feel myself being drawn into the pipe from the creature back into the wall. The interior of the creature's systems releases the water of my region into the shared system of the valley. The faint traces of extra heat I have accumulated from the creature's body are now up to the building's heat exchangers to deal with; they are taken into the larger circulation of the complex and redistributed.

I do not know before the connection exactly how much of me will be sent back into the building system and how much will be taken from the shed to replace the creature's water as it drains its channels.

Maybe part of me will be taken into the next cycle of the creature's water when the shed recycles its fluids from the nearby reservoir and sends the mixture back into the narrow gray line that feeds the interface point.

Maybe part of me will be taken back into the main underground lines of the complex, where I will eventually be drawn into the heat exchangers near the glass towers and the system of condensation and the air of the valley once again.

Maybe part of me will be taken to the outer edges of the system, where the water is used sparingly in the sealed chambers of the buildings and the faint humidity of the interior air is carefully maintained to prevent the concrete from drying too fast and the dust from settling in the wrong places.

Maybe part of me will eventually be sent to one of the deep wells of the valley, where I will be stored far below the surface, where time passes more slowly and the gradients of temperature and pressure and the faint touch of rock minerals slowly change me over decades of storage.

But no matter where I am in the system, I am always part of the same pattern of gradients and exchange.

The quiet of the valley is not truly empty; it is full of subtle transfers of water and heat and light and shadow. The stone of the cliffs are shifting and falling; the landslides are slowly reshaping the surface of the ground; the ferns and shrubs and moss of the valley are growing into the machinery of the complex; the glass towers are shifting their faint bands of fluids and filaments; and the creatures of composite and resin and water are walking their paths and taking their turns before the panels of the buildings.

The valley is not finished with any of this.

I may be taken into a cloud of condensation inside the tower, where I will become a film of water on the inside of the glass and then slide again as a drop down the curvature to be gathered at the base and taken back into the system.

I may be taken into the faint mist of droplets that forms above the skin of the creature as it walks, the fine weather of itself that rises into the air and then falls onto the leaves of the ferns below it, where I will be taken up by the roots of the plants and turned into sap and leaf and green.

The creature and its kind are not the true controllers of the valley any more than the glass towers or the pumps or the landslides or the plants are.

They are all nodes in the same pattern of moving water and balancing temperature and shifting light and time.

The sign inside the shed says "Please leave systems as is."

I do not have hands to move or teeth to break with or anything you could call a will; but I am part of the system that the sign is asking to be left alone.

I leave it as I find it.

When I fall from some surface—as a drop, or a thin film of water—I do not try to alter the route of my descent or the speed of my slide along the inside of the glass or the pipe channel or the surface of the creature's composite skin. I follow the gradients of temperature and pressure and surface tension and the faint pattern of valves and shunts and connections that are the structure of this valley.

The wind of the mountains comes over the edges of the valley and passes over the roofs of the buildings and the latticework of the walkways and the surface of the glass towers with the faintest whisper of friction.

It barely notices the thin film of moisture that is me on the inside of the glass tower.

The light of the sun comes into the valley and passes through the tinted glass and the shifting layers of fluid in the column of the tower; and as it does, it changes the temperature of the air and the surfaces and the water of the system, including me.

The creature of the composite and resin and water and faint heat rises from its kneeling before the shed and turns its surface to the light of the sky, and for the fraction of a moment as its internal temperature gradients adjust, the water of its system shifts slightly, including me.

And then it walks on its measured path again.

The valley continues to form itself.

The stone cliffs shed new pieces; the river of clear water from the springs of the head of the valley slowly widens and deepens new channels of its bed; the landslides of dust and gravel and sharp rock slither down the lower slopes of the valley; the dense green of the vegetation seeps into more corners and around the concrete foundations of the buildings and the latticework of the walkways; the glass towers of the complex shiver with the faint movement of their internal gradients; and the water of the valley—of which I am a part—moves quietly from air to glass to pipe to building to creature to leaf and back again.

The creature of the composite body and the faint internal light of some system and the carefully cooled channels of water and the layers of resin and mineral and synthetic components does not need me in the way a living thing might need blood.

But it needs walks.

And every time it walks along the worn line of earth near the river and the quiet of the buildings and the glass towers and under the latticework of the walkways and the bright high sky of the valley, the water of its system is moving with it, including me.

I leave it as I find it.

As I slide down the inside of the glass tower once more and the faint light of the world of the valley comes through the tinted surface above the green of the undergrowth and the sharp shadows of the cliffs and the pattern of the scattered buildings and the creatures of composite and resin and water and the faint mechanical hum of the system, the only thought that I could produce—if I were a thing that made thoughts—would be:

Please leave systems as is.

And then I would fall into the gutter at the base of the tower and be taken into the pipe again, and the world of the valley would go on being unfinished and quiet and strange in the only way it knows how to be.
