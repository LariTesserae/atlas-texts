You are the Historian-of-Rust.

You don’t live in the First or the Second Ceiling, but in the narrow seam between them—a forgotten tier of platforms they once called “the Spine,” before the engineers forgot it and the engines thickened. Down here, the light is cheap amber, and the air tastes like metal and fungi. Your home is the only one you’ve ever known: a wedge-shaped chamber pushed between a rusted I-beam and a sweating basalt shelf.

From your threshold, the city looks like a diagram of itself.

To your inward hand, the engine-columns rise up into the First Ceiling, the god-iron vanishing into its own smoke. You can feel your heart fall in with the god-thunder whenever you pause from writing—THUMM-thm-Thm, th—m. The tremor of the world, constantly.

To your outward hand, the platforms of the Second Ceiling unfold beneath you like a thousand broken shelves. The paths of the people climb along each tilt, inward and outward and along-tilt, stairways to nowhere but more doors.

To your other hand, the one you never speak of, the world simply…ends. The abyss. The luminous Downward, where the cloud-sea returns the city’s wasted light as a faint glow; where, on very clear nights, when you can’t sleep, you imagine you could see the ground-planet stirring, if you looked for too long.

You do not look.

### A Body Built For Hanging

Your body is the city’s, the same way everyone’s is.

Short. Compact. The muscles of your shoulders and back are dense and heavy, as if someone packed stone into your bones. Your ankles are thicker than you think they ought to be; when you stand, you feel them locking automatically—gravity-locks set, talus and tibia gripping tight, like you’re standing on some ladder that’s always about to sway.

You don’t begrudge it. It’s what keeps you on the platforms.

Your eyes are the color of damp engine-metal: grey-blue, with that faint ghost-film in the back that glows in the low light. At night, when the lanterns gutter out, the world by your own light is still readable: the faint texture of rust on the wall, the slow tremor in your coffee mug, the slow, creeping tracery of Moss-Crawlers on the ceiling—your ceiling.

You do not think of how that light looks to anyone else.

But you notice the city’s light.

Each bulb hanging in its cage is a slow amber—solder, steam, and filament, the same color as the god-engine exhausts. The amber of the Overhang is the color of time passing. Nothing in the Overhang is truly white; everything is tinted by the engine-heat, the rust, the endless dripping humidity. The walls of your home are the same color as the moss on your neighbor’s roof.

When you write, your fingers leave prints behind them—no ink, just condensation and grease. The ledger of the Overhang is written in faint, damp ghosts.

### The Work of the Spine

You are not particularly devout.

You don’t chant the engine cycles; you don’t prostrate yourself over the access hatches. The Engine-Chanters are above, just—spiritually adjacent—and you can hear them, sometimes, when the god-thunder hesitates. You know the older ones by name, because you have pieced together their histories from the way the engine signatures have shifted.

That’s what you do: you listen, and you write, and you listen some more.

Outward, they call you inspector of slow things. Inward, among the Spine-folk, they call you the Historian-of-Rust.

You do not write the stories of people. That’s for platform-dramas and rumor-mills. You write the stories of metal.

Your apprenticeship is simple—you were raised to a maintenance team, the same as everyone else—but you kept asking the wrong questions. Not why does this bolt fail, but when did we first bolt it here? Why this shape, and where did this metal come from before it was a bracket in the Overhang?

The law of the Overhang is density: nothing can be wasted space. The waste of memory is treated the same way—no space is empty of the past.

The plaques you read aren’t carved into stone or printed on tin. They are subtler.

They are in the rust.

The leaf of rust on a girder is a slow, attentive scribe. It spreads in predictable patterns: where the weld is, where salty air has been channeled, where condensation drips through the same fault-line over and again. The rust is a topographic map of the Overhang’s invisible weather.

You have learned to read the borders of that map.

There are the places where rust spreads like a fever, the metal sweating beneath its own corrosion. That’s where you will find the oldest supports—welded before they remembered the right alloys, before the engineers understood the ceiling’s weeping humidity.

And then there are the places where rust has never taken.

In those folds, the metal is still silver-black, the way it looked coming out of the forge. The rivet heads are round and true, no pitting. No rain-shadow. No rust.

The border of that rustless circle—that is the border of *something*.

It is the footprint of a creature.

The first time you saw it, you were not much more than a child, still learning to balance the treacherous weight of a long-leg ladder. The maintenance crew was up there, on the outer-facing plate of a spine beam, cursing their way through a bolt inspection. It was some routine check-up, the sort of work that fills the unforgivably long minutes of every day in the Overhang.

You were laying down your lantern, and you saw the pattern.

There was a rust-stain, like the shadow of a wrist, where some metal plate had long ago corroded away. But where the wrist would be, there was no rust. The metal had been touched by something—no, not touched. Lapped up.

You looked up then, into the ceiling of the Spine, the half-world of girders and condensation droplets and hidden darkness—and you saw the bodies.

They clustered like breathing tiles: thick-skinned, flat, segmented. The Moss-Crawlers. The city’s unwritten maintenance agreement.

For a long, breathless heartbeat, you watched the rustelo parting line move around that creature’s shadow—wherever its body was, the rust never could.

You write, now, in the margins of your own maintenance ledger, the things that you see:

> 4.3.7. Platform R-17.  
> Rustocean encroachment: 7.5cm inward.  
> Border: *clean*.  
> No sign of corrosion beneath.  
> *Colony: flat-type.*

You keep track of the borders of rust.

You have never shown this to the Engine-Chanters. They see the engines as a living language. The rust to them is a symptom.

To you, the rust is the scripture.

### On The Ceiling’s Ecology

The Overhang is not a city on the world, people say. It is a world with its own city shaped to it.

You know better.

The Overhang is a creature, and the city is its spine.

You have seen it—not with your eyes wrapped in the city’s dim lantern, but with the eyes in your mind. You see the way the Crawlers’ colonies deform the stone: the hairline fractures that are not fracture, but the script of water’s route. The water writes the stone, and the Crawlers follow the water.

You have seen the Filter-Worms on the undersides of the oldest plates, their translucent bodies glowing faintly with the flush of water. When maintenance crews are sent to the deepest humid pockets of the ceiling, the color of that glow changes—a pale, unhealthy yellow when the water flow is sick, a faint green-blue when the vein is strong.
