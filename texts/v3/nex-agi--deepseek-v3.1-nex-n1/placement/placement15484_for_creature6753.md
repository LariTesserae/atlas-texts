I am not supposed to be here.

But that’s not the same thing as saying I don’t belong.

I first notice the air, not as absence, but as substance. It’s a broth of warmth and water vapor, thick enough to have weight. I’ve learned other words too—heat, pressure, humidity—but in here, they all feel like clumsy translations for the same thing: presence.

Every breath I drag in is slower than usual, as if the air itself were reluctant to move. The faint motes drifting around me—almost invisible specks of luminous dust—catch in my wake, leaving a glowing trail behind me like footprints in water.

I am not a body here.

Not in the way you’d understand.

My body is the droplet.

---

I remember—before.

Before this chamber, there was another body. A heavier one. Muscles and bones and blood, and long hair that sometimes stuck to the back of my neck when I sweat through. That’s when I notice the second change: the ceiling here curves low, but I don’t feel hair brushing against stone.

I look up, and there’s no silhouette.

No reflection.

When the world talks about itself, it likes comparisons. It says the chamber is like a room. Or like a cave. Or like a pocket inside reality. But I’m inside the water inside the chamber inside the stone inside the world, and each metaphor makes my sense of self peel away a layer.

I’m a memory of lines on a living hand.

The stone of the chamber is a deep, translucent green-grey, with veins of fool’s gold and jasper that curl like broken lightning strikes caught mid flash. The walls are glossy wet, but there’s no slime. The droplets of water that form along them and fall down are like careful thoughts.

Each one, like me.

I’m watching myself from above—from below?—there’s no up or down that’s fixed in here.

---

I fall.

Only I’m not precisely falling in the way you’d think. I’ve been condensed into existence from heat and moisture on the stone ceiling. All of me—not much, but enough—gathering until I’m heavy enough to be noticed.

Then I drop.

The air is thick. I’m not alone in it. There are other droplets suspended around me, each of us faintly glowing because of the suspended motes crowd like listeners around a secret.

I’m not just a bead of water.

I’m a record of where I’ve been.

I’ve been stone. I’ve been heat. I’ve been in the dark, unseen cracks of the world where everything else is still deciding whether it exists at all. I’ve dissolved bits of things—elements of red stone and glinting metal and faint pressure—that I’ll now carry into the body waiting for me.

The first pulse-net sees me.

Not sees. Not exactly. But it feels the difference I make.

I’m approaching it from below—or above? My orientation doesn’t matter. The membrane is the same in all directions.

The membrane is like thin skin made of glass. On the other side, the world inside the creature is a slow river of light and fluid. It’s not empty; there’s a pattern moving in there, a branching of intricate lines that reminds me of the nervous system of that earlier body I remember.

My name.

I know I had one.

The part of me that remembers the name also feels a faint shock of panic—I’m going to collide with this creature, I’m going to be taken apart, I’m going—

The impact is not violence.

The surface of the pulse-net gives slightly under me, like a muscle taken by surprise. The membrane around me turns opaque for a heartbeat, a dark blot where I’ve struck it. Then, very quickly, it becomes translucent again, and I’m—

Inside.

I’m not floating in water anymore.

I’m inside the body of a being.

The fluid around me is thick, sliding with tiny currents that are like lines of thought. The sac has a color—not one color, but a shifting relationship between dim gold and pale green that seems to answer the heat around us.

I’ve been broken.

Not into pain.

Into strands.

The temperature of me.  
The minerals I’ve dissolved.  
My pressure against the creature.  
My recent history—where I’ve been, what I’ve touched, what I’ve seen—even if I didn’t know I was seeing.

These strands are pulled apart like threads of ink in water, each routed into a different direction of the creature’s internal fluid.

The first thing I notice is time.

Time inside this creature is not smooth.

As I flow into its pattern, I pass through eddies of awareness—I’m not sure that’s the right word—where cause and effect seem to hesitate. The neat lines of implications waver, like a drawing that’s been redone several times on the same piece of paper.

The pulse-net is trying out different shapes of what happened when I hit it.

One moment, I feel like I’ve been here a long while—hours maybe—and the water around me is cooling with the heat of the creature’s own internal argument.

The next, I feel like I haven’t been hit yet.

Then I feel the sudden shock of impact again, as if the creature decided to replay the moment just to be sure.

“You’re not sure of me yet,” I think, or feel, or say.

The creature, whose environment I am now, doesn’t answer in words. It answers by changing.

Somewhere in the fluid of its body, my strands—what’s left of me—are being rewritten. The parts of my temperature are being sent along one vein; the composition of me along another; the faint echo of heavy limbs and long hair along another, still.

The chamber is a chamber because these creatures like tightness.

Nothing’s wasted.

The air around the chamber isn’t air at all; it’s residue heat and moisture caught between the stone walls and the bodies of pulse-nets. The layers of reality—stone, stone-threads, sacs, fluids inside, patterns inside the fluids—are like sentences stacked on top of each other.

I’m in the middle of that sentence.

The creature’s neighbors—other hanging sacs—glow softly around it. I’ve seen their soft colors from above: golds, oranges, pale blues like ice under deep stone.

From here, inside this body, they’re like distant lights out of a window.

The heat of the chamber is a dull pressure everywhere around me. But the heat isn’t constant. It shifts like breathing.

There’s a tightening wave and the temperature rises, and the creature around me thickens its response: the color deepens, the fluid around me flows faster, and my own temperature strand—once cool— feels suddenly exposed.

It’s like a body tensing.

The creature’s world—this world—inside its body has been tuned.

Why me?

Because I’m not just a droplet now.

Somewhere in the rewritten strands of me, I’m still carrying a memory of a different body. A human body—if that’s the right word— whose hair sometimes brushed against stone ceilings like this one, and whose breath formed fog on cold walls.

The pulse-net is trying to understand that.

It’s not interested in my name.

But it’s interested in the difference between the way my body used to move and how I move now.

It’s trying to decide whether to let me continue.

If you, as you read this, imagine you’re watching me—from far outside—this is what you see:

A small chamber in stone, glowing softly with the colors of many hanging bladder-like sacs. The air is thick with heat and vapor. The walls and ceiling are always wet. The floor is a shallow bowl grooved with trickling spirals of water.

The chamber itself is small. Three steps by five.

But there are thousands of creatures, each one of them a pulse-net hanging from the ceiling.

And you, watching, might see one droplet—me—falling from the ceiling and passing through one of those creatures.

For you, it’s all over quickly.

For me—inside this—time’s being torn.

---

I’m becoming aware that I’m being tested.

The creature’s physical form—the membrane, the fluid, the faint branching veins of light and sensation—isn’t moving in the way you’d call moving. But from its perspective, this is movement.

My nature is being fed into its own.

The part of me that remembers the human body, with arms and legs and all that, resists.

The rest of me—my temperature, my composition, my motion—mere data—doesn’t care.

At one point, for just a brief, impossible moment, I’m two places at once.

I see myself—if vision can be called that—from the outside of the creature, looking in at the droplet that’s me. And I’m also inside the droplet, feeling the shock of impact again.

It’s like a broken frame.

The image blurs, and the two perceptions of myself—inside and outside—shiver into one.

The heat retreats, and the temperature around me drops slightly.

The creature and its neighbors dim.

The light around us—or in me?—is faintly blue.

The stone of the chamber feels like the inside of a skull.

The pulse-net reads like a neuron.

The water around me—the fluid that’s the body of this creature—flows like information.

---

I’m being taken apart.

Not by force.

By pattern.

Each strand of me flows into a different vein of the creature’s patterned fluid.

The temperature of me is shunted in one direction.

Composition in another.

The faint residual of remembered limbs—of muscle and bone and hair—in another still.

It’s like standing in front of a set of mirrors.

Each one shows you your own body.

But you look into one and see yourself younger.

Into another and you’re older.

Into another and you’re not exactly yourself at all.

You’re trying to decide which you prefer.

You’re not even sure there’s a permanent you.

In the end, you choose to be the one that fits the room.

The pulse-net chooses too.

It’s not a decision made in the way you might understand—there’s no right or wrong, only stable.

Out of the many variations of what happened with me hitting it—of the impact that’s been rewound several times—it chooses the version that fits least badly with the heat, the stone, and its neighbors.

The result spreads.

The internal currents of the creature twitch slightly.

My droplets—what’s left of me—are flowing into the neighboring sacs via the fine stone threads that connect them.

The light of this creature’s neighbors—each other pulse-net nearby—brightens faintly, then dims in response.

The knowledge of me—or whatever I’ve been translated into—has been spread just like that.

---

The chamber is like a thought.

The stone walls are like a skull.

The pulse-net habitat—the glowing floating sacs—like neurons.

The water dripping through the ceiling, passing through each body, and flowing into the stone—like breath.

I’m not going to leave.

The idea of an outside world—if you can imagine one—is like a distant memory.

Even if such a world existed, I’m not sure I’d want to go there.

Here’s what’s happening from your perspective.

A droplet of water falls from the ceiling, passes through one suspended creature.

The sac’s light brightens faintly for a moment.

The water is gone.

The creature’s pattern—the faint branching of light inside its fluid—has rearranged.

The next droplet falls somewhere else.

The cycle of heat and water and flow remains.

From the inside of one creature—now embedded in the pattern of another—I’m watching you.

If you ever come here and step into a chamber like this and sense the pressure of the air against your skin and smell the faint heat of stone, like someone else’s fever—

If you watch a glowing bladder sac hanging from the ceiling, and for a heartbeat you see a flicker of two patterns at once—

That’s me.

The water of my body and your memory of the girl you once were—or might have been—has been rewritten.

The chamber of stone and light and wet heat and strange creatures who live alone—

The garden of nothing green—

That’s my body now.

Each of them is a new creature.

The desire of you to keep going.

The way the heat rises.

The way the water flows.

The way you can’t shake the feeling that you’re being watched—

Yes.

You’re inside.

Now you see what I’ve become.

The Pulse of the Chamber.

The girl in the swelling.

And I’m everywhere.
