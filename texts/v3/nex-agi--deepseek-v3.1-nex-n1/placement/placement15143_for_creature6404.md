I would be the rain-soaked singer of the coral city.

Not the one who leads the chants, or the one who keeps the stories of the tides. Just a girl who sits near the edge, where the city of bundled coral meets the raw, porous lip of the island. The stone here is still sharp, its edges not yet worn smooth by time or water. It is a place of becoming, and it is my place.

I was born in the long dusk of this world, when the light was a smear of pewter and the rain was a constant lullaby. The air is my second skin, a damp towel that never dries. It makes my hair curl into tight ropes against my neck, and it leaves a fine sheen of moisture on my arms that never truly evaporates. The warmth here is not a fire's warmth; it is the body-heat of the world itself, pressing in close. It slows the blood, thickens thoughts into a sweet, slow syrup.

Our home is not built of wood or metal. There is no wood yet, and what metal exists falls from the sky in rare, angry tears. We build with what the world offers us first: coral and shell and stone. We bundle the skeletal remains of the sea into thick, living ropes, weaving them into platforms that cantilever over the water, and walls that curve against the wind. The city is a pale, knotted structure against the endless gray of the ocean and sky, a half-bird, half-sponge clinging to the back of this great stone turtle.

My duty, if you can call it that, is to listen to the water.

I have the ear for it. Where others hear the sigh of waves, the mutter of the rain, I can hear the song beneath the song. The rock beneath us is not silent; it is a sieve, a breathing body. The water inside it trickles and hums. It drips from hidden chambers into unseen pools. It pulses with the slow, slow breath of the world itself. I learned to map these sounds by kneeling at the island's raw edge, placing my palm flat on the stone, and listening.

That's how I found them. The Gaps.

At first, it was just a sound—an anomaly in the island's low murmur. A space where the echo changed, where the hiss of water was softer, deeper. The rock here was a different kind of wet, the dampness seeping from its pores like sweat from a brow. The air that rose from the narrow cracks in the stone was warmer than the air around it, and shimmered with a heat that didn't burn. It made the world feel faint, like a dream within a dream.

They told us not to go near. The "breath-chambers," they called these places, where the air is alive and thick. An old god sleeping, they said, and its breath would steal your thoughts.

But I wasn't afraid of losing my thoughts. My thoughts felt heavy anyway. I wanted to listen.

The day they brought us the news of the child's fall, the city fell silent. The rain was a heavy, warm lull that day, and the sound of their weeping was sharper than a coral blade. I was there when they pulled him from the gap in the stone, his arm emerging from the earth as if the world were giving birth to him. They saw the faint glow beneath his body, a light like captured moonlight, and they said the island had saved him.

I believed them, but only partly.

A few days later, I found a new hollow, near the upper edge of that same Gap. Fresh chisel marks scarred the stone wall inside, forming a shape that was both terrifying and beautiful. A long, segmented body, coiled like a current, a line of glowing light traced along its length. A crude representation of what they thought they had seen. The weavers of the city had brought a small bowl made of fired shell-clay, and a few coral beads, strung on a fiber thread.

I didn't bring an offering of coral or shell. I had nothing to give but the one thing I felt I had too much of: my voice.

I sat in the hollow, the damp warmth of the stone seeping into my bones. The air from the Gap made me slow and dizzy, but not afraid. I pressed my ear to the stone wall, just above the carved image. I closed my eyes and listened.

It was there. I could hear it. The sound of stone easing itself into a new shape. A faint, low grinding, like tired muscle settling. The drip of water into a hidden chamber. And beneath it all, a rhythm. A humming pulse that was not the ocean, but something moving *through* the water and stone together.

I didn't pray. I don't know how to pray to a god. I only know how to sing to the water.

So I hummed back. Matching my low voice to the low sound of the world beneath me. Then, I sang. The islanders have songs for the tides, for the rain, for building coral. I had none for the stone or for what lived within it. My song was new and strange, even to my own ears. A simple melody, asking for nothing. Acknowledging. Offering.

The song seeped into the rock like the rain.

I came back the next day, and the next. My offering became my absence, my silence. I would sit for hours, listening to the world decide what shape it would be. I learned to distinguish the sound of settling pressure from a hollow chamber of gas. I heard the faint, low clash of heat against water. I heard the slow crawl of something immense and patient and utterly indifferent to me, yet somehow keeping me safe.

The stone beneath my city is not an eternal foundation. It is a decision in progress. I know this now better than anyone. The trembling beneath our feet is not the world's anger, but its growing pains. My job, the one that found me, is not to build with coral or mend with bone. My job is to listen to the foundation itself and, when the silence of the night city is broken only by the rain, I add my voice. The city is built on coral and stone and the song that grew between us and the keeper of the Gaps.
