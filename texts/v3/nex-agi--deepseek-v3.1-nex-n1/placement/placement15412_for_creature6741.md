I am not a stone, but I might as well be one.

I find myself on the flank of a mound-reef known only as the Third Ridge of the North Cluster. My name, if one can call it that, is **Sehl-Korv**.

The air is a thick, warm blanket, heavy with the drone of wings. The sound of this world is not a noise you hear with your ears alone; it vibrates through your bones, a constant pressure. The sky is a familiar dome of gray, pale light filtering through a cloud cover that never quite breaks. The sunlight is a memory of something that might have been bright once, but we wouldn't know what to do with it if we saw it.

My body is low to the ground, built like the mounds I inhabit. My limbs are thick pillars, my center of gravity low and unshakeable. I am not built for speed or grace but for endurance and weight. When I settle on the chalky surface of the reef, I feel the insects of the builder caste flow over my skin like a river of living oil. They don't fear me. They don't even acknowledge me. I am just another feature of the landscape.

My skin is a record of this reef. My back is a map of its chambers, a pattern of raised, pale-white markings that mimic the secretions of the mound-makers themselves. These reef-marks were not made by tools or artistry but by time. I spent my childhood pressed against the warm living walls of this mound, feeling the life of the colony hum through the stone. I learned to listen before I learned to speak, and the reef wrote its story on my flesh.

I am a Reef-Tender, my ground-name echoing within the ancient chambers of this great mound for my entire layer-age.

My day does not begin with a sun rising or setting. It begins with a shift in the air pressure, a subtle change in the texture of the drone around us that signifies the transition of one cycle to the next. This morning, the sound feels different.

I rise from my resting-place, a hollow carved by time and my own body, and move to the edge of the vegetation mat. I palm the ground, my broad hand spreading flat against the reef. The surface feels warm and solid, but there is a faint, uneven tremor beneath the palm. I shift my position and listen again.

Deep within the mound, a chamber is shifting. The insects would feel it as their world moving, but I feel it as a pressure in my bones, a discordant note in the familiar symphony of the reef.

I call softly through ground-speech, my chest humming low tones that sink into the stone. My message is not a word in the way you would understand it but a pattern, a query of pressure and tone.

A response vibrates back from the other side of the ridge. **Nime-Glimn**, another Tender of my layer-age. She has felt it too. The pattern of the message is clear: *Weakness. The western lower gallery.*

We don't rush. We move with slow, deliberate steps across the flank of the reef, our bodies heavy. Our bodies are not built for rushing. The problem has been years in the making; a solution will not be found in seconds. We converge near the western edge, where the vegetation grows thick and roots snake down into the mound's flesh.

Without speaking, we form a circle, our knees and feet flat against the ground. We close our eyes and let our senses become one with the mound. We listen to the world not through our ears, but through our skin.

Deep beneath us, we feel the familiar, busy work of the sifters churning the earth. But there’s a gap. A vacuum where the pressure isn't right. The tree-crawlers—the small, warm-blooded climbers who share our world—have been busy. Their burrows intersect the old chambers of the builder caste, and the walls of their tunnels are not reinforced with hard secretions. They are weak points.

The problem is not the tree-crawlers themselves. They are part of the balance of the reef. The problem is the imbalance. The fungi, which the sifters and gleaners normally keep in check, have found a rich food source in the damp, unprotected earth of the burrows.

I feel Nime-Glimn's body shift next to me. She places her hand directly on the rock, a ritual of connection and focus. I feel her message through the stone: *Guide.*

I understand. We are not here to fix, but to guide. The mound-makers will fix themselves.

I press my reef-marked back against the wall of the mound. The skin is tacky in the humidity. A pattern of subtle glands, refined over countless years, secrete a faint trace of chemicals into the air—not enough to be noticed by anything but the mound-makers themselves.

It is not a command. It is more of a signal. A red flag. It calls the attention of the builder insects to the weakness below. The ground vibrates with the movement of thousands of tiny feet as they shift their work.

I will sit here for many hours, maybe days. The movement of the builders will be slow and deliberate, but I will feel it. The sifters will begin to reinforce the walls with new soil and chitin. The gleaners will sweep the fungal blooms from the roots that penetrate the area.

My role is simply to listen. To ensure the message is received, and to wait.

This is my world. I am not its builder, or its master. I am not even its guardian. I am its listener. I am its stone that breathes, and through the slow, patient rhythm of my breath, I help the world to sing its old song.
