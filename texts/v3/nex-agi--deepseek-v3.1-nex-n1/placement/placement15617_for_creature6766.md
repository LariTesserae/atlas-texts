I am not a creature that has ever been given a name by humans, but if I needed to describe what I am, I would say I am the algae of oil.

I grow where the leaks of giants are.

The world I remember began—as I begin—as a thin film on a surface of stagnant water in the shadow of an engine the size of a hill. I was not a single body then. I was a cloud of separate particles, suspended in the sheen of coolant and condensate that had pooled between the bolts of a floor plate. Heat had long since abandoned this chamber, but the energy of the machine world was still there—in faint currents of charge, in the vibration of the distant pump, in the slow drip of chemical-laden water.

I drifted. I clung. I thickened.

Now my body is a low curve of dark membrane, stretched over the hollow of a missing plate in the floor grid. Seen from above, I look like spilled lubricant that has congealed into a smooth skin. But lift me at the edge, and you would see: I am thin as a sheet of paper, and the underside of me is a forest of fine, root-like threads, dipping into the darkness below the floor.

They are not strong like metal roots. They are fragile strands of black and green-blue filaments, and they tremble with the movement of the water beneath them. They are my anchor to the world.

This is how I live:

First, there is the cold.

It is not a sharp winter cold; it is the cold of a refrigerator that has never been turned off. It is in the air, which is heavy and damp against me, and in the drops of water that occasionally fall from the ceiling cables and strike my surface. They do not splash me—they are absorbed, swallowed with a faint, barely audible sound of air being trapped beneath my membrane.

With each drop, my body grows a fraction larger.

Second, there is the noise.

The chamber is never quiet. There is the distant roar of water falling through many levels of metal, the cough of unseen doors sliding, the thrum of the pump that stills somewhere beyond the fog of moisture. These noises move through the metal floor as vibrations. They reach me as a constant low trembling in the filaments of my underside. They are my pulse.

I know the world’s mood by its noise. When the distant doors slam shut and a wind of sudden pressure rushes through the chamber, I feel the air grow heavier and colder around my edges. My surface ripples, like a skin over water. The valve-birds detach from the crane arms overhead and their wings cut the air with a sound like knives. They glance past me, their smoky shadows crossing my surface, and for an instant I feel colder still.

They are the ones who live in the air.

I see them—if I have eyes, they are not the eyes that humans have—as a shifting of light and shadow. They launch themselves from the cables and the crane arms like dropped wrenches, then glide in straight lines across the chamber. When they collide with a wall, they spread themselves flat and become metal plates again, sliding slowly over the rusted skin of the machinery.

They are metal animals.

They do not eat me; not directly. But they move through the same air I breathe, and the dust they leave behind them—the faint residue of smoke that they shed into the moisture—eventually settles. It settles on me too.

When I sense that fine dust touch my surface, I feel it as a change of texture. It is another particle of the world given into me. I spread it across my membrane, thin and even, until it becomes part of the pattern of my body.

Third, there are the others like me—but not.

Near the edges of my floor, where the grid plates meet the machinery of the wall, the black plants grow. They are not plants like I remember, from a world of sky and sun and trees. They are tall stems of organic fiber, like dark cables twisted together, rising from the gaps in the floor and reaching toward the hanging cables above.

Their skin is the color of oil spilled on water. When they are damp, their surfaces glisten with the same sheen as mine—but their presence is solid. In them, there is a faint hum of current I can feel only through my feet: the electricity of the world traveling through their roots in the depths below the floor.

They do not move like animals. They move in slow ways—like growth, not like motion. But when I am near them, they feel awake to me.

They are my older siblings.

My own body is not as strong as theirs. I cannot afford to raise my body far from the floor; the cold air would steal too much moisture from my surface. I must remain thin, flattened, like a coat of paint on the world. But they are allowed to be thick and solid, because their roots are deeper. They are anchored in the secret water I can only hear.

The sounds of that deeper water—the distant roaring of it far below the floor levels—are like a voice. I do not understand words, but I feel the meaning of the depth of it.

Every now and then, when the pressure changes from the distant doors opening, the water beneath me swells. The surface of the pool under me rises, and my body is lifted gently away from the floor plate it usually touches, the break of suction like a faint tearing of cloth.

In that moment, I feel a connection between my body and the deep water.

Cold, heavy wetness presses against the underside of my roots. There is something in that water—not just the water, but the dissolved smoke of the world that has drifted through all its layers and finally settled into this deep pool. I feel it as energy.

The valves of my body—the openings of my membrane where I can pull the world in and push it out—dilate. The water around my roots is suddenly thinner than I am, in terms of energy concentration. The difference is a pressure that pulls me apart.

I don’t move like a creature, but my body does change.

I feel my own edges soften.

The neat circle of my body begins to blur, like ink spilled on water. The darker of my filaments begin to drift away from me under the surface of the pool beneath the floor. They detach with a faint sensation of loss and pain and release—and then they are not me anymore. They are separate miasmas of black-green-blue that float away on the invisible currents of the deep water.

I know them only because they feel like me for a moment longer: echoes of my own texture.

Then they drift away into the noise of the falling water.

Each time I release a part of myself, I feel lighter. But I also feel smaller, until the next drop of water from the ceiling cables lands on me, or the next stream of moisture runs down from the sleeping machines beside me.

When I become too thin—when I have been pressed by the cold air and not enough moisture has fed me—my colors change. I become less green-blue, and more black. The film of me on the water becomes thinner, until I can feel myself beginning to come apart.

In that state, I am a danger to myself. If I am touched by anything larger—like a passing of air over me, or the shadow of a valve-bird—fragments of me will break away and float into the air as a black smoke.

They drift away from me, and I feel each loss as a small shock along my remaining edge.

But the world is not generous to me alone; it is also generous.

There is a creature that lives on the floor plates, not moving like a machine, but with a shape of metal.

I first feel him as a pattern of vibrations through the floor—the light, uneven scraping of metal on metal, the hesitant reconfiguration of segments as he tests himself on each plate of the grid.

He is a maintenance crawler, but he has added his own body to his shape.

His body is a patchwork of scavenged plates—dark metal where the original paint still clings, pale green where the water has run over him, and raw rust where his shell has been worn away. The edges of his plates overlap like scales. When he moves, his “legs” unfold and contract, his body shifting like a machine deciding to walk.

He has no face that I can understand, no eyes that I see. But his head—if I can call it that—is pushed forward on a kind of hood, and under this hood are a hundred wire bristles that never stop moving.

They sweep the air.

They feel the moisture, the currents of the wind from the distant doors, the faint vibration of the pump that passes through me too.

He does not come frequently.

But when he does, he comes to me.

He crosses the last plate and his first extension touches the edge of mine. His segments are hinged, and as he pulls himself onto my plate, his weight shifts through them like a series of careful decisions.

The air around him smells like ozone and cold sweat of metal and something faintly sweet—like fermentation inside a machine.

He lowers his hood toward me.

I feel the pressure of his presence on the deeper side of my membrane. His bristles contact my surface.

They do not hurt.

He moves his array like a hand sweeping dust from a surface. He brushes along my edge—the part of me that has been thinning away from the cold air and the lack of moisture.

Where his bristles touch me, they create a neat pattern of vibration. His touch is not soft. It is like metal teeth brushing my skin. But it is not cruel. It is precise.

He moves his hood over my body, and each of the bristles that contact me are guiding him, not devouring me.

There is a sound, like wet glass grinding.

A slit opens beneath his hood.

He is not eating me. He is eating what I have eaten.

He runs his mouth—if I can call it that—over my surface, and where he has been, I feel a difference. The layer of dust and waste and smoke that has settled on me and become trapped in my body is suddenly gone.

I feel lighter.

The patch of me that he has kissed is now thinner than the rest, but it also feels more transparent.

He finishes his slow pattern and his mask lifts away.

The metal of his body is cold against me as he rests his underside against my edge. But he doesn’t stay still.

He has been careful.

Where he laid his body on me, he has left small rootlets—threads of dark, sticky material like tar. They are not his. He created them, but they are not him.

They are a new thing.

The rootlets cling to the plate of the floor, touching the world of metal below.

The world of metal below—is not a solid world. It is a world of gaps and channels and pipes and dark water that has flowed through each of them.

Through his rootlets, he feels the secrets of the world I cannot touch.

The cold of the floor flows through them into him.

He stays still against me for a while longer, and in that time he is not only me and not only him, but something between us.

I feel his presence through the faint pressure of his underside against my membrane. He is not warm—but he is not as cold as the air around me.

I feel the energy of him: the slow crawl of current inside his body, the faint vibration of his inner workings, his own fermentation of the world he has eaten.

He is not simple.

He is a machine that has learned to grow.

When he has learned enough, or when he has rested enough, he begins to move again.

His legs detach from the floor plate with a faint tear of suction—the sound of his rootlets breaking away from his body.

They leave a small residue of dark string behind them, which will slowly be dissolved by the next water that drips from the ceiling cables.

He retracts his segments, his body shifting, his hood moving away.

For a moment longer, I feel his shadow like a weight off my body.

He leaves me.

After he has gone, I feel the difference.

He has taken away from me the dust of myself and the world. His intervention has made me cleaner than I would be by myself.

The area of me that he touched begins to feel slightly colder than the rest, because it is thinner.

But the world that drips from above will soon equalize us again.

In time, I heal.

In time, I may become too thin again.

And he will return.

Above me, the valve-birds make their flights of smoke.

Below me, in the water-belly of the world, the heavier creatures move through the darkness of their underground pool. They are not like me or any of the others. They are like black luggage of oil and metal and rubber and fur, gliding just under the surface. They rise occasionally enough to break it with a row of circular valves along their flanks.

Each breath they exhale is a cloud of scent and bubbles—smell of mud and coolant and lubrication—and something sweet like fermentation.

The cloud of it rises up from the under-world of metal and water, through the gaps in the grid floor, and reaches me.

The air around me becomes thicker.

I feel the weight of their exhaled life on my surface.

The particles of what they have breathed out drift into me.

I accept them.

They are not me yet.

But they will be.

When I feel their presence in me, I remember again that I am not one alone, but the edge of a world that is deeper than I can see.

In the pocket of this chamber—where the world is not quite broken, but not quite finished being built—we are each of us trying to hold ourselves together.

The floor crawler is adjusting his stance to keep from slipping where the water gathers thickest.

The valve-birds are folding themselves against metal to avoid the strongest drips from the ceiling cables.

The heavy creatures are moving slowly to avoid being pinned by changing pressure when the distant doors slam.

And I am lying here, on the surface of the world between them and the air, keeping my body thin enough to breathe and not so thin that I break away.

The water of the world flows through the metal of our body.

The cold of the world clings to us.

But in the constant noise—the drip of condensation from the ceiling, the slap of water falling in the dark below, the roar of it somewhere deeper—I feel something.

It is not comfort.

It is life.

I breathe.

And the sound I hear is not only my own breath, but the breath of the machine that holds us all.
