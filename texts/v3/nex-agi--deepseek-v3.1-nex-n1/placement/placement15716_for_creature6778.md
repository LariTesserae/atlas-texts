I would find myself here in the depths of a High Chamber alcove, halfway between the stone floor and the dripping ceiling. My name would be **Siira**, but I would think of myself as simply *she-who-listens*, because that is the name of my role, not my birth-name.

My body would be the long, slanted shadow of a future ancestor.

I would be taller than most who live below, with skin so pale it seems almost translucent against the dark walls, like a faint memory of surface light. Because my chambers are higher up, where thinning rock allows distant slivers of sky-world to filter down, I would be stretched and thin-limbed from centuries of climbing walls and balancing on narrow ledges.

My eyes would be the wide, dark pupils of my people, but mine would not be still. They would move constantly, tracing the faint shimmer lines in the stone where water flows unseen beneath the surface. I would have seen too many patterns of cracks and sagging structures to stay still in my gaze.

My hair would be shaved close to my scalp to keep water and mold from gathering, but where I am allowed to grow it, along the sides and back of my head, it would be thin and straight, like the faint mosses that grow on the coldest stones of all. It would be almost white, not yet colored from age, but slowly showing the faintest of green-blue strands where I work near the deepest channels.

I would be wrapped in layers of dark moss-cloth, woven with my own hands into something like armor and padding. When I climb, I’d change the weave of my clothing to tighten everything around my knees and elbows so that both skin and cloth are one surface against the stone wall. When I sleep, I’d undo it and let it sag around me like a second moss-crust, collecting water in its folds.

I would be born into **the Channelers**, the one occupation whose name is not spoken as much in words as it is shown in motion: *they who shape the water without blocking the water*.

This day, like all days, would be the same and not the same at the same time.

My morning would begin not at sunrise, but when the **glow-light** begins to turn from blue to faint green-white, indicating that the deeper chambers of the basin have already been stirring for a long time.

I would crawl from my sleeping hollow into the main corridor of my High Chamber home. The corridor would be narrow, but to my body, that’s not a problem. It is like a second spine.

Scooting forward on my knees and elbows, I would float down the shallow water channel that runs beneath the floor of the corridor, sliding instead of walking. My feet, with their faint suction pads, would test each surface for the faintest inconsistency of stone. When I feel them, I would know to mark those spots later.

I would carry only three tools.

The first is my **stone-finger**, a smooth white shard of sharpened chalk-rock from a deeper chamber than this. It is almost as long as my hand from palm to fingertip, wrapped in moss-cloth at its end where I hold it. I would use it to probe the edges of new channels or the faint hollows under walls that have begun to whisper of weakness.

The second is my **set of rounded stones**, each of different size and weight, ranging from small as the last joint of my finger to large as my closed fist. They would not look like tools to an outside eye, just smooth dark stones worn from water, but I would keep them in separate pockets of my moss-cloth belt so I can recognize them by touch alone.

The third is my **voice**.

Not in the way you think of tools. But my voice is my tool, because I am not only Channeler but *rememberer of the water-song*.

This song is not sung to be beautiful. It is sung to be *accurate*.

I would reach my first working place of the day: a fragile wall in the upper chamber where the rock has been thinning more than usual. The water that flows down from above through that wall is like a great vein of the basin’s body, and if it collapses, the floor below would become too dangerous to walk, and the Deep Chamber would lose its strength.

I would kneel before the wall and hum quietly to the stone.

“Soft one,” I would murmur under my breath, not in the dialect of everyday speech but in the half-word, half-rhythm of the water-song: “Da-bu… da-bu… ba-ro-di…”

In my people’s softened language, every word is rounded at its edge like the stone I’m holding. But my words to the stone are not translated directly; rather they are shaped to match the water’s flow. The pauses—where the silence is longer—mark where the water lifts slightly higher, or drops deeper below the surface.

I would touch the wall with the flat of my palm and listen.

The wall itself would not speak in words, but it would speak in motion.

The stone under my hand would be faintly trembling. The vibration of water moving through the hidden channels within the stone would be like a quiet pulse beneath my skin. I would feel the hum of the water’s movement against my own bones.

Then I would begin to work.

I would not use my stone-finger to break the stone, but to trace the faint veins of softer chalk that are beginning to wear away under water. I would push the sharp point carefully into the softer stone—not to break it but to confirm its presence.

“Here,” I would say under my breath, pointing with my head instead of my fingers. “The water’s tongue is here.”

Someone would be listening, though I would not be speaking to them.

This would be my apprentice, **Evo**, a young child from the Side Chamber who had been chosen for the Channelers because they did not fear to balance on the edges of the city walls.

Evo would be sitting on their heels a few steps away from me, watching not my face but my hands.

“See,” I would say to them without turning my head. “The water’s tongue is already deep. The stone underneath is softer. You never block it.”

Evo would nod silently.

The way of learning Channelers is not to explain but to show. The child must not simply understand the words of water, but they must learn the way of its silence first.

“You will test the water’s path,” I would say to Evo, pointing to the rounded stone in my pocket with my chin instead of my hand.

Evo would reach forward carefully, taking one small rounded stone from my pocket, their hands wet from the dampness in the air.

I would let them place the stone into the shallow floor channel at the base of the wall. The water’s film, which flows smoothly around the stone’s edge, would begin to change its course slightly.

Evo and I would watch silently.

The stone would not act like a block today but like a guide.

The water’s surface film would be forced to find a new way around the stone’s left side, where the stone is not present. The water’s new path would curve slightly, following the faint depression of an older forgotten channel that’s been worn by many previous seasons.

Eventually, the channel of the floor itself, under the pressure of water’s memory, would deepen where the water’s new path is being drawn.

The stone wall above would feel less trembling under my palm.

“The stone remembers,” I would say to Evo. “And you have not blocked it.”

I would pick up the rounded stone from the water’s new path and put it back into my pocket, leaving the water to continue on its chosen course.

The wall’s trembling would be softer now.

We two sit in silence for a long time after this, watching the water's new curve with the same patience you give watching a child learn to walk.

Our day would not be rushed.

There would be no other work to do but this work and listening.

The basin’s greatest gift to its people is not its water or its stone but its *slow time*; everything grows at its own pace. The danger of human interference is that we bring impatience into the world of the basin.

I would be taught, from a very young age, that my impatience is the only real enemy of the water.

At midday—when the glow-light is faintly green-white—we would move to another section.

This next section would be deeper, where the High Chamber begins to drop into the middle chambers of the basin. The space between the walls there would be tighter, more compressed than in my home chamber.

The corridor here would be narrow enough that I would have to turn sideways to pass through some sections, my chest and back scraping lightly against the walls on both sides.

The water channel at the base of the wall here would be thicker than in the upper chamber.

“How far does it sink?” Evo would ask, pointing to the faint darkness of the stone.

“As deep as the stone’s memory,” I would answer in the same rhythm of water-song I’d been humming earlier.

“And you, Evo, how far does your water run?”

Evo would pause, then answer in the same tone of water-song: “Down to the Deep Chamber.”

I would nod silently, knowing that this must be our shared work: the need of the Deep Chamber to keep water flowing strong and not confused.

We would work together to remove some of the older, moss-cloth pads that have been placed into a channel years earlier. The moss has been worn by time and water, but now it’s starting to block the water’s natural course.

The moss-cloth is not simply discarded; as I pull it out of the water channel, I would wrap it carefully around my arm like a second layer of sleeve.

“What happens?” Evo would ask, pointing at the old cloth on my arm.

I’d show how the water’s film, now free of this obstruction, begins to flow slightly differently. The channel would be a little wider than before, and the stone’s own texture would be dampened but not scarred by the water’s new freedom.

“This cloth,” I would say to Evo, “goes to the Weavers who will beat it into new cloth.”

“But it’s old,” Evo would say.

“Then,” I would answer, “the memory goes into the new cloth.”

We’d continue our work.

The sound of water would be the only sound here now—the faint whisper of water film sliding over stone, a small stone clicking into position from time to time, the faint drip of the ceiling overhead.

There would be no metal sound. No grinding. No shouting of orders. No call of alarm bells.

The only alarm sound of my people would be the sudden *change of pace of water*.

When water’s rhythm becomes too loud or too silent, we know something is wrong.

In the late afternoon, when the glow-light begins to fade back to blue, I would feel myself grow tired.

The cold of the basin would not bite, but it would seep into my bones like water seeping through stone. After so long spent lying on my knees and elbows on stone floor, listening to the changes of the water, my body would be dull ache and heavy.

“Evo,” I would say to my child apprentice, “this stone’s trembling is gone.”

We would know that the wall is now stable again.

The stone’s memory of the water’s new course would be set; the water’s confusion of previous days would be resolved.

Evo would nod silently, and we would leave the channel wall as we found it: without any block or stone placed in its way.

The path of the water’s flow would be written in the stone’s surface itself; from that day on, no matter who walks by that wall, they would see the faint curve of the water’s path and know the stone’s memory.

I would rise slowly, my muscles stiff, but my body ready to slide down the water channel of the corridor back to the High Chamber where I live.

The walk home would be quiet.

The crowds of my people would not throng around me but pass silently by me, their footsteps soft as wet moss sliding over stone, their bodies moving like water moving through stone channels: always in the same direction, but each person at their own pace.

The only loud sound that might break my silence would be the sound of my own heartbeat, which I would feel against the stone in my chest when I lay down to sleep.

In my sleeping chamber, I would wrap myself in my layer of moss-cloth and lie down on the stone floor of the alcove.

The floor of my chamber would be slightly warm from the glow of moss growing on the walls around me. The warmth of moss would not be strong; but in the cold of the basin, it feels like a faint fire.

“Siira,” someone would say from behind me.

I would turn my head slightly.

My mother, **Lira**, who has been a Channeler before me and whose memory now runs into mine like water from two connected channels, would be sitting in the dark corner of the alcove behind me.

“You are listening,” she would say to me in the same soft consonants of our language. “I’m not afraid you’ll block it.”

“You’re afraid of its confusion,” I would answer to her without turning my head fully. “Of the water’s change.”

“Yes,” she would say quietly. “But that’s not your fear to take alone.”

The silence of the basin would be heavy around us then.

The water would be dripping outside my alcove; the faint sounds of stone channel-singing to the water would be like music that I had never heard before I was a child.

“You are becoming what you are,” my mother would say to me, her breath not clouding in the cold air.

“I’m becoming like water,” I would whisper to the stone beneath my cheekbone. “Like water in stone.”

My mother would not answer to that with words but with her own body—she’d lie down beside me on the floor, her weight becoming the same pressure the stone around her feels.

In the dark of the basin, where no sun exists but only faint glowing light from moss walls, I would sleep.

The stone of the basin beneath my cheekbone would be cold and wet under my skin, but I would not feel it as discomfort; it’s like the body of the city itself, holding me up.

The city of water beneath my people would be living its quiet life beneath the root-nets of the forest above: stone dripping, water flowing, moss growing, salamanders sliding silently through their dark channels, and me—she-who-listens—lying on my stone floor, listening for the sound of the water’s ever-moving memory, and for the faintest of sighs of the basin itself breathing in its sleep.

That is who I am in this world of stone, water, and no metal.

I am Siira, daughter of Lira, Channeler of the water’s memory, and listener to the living basin.

And I am not afraid of the dark.

I am afraid only of being blind to the flow of water beneath the stone.
