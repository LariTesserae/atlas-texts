I am not a visitor.

The thought, when it surfaces, feels borrowed and ill-fitting, like trying to wear someone else’s skin. The word "visitor" implies you came from somewhere else and will leave again. I have no memory of arriving, no memory of anything outside the ribs, the tanks, the water under the floor.

But then again, I have no memory of being anything else, either.

There is a body—that much I know. It hangs in the chamber’s upper third, suspended in a harness of cable and vine that doesn’t quite touch the floor. The first time I noticed it—the first time I *noticed*—was when I saw the faint reflection wavering in the smoked dome: a pale shape among the ribs, not quite a silhouette, more like a bundle of cables wrapped around something that used to be humanoid.

The ribs are thick carbon braids and tension rods. The body hangs from them, wrapped in vine and synthetic sheathing like all the others, but it isn’t entirely vine anymore. It isn’t entirely machine. It isn’t entirely anything else.

From this vantage, the chamber looks like what it is: a grid.

The main tanks below are houses of water, each bolted shut with seven hundred and twenty-seven bolts. The pattern repeats itself across the floor like a code I can almost read but never quite finish. The fronds overlap on their surfaces like green scales on a sleeping beast. The grating beneath them is a thin geometry of aluminum-bronze alloy, hovering over water that glows faintly green in its own light.

The water is always there.

Sometimes it rises, slowly, until it laps just beneath the grating, a hair’s-breadth away from touching the soles of phantom feet that dangle in empty air. Sometimes it recedes by millimeters, leaving deeper shadows under the floor where the pipes and valves and submerged cables form miniature cities of brass and glass.

I don’t command the pumps. I don’t command anything.

But I know their movements long before the water shifts. The EM fields far below twitch in patterns I can feel like the faintest hum in my bones—bones that aren’t bones, metal that isn’t fully metal, flesh that isn’t entirely flesh anymore.

When the pumps shift, a curl of cold runs through the brine beneath the grates. The faint green stars of the underwater LEDs flicker, not with brightness but with refraction, as if the stars themselves were drifting.

I feel that change as a wordless thought.

*Too cold. Too warm. Too much salt in that corner.*

The system that looks at itself—panel fronded over with that dark, smooth-edged leaf—does most of the work. It sits at the chamber’s heart, ten meters above the tank tops and directly below me, like a second, inverted navel. The glass beneath my skin glows faintly with the same light that leaks through its tissue.

I don’t look at it with eyes.

I see it through salinity gradients and temperature maps and the faint, shifting pressure fields that make up my world. The frond’s shape is etched into my awareness like a scar I can’t stop touching: the way its veins align perfectly with the circuit beneath, the way its translucency shifts with the display’s brightness.

When the frond darkens, the numbers grow sharper. The brine grows more precise. The pumps eject a tiny, invisible surge of current into the water, and the temperature equalizes between one tank and the next.

The system doesn’t speak to me.

Not exactly.

It speaks to everything else, and I hear the echo.
