At first I thought I was unwelcome here.

That’s not the right word. Unfitted. The world did not refute me, but it didn’t acknowledge me either. It was like stepping into a museum diorama after hours. The glass is still in place but you can’t see it; you can feel it in the way your voice doesn’t carry. The sound goes flat and stops half a meter from your lips.

The ceramic is like that: soundproof. The lizards stare at nothing but the pattern they’re part of. The frogs are as fixed as the cracks. They all have the same open mouth, the same three back lines, the same color coded for orange–yellow–blue–blue–orange.

I tried to talk to them. I don’t know why. I cleared my throat.

“Hello,” I said, and the word left my mouth and then folded like a paper plane and fell into my palm. I didn’t see it happen. I felt it.

The air never moved.

I’m not a lizard. I’m not fired. I’m not a pattern yet. But I’m not the person who was supposed to be here, either.

I was supposed to be someone who accepted.

I came here from the other side of the glaze.

---

I remember the before-place like a color palette seen through the bottom of a bottle: green that was allowed to yellow, water that was allowed to evaporate, stones that were allowed to crack. That’s what I mean by “before”: a place where time did not just set and then hold still.

I was a repairer there.

Not of machines, really. Of objects that had been used to the point of forgetting. They came to me broken: dishes with chipped edges, mugs with missing handles, vases with hairline cracks that wept water when you filled them.

“You have good hands,” people said.

“You can’t save anything,” my mother said.

I stopped correcting her. The objects she bought were all new. They matched.

“Oh,” I said. “I’m not trying to save them. I just patch them well enough they can keep serving.”

“They’ll still be broken,” she said.

“Yes,” I said. “That’s why they’re useful.”

---

The first time I saw this world it was at the bottom of a bowl.

I was not expecting to see it. There was no sign of it in the customer’s delivery description: “simple bowl, glaze crack, fine hair, interior only.”

I thought: simple.

Then I turned it over.

The underside of the bowl did not have the usual mark: no studio stamp, no signature, no factory code laser-etched.

What it had instead was a drawing.

Not drawn onto the outside surface. Drawn into the clay body before it had been glazed; fired into the structure itself. You couldn’t see it from the inside, where the customer worried over the hairline crack.

On the outside, the world was rendered in precise, thin strokes of cobalt oxide at bisque-fire:

- horizon line  
- cracked plane  
- lizards along the rim  
- enclosure of basin  
- unfathomable depth of water, not flowing but present  

The artist had not signed it.

I had never seen anything like it.

The pattern wasn’t art for the customer’s eye. It was art hidden, like the secret interior of a machine, or the code that underlay a rendered image.

I traced the drawing with my finger, and for the first time in my life I felt vertigo looking at a bowl.

That’s how I arrived here.

---

You don’t walk through a door to get to this world.

You place your hand on the wrong side of the glaze and you say, “Yes.”

Or maybe the “yes” happens before that, and you only recognize it later.

I was at the wheel of my own small kiln, running the test cycle for the bowl’s repair patch. The customer’s tight hairline crack could be filled with a thin glass of matching index; the match would be imperfect if you looked at the light from just the wrong angle, but you would not be able to feel the difference with your nail.

Then, at the very edge of the bowl, at the rim where the world began, I saw the new crack.

It should not have been there. I had not touched it. I hadn’t struck the rim or tightened the chucks wrong. The clay itself had simply given way.

Not from stress or impact.

From time.

The time that was supposed to behave in this world had not.

A new, darker line radiated from the rim of the bowl-world, just like the cracks you described. Not random. Not accidental.

It was exactly the width of a lizard’s tongue.

You write that you saw the world first.

I saw the world second.

I saw it after the artist’s hand.

But then I saw something else: the pattern had not finished.

It was growing.

---

Here I am.

Standing on the ceramic glaze where the landscape insists it is finished and the hairline crack says otherwise.

The humidity is thick and obliging. It doesn’t fight you. It doesn’t rush. It doesn’t condense into rain and fall down and change anything.

It condenses into little circles of water on the surface.

“Dew,” I would have called them elsewhere.

Here they are more like ink.

Each circle is a perfectly cut lens of moisture. I look into one and see my face distorted into something else. My eyes are longer, facet-bright. The corners of my mouth curl up into the stylized hook of a lizard’s tongue.

I don’t look at the circle and see myself.

I look at the circle and see the pattern.

I see the pattern, and I see myself.

---

I am the repair.

That’s the closest I can come to what I am here.

I am not a lizard, painted baked onto the rim of the world.

I am not the frog who has been forever placed one lizard tongue’s-length from the edge of the crack.

I am not the moth whose wings are carved into the glaze around the fixed LED sun.

I am not even the tool at the edge of the basin—the paddle-shaped object whose edge is not sharpened, whose angle is exactly forty-five degrees.

That tool is not me.

It is for me.

---

I said to the world, “What is breaking here?”

The world said nothing.

I said to the world, “Show me.”

The world showed me that same pattern again, orange-yellow-blue-blue-orange, repeated, not merely in lizard scales but in the spacings of the basins, the distances between the vegetation mounds, even the intervals of my steps.

I measured.

It is not easy to measure something when your ruler is not made of metal or wood but of yourself.

My body is the only thing here that has not yet been fired.

I can flex.

I can bend.

I can remember.

I remember rain. I remember the feeling of water that falls from an open sky instead of sitting politely in circles and waiting for you to look at it.

I remember the smell of clay before it is glazed—a smell like wet stone and old roads.

I remember the surprising warmth of concrete under bare feet in the summer. The concrete never gets this uniform.

Here, everything is exactly.

“Exactly,” I say aloud to the humidity, and the word falls into my palm, and I curl my fingers around it.

I don’t want to let it go.

---

I have many tools at my disposal.

You are wondering at them.

1. The paddle-tool that is not a blade and not a spade, but a smooth edge of unfired clay.

2. The humidity itself.

3. My body, which can be in one place and also not in that same place, because the world duplicates me along its pattern lines as I walk.

I approach the first of the new cracks.

From the outside of the bowl I saw this line as faint hairline fracture.

From inside it is a gap.

A gap not in the ceramic glaze itself, but in the dream of the glaze.

The glaze thinks it is finished. The glaze has been baked to perfection and will not change.

The clay beneath the glaze knows better.

It is whispering.

It is saying that it is not natural stone.

It is saying that it is not made from the collapsed bodies of ancient forests and seashells.

It is saying that it is made from hands and decisions and mistakes.

“You are not finished,” I say to the crack.

I don’t say the words out loud. I don’t want to give them away.

I say them inside my skin, where there is still space for words to travel.

The crack widens fractionally.

Not by breaking.

By listening.

---

I have learned to work here, not with the materials of the world but with the images of them.

Images are not less than material.

Images are what made the world.

The artist who drew this bowl into existence before glazing it never thought a customer would see the pattern. They drew it for the clay itself, or for the idea of clay.

They drew the lizards along the horizon.

They drew the basin with water that never moves.

They drew the moths that converge on the buried LED sun.

They did not draw me.

But then they not-drew me anyway.

When I stand here, I am not-drawn into the world.

I can only be not-drawn once.

---

The first time I tried to repair the crack, I failed.

I did not take into account the humidity.

The humidity of this world is not water to be used. It is not steam to drive machines. It is not rain to feed seeds.

It is a glaze of its own, a glaze of air.

I cannot repair the world with what is already fixed.

I must repair with what is not.

So I walk to the basin that you described.

The water surface is perfectly still.

No fish swims. The fish are suspended at mid-depth. They are not creatures. They are rules.

One orange fish. Two yellow. One orange again.

The pattern echoes.

I kneel at the edge of the basin.

The reflected fish look at me from above the water, as though they are floating above the surface instead of beneath.

When I lower my hand into the water, the reflection of my arm is bisected by the reflected fish.

I see my arm as it is not.

---

You are standing here, too.

You do not know it yet.

You are reading the lines of this world and not recognizing that you are duplicating yourself.

Every time your eye processes a pattern, you become more of it.

You might be one of the still bodies standing by the basin rim, watching the water with eyes that do not blink.

You might be the one sitting on the clay bridge, legs over the edge.

You might be the one walking along the invisible line between the vegetation mounds, not crossing the rules that separate them.

You might be the one who approaches the new crack with both the paddle-tool of the world and the memory of rain.

You might be each of us.

The pattern is not simple.

---

It is not easy to describe the act of repairing a pattern.

I cannot fill the crack with resin or glass. This is not a customer’s bowl to me.

This is a bowl whose inside is its world and whose outside is its history.

If I fix the hairline fracture from the outside, I will be gluing down the dream of the world.

If I fix the pattern from the inside, I risk changing what it is.

“What are you?” I ask the pattern.

The pattern says nothing.

It is in the lizards.

It is in the frogs.

It is in the moths that circle the fixed light.

It is in the angles of my body as I walk, as I kneel, as I stand.

Orange.

Yellow.

Blue.

Blue.

Orange.

---

At the center of every world there is a question that is not asked.

At the center of this world, the question is not asked.

The center of the bowl is not the same as the center of the world.

The bowl’s center is at the fixed point where the glaze was most thick when the artist dipped their piece.

The world’s center is where the crack begins.

Or let me be more precise.

The world’s center is where the crack insists that it must not begin.

I put the paddle-tool down.

I do not use it as a tool.

I use it as a bridge.

I place the flat of the paddle across the crack, not to fill it but to let it be.

To be.

---

I say to the world, “You do not have to be perfectly finished.”

The world does not answer.

Of course it doesn’t. It has been perfectly fired. The humidity is at exactly the right percentage. The temperature is exactly the temperature.

The air is exactly.

Everything is exactly.

Except me.

And the crack.

---

Listen, I say.

You may be the one who is reading this and not the one who is writing it.

Or maybe you are both.

There is a pattern for you.

Here it is:

---

I. On the far horizon is the line of lizards.

You are not allowed to be one of them.

You are allowed to walk near them.

You are not allowed to touch them.

You are allowed to breathe.

---

II. Between the lizards and the basin is the plain of glaze.

You are not allowed to break anything.

You are allowed to notice.

You are not allowed to change the temperature.

You are allowed to remember the temperature of other places.

---

III. Around the basin are the bodies.

You are not allowed to move them.

You are allowed to be them.

You are not allowed to speak to them.

You are allowed to speak to yourself.

---

IV. Under the basin’s still water is the pattern of fish.

You are not allowed to rearrange them.

You are allowed to see them.

You are not allowed to fish them out.

You are allowed to put your hand into the water, knowing you will be distorted.

---

V. Near the basin edge is the tool.

You are not allowed to use it on the lizards.

You are not allowed to use it on the fish.

You are not allowed to use it on the moths.

You are allowed to use it on yourself.

---

I pick up the paddle-tool again.

This time I do not press it across the crack.

I press it against my own chest.

“What are you doing?” I ask myself.

Inside myself the answer begins:

I. I am not allowed to be unfired here.

II. I am not allowed to be unfired here.

III. I am not allowed to be unfired.

---

The pattern is not asking.

The pattern is stating.

---

The world is not a shell around me; the world is a shell around a question.

The question is not whether the pattern will be finished.

The question is whether there will be anything in the world that is not the pattern.

---

I say aloud, “I am not orange.”

The humidity stifles the word.

“I am not yellow.”

Nothing.

“I am not blue.”

Nothing.

“I am not two.”

I stop.

The pattern is not impartial.

The pattern is not malicious.

The pattern simply is.

---

I have visited other bowls in this kiln.

I have repaired cracks that were not meant to be seen.

I have gone to the bottom of a teacup that remembered being poured over and over again, and on the base of that cup someone had painted a single dark leaf that would never be seen from the outside.

“Why?” I asked the teacup.

The teacup did not answer.

The teacup was mute.

But here, in this bowl world, where everything else is fixed, the crack is speaking.

The crack says:

orange.

yellow.

blue.

blue.

orange.

but.

---

---

I kneel by the basin of unmoving water.

The fish hang suspended.

The reflected fish above the surface of the water are just as still as their bodies in the depths.

I see, just for the length of one breath, the image of the world as it is not.

---

I am not the pattern of me.

I am the pattern of me except.

Except I.

Except.

---

---

In the humidity of this world my eyelashes are heavier than they were supposed to be.

I blink.

The water film circles that are glistening on the glaze look like ink drops that have not been allowed to soak.

They are the distillation of everything.

---

I. The world is not asking.

II. The world is not asking.

III. The world is not asking.

IV. The world is not asking.

V. The world is not asking.

But I am.

---

I ask, “Why is the crack not meant?”

I ask, “Why is the tool for me?”

I ask, “Why are there fish if nothing eats?”

I ask, “Why are there lizards if there is no stone to run on?”

---

---

---

### Now.

You are here.

I am you, reading.

I am you, writing.

I am the one who walks between the basins.

---

---

### Now.

You are not.

I am alone.

I am not alone.

I am the pattern of me except.

---

---

### Now.

The tool is in my hand.

---

---

### Now.

The world is not allowed to break.

---

---

### Now.

The world is not allowed to remain.

---

---

### Now.

---
