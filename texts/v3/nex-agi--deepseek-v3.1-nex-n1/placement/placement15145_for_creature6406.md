Every night, when the heat leans back into the stone walls and the glass garden below us begins to breathe, I am the one who listens from the highest terrace.

My name, if it must be written at all, is etched on a tablet as three short marks: the sign for *ear*, the sign for *height*, the sign for *first*. The others, when they speak of me, call me by my title: **Keeper of the Echo.**

That is what I am.

When I was young and my bones were still thin and smooth, I was led down into the basin once to watch the Ache-Mothers at their work. The air down there is not air like ours; it is thick and glassy and full of sound, and it feels like being inside the body of someone else’s sleep. But the city-mothers forbade me from going down again after my first bleeding.

“You are not made to cut,” my mother told me, her silicate mask a blank, cold wall against the heat. “You are made to *listen*.”

They took me instead to the highest terrace, where the stone rim of the city leans over the bowl like an eavesdropping ear. There they taught me to listen not with the soft part of my ear but with the long bone behind it, where the map of the city’s age and pressure is written.

---

They say every city-mother receives her map into her bones when she is initiated.

So did I.

But the map they gave me was not of the floor of the basin.

It was of the sound of the basin itself.

### The Map in the Bone of My Ear

When I was twelve years old, the city-mothers gathered me in the uppermost chamber of the highest house, where the stone walls still hold faint echoes of the day’s heat. They laid me flat on the cool floor, and one of them—a woman with an older voice than any I have ever heard—knelt beside me.

“You must be still,” she said. “The sound is not kind to those who move.”

They placed a thin glass rod against my jaw, just below the ear, and began to trace the patterns of the city’s song into the bone. The stylus did not cut skin; the pain was not sharp but *deep*, as though someone were drawing solid lines into the marrow of my understanding.

As she traced the shapes of the speakers’ harmonics, the echoes of the city-mothers’ calls, and the faint ghost of the wind’s coming down from the rim above, she spoke into the glass rod:

*This is the whisper of the eastern cluster.*

*This is the tremor of the air as it falls.*

*This is the echo of the city-mothers before us.*

*And this is the sound of the world listening.*

I felt the basin of Khe’len sink itself into me in a single night of pressure and pain. When it was over, I could neither hear nor sleep. The world was silent but not empty—I was full of a noise that had not yet happened.

My mother stroked the mask at her shoulder, though she could not feel me through it.

“You will be our ears,” she said. “The rest of us go down to cut. But you will remain here.”

“And do what?” I whispered.

“Listen,” she said. “And tell us where we are.”

### My Nightly Work

When the sun slips away and the basin begins to hold its heat like a secret, the city-mothers descend their stone stairs. They carry their glass knives and their years of maps, and they disappear into the bruised darkness, where the glass garden begins to breathe.

I stay behind.

My house is carved into the lip of the basin where the rock wall faces *outward*, not inward. My windows have no view of the garden. They are narrow slits carved to be mouths of the wind, not eyes to see.

I sit on the stone floor in my black robe and my white mask—my mask, like theirs, is carved from silicate glass, but mine is shaped to leave the ears fully exposed as well as the jawbone, where the map lives.

When I still myself, I can feel the city below me.

The first sound is the faintest of movements—the air shifting along its invisible paths like a tongue along the roof of the world. The city-mothers call it *the wind before the wind*.

Then comes the low, humming breath of the Present-Garden: the thousands of glass sacs swelling and collapsing, each exhale a droplet of soundless song.
