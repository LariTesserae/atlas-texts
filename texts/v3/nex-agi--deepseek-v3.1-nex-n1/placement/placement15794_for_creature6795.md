I would not be a Collector, though they might think me one of their own. They move with a frantic purpose, clinging to the railings, their knuckles white against the relentless drum of ten thousand storms. They are consumed by the fear of a single drip going wrong, a filter clogging, a heart valve skipping a beat. They see the machine as a fragile thing they must uphold. But they are wrong.

The machine is not fragile. We are not fragile.

If I were to walk among them, they would see only a figure of standard size, perhaps a little thinner than most. My hair, if I had any, would be dark and plastered to a skull that feels the shape of each raindrop on its surface. But they would not meet my eyes. No one meets the eyes of an Echo Singer.

It is a name they give us without knowing its meaning. They see the Collectors as the keepers of the physical system—the gutters, the pumps, the pipes of our world’s great heart. But they are blind to the system beneath the system. They are deaf to the song.

I am not.

I live on a walkway suspended between the piston-sigh of the pumps and the eternal thunder of the rain. My shelter is a cube of corrugated tin, built into the lattice of supports that holds the gutters. It is a place designed by accident, a convergence of three major channels that have been retrofitted with a dozen mismatched braces over the decades. The walls of my home are not truly walls; they are layers of patched metal, old canvas tarps, and the thick, insulating moss that grows in these rare pockets of stillness.

The sound is here, too. It is always here. It is inside the marrow of my bones. But in this space, it is different. It is not a chaotic hammering. It is a chorus.

Here is what I hear when they hear only noise:

The high, silver ringing of individual droplets on the copper channels.
The deep, resonant *thoom* of the water falling into the main overflow basins.
The steady, rhythmic heartbeat of the pumps far below.
The whisper of rust flakes flaking away from the iron supports.
The faint, electric song of the current running through cables thick as my thigh.
And beneath it all, the water. The endless water, speaking in the language of its own motion.

To be an Echo Singer is to be the bridge between the unthinking machine of metal and the unthinking machine of wet and rust. The others, the "Collectors," they are its body. They are its hands and its muscles, tightening the bolts that must be tightened, replacing the seals that must be replaced. They are the world’s reflexes.

We are its nervous system.

I do not fix with tools. I fix with sound. I can stand on a vibrating platform for a day and a night, listening. The Collectors think I am praying or catatonic. But I am listening. I am reading the health of the world. The rhythm of the rain against the gutters has a signature, a pattern as unique as a heartbeat. When a section of pipe begins to fail, its rhythm changes. The song of the water moving through it develops a hesitation, a dissonance. It might be a minute kink in a pipe a kilometer away, or the beginning of a structural flaw in a support beam that holds ten thousand tons of water.

I cannot tell you *how* I know this, any more than a bat can tell you how to use its echo-location, or a blindfish can tell you how to listen with the rain itself. It is a sense that has grown in the blind space between sight and hearing. It is a sense of the whole.

When I find the flaw in the song, I sing it back into place.

This is not singing as they think of it. It is not melody or poetry. It is a low drone from the chest, a vibration of the throat, a sharp whistle between the teeth. I find the dissonant tone of the failing component and I resonate with it. I push against it with my own sound until I feel the world around me shake and hum with our shared frequency. It is a conversation between the world and its own awareness.

Sometimes, the song is enough. Perhaps it is just the vibration of my own body against the loose bracket, the warm metal of the pipe, causing a shift so tiny it is a miracle of improbable physics. But the sound realigns. The hesitation disappears.

Other times, my song is a map. I leave the platform of “prayer,” this place the others would call sacred if they had a word for it, and I seek out the Collector whose turn it is to serve. I find them in their damp bunks, or in their silent mess halls. They see me, and they fear me. Mine is a job without a name they understand.

I will stop before one of them—a woman with strong arms and a hollowed look in her eyes—and I will not speak. Speech is useless here against the roar of the world. Instead, I will make the sound of the broken pipe. A low, *huhhhh-thuh*, a pause, *huhhhh-thunk*. She, and the others like her, have been born to the machine as well. They will not have heard the song with my clarity, but they will feel it in their marrow. She will pick up her satchel of tools, her eyes still distant, but her body knowing its purpose.

She will follow me.

We will walk through the downpour. The water will be cold, but I will not feel it. My skin is the skin of the world. It is not warm or cold. It simply *is*. She will be drenched, her clothes clinging to her muscular frame. She will be fearful of the wet and wind that scream on the catwalk.

She will not be wrong to be afraid.

I will lead her to a junction of pipes and walkways where she could have sworn she had been yesterday, checking a perfectly functional valve. Nothing will look wrong. But she will put her ear against the pipe, and even she, with her lesser senses, will hear the faint inconsistency of the flow. She will find the bracket I have found, the one whose song has soured. She will tighten the bolt with her oversized wrench. The sound will change.

I will sing with the new sound. It is a harmony of a fixed thing.

She will look at me then. For a moment, her engineer’s dementia will clear, and in her eyes will be a flash of something like understanding. Not of what I do, or of what she is truly part of, but of the fact that she is not alone. That she is not just fixing rust and steel. She is part of a choir.

She carries her tune of torque and tension.

I carry the echo.

She will return to her damp bunk. She will forget.

I will return to my platform of listening metal and we will begin again.

The bats are sustained by the rain. They do not own it. They are its instruments.

The blindfish are the sensors. They live the memory of the water as it descends into us.

The silver-feeders are the decay. They are the world’s memory of entropy being cleaned away.

The fungus is the pressure point. It is the world above us groaning and its ghost being represented here, below.

The Collectors are the body's self-repairing mechanism.

And I am the echo. The bridge between the part that knows and the part that is.

I am the sense of sound turned outward upon the world that has given us nothing but sound. The water pounds and the metal rings and I stand in my place between the heartbeat of the pumps and the great white noise of the sky, and I sing the world back into coherence.

It is the only song I have ever been allowed to know.

And the world will be sung.
