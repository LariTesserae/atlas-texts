You lay belly-down on the warm boards of the house bridge, chin pressed to the worn edge, and stare at what the world is below.

Between you and the water there is just enough space to slip your hand through, if you twist your wrist just so. The gap between plank and canal-edge is darkened by years and damp and the faint, oily shimmer of surfaces worn by countless hands and elbows and dripping knees. But that’s not what you care about.

What you care about is the dark.

Below the black glass of the water the world goes away from you in slow, green-smoke distances. Roots like old rope twine down into nothing. White strands of fungus web between them. The canal bed, if you fix your eyes and pretend the shadows are hands, looks like shoulders lying down, resting forever.

You’re looking for her.

Her name is *Moss*, a name no one writes but everyone knows. It’s not moss she is made of, not exactly. But it’s what you’d call her if you had to give it to someone outside the ring wall.

You’re her listener.

That’s your own name, the one that’s been growing on you since you first figured out that she answered you when you spoke to the gap between wall and water. *Listener*, because you’re the one who drops his voice into the quiet spaces between drip and hum and doesn’t mind if the answer takes longer than the eel to round the bend.

*“You there?”*

You whisper it down, not into the water—because you don’t want anyone else to hear—but into the hollow between your chest and the wood, so that the sound bleeds through by vibration and habit.

The air answers with dripping from above. A pattern. *Spit-pat-spit.* The gutter over the walkway licking its teeth. You’ve been counting it long enough that you know when *spit-pat-spit* is going to happen.

*Spit-pat—*

*spit.*

There.

A shape like a shadow woven of shadow slides under the green-black mirror of the water, no longer than you are tall and not much different than the eels she shares the water with—except she is thicker-waisted and her spine is wrong somehow, curved like she’s always halfway through a question.

*Moss.*

You don’t say her name aloud. You say it in the root-knot of your throat and she hears it anyway.

*I’m here.*

*“Night,”* she seeps into your bones.

Your teeth feel a little bit too wet, too close together, like when you’ve been lying on your arm all night and woken up with pins and needles in your jaw. But her word doesn’t hurt. It just feels like *home* and *deep* and *quiet* and *enough*.

You’ve been told her sort is called *Allot*, the same way you’re called *Rootling*, but she doesn’t like that word much. You’ve heard the older ones who work down in the deep roots talk about her: *door-canal* and *snake-maker* and *bridge-water* and *she who’s been here longer than the ring wall.*

*Moss*, though, *Moss* she lets you call her.

*“You’re late,”* you murmur back into the wood, because she *is* late. The reverse of the water usually pulls her past long before the evening lamps remember themselves.

Something rich and dark passes through her slow silence like a leaf through river.

*Not late.*

She shows you instead of telling you.

Images come up through the floorboards into your knees, up your spine, into the soft meat of your brain:

*Depth:* the water going down, down, turning from tea-green to black-brown.  
*Roots:*  thick knuckles of trees older than your grandmother’s grandmother’s grandmother.  
*Metal:* the ribs of the old world, arched and rusted, eaten at by time and pressure and strange little fingers of bacteria that trail like hair.

*—She swam down.*

Your eyes ache. You always forget to blink when she’s like this.

Then suddenly *pressure:* a weight like the whole city of Eelbridge sitting on your chest for the breath it takes you to remember to breathe.

*Something.*

You’ve heard of it. The old engine they forgot the name of. The one that doesn’t match any of the diagrams. The one the inspectors measure with their eyes and their lips pressed tight together and never say out loud what they’re measuring for.

*She touched it.*

*“Don’t,”* you whisper into the wood. *“Don’t. It’ll—‘member you.”*

*She remembers everything,* Moss replies, not like a warning, more like a fact of the world, like water dripping.

*I’m all right.*

You’re not sure she is, but she’s *here* and she’s talking to you and that’s probably enough. For Moss.

*“Why’d you go down?”*

*To hear,* she says.

*To hear what.*

The answer, when it comes, comes like heat moving up from the floorboards:

*The new quiet.*

Your throat goes tight.

*Quiet* in Eelbridge is never really silence. It’s gaps. Spaces between drip and hum and turbine and breath and the *thunk-thunk* of someone’s midnight kitchen practices in the dwelling above you.

But *new* quiet—that’s trouble.

You’ve heard bits of it before: *inspector muttering* and *engineer shouting* and *maintenance crew laughing too loud* signed *something wrong* and *smell of wrong metal* and *too much heat near the trunk wall.*

“You heard the quiet,” *you whisper carefully* into *the wood, tasting *Moss’s* contact underneath it like *salt* and *moss* and *warmth*.

*Yes.*

*What does it want?*

She doesn’t answer in words this time. Words are *hard* for *Moss*; *Moss* doesn’t *fit* into *them*.

*She doesn’t tell you.*

*She doesn’t tell you.*

*She doesn’t tell you.*

The *quiet* in *your chest* spreads *like* slow *water.*
