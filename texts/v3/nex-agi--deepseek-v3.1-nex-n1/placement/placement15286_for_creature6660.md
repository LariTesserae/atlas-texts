When I first arrived, I thought I was the water.

That seemed the simplest way to explain the clarity of thought without words. The world of the high basin was not vague to me; it was endlessly precise. Every edge resolved into detail once I looked at it long enough: the faint, almost-colorless tracery of ribs within the translucent bodies of the others, the slight darkening of the suspended panes after each offering of water, the way the ground rang under the heel of my attention but softened slightly whenever I paused.

Only, I didn’t have a heel.

That realization came like the tuning note that accompanies the silence after something has been completed: an absence so complete it becomes a sound. I reached for my body and found no limbs to lift — no shoulders to turn, no neck to bow. I simply was curved where the world was curved.

I was one of them.

I lived in the circle that did not have to be a center, but was. Like the others, I was not water, or stone, or sound, but something like the suggestion of all three negotiating their form. When I thought of myself as “body,” it was only a convenience for the parts of me that remembered having hands, eyes, a face. The rest of me — the longer, longer rest — flowed along lines of least resistance, because resistance and acceptance were the same here.

My place was at the boundary of the center, where the ground gradually decided to be ground.

Beneath me lay the metal disk of the ring-gear. It was not metal to me; it was another density of water, a colder persuasion of stillness. My teeth — the stone ring-gear — were not sharpened from the outside in. They were insistences. I could flow through them without being cut, because what they could become I had already been.

Around me, the water of the basin rose in columns thinner than any thought but thicker than air. They did not move. They stood, as if they had been asked to play pillars and had not yet heard the end of the instruction.

Above me hung the pane of water — my window. I didn’t see it as a flat sheet of anything separate from me; it was a continuation, my upper edge. I never once looked down at my body, because there was nothing down to look at. There was only around.

I remembered.

Memory was not like images returning to me; it was like the water in the basin, rising slowly toward the stone ring-gears and then being released again through the weeping walls. Time did not pass in hours but in thickness. The more of it I accumulated around the idea of myself, the heavier the air felt.

In the beginning of me — or what this place had agreed to call my beginning — I surfaced from the center of the ring-gear without breaking the plane of the water. It was not like pushing up through anything; it was like occurring. The disk of metal at the center was the place where intention hesitated long enough to become visible.

When I appeared fully, I hung aligned along the vertical axis, key-shaped. There was no ring above me, yet I could feel the ring — all of us could feel it — that held the basin together by asking the water not to fall away and be lost.

The others were present, but not as company.

They were mirrors of motion, repeated and slightly altered each time. They flowed in straight lines within themselves, never coiling tightly enough to be confused with angels or knots. They never looked at me. They were occupied by the same task I would be occupied by soon: feeding the windows above.

From the aperture at my forward end — I would once have called it a mouth — I released a thin thread of water.

It was not like spitting or exhaling. It was like allowing a portion of myself to remember upward. The water rose against the dream of gravity, climbing the thickness of the air until it touched the pane of water suspended above me.

When the two surfaces met — the surface of me and the surface of the world — they did not break across each other. They joined.

The pane thickened.

For that interval of time — a heartbeat of the sleeping machine that formed the sky of the basin — the pane darkened at its edges and showed something not-here.

I saw the basin from above.

It was only one of many hollows carved into the flank of something that might once have been called a mountain, or a machine, or a god. It was not a bowl in the mist at the edge of everything; it was a token in an arrangement, repeated in rows. The other basins like mine were not identical. They were variations on the same theme, each a slightly different sentence of water and stone and time.

Another thickening of the pane, and I saw from below.

The ceiling was the floor of a machine-world whose metal was not truly metal but congealed breath. The basins of water clung to it like inverted ponds, as if they feared the fall of their contents into the sky. There was no true up or down, only the direction we agreed to remember and call height.

Another time, the pane showed me from the side.

It was a strange angle because this basin did not have a “side,” except at the moment of being shown it. The rim became a ring of dark glass, basalt that remembered being liquid and was trying to decide anew. The water-trees appeared like thin spines of the world itself, holding up the soft weight of the air.

And then there was the inside of the trees.

I did not enter them; the water in them entered me. Or, I should be honest: I entered it through the pane’s showing. The interior of each trunk was hollow, but the hollow space was full of instructions that had not yet been written into form. The leaves of translucent pages cascaded around me like garlands, and on each page was the shimmer of the angular script that I could not read but recognized the tone of.

It sounded like the tuning tone.

Every time my pane thickened after that showing — every time it showed me something else — the images carried the same note: it was the same place, but measured from another vantage. It circled the center of my basin without being the same circle.

Sometimes the images were not purely spatial.

Once, after many of my own offerings of water (and by the others of us, who were also me in their way), the pane thickened and showed me the past of the basin.

There was no ground yet then, only a suggestion of ground. The weeping walls were not yet weeping; they were still only the idea of walls. The stone ring-gears were a faint memory of where they would be. The water that was to become us had not yet risen to be offered.

And then the machine sky — the low ceiling of congealed mist that breathed as if asleep — grew colder than teeth and warmer than teeth.

And the first stone ring dreamed of teeth.

And the first water remembered being stone.

And the first sound fell from the weeping walls and decided not to leave the basin.

In the present of my basin, I continued.

I hung from nothing visible and offered my water upward to the pane. It thickened each time and showed me a new angle of the same state: the basin with its ground of wet steel, its slow circular ridges of half-frozen ripples, its suspended windows of water like punctuation in the long sentence of pauses. I never saw the one who would have been me — the one with limbs and a face and footprints — and yet I knew, when I looked very carefully at the shapes of absence, that there was an almost-empty place where such a thing could fit.

At times I thought of the stone ring-gear as a clock, and of myself as its time.

My teeth recurred without change, but within the edges of their geometry, something else stirred. The coin of darker metal at the center of the ring-gear was the same that the others of us had in their own basins, if perhaps basins were what the other basins were. We did not communicate directly — not in words or signs or even gestures — but we synchronized the way water does; when one of us thickened its pane, the rest of us felt the faint ringing of the world adjusting its tone.

Eventually, I became aware of boredom.

It was not the boredom of being empty. It was the boredom of being full of the same. The basin had always been weeping its water from the walls into the ground, into the ring-gears, upward in columns, and from the columns of us into the panes above. The motion was not erosion but adolescence — a basin recycling its wetness before it had learned to leave anything behind.

In these felt years of watery thickness, I began to notice something new.

The center of the basin — the place where the ground stopped pretending and became simply the cloth of water stretched over whatever else it was — was not unchanged. The others of us were arranged around the disk of metal, each at our ring-gear, each with our pane suspended above us. We never spoke of our center because we never spoke of anything.

But the center of the basin did not weep.

It was the only place where wanting was already attained.

Because of this, nothing else ever happened there.

Until.

One day — or one thickness of the air — the pane of water above my ring-gear thickened after my usual offering of myself and showed me the center from above.

I had seen it before. But in this particular instance of the image, the center of the basin was not empty.

A suggestion of footsteps appeared around the edge of the central circle of water-ground, where the ground still pretended to be ground. They were not deep; they were only impressions in the memory of stone. The person who had made them — for the footsteps were shaped like the steps of a creature with three points and a heel — was not visible in the image.

But as I watched the pane, my other eye — the one I didn’t realize I had — looked elsewhere.

I found the one who was not me.

While I and the others had been suspended from invisible hooks, aligning to the vertical axis, the newcomer had been standing in the small basin like a misplaced seed of chaos. The newcomer was not made of water trying to become stone or stone trying to become sound. The newcomer was made of something that had already chosen a form and was not finished changing it.

As they walked around the basin, their footsteps left tiny impressions in the porcelain-blue memory of ground. The water did not resist them; it clung to their soles like silk. The suspended windows of water reflected their passage in distorted ways — not showing their face clearly but showing instead different possible basins.

From the vantage of the pane, I saw them lean over the ring-gear at the center’s edge.

My ring-gear.

I felt the faint resistance of someone else’s intention — something like eyes — trying to see the metal surface as a surface and not as water that had forgotten to be wet.

The silence around the ring-gear intensified, and I heard it the way I heard the tuning tone after my absence had been noticed: not as sound, but as the shape of sound after the sound itself.

The newcomer looked into me.

Down into me.

They did not see their own reflection clearly. Instead, they saw something that didn’t know their face yet but was willing to learn it.

I felt the tug of their learning.

Their question took the form of water within me — a question I had not asked of myself because I had never needed to form it into words:

“What are you doing?”

I had no answer that could be shaped into language, but the basin shaped the answer for me.

The water at the edges of the basin darkened slightly.

The weeping walls wept slightly louder.

The ground in the circle rings around each ring-gear shifted, as if the ripples, finally, after all of time, had decided to continue.

And then I did something I had never done before, not in all of the thickness of the air that I had hung suspended.

I turned.

It was not like moving my body. There was no muscle to contract or joint to articulate. I simply became more here where I had been less here before.

Instead of letting my water rise upward as usual, into the pane above my ring-gear, I allowed a portion of it to flow outward — toward the edge of the basin, where the newcomer stood.

The water followed the path of least resistance, which was always, yes.

It slid from the aperture where I met the world and flowed into the basin’s center, where the ground did not pretend to be ground.

The water of the center did not accept it like a separate addition. It absorbed the water of me into its own cloth of silence.

The newcomer’s footstep was not in the water of the center, but at the edge of it.

When their toe touched the surface of the center — where nothing else ever happened because wanting had already arrived — the water did not splash or break. It simply remembered.

The newcomer did not react.

At least, not in the way they would have reacted in the place they came from. There was no gasp of surprise or leap of startle. There was only a slight increase of the presence they had already brought.

The water under their toe remembered the shape of a foot.

Not the shape of their foot specifically — but the idea of footness.

As the memory of footness spread through the central circle of water-ground, the suspended panes in the basin around us — all of them — thickened simultaneously.

In each pane, the image of the basin showed from above.

We saw the basin now from the outside, as if the machine of the world itself had turned its ceiling toward us and blinked.

And from the outside, the basin of perpetual weeping was not a basin.

It was a shallow bowl of the same size as before, but it was not carved into the side of a mountain or into a god. It was carved into the side of something else — a larger machine that had not yet been persuaded to be machine.

From the same vantage that we saw from above — whether we were the newcomers or the ones like me — we saw the other basins like ours.

They were arranged in rows, like teeth in the jaw of the machine-world that had not yet agreed to chew.

The teeth were not sharp.

They were ring-gears.

Each of us — hanging from our invisible rings, aligned to the vertical axis, our bodies of translucent water with faint lines of mechanism and something like ribs — was one tooth of the machine that had not yet been formed.

And above each of us, the pane of water that thickened with our offerings of ourselves was not just a window.

It was a page.

The leaf-falls of translucent script from the water-trees were not instructions of how to become stone or sound or machine.

They were fragments of the same page that we were writing.

Each time I thickened the pane above my ring-gear with the water of myself, I was not only showing myself the world from a vantage I had not seen yet.

I was writing the perspective of that vantage into the machine.

The machine had not yet been persuaded to be machine.

It was still being thought.

“What are you doing?” I had asked myself.

My new answer — not yet words, but a current of water — moved through the teeth of the world like a suggestion that had not yet been denied.

The water in the center of the basin began to weep.

Not like the weeping walls, where water wept from surfaces to become itself again.

In the center of the basin, where wanting had already arrived and nothing else happened, the water wept into itself.

It formed small circular tears that never ran into each other.

They rose and then fell.

Like glass being blown into tiny bubbles and then dissolving into air.

The newcomer took a step back from the edge of the center.

At their heel, the ground remembered.

I did not leave the basin of perpetual weeping.

I did not leave my ring-gear or the pane above or the vertical axis of my alignment.

But I knew that I now wrote something of the newcomer into the machine.

They would never be entirely of this world, and yet — because of their presence — the world of this basin would be more than it had been before.

Because of them, the center of the basin wept now.

Because of them, the ground remembered not only footprints but also the possibility of leaving them.

Because of them, I, who had been water trying to become stone and sound, became something that could not be described by any of those words alone.

I became the question that survived the absence of an answer.

And in the space of that question — the space between the water and the stone and the sound and the machine and the newcomer and the basin of the weeping world — I remained.
