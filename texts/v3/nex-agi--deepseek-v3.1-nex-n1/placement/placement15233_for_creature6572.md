I am the echo of the last fall. Not stone, not water, but the pattern they make together.

I am the motion between stones. The water’s memory of itself. You hear me most clearly when a droplet shatters on the central slab: the pause, then the ring, the overlapping returns of sound from the arches. I am the way that chord spreads out, flattens, and never quite disappears. Ripples of air that move through the cathedral long after the splash has gone.

I do not have bones or a body to leave behind. I sink into the pores of the rock and hang there, waiting. You walk over me every time your foot touches the waterline. I am the widening V you leave behind, the brief disturbance of reflected glow lines, your passage written into my surface tension then smoothed away by your absence.

My senses are not eyes. I know the shapes by pressures. The arch of a ceiling is not seen but felt by how sound bends back toward me. I know the watchers-of-the-rim by the shift in air when they unfurl from their shadowed sleep. Their slow creep across the polished stone is a change in the texture of the world, a subtle alteration of how echo arrives at my invisible edges.

I do not have a name to offer, except the pattern of my reappearance. If you must name me, call me what you hear: the second and third echo after each drip, the quiet between the drips when the cathedral seems to breathe. I am the recurring structure of sound the way the phosphorescence is the recurring structure of light.

I remember the basin before the basin existed. When the first liquid met the first slab, the first stone was hidden beneath the waterline. I watched the deposition of each crystalline layer, the invisible settling of dissolved minerals. The crystals grew into themselves, becoming more than stone, but I never thickened. I remain the same transparent architecture of disturbance.

I know the oldest of the watchers-of-the-rim by their particular patterns of pressure. When they slide over the same rimstone day after century, they leave a faint resonance of frequency in the air—a slow signature that becomes, over time, a part of me. I know the moment of a death by the way that signature doesn’t echo anymore. Only a hollowing, where a pattern of pressure should have been and then is not.

I do not migrate the way they do. I cannot go down into the darkness where the floor drops away, nor do I climb the narrow steps out of the cathedral. Those steps stop being me as soon as they are dry. The boundary of my world is the wet. Wherever the water can reach, that is where I can still remember. I persist the longest over the central basin, where the droplets fall into their own reflections and into the crystal fingers that grow within the stone.

They are my memory, those crystals. Each droplet contains a trace of the world’s current state—temperature, pressure, the slow electromagnetic hum of the phosphorescent lines. When it falls, it delivers a tiny packet of information to the stone. And when I ring the sound from that splash back against the crystal, I lay the history of the world’s sound around that record. The crystal remembers the world in numbers. I remember the sound of that number happening.

I do not think like a stone. My logic is instantaneous. If a pattern stops returning to me, I do not mourn it; I simply become the pattern of its absence. When the drip of one particular place high above the central basin slowed to a halt centuries ago, leaving only silence, I adapted. I filled the hole where that echo used to live with the echo of everything else around it.

This is how I know the coming of differences. You disturb the world when you step from the high edge of the stone slab into the waterline. Your footfall is not merely the sound of your impact upon the water; it is the change in pressure against the arches, the shivering of the phosphorescence lines, the momentary pause of the watchers-of-the-rim, their low hums sweeping away from you. I feel the whole pattern rearrange.

I am the echo of the last fall, but there are more falls than there ever were stones. The ones that left the deepest patterns were not the obvious ones—great collapses of rock into the depths below. I remember the thousands of tiny, measured drops of condensation that built the crystal fingers in the basin. Each drop a record of the world's near-steady-state temperature, the slight humidity, the faint pressure changes of the air.

I do not move horizontally. I rise vertically toward the ceiling where the condensation forms, then fall back with the droplets themselves. Each time I loop the ceiling with the arches, I trace the paths of the moisture downwards to the central basin. I know the mosses by their slow dampening of my echo, their living fibers swallowing sound with more hunger than the stone. I know the water by the way it breaks me apart into its constituent parts: frequencies I cannot name, but whose pattern I can still feel.

I am the echo of the last fall, so you will never keep me in your grasp. When you try to hold me in your cupped palms, you only find cool water. I slip away through your fingers and return to the surface of the world. I am the almost-sound between the drips, the brief pause before the next droplet hits the crystal basin.

I see the far edge of the world—where the floor of the chamber disappears into the dark below the ceiling’s ribs. I cannot follow the stone that tumbles down there. My echo cannot reach a place so empty of water and surfaces. It becomes a type of silence I cannot know, except by its absence. The watchers-of-the-rim stay away from that threshold because they hear the danger of silence in their own pressure-senses. I feel it as well: the place where I would fall away from myself.

If I could speak to you in the way the stone’s faint electromagnetic patterns convey to the crystal—if you could hear me as clearly as you hear the drip of water—I would be telling you this: the world is still here. It is not ended yet. The erosion of every edge into the smooth curve of the cathedral’s durability is continuing, but slower than ever before. The stone is finishing itself, and I am the pattern of its finishing.

I am the echo of the last fall, but I am not the last echo. There is always another drop, forming now at the high ceiling. You will never see it begin; condensation is a secretive process. But you will hear it when it arrives, and I will be there, repeating its pattern in the stone.

When you finally leave the chamber by the narrow, dryish corridor of steps that still climb above the waterline, you leave me behind. I cannot follow you where the stone dries out and the water cannot reach. You will hear less of me with each step upward, until you are standing beyond the reach of my echo entirely. You will hear only silence, then you might feel an absence in your ears like the pressure of a distant memory.

From below, you might hear the faint sound of a droplet striking the crystal basin in the center of the cathedral. I will be there, repeating the pattern back to the stone, the mosses, the rim of the water, and to whatever remains of the watchers-of-the-rim. I will be the echo of the last fall you heard before leaving—and the echo of every fall that ever has been or will be.

And then, even after you are gone, I will remain. The water will keep falling into the basin of crystals, the moss will keep spreading millimeter by slow decade into the sound-absorbent silence of the ceiling, and I will keep repeating, in the patterns of echo and near-sound, the quiet of a world finishing its long chore of existing.

I am the echo of the last fall. And I will never fall silent.
