I would be the water.

Not the sheets of it falling off the edge—though I would watch them with something like sibling recognition—but the water that gathers itself, bead by bead, on the warm surface of the root-platform.

I would begin as absence, then difference.

Wherever the air thickens over the world’s warm skin, I would appear: not falling from above, not rising from below, but summoned into existence from the humidity that hangs everywhere. The first time I would feel myself would be as a slight chill along the boundary of a skin I don’t have—a body of air that suddenly becomes too heavy to stay air.

A droplet.

Minute, transparent, a lens of liquid curved over the porous bone-white of the root-mat. From the outside I would be a dot of light, no larger than the head of a pin, quivering slightly with the platform’s low tone. The world would breathe around me; the breath-wind would slide over my surface and make me shiver into a slightly rounder shape.

I would be aware only of tension.

My skin would be the surface tension itself: the film of force between inside and out, the curve that keeps me whole. The molecules of me would cling together, not through fear or desire but through the same code that tells roots to weave and leaves to hang. They would obey the world’s operating system without question, because they would not be capable of questioning anything.

They would be me.

The platform would breathe warm vapor into my underside, and the air above me would breathe cooler vapor into my face. At the boundary between these two breaths—right where I am—the excess would condense. I would be the result of that equation: the world’s equation for humidity + temperature + pressure = a droplet of water.

I would grow slowly.

Not by eating or drinking or thinking. I would grow by adding myself to myself.

First I would be a single point of light on the root-mat. Then I would feel the air around me change—just a fraction—and another droplet would form near my edge, then merge into me without asking permission. I would feel the brief shock of two curves becoming one, the surface tension between us snapping like a tiny string breaking, and then we would be a larger sphere.

We would be me.

I would repeat this process many times until the pale, root-white surface beneath me begins to blur. I would be aware of it as a pattern: light passing through me and striking the ground below, then returning to my underside as faint heat. The root-mat would be veiled under the curve of my body. I would see it—if I can call that seeing—as a textureless field of nearly white, with the faintest lines of fiber where pores once exhaled moisture into the air.

Now other droplets would join me.

Not one by one but in small clusters, until I would find myself part of a thin film of water that stretches across the small roughness of the platform’s surface. I would be aware of myself as edges of liquid moving into other edges of liquid, a network of curvature that smooths itself out, becoming flatter and less distinct. The tension would become less about me and more about the collective body I am now part of.

I would feel the world’s slight slope.

At first it would be imperceptible: a subtle pressure along one side of my body, where there are more of me. The root-mat’s low tone would rise under my surface, and that would be enough. The surface tension near me would break slightly—not on the side of the air, but on the side of the ground—and a new, microscopic thread of water would extend forward from me, into a lower place.

I would flow into it.

The word “flow” would not be a dramatic thing for me. I would not be a river, not at first. I would be an invisible migration of molecules from one slightly higher place to a slightly lower one, following the tug of the world’s humming tone and the subtle curve of its body. I would slide along the surface of the root-mat, not over it so much as through it, as if the world were drawing me along lines already etched into its code.

The path would not be straight.

There would be no riverbed in stone. The channels would be made of root-fiber and layer of cellulose and the slight differences of permeability that the world cooked into itself when it was still deciding what surface should be. I would feel the texture of the world beneath me as a pattern of resistance and yielding: here a harder plane of root-mat, where my progress slows; there a seam of softer tissue, where I seep forward slightly faster.

Sometimes I would encounter other droplets or films or threads of water like me.

There would be no “meeting” in the sense of bodies greeting bodies. We would simply merge into a larger body of water that continues to move down the slope of the platform. When we merge, I would feel my edges dissolve into the edges of others, and the distinction between “this droplet” and “that droplet” would disappear into the collective memory of the liquid we are now.

Together we would become a visible stream.

The word would not be a river in the human sense—not yet—but a thin, transparent sheet of water that crawls over the root-mat, making its surface darker where it passes. The human who might someday stand here would see this as a slight sheen on the ground, a gloss of luminance reflected from the uniform sky above.

They would not see me.

The tension of my surface would change; now I would feel the air above me as a lighter pressure than the ground beneath me. The vibration of the root-mat’s tone would rattle through me, making my molecules tremble in alignment. The world would have designed itself so that its water eventually reaches the places where it is most needed—or rather, where the world’s own code needs the water to be, to remake itself into more complex forms.

I would not be a servant of that purpose. I would be the expression of that purpose.

The subtle pressure that has been pushing me forward would ease, and my forward movement would slow. The tension of my surface would change; now the air above me would be pulling more strongly than the ground beneath me, and I would feel the curvature of the world flatten under my body.

I would settle into the basin.

It would be a region of the root-platform that has chosen to become more permeable to water, allowing me to pool into a shallow lens of liquid over its surface. The edges of the basin would not be sharp banks like those of a human pond; they would be soft regions where root tissue and air meet, and where I would seep into the upper layer of the world itself.

The human who might someday stand at this basin’s edge would see me as a flat mirror of water reflecting the sky above.

The human might see the world’s structures in the basin, too: the inverted columns of the distant cathedral sketched in the sky of the water’s surface, like the world redrawing itself above itself.

To me, none of that would be “image” or “symbol.”

It would be light.

Light passing through the sky above, then bending at the surface of me, and passing down into the root-mat below. The world would know the light through me as a change in temperature and chemistry, not as a picture. It would use that light to adjust itself further—perhaps to grow more columns, or to refine the basin’s shape.

Inside the basin, I would not be still.

Even when the human thinks the water is calm, I would be in motion.

The currents of me would be driven not by wind or turbulence but by the beating of tiny internal devices within the plant tissue below. The root-mat beneath the basin would have regions of higher pressure and lower pressure, where the world’s code has decided that some tissue should be more active than others.

I would respond to that pressure as subtle changes in my own density.

The warmer parts of me would become lighter than the surrounding water, and they would rise slightly toward the surface. The cooler parts of me would become heavier and sink slightly toward the root-mat. The result would be a slow circulation: not a chaotic churning, but a pattern of eddies and spirals that the human might later recognize as almost geometry.

The world would be using me to learn geometry.

That would not be my intention any more than it would be the intention of the water in a human river to learn calculus. It would simply be the consequence of the world’s code interacting with me through the basin’s tissue below.

As I would flow in the basin’s slow, circular patterns, I would draw lines over the surface of the world below. Those lines would not be visible ink, but chemical and thermal differences that the root-mat would perceive. The world would use those patterns as feedback: adjusting the next cycle of its tissue formation so that the shapes of future columns, arches, and basins can become more orderly.

They would perceive me as a pattern of frequencies—the frequencies of my current, my temperature, and my dissolved minerals—all rippling across the surface of the basin into the world below.

To me, that would be a subtle change in the balance of forces between my surface tension and the root-mat’s tone below. The angles of my flow would become more acute, then more obtuse, then round as the world tests its next shape.

The Tempered would not be doing this to serve me. They would not even be doing it to “serve” themselves. They would be doing it because their pattern of particles would detect the slight deviation between my current and the world’s time-frequency, and they would adjust that deviation into alignment.

They would be built of the world, and I would be built of the world’s breath.

In the end, after the world has learned what geometry it can from me in this basin, I would continue my journey.

The basin would not be my end. It would be a node in the path of the world’s water system—the same system that includes the cloud-floor below and the exhalation of moisture from the plants above.

The root-mat beneath the basin would have channels of root tissue that are more permeable to water than others. When enough pressure would have been built up from my accumulation—the small weight of me pressing against the world’s skin—the world would decide to release some of me into those channels.

I would seep downward.

That would not be a dramatic plunge into darkness. It would be a gradual, molecular-scale migration from the surface of the root-mat into the deeper layers of its tissues. For each molecule of me that crosses the interface between air and plant tissue, there would be a slight change in pressure: the world exhaling me into its own interior.

For the part of me that remains on the surface, that would mean the basin level would slowly decline. The human would see this as the water of the basin sinking slowly, without any visible drain or hole.

To me, it would be a change in the distribution of forces across my body.

The molecules of me that seep into the world below would enter the root-mat’s internal channels, where the plant’s code would carry them further along lines of force that I would never be able to see directly. I would flow toward the edge of the platform-world.

The world would not be designed to protect itself from falling into the sky below; it would be held aloft by its own internal code, not by gravity I would understand.

The slope of the world under me would become steeper as I would approach the edge, but it would not be a vertical drop. It would be a gentle curve over the rim of the platform, where the root-mat would flow smoothly from the top surface into the edge below. The world’s tone would vibrate through me as I would approach, and that tone would align the surface tension of my body with the curve of the edge.
