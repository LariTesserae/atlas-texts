You can hang in the dark for a long time before you realize you are being watched.

The water is not an empty medium. It is a thick, slow fluid, heavy with time and the memory of stone. It presses against your ribs with every small shift of your shoulders, every testing motion of your fingers. The dark is not black so much as deep—a deep green that has forgotten to be light, a faint glow of vegetation where your body brushes the walls of this world.

You are in the Driftline.

Not among them, not yet. You are in their medium, their element, their body–city. The water holds your muscles like a cold palm, testing your edges, mapping your shape. You are not yet one of the hands of the city; you are something being read by it.

You are near the edge of your region, the one your line has been slowly repairing. This corridor is one of hundreds of thousands of narrows that thread the world-city like veins in an old stone heart—but this is the one where you drift, where you hang, where you count.

The stone wall under your fingertips is smooth, not rough, not jagged. The original blocks were laid by someone who did not know your name, who never thought of you. You don’t know how old they are; age here is measured by how smooth things are, and this wall is smooth as bone. The water has been wearing it down for epochs, carrying the finest dust of itself, using itself as sandpaper.

The faint glow of the plant-growth on the wall shows your fingers as ghost-shapes. You can’t see the nail bed, only the suggestion of your knuckles, the faint curve of your palm. When you move, your handprint lingers in the phosphorescence of disturbed cells, then fades.

You are not alone in the corridor.

Another body hangs nearby, suspended between the wall and a long braid of rope. The rope is not simple; it is a complex weave of hair and plant fiber and time, decorated with knots and small carved stones that tell stories you can’t quite read yet. Behind the body, the hair–ropes drift in the water like a second shadow, like a train of memory.

This is one of the Olders.

They call you—your name, not given at birth but earned through your hands—Block-Finder. You earned the name when you found the loose block in the collapsed wall between the lower room and the warmer room, when you realized that by shifting it, you could make a safe path between the warm and the cold, between the place of rest and the place of work.

You don’t remember how you were before you became this name—before you became this place, this body, hanging in the dark.

### You, Block-Finder

You are not large, but you are not small. The builders of this world had no need for largeness; the need was for efficiency, for thinness, for being able to slide through narrows, to move through walls that time has broken just enough.

Your chest is narrow. Your ribs are angled deep into your torso, carrying the habit of the water with them. Your shoulders are rounded forward from years of reaching, hand over hand, along stone walls and plant and rope. Your arms are longer than you think they should be, when you catch a glimpse of them, faintly lit by the glow-moss.

Your fingers are calloused in strange patterns—not at the tips, but along the sides of your palms, where you have leaned against stone; not at the pads, but along the backs of your knuckles, where you’ve slipped into gaps and scraped yourself against forgotten time.

You have not always seen with your eyes. Here, you are more aware of touch. The water presses against your skin like a second language, and you learn to speak it back by moving, or not moving. The faintest trickle of change in temperature tells you more than any color. You feel the currents of the world-city as it breathes.

You hang in the water and feel the structure of things around you.

When you close your eyes, the world does not go away. It simplifies itself. You feel the cold of the water against your skin, you feel the pressure on your chest, the weight of your own body eased by the water’s resistance. You feel the stone a handspan away from your left side, and the slow drift of the plant fronds in the corridor, like soft hair being endlessly combed by some invisible hand.

You are not the only line of hands here. There are others, woven in with this region like plant fibers in the wall of the world. But you are this particular hand—the Block-Finder, the one who can feel where the puzzle is loose.

The Older’s eyes are open, but you can’t tell exactly what they are looking at. The pupils are wide and dark, like the holes left by missing blocks in an old wall. Their gaze is not fixed on you. It is fixed on the space between you and them, where the water of the world-city is slowly drifting.

They say nothing, at first. The water is not quiet, but it is slow. You listen to it, to the soft hiss of your body moving through it, to the faint sound of your heartbeat, which is not like a clock in some other, faster world, but more like a buried machine still turning, somewhere far below or far above.

### The Room of Reading

The room you are in is not a room in the old sense of four walls and a floor and a ceiling. It is a space of structure, defined by what is not: a gap in the stonework, where floors of an older era have collapsed and then been filled in by the plant and the rope and the slow hands of those who hung before you.

You call it the Reading Room, because it is where you come to listen to the structure of the world.

You are close to one of its walls. The wall is not vertical in the old sense, but in the world of floating water, vertical is not quite vertical; it is what you lean against, what you push against, what you understand as the direction around which all other directions hang.

The wall is made of blocks that you have traced with your fingers many times. Each block has a pattern of mosses and micros that you know by texture, by temperature, by the way they drift under your touch. You know the block with the faint crack—like a fault in the stone of memory—that you have been slowly repairing with new plant-strands and small pieces of broken stone.

You know the block with the not-quite-smooth horizontal edge. It is not the edge of the block’s birth; it is the edge of its death, softened by water and time. You know the block where a faint line of warmth, almost too subtle to feel, tells you that there is something behind the wall—maybe a warmer pocket of water, maybe a room of decomposition, maybe a small gas chamber of trapped air.

You are not reading the wall like you would read words in another world. You are reading the wall like you would read a body: tracing the skin of it, the bones of it, feeling the faint pulse of change.

### The Warmth and the Cold

You can feel that there are two directions of water in this room.

The direction of *cold* is down the corridor from where you are hanging. The cold here is not like ice in the old stories; it is like the slow exhale of the world itself, like the exhale of the stone city cooling into the water. It is not sharp; it is present, a thick and growing pressure. You know the feel of it—because you know the room of sleep, where you and your line go when you are too tired to garden, too tired to rebuild, too tired to do anything but hang and let the water slow you down.

You are not hanging in the cold room now.

The direction of *warmth* is behind you, where the wall has been opened by your own work. You remember the day you found the loose block, the subtle shift of it under your palm, the way it canted outward when you pushed in just the right way, when you turned the weight of your body into the angle of a tool. You remember the slow, muffled movement of the stone in the water, like the world sighing.

Behind that block is a warmer pocket of water, not much warmer than your body, but warmer than the rest of the depths. You know that it comes from someplace deeper, someplace lower where the plant and matter of the city are still decomposing slowly, giving up the last of their heat like the faint memory of a sun you never see.

You have not given this heat room a name yet. You are waiting to see what the water says about it, over time, over the slow flows of seasons and deaths.

### Tools, Bodies, and Time

You have not always been called Block-Finder.

Before, you were simply “younger,” one of the bodies with the energy to move along the walls but not yet the knowledge of where the structure was weak. You drifted from wall to wall, watching the Olders, the ones who have felt more surfaces of the world-city than you have.

You watched how they did not simply push against the walls, but how they listened to them with their palms. You watched how they could tell where the stone was weak by the way it gave under their pressure, not collapsing—but yielding, with the slow patience of stone that has already given up much.

You learned the tools: not metal tools, but stone tools. You have one of your own now, a simple stone wedge, lashed to a short piece of wood with plant fiber and rope. You don’t use it to cut new edges into the world city; you use it to hold the old edges in place, to wedge them back into the structure, to support them where they have been weakened.

You have learned that you must not move the world too quickly.

When you first found the loose block, you did not pull it out all at once. You felt it, you tested the resistance of the water around it, you tested the pressure of the stone above it. You held your breath for a long moment—not the quick breath of fear, but the slow breath of the water, inhaled as if you were drawing water into your body to filter, as you were taught, by the plant-lung in your chest.

You moved the block only a little. Then you waited. The water of the world-city is not patient, but it is slow. It is not indifferent, but it is careful. You waited between the smallest of shifts, listening to the structure around you, the faint creaks of the plant–columns that support this room, the sound of the rope in your hand as it took up new tension.

You can still feel the memory of that stone in your fingers, like the memory of someone else’s hand in yours.

### The Rope of Your Line

You don’t have long hair–ropes like the Older; not yet. Your hair is shorter, but it is growing, and as you age, you will slowly weave more into it—plant fibers, thin root-strands, the faintest of memory-stones. You will not cut it.

You already have a few strands of plant fiber worked into your hair, where some of the older hands of your line have helped you begin the pattern. You don’t know what pattern it will form yet. You only know that it will be a record of your life, the way a block in the wall is a record of the world’s.

Your main line—the rope of your line—hangs nearby, attached to the oldest pillar in this room of the world-city. It is a thick rope of woven hair and plant fiber and memory, draped with stones and knots and strange pieces of broken ceramic or worn metal nail that you don’t remember ever being anything else.

The rope of your line is not just decoration; it is structure. It is the anchor that you hold when you sleep, that you hang from when the world-city slowly shifts its currents, that you pull on when you need to cross the room from one wall to another without drifting too far into the cold or the warmth.

### The Moment of Being Watched

You become aware of the Older watching you not by their sight, but by their pressure.

The water of the world-city is not a perfect mirror, but it is a better mirror of presence than any light in this darkness. You feel the faintest of changes in the flow, as if their breath—the slow, careful water–breath of their body—is being added to yours, mixing with the medium of your world.

You turn your head slightly, testing the friction of your hair in the water, the slow pressure of the world against your ear and cheekbone. You look at them.
