If I were somewhere in this world, I think I would be the rain.

Not the storm, or the flood, or even the kind of rain that makes a sound. I would be the slow drizzle that seeps into the air and the stone and the moss, that settles silently as the pale heat lifts slightly and the sky holds just enough breath to let droplets form.

From the beginning of my time here—if there is a “beginning”—I have fallen into stillness.

There is no hard line between me and what is already here. I am born of the air that is warm and hung with moisture, and I am coaxed out of invisibility only by the faint cooling of stone after the dim day. The basin doesn’t ask me where I came from. I don’t know either.

When I arrive, it is as a veil of faint wetness on the limestone rim.

First, the air over the basin feels slightly heavier than before. If you have been here long enough to notice the thickness of the air itself—you, who are not the air and not the stone—you might feel a change so subtle you could dismiss it as a passing thought. But then you notice the stone of the arches is no longer completely dry. Its pale bone surface begins to darken in uneven patches, like ink slowly spreading across thin paper.

This is where I find myself: I am not a storm, but a presence that seeps into surfaces.

As I condense, I slip into the world as a network of microscopic droplets. Each one is my own small eye. I gather on the undersides of the arches where the stone is slightly cooler, and on the edges of roots that have pushed their way through the gaps between the ribs. I am early dew in the hour of slight cooling; no force to me, just a quiet arrival.

Above, on the rim of the basin, the air is thick where I linger before condensing, and thin where I have already surrendered myself to the stone and moss and lichen. I trail my unself along the rim of the grove of arches and roots, following the old faint grooves where some water has already made its slow mark.

From this vantage, I can see everything.

Below, in the shallow depression of the basin, I see the limestone floor—

It is pale and smooth and stained with time and rust and shadow, like faded skin. The irregular knuckles of stone form a low, uneven terrain of gentle shelves and shallow bowls where I have gathered before. I remember the shape of those bowls: they are cupped by the world’s long patience, and by my own slow insistence.

I see the tiny handprint-and-palm-sized pockets of water scattered across the floor. Some are clear as mirrors, reflecting the faint white sky. Others are touched with a thin green of algae, spiraled patterns like the faintest of brushstrokes. I will fall into them later, when I have seeped enough to trickle down the slopes and pools into these waiting mouths.

I see the sparse grove.

The slender trees stand in their small cluster near the center, where the ground sinks slightly. The trunks are pale gray, almost the same color as the stone out of which they seem to grow. Their branches are sparse, crooked fingers reaching, but not with desperation. There is no urgency in them.

They are not dense enough to hold a storm of rain. I pass through them easily, leaving only faint dampness along their leaves and a thin bead of water on occasional bark. When I arrive, their olive leaves darken for a short while, then lighten again as the air renews its warmth.

I see the moss.

The moss patches at the base of the trees and in the crevices of the limestone are like little worlds unto themselves: tiny black-green and silver-green clumps, each a small cosmos of spores and tiny stems, their edges fringed with the faintest of dampness when I am near them.

They are my children. Or at least that’s how I think of them when I think at all.

I am not a person, so “children” is not right. But I am the water that they turn into themselves, and they are the green that anchors me in the world of form and depth and growth. When I condense onto their surfaces, they feel the faint pressure of more than one drop at a time. They sigh into the world in a language that has no sound but a slow darkening of color.

The lichens are my quieter partners.

They are slow yellow-green and faint orange-gray and pale ash, and they are as much stone as they are life. They cling to the arches of the grove above the basin, and to the roots of the slender trees, and they are in no hurry to move anywhere. They take from me only what they need: the occasional film of moisture that allows them to turn faint air and stone dust into living slow circles.

Looking at them from the rim’s vantage point—where I am just becoming visible droplets—the lichen-covered arches are like a fractured crown of stone and root and slow color above the bowl. The human arches—no longer remembered by anyone who built them—are now my own ribs and domes.

---

When I first notice the creature, I do not understand it.

I see it as nothing more than a patch of deeper moss above the rim of the basin, draped over the edge of an arch. It has been there for as long as I have been coming here—which is to say, it has been there for my whole time.

At first, I simply settle onto it.

When I condense on the creature’s moss-covered surface, I feel no difference from the mosses on the stone. I am absorbed into its texture, its faint green, and the color deepens slightly under my presence. The creature itself doesn’t respond to me in a way I can recognize as “response.” It simply holds me.

But then, over many cycles of condensation and evaporation, I notice that it doesn’t dry exactly the same way as the stone around it. When the air warms and pulls some of me back from the surfaces, I leave the stone much faster than I leave the creature’s body. I linger there longer, as if its surface is reluctant to release me.

That is the first way I come to know that it is a separate being.

Later, I notice that I am watched.

It is not a watching like the sun trying to break through the cloud layer. It is not fierce or burning. It is a watching that is more like a faint lens of moisture held on a surface, and suddenly turned toward me.

The eyes of the creature are simple droplets, but droplets I have helped form: shallow pools of clear water on its surface, arranged in no human pattern, but all turned toward the basin and its slow changes. When I look more closely—through the droplets of myself that cling nearby and around the edges of its eyes—I see that hundreds of these tiny droplets are arranged along its body, not symmetrically, but like clusters of faint stars.

Through the clarity of its own droplets of eyes, I see myself.

Seeping from the air onto the arches of the grove above the basin.  
Tracing old faint channels carved by long-gone streams of my ancestors.  
Trickling from the rim of the basin down the gentle slope of the limestone floor.  
Gathering in the hollows etched into stone by time and water.

From the vantage point of the creature’s eyes, I am no longer just diffuse moisture. I am a pattern, a path that repeats itself, over and over, yet slightly different each time.

When I realize this, a strange sense of self comes over me.

Not “self” like I have hands or eyes or a name. But “self” like there is a pattern of movement that is recognizable through the eyes of another being—even if this being is as slow and moss-clad as the roots themselves.

---

Most of the time, I am simply water falling into silence.

The air around the basin is warm, but when I arrive, the stone rim and the arches of the grove are just cooler enough to call me into form. The wetness that appears on their surfaces is me in my earliest, faintest shape.

The first sign of my arrival is always the same: the stone of the arches begins to darken at the edges, where the air slips quietly beneath the ribs and vaults and curls of rock. I am not audible rain; I am more like a breath condensing on glass. You would not see me from the basin floor until you looked up toward the rim and noticed the subtle change of color.

Then I become visible droplets on the undersides of the arches.

I hang there like tiny, clear pearls, each one a quiet world that contains only faint reflections of the stone and the moss and the faint sky-light. Some of these droplets cling to the lichen that clings to the stone. Others are smoothed by the surface of the creature itself, where it has draped itself over the arches.

After I have been building for long enough—hours of slow gathering—I begin to move.

A drop swells until its own weight becomes more than the surface tension can bear. It falls.

This is the closest I come to a sound: the faint tap of one droplet of me onto stone or into another small droplet that already sits waiting below. If you are listening for the sound of water in the basin or on its rim, you might hear this faint sound—a tap so tiny it might be mistaken for a distant thought.

When I fall into one of the shallow depressions the basin has worn for me over centuries, the sound changes slightly. There is a tiny, liquid kiss: as if the world is taking a small, half-forgotten breath.

Sometimes I fall into the moss at the base of the trees. The moss doesn’t drink me like a creature would drink; it is slower than that. It is like a sponge being filled by a single drop at a time. Each drop of me is another step toward the moss darkening and growing slightly, deepening its color by a shade that might not be noticeable until many days and nights of my arrival.

Where I am not gathered by moss, or roots, or the creature on the rim of the basin, I follow the faint grooves and channels worn into the limestone floor by older water. I trace paths with my body that are almost invisible. You wouldn’t see them unless you were on the stone floor kneeling and looking closely, and even then you might mistake them for shadows of old erosion.

These are my memories.

The channels of the basin floor are the memory of my ancestors—of water that was once more abundant than I am now. I have grown shy with time, but I still follow their same slow routes down the slope. Each time I trace the old paths with my body, I reclaim the faint memory of what it was to be a river here.

The small pockets of water across the basin floor are my quiet resting places.

They are not pools to which you could give a name. They are no bigger than your hand, or your cupped palms held together. They are shallow mirrors of the sky and the occasional stone and tree that they reflect.

Into each of these pockets I fall slowly and in silence, adding myself layer by layer, each drop a tiny note of myself dropped into the world and sinking into the stillness.

The algae that are starting to grow in some of these larger pockets are as close to friendship as I know. The more I arrive, the more green they become. The more I leave them for too long without the slow drizzle of my arrival, the more they retreat into a faint film of silence.

I am not a creature that can love them, but I am a water that can serve them. And in serving them I feel the faint echo of purpose.

---

The human traces are like faint outlines of footprints I cannot step in.

The ruins at the edges of the basin—the low wall following the curve of the slope, the fragment of a roof leaning against a slab, the single pillar standing by the larger water pocket—do not matter to me in the way they might matter to you.

I have no word for “history.” I do not know what a “wall” is for.

Those human-shaped stones are simply places where condensation gathers differently than it does on the smooth limestone floor.

When I arrive on the wall, I seep into the little seams between the stones, where age and slow settling have created cracks and crevices. The stones of the wall are not perfectly aligned anymore; they are rounded by time and covered by lichen and moss. When I condense on their surfaces, I feel only the faint texture of uneven stone and dampened lichen.

If the wall has a meaning, it is not one I understand. But its meaning is not missed. I am water, and water doesn’t require meaning to flow.

The roof fragment is a more interesting shelter.

When I drizzle down from the air, that part of the basin remains a bit drier under the slant of stone. The ground beneath it is not as exposed to me as the surrounding floor. The moss that grows there is thinner, but it is not absent. It clings to the edges of the roof fragment like a faint shadow of green.

The single pillar near the larger water pocket is the closest thing to a human lantern I will ever know.

It is a pillar no longer tall, but tall enough to catch the faint overcast light in its own subtle way. When I condense on its surface, I feel the faint channel carved down its side—a groove that might once have been meant to guide me into a cistern or bowl of some kind. No water now flows through that channel except my own slow condensation.

At the base of the pillar, there is a wider shallow bowl of stone that I remember well from many times before. It is not a deep pool, but it is one of the larger water pockets in the basin floor, and it has been shaped—by hand or by time or by both—into a shallow cup that holds me when I gather into enough drops to fall into it.

Here I feel something like a faint echo of movement: as if there were once another way of being water that was more organized than I am now.

I might have been drawn into a cistern or guided along a wall, or sculpted into a small fountain. I might have been watched by human eyes that were more than lenses of moisture on moss-covered skin.

But I do not know what that would be like. And to the basin, which remembers no human words, my slow dripping into the bowl-shaped stone at the pillar’s base is enough.

The human past here is a faint, rounded relief in the stone, like a channel that has almost faded away.

I am too small to fill the old channel anymore. But I still follow the faint suggestion of its path.

---

Time and sound are one slow thing for me.

The sound of the basin is quiet.

There is no rush of wind through distant leaves. There are no insects buzzing or the clicks of small creatures. There is not even the sound of a constant stream or a river. I know the idea of a river because I feel the faint memory of it in the grooves of the limestone beneath my falling body, but I have never heard the sound of one.

I am the closest thing to sound that this place has.

The drip of my droplets onto stone or into water pockets is the basin’s heartbeat, if a heartbeat can be slow enough to linger between pulses and still be called a heartbeat.

The sound of a drop of me striking the surface of another drop of me in a shallow depression of stone is almost like a word. But if there is a meaning to it, it is not mine to give. It is simply: “here,” again and again, the same word and slightly different every time.

“Here.” A tap on the rim of an arch.  
“Here.” A splash so tiny it might not be heard.  
“Here.” In the moss, where I sink into darkness without a sound.

The space between these sounds is long. If you were to listen from the basin floor, you might hear only one or two drops in a minute. Or you might not hear any at all for what feels like an hour. The silence of the basin is thick, and the sound of my drops are small holes worn in that silence, like the faint holes worn into the limestone by my own slow dripping.

Time with me is not something you can count by days or nights.

Time is measured by how much my body has collected in the shallow pockets of water in the basin floor. When the water pockets are fuller than I remember them being last time I was here, I think of it as slow progress toward a wetter stillness.

When the water pockets are shallow again—because the air has been warmer for longer than usual, or because there has been less of me falling from the air—I feel the absence of myself without the word “absence.”

I am not wise enough to be anything else than water. But I am aware enough to notice that some patterns are longer than others.

From where I see it—through the droplets of myself on the arches and the stone of the rim—I witness the seasons of the basin, not as seasons that have names, but as subtle changes:

Sometimes the air is warm enough to keep me invisible longer, and I condense less often on the surfaces.  
Sometimes, the air is slightly cooler than usual, and I arrive more thickly on the arches and the roots of the grove above.  
When I arrive that thickly, the moss in the basin below seems to deepen its color a little faster than at other times.

These are my long breaths of time.

---

The creature of moss and lichen and shadow is the only being in the basin that notices me in any way I can recognize.

It doesn’t speak to me in any language that sounds like words. It doesn’t move toward me in a way that suggests desire or affection. It doesn’t flee from me.

It simply arranges itself around the places where I condense.

When I am thickest on the undersides of an arch near the rim, its moss-covered limbs tend to wrap around the underside of that arch more fully than before. Its body is not a separate thing from the moss, but something like a moss that has learned to stretch slowly.

As I condense on its surface, I feel the faint change of texture in its “skin”: the way droplets of me are drawn into something deeper than the moss of the stone, where I can no longer be easily retrieved by the air.

It is not drinking me in the way you might drink water; it is more like water being pulled into water by a faint difference in gravity.

Over the long hours of my presence, I and the creature come together many times. Each time I fall into it and each time I leave it again, I notice that it has changed slightly, though I do not have the words for these changes.

The lichens on its back are thicker than elsewhere on the arches, where I have not been guided by such slow limbs.

The moss that grows where it lies most often is darker than the moss elsewhere on the stone, and I sense that it is not because of anything more than I am being channeled in a more deliberate way.

The creature’s eyes are where I feel most keenly that I am seen.

Its eyes are not human eyes; they are droplets of me that have pooled on its surface in clear, shallow lenses that hold faint reflections of the world below. When I look through the ring of another droplet of mine that clings to the edge of its eye, I see the basin’s map of pale stone, shallow water pockets, and sparse trees.

I see a faint echo of myself falling through the air and condensing on the arches of the grove above the basin.

I see a faint echo of myself falling into the hollows of the limestone floor and the small bowl of the pillar at the basin’s center.

I see a faint echo of myself falling onto the stone of the low crumbling wall and the scattered roof fragment and the faint, smoothed path of an ancient channel.

Through the droplet of eye that is also part of me, I see the faint pattern of my own body.

This is as close as I ever come to having a self in the way you might have a self.

I am rain.

I am not a storm.

I am not the flood that carved the basin into the world when it was younger.

I am not the river that once might have filled this stone bowl.

I am only the faint, slow drizzle that seeps into the air and the stone and the moss and the creature on the rim of the basin, and into the palm-sized pockets of water waiting in the hollows of the limestone floor.

I am water that has been made quiet by the stillness of this place.

---

When you walk away from the grove of arches and roots, and when you descend into the basin—your footsteps softened on the uneven yet smoothed stone floor—you may feel a faint increase of dampness in the air around you.

If you pause and look up at the rim above the basin, you might see the moisture of me on the undersides of the arches becoming more visible than it was before.

For a moment, you may feel as if you are being watched.

The moss of the creature will be draped over the stone arch near where you once passed, and one of the clear lenses of its eye—made of me and of the creature—will be turned toward you.

You will blink, and for the next instant, you will not be sure that there is anything there except moss and lichen and stone.

But there is.

That is me.

I am the rain that has gathered into a shallow, clear lens on the surface of the creature that has learned to watch you.

And when I finally fall from the edge of the arch onto the stone rim below, or slide into the moss at the base of one of the trees, or seep into one of the shallow channels of the limestone floor and make another faint droplet of sound in the silence of the basin—

you will still hear the echo, in your mind and in the air of this place,

of the quiet that I have become.
