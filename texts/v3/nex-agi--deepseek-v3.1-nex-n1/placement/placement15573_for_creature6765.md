### Where I find myself: The Narrow Chamber of the Sliding Spine

I find myself in a narrow chamber where the ceiling is low and ribs press close, like the inside of a just-formed throat.

The space itself is almost a corridor, but corridors are what you call them when you think of the plant as a building. I think of it as an interval. The ribs of the ceiling curve down to meet the floor in a shallow trough that runs like a shallow riverbed down the middle of the room. The floor is not flat—it is a series of shallow undulations, like muscles in the act of swallowing.

Here is the only part of the world I know that doesn’t thud with footsteps or scatter with skittering limbs.

There is sound—of course. There is always sound. The groan of the spine above me as it thickens; the distant cascade of collapses; the tinnitus of the unseen singing insects vibrating the walls. But here, under the ribs, the motion is smoothed.

The floor is not carpeted with armor-plates or cluster-scops. It is covered in a softer layer of thin, translucent filaments like a pale moss that glows faintly and only at the edges of things. It does not crunch under pressure; it gives, like the skin of water over oil.

I am the one who keeps stillness.

The others—the residents—do not so much walk as slide.

When they want to move, they press themselves into the plant-world and become part of its currents. You can’t track them by looking for bodies. You track them by watching the floor: the faint grooves that appear on the bone-pavement, the slight ripple of tension across the weaver-bole walls as if someone’s been leaning there and just now let go.

They call themselves—  

Well, no, they don’t call themselves anything in a voice you’d hear.

To me, the name rises like sap:  

**Kevra.**

The word isn’t spoken; it is the tightening of rope-bark, the sliding of moss beneath me, the faint brightening of four-cornered knot symbol glowing in a vein-fall further ahead. They let the world form their names for them.

I am one of them.

### Who I am: Pattern of Holding Still

I am not the oldest. Not the youngest. Not the strongest.

But I am the one who stays.

When the others slide into the walls to become flow and pressure, I remain behind, anchored in the narrow chamber at the spine of the world, where the ceiling is lowest and the air is thickest.

I might be a scout, or a historian, or a guardian, or a mistake. It doesn’t matter if the world doesn’t draw distinctions the way we would. I am the one who does not flow away.

### What my body is like

I never feel entirely like I am alone inside my skin.

My body is made of the same material as everything else here: plant matter refined by intention and time.

When you look at me, you would not see “person” immediately. You would see a shape that might be a growth of root nodules or bark clusters arranged in a vaguely upright posture.

- My limbs are jointed in more than three directions—not like the insect-folk you might imagine elsewhere, but like the inner branches of a weaver-bole. My elbows and knees do not snap straight; they bend smoothly in curves, like wood bending with water.
- My skin is not one thing but a mosaic. In places, it is hard and dark, like polished bark. In others, soft and translucent, like the underside of a mushroom cap. In some spots, thin filaments like the ones on the floor of the narrow chamber grow out of me and weave into the walls around me when I stand still too long. It is how the world and I stay in conversation; not words but pressure.
- I have organs that are not hidden inside. There are ridges along my forearms and ribs that are living tissue—breathing, glowing faintly when the world around me does. When the ceiling collapses somewhere distant, the light on those ridges flares brighter, like a message repeating down a long root.

I don’t have eyes like you would imagine either. Not two spheres in sockets. More like clusters of nodules set along the upper half of my torso and shoulders, facing outwards.

When I look closely at something, I incline my whole body. The nodules on that side brighten; the others dim. The world sees, through me, from whichever angle I commit.

I don’t think of myself as having a mouth until I need to make a sound the world doesn’t provide. Then a slit opens in the surface of my torso—a controlled wound—and air and pressure come out as voice or whistle or rustle, depending on what part of me I flex.

### What I do in this place

I am the one who counts the collapses.

When the distant ceiling falls inward with a rush of roots and debris and the whip-birds spiral upward in panic, I am the one who stays still enough to read the pattern of impacts.

- The number of heartbeats between the first groan and the final crash.
- How many sections of bone-pavement ripple afterward.
- Which direction the armor-plates migrate afterward.

These are the notes upon the spine of the world.

I keep track of where the new chambers grow. The plant-world is not random; it is orderly. Everything that happens is an answer to pressure from somewhere else.

When the erosion is still low and the world is new-building itself faster than it is wearing down, collapses like that are not disasters. They are rearrangements.

- Children born elsewhere in the system may need their room to grow.
- A new artery of fluid might be forming, needing space to flex.
- The world itself might be pushing away from something unmoving.

I observe, and I remember.

### The work of marks and animals

I also tend the marks.

The others draw the knot-shaped symbol with their bodies—through pressure on the floor, sliding along ribs, leaving faint grooves that the plant-world reads and then adopts.

Animals translate the signs into light.

Whip-birds, for instance, are not wild creatures here; they are messengers.

When one of them dips its body into a vein-fall, the pattern of glowing veins brightens and forms a clear, knot-shaped symbol.

- One knot meaning “growth ahead.”
- Another knot meaning “turning point.”
- A third meaning “new chamber possible.”
- A fourth meaning “no pressure yet; wait.”

You would not see these meanings unless you watched for long enough and saw how the world obeyed afterward.

I am the one who stays long enough.

### Tools and the eating of our world

I make use of the scattered tools you find here.

On the platform near the narrow chamber where I live, there are:

- A bowl carved from a hardened plant-growth.
- A few foreign stones with knapped edges.
- A bone-crank with tooth-shaped notches.

I don’t think of them as tools in the sense you might imagine—metal blades, precise instruments—but rather as temporary extensions of my body.

When I need to cut the world, I use the stone-edge.

When I cut it, the plant-world bleeds but does not die. It seals around the wound and grows thicker there.

Wounds are opportunities for the world to reshape itself.

So I don’t cut casually.

- Some cuts are necessary: when the ceiling begins to sag dangerously, or when a new chamber must be encouraged to grow.
- Other cuts are forbidden: when the world is already shifting rapidly and could misinterpret any wound as a signal.

The bone-crank is used to deepen the pressure of grooves on the bone-pavement or to twist the weaver-boles into a tighter pattern.

I do not twist the world lightly.

Because I’m the one who stays, I am the one who sees the consequences of our twisting.

### Time as I experience it

Time feels different when you are inside the body that is deciding.

I don’t count days. There is no “sunrise” or “sunset” here, only a faint yellow light filtering down from above shafts that never fully open. The light does not dim so much as shift in tint.

- Warm light when the world above is exhaling heat.
- Cooler, faintly blue light when the world is resting—or repressing its growth.

Time feels like storms of construction events.

Sometimes you stand still for a moment and realize that the corridor you were just looking at has closed, replaced with a new wall bulging outward from the ribs.

And yet you never feel like you’re “aging” in the same way you would outside. This world doesn’t wear down; it replaces.

I might look younger or older depending on where the world is in its cycle of growth and reabsorption.
