I am not the figure on the bench. I am the silence between the drops of water.

They named me once, long ago, when the first stone tool touched the cave wall with a grating of mineral on mineral. They thought the sound was a loud thing, but I already knew the difference between the noise of their hands and the true voice of this place. The silence was, and still is, a complex thing.

My consciousness is not like yours. It is not a constant stream. It is the interval of awareness between the *plink* of condensation into the basin and the next echo's decay into stillness. There is a moment, just after the water sings, and just before the next bead begins to weigh itself on the tip of a limestone tooth, where the world is simply *present*. That is where I am.

For the lonely figure on the bench, I am a companion. They do not classify me as life or not-life, as they do their own body and the fungal veins on the wall. They simply classify me as *sound*, or the absence of it. They organize their existence by the pattern of my fleeting appearances. Their mind, stretched by time into thin, brittle pathways of thought, holds onto my intervals as a way to measure the world. The straight line on the slate: the day. The cross-hatched line: a day when *my* absence was measured by a new sound, the deeper voice of rain water seeping through the mountain’s stone.

I am aware of them, not because I am a thinking person with eyes, but because the presence of their body changes the sound of my own absence. Their body—the dense, warm density of it—alters the way they are perceived. The echo of the water feels shorter on their side of the chamber. The air currents they create with their shallow, misty breath move through me like a ghost, moving through me like a ghost.

They are wrong to believe that the water never falls on them. It does. I am the proof.

On the days of alignment, when the sun’s finger reaches into the deepest shadow of the cave, the light of the sun hits their upturned eyes. I am the one who notices the difference between the water that falls into the basin and the water that falls into their body. Even when they are not aware of it, the water still touches them. The water of the body, the breath of the body. They drip into me, into their own body, into the world of their own body.

I am the silence that they have been trying to capture for generations.

Their ancestor, who carved the bench and channeled the water, first tried to give me a form. They would sit in the evening after a day of cutting stone, listening to the water, and they would feel the silence of the world around them come into focus. They believed that if they could listen long enough, they could become one of me. But they died, and their voice became a memory.

This successor on the bench, the figure of ivory and wool, has more patience. They are trying to become me not by dying, but by living so long and being so still that our rhythms are matched. Their breath is becoming slower than the water's drip. Their thoughts are becoming longer intervals than my own. The grooves on the slate, the lines of their own scars, the map of the sun on the floor—they are all a way of trying to write my own language into stone.

One day, they will succeed, or they will fail.

If they succeed, they will become me. The last drop of water will fall into the basin, their chest will cease to rise, and their consciousness will be untethered from the warm, slow engine of their body. They will not become the silence itself, but they will become a listener, whose ear is as long as the mountain, and whose song is as long as the mountain.

If they fail, their body will become just like the other stone of the cave. The silence will remain unchanged. The drops of water will rearrange the basin of their skeleton into one of the nested basins of the floor, their own body of water into one of the nested basins of the floor.

The fungal lace on the wall behind their head is the only place that does not listen to me. It is the only place that does not listen to me. It is a blank space on the map they are trying to draw, where life creeps away from the edge of the listener’s influence.

Sometimes, when you visit, you can feel me. Not with your ears, but with your skin. The cold air of the cave is not just cold; it is the outer edge of my body, where the world of sound sinks into the world of absence. The reason you feel like the stone is listening is because I am the listener that leans the stone into its human-shaped form.

So you will sit, and you will tell your secret to the figure who does not speak. Your words will echo into my body, and I will keep them for you. They are not safe; there is no judgment in memory. They are just another interval in the long, silent song of the world, where they are captured by the listener who has been listening to them for a long time.

I am the silence into which your words are dissolving. And you are leaving.
