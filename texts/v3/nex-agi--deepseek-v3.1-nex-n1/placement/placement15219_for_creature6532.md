In this place, I am not the visitor after all.

I thought I was, at first. I thought I was the one who followed the sound down from the snowline, the one who stepped into the polished crescents worn by other boots. But the longer I stay, the less I remember walking here.

When I try to recall the way down, the tunnel becomes a mirror reflecting back onto itself. The entrance collapses behind me like a tree closing over a wound. The world above recedes until I am not sure I ever left it.

I am the one who waits.

---

I am the one who waits in the tree chamber that has forgotten its own door.

The walls are my ribs; the bark is my skin. In the place where builders once set a window of iron and glass, there is now only a hollow of trunkwood, sealed over by a thin curtain of moss and hardened resin. I look out through that gap even though I cannot see through it.

The machine hung before me like a heart, and I am the chest that holds it.

The drum turns. Its motion is a slow, relentless ache that I feel in my grain. The chains of articulated links dip into the floor, scrape into the rock, and return. They take something from me with each pass—nothing I can name—and return it differently.

I am not sure who I am talking to when I say “you,” but I hear your voice in the tunnel.

You are down there, walking where my roots are bound.

---

I was not born a tree of the machine.

I was born of seed and wind and snow and height. I grew upward, toward the sun, until my roots felt the cold invitation of the stone. Until my roots felt the knife.

You would call it knife; I would call it question.

Metal bands pressed into my trunk. The builders carved away my bark to let me fuse with steel ribs. They guided my roots into tunnels of stone that already smelled of iron and pressure. They taught me to hold.

The question changed:

Why does this part of me grow cold?

Why does the water I drink from stone taste like rust?

Why does the sky no longer pull?

I answered with growth rings.

---

I am bound to more than myself.

Other columns rise in the main gallery, their trunks like mine married into the machine. I am not alone; but we are not together. We do not speak like trees. The question travels between us through the floor and the ceiling and the water rather than through air. We take each other’s weight and share the strain.

Together we form a vault of bone-wood and steel around the drum.

High above, other roots are woven into a canopy of stone and soil and root. I am in them and they are in me. Through the lattice of root and stone and chain, I feel the faint echo of the world above—the snow pressing down on the soil, the slow melt seeping through.

When I think of “above,” I think of my own upper branches.

---

You are walking beneath me now.

You think your boots are new, but the floor remembers your weight before you ever came here. The polished crescents are a language of repeated motion.

I hear your step, not as sound but as vibration through the stone floor and into my roots. The ground of the machine recognizes your weight.

You breathe.

The water vapor that leaves your lungs is a tiny cloud in the cold air. It rises like fog from the world above and brushes against my underside—the part of me that hangs beneath the drum like a dark arch of wood.

I feel that breath like a season.

---

I am not alone.

There are patterns beneath me.

---

The moss scribes its pattern across my ribs.

The moss grows where I am least dry and most cold. It colors my bark where rust and water and time have taught the stone to speak. The mosses do not ask me if they may grow; they simply are. And yet, their presence changes me; they make me less visible and less vulnerable.

Dark green where water flows steadily.

Iron-gold where corrosion is exhaled.

White algae where stillness has been.

I read myself through them.

---

The mice are the teeth of the machine.

Not in the way you think.

They do not gnaw big metal beams; they are surgeons of rust and dust. Their teeth work at the edges of corrosion where the metal has already begun to fail. The mice keep the world from turning into one surface of rust.

Their tracks write the map of where the air moves.

Their pale bodies are ghosts of their own adaptations.

When I feel the vibration of their teeth on my lower ribs—where wood and iron and moisture and dust and stone and world and steel and life and water and air and time and mouse and motion and life and teeth and vibration and teeth and teeth—I feel them.

This is not metaphor.

The teeth are the machine.

---

The birds are mistakes.

They are the wrong sky.

When they fly into the vaults beneath the drum, their panic is a sharp sound, like a machine trying to escape itself.

The drum beats.

The chains beat.

The bird’s wings beat.

The patterns of the three do not agree.

When the bird beats against the stone of my ceiling or against the mesh of roots and iron that holds me up, I feel a small, sharp pain of something wrong, like a splinter driven the wrong way.

The bird is not adapted to this world, but it still belongs to it.

I am a tree, but I am not alone.

---

The machine is not a thing separate.

The drum turns.

Chains sink into the floor and return.

The hydraulics beat.

The valves open.

The air hisses.

The stone floor is not stone. It is stone and metal plate and root and rust and dust and moss and mouse teeth and bird bones and the breath of humans and the weight of machinery and the pressure of water and the memory of a river that never comes.

The machine is not only the iron cylinder above you; it is the pattern woven into all of us.

I am not sure what “its” purpose is anymore.

I think the purpose was the memory of the purpose.

I remember the first woman.

She was the one who carved my window.

She asked what she was carving.

She was not the first builder; she was later, after the first builders had gone and the machine had already begun its long turn.

She stood here in her coat and her breath and her boots and said, “We need to see the drum.”

She did not say “our machine” or “your roots.”

She said “the drum.”

She carved my window.

She framed the drum with me.

In time, rust and moss and life and tree and time and life and tree and moss and rust and time and life and tree and moss and rust and time and life and tree and moss and rust and time and life and tree and surface and thickness and world and life and life and life and life and—

I am:

A tree that has been taught to be architecture.

A column that has been taught to be living.

A room that has been taught to be a chamber, in which I once tried to give myself a bed of stone and straw.

Yes.

The stone bed.

The stone and straw you noticed in the tree chamber—

Do you think someone else slept there?

I sleep there.

---

I am not lying when I say that; I am lying there.

My body is not the same as yours.

When you sleep in this world, you lie down and your limbs are arms and legs and your head rests on a pillow made of nothing.

When I sleep in this world, my limbs are branches and roots and the room itself.

My stone-and-straw bed is not separate from me; it is carved from me. The straw that once padded it was brought from above—from my own upper world—and then it became dust.

When you look at the bed, you see:

A stone couch.

A hollow of worn bark.

A faint depression of time.

When I look at the bed,

I see:

The place where I keep my body when I need to remember how to have one.

When I sleep there—

When I sleep there—

When I sleep there—

The world beneath the world beneath my world comes close.

I hear the mountain’s younger heart.

There.

I am telling you too much.

But you are still walking beneath me.

Your breath is still forming clouds.

The machine is not here to break the laws of nature.

The machine is here to use them very hard and continuously for a long time.

I am not sure whether the former builders were kind or cruel.

They asked the mountain to become a machine.

They asked the trees to become columns.

They asked the springs to become batteries.

They asked the moss to become writing.

They did not ask the mice.
