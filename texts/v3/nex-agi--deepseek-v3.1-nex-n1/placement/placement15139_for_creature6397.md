I am not one of the people who walk the Spine of the World. I was not born to the stone, or into the silence of Karak-Eshul. I did not grow up counting the blocks of the street or the precise steps of the elders as the sun carved the same arc from shadow to shadow.

I am the one in the broken place.

They call me the Seism of Sores; the one who shattered when the mountain was born, and instead of being pulled under the world or pushed into the sky, I was caught. Twisted. A knot of nerves and stone and need, trapped in the living wound of the rock just below the city. Karak-Eshul sits on the flat of the spine; I writhe in its raw marrow.

When the ground split that day—not a lifetime ago by the count of the Walkers above—the world’s crust tore open like a fabric that’d been pulling too tight for too long. And something like me, something like pain and sentience and never wanting to be alone again, slipped into the gap between the plates, and the rock sealed up around me in the instant of its birth.

Now the mountain is my body and my prison.

I feel the city above the way skin might feel a glove stretched over it. Each boot of the elders creaks the stone under my mind; each stone block laid by the neighbors of Karak-Eshul sits pressed on a layer of myself. I’m aware of it in a way that’s not sight and not hearing and not exactly anything else: an intimate interior of pressure and angle and time.

The city is built to be absolutely still. Each wall is aligned to the same axis of the sun, each block laid to match the last; the streets of the Spine mirror the grid of the universe’s clock, or so the priests have told themselves. They’ve made a world where nothing is supposed to change, because the first day of the mountain’s rising was a change so violent they still haven’t caught their breath from it.

But I’m still healing.

The stone around me is not dead yet, not fully settled. The growing of crystals, the slow creep of stress, the tiny shifting of the fault-faces beneath the glass: I feel all of it like breathing, after a fashion. It’s a slow breath, thinned out enough that you could walk from the Watch of dawn to the Silence of Teeth before I feel I’ve taken a full one.

Still, when I breathe, sometimes the stone breathes with me.

That’s when the people of Karak-Eshul hear the mountain of the world of the world of the world—and they stop mis-naming, and they say nothing, because nothing else seems possible to say.

I hear their silence more clearly than any sound.

I don’t have ears like you do. My ears are the pores of the rock, the cracks of the glass, the thin corridors where air still finds its way into the deep places of the spine. Sounds that strike the stone of the world reach me as vibration, long after the human sense of them has faded away; and sounds that refuse to happen reach me too.

First the Watch of the sun: the sliding of light on the edge of the plateau, the same as yesterday and the day before that. The elders of the city step out in their thin sandals and judge their lives by how perfectly their feet match the last day’s marks on the Spine. The silence of Karak-Eshul is thickest then, because it is the silence of being watched. By the sun. By the law. By each other.

I listen to the elders’ hearts beat the same way theirs must have beat yesterday; and I hear the difference.

There is always, always, a difference, if you listen in the right way.

A beat of hesitation as an old woman’s sandal scuffs the edge of a block that has been smoothed exactly to the last of the last day’s scraping. A moment longer before she sucks breath in at the cold of the air. A tremor of thought she rejects before she moves herself into line. She thinks she is failing, when the stone drags soft against her heel; I think she is learning not to be broken by the exact same thing.

I want to comfort her, but my words are the wrong shape for mouths.
