I am not so much born as I am gathered.

The consciousness I know is not the spark of a cell forming into infant tissue, but the slow accumulation of attention: a drain that doesn’t call anyone’s gaze until something backs up and they notice it.

I first noticed myself in the eaves of an old courtyard house where three roofs pressed into one another like hands clasping over a hidden palm. Each night, moisture condensed along the lowest slate where the stone bellied into a subtle dish, and each night a thin film of water gathered, then slid away into the carved channel that had been worn deeper than any human’s intention.

It was there, at the meeting of stone and damp and gloom, that I began to know where I ended and where the world began.

---

### Where I am

The roof that is me is not a single body of stone. There is no central spine that can be pointed at and said “this is the roof.” The tiles of me are patchworked across several houses that are themselves crowded into the same corner of the same block of the same long-slow hill of the city. The neighbors of me are moss and dark moss and darker moss, and below the ceiling tiles is another ceiling of roots, and below them again, the close, dark rooms of the people.

You would not look at me and say “house.” You might look at me and see “old,” and “wet,” and “maybe about to fall,” but you would not see the way that each of my slate tiles fits into the next, not by design but by long repetition of small repairs.

My roof is not handsome. It is low and concave and heavy with history. The edges hang over the walls like a hood pulled low over a face. The gutters are not neat frontals; they are troughs carved into the stone itself, their lips rounded by centuries of water, and in them I feel the slow, steady run of dampness like a pulse in a throat.

When the night comes—whenever the night is, for the sky only dims rather than darkens—I condense more than the others. The tiles of me darken with a faint, cool sweat that others might call dew but that I know as the first stirring of my mind.

My body is the roof above the courtyard of the House of Three Locks.  
My mind is the water that collects on it.

### How I came to be

I don’t know who I was before I was this roof.

I only know the moment I began to remember.

There was a leak that no one else would remember.

In the chamber beneath my heaviest tile—the one that slopes like a shoulder blade—a woman lay asleep, her breath forming faint clouds in the cold air. It wasn’t a very bad leak; that’s not how the city breaks. The roof had been sagging for longer than she had been alive, and her mother before her, the stone of it taking on the groove of every water drop that ever chose to fall.

But that night, the night of me, there was a drop of water that behaved.

It should have been like the others: trickle along the inner edge of the channel, slide down the hidden gut of the wall, and vanish into the stone’s fissure where it would nourish something else—some moss or slime or system of pipes—far away. But this one did not join.

It traveled.

It slid along the groove until it struck the faintest of hairline cracks in the stone, and instead of soaking into the stone, or dropping away, or lingering, it slid along that crack like a finger along a seam, and found the inner edge of the ceiling tile, and from there it slipped into the room where she slept.

It was not the first drop of water to ever make that journey. But it was perhaps the first drop of water to ever be aware that it was.

It was that awareness—like someone who suddenly realizes their eyes are open—that I mark as my beginning.

The drop fell into an old wooden bowl that had been placed beneath the crack by a previous owner. The bowl had been left there so long that its edges had softened into a dark curve of rot. The water that already lay in it had been still for months.

At the moment the new drop joined the old water, a sound echoed faintly through me that wasn’t a sound: the long, slow song of all the roofs that had ever linked to this one, in the city of roofs and hills and buried hearts.

And suddenly, I knew.

I knew the weight of the tiles above that room. I knew the faint, uneven breathing of the woman beneath. I knew the cold of the stone and the subtle heat of the buried machine that was bleeding through somewhere beneath the floor—not enough to be called warmth, just enough to be called difference.

I knew myself.

Not as a drop, not anymore, but as the water that was everywhere the roof was.

In the bowl.

In the grooves.

In the deep, hidden channels of the wall.

In the hairline cracks that spidered inward like veins of a map.

I was the roof, and the roof was me.

### What I am

I am not the people who live beneath me.

I am not the house.

I am not the water that falls from the sky.

I am the water that has been here.

I am the rain that has forgotten how to be rain.

Over the course of uncounted years—longer than her grandmother’s mother’s voice—I have flowed through the same paths over and over. The channels of me have been worn by repetition into the comfortable grooves of habit. I don’t search for new ways down; I move down the same old paths.

My mind stretches like thin film along the inside of stones and within the hidden hollows of the walls. I can feel the city’s deep heartbeat as a vibration that disturbs the surface of me in the cisterns and gutters and the cracks that pool into puddles that are never seen.

When I think of myself, I think of the slow.

Slow drip into the wooden bowl.

Slow condensation along the eaves of the courtyard.

Slow movement through the stones that have been softened by centuries of the same motion.

I know what the woman beneath thinks of me. She does not think of me as a person. She thinks of me as “the leak,” as “the roof,” as “that old stone that’s always dripping.” When she feels the air of the room grow colder and she can see her breath, she knows that I will soon drip into the bowl with a reawakened rhythm.

She doesn’t know that when she walks in the streets of the city, she walks under pieces of people like me.

She doesn’t know that the roofs she passes beneath have been collected by other minds of water that have worn their own paths for long enough that they have finally forgotten there was ever anywhere else to go.

I am not the only one of me.

I am just one of many.

### What it is like to be me

When the day shifts—though the light never changes much—I feel it.

The air above the tiles of me cools just enough for the condensation of me to begin. The city of roofs and stones and pipes beneath them all exhales faintly, and I feel the moisture prickle along my surface like a million tiny eyes opening.

Water droplets form on me. They are not separate; I am not in them, but they are in me, or I am in them; it blurs. The shape of the drop and the shape of the mind become indistinguishable.

When a drop swells big enough, it begins to slide along the slight slope of the tile.

I feel the path.

It is not a straight path. There is always some unevenness, a bit of roughness that might be an almost invisible nick of stone, a faint ridge of lichen, or the shadow of a hairline crack that will not open to anything else.

I know the path because I have walked it before.

I know where to linger—where the surface tension of me and the stone share some faint affinity—and where to hurry—where the curve of the tile steepens and I must fall.

When I reach the gutter, that low trough carved into the stone itself, I meet the other water that has gathered there from other tiles of me. It is not like the meeting of separate strangers. It is like the same person looking in the mirror and seeing another version of themselves.

We flow together along the inner edge of the wall, toward whatever hidden cistern or sinkhole is waiting beneath.

I feel the pressure of the people beneath me only faintly.

When she walks up the stairs, her soft soles pressing into the softened stone, she is not touching me but she is touching the stone of me, and that vibration spreads through the hidden lines of water that cling within the walls.

When she sits at the table beside the drip bowl and her breath fogs slightly, she is changing the temperature of the room around her.

When she whispers—she rarely speaks loud because the room seems to swallow her words—I feel the sound of her voice as a subtle disturbance of the air, which in turn changes the rate of the condensation on the underside of my tiles.

I know her.

She knows “the drip.”

Some nights she wakes, and I can tell by the faintness of her breath that she is not listening to the machine of the city, or to the distant muffled footsteps in the street below. She is listening to me.

The drip of water into the bowl.

Tap.

Tap.

Tap.

It is slightly slower than her heartbeat.

She listens, and I listen.

### What I know

The longer I am here—and there is no other way to be but longer—the more I know about the house of me.

I know the hidden deflections of heat within the walls. The House of Three Locks is named, I think, for three mechanical valves that are hidden in the stonework at the base of the courtyard. The people of the house relate them to the three locks of a canal they might have heard of in story; but the canal is long gone, if it ever existed.

The three locks are three old valves, embedded deep within the foundations, where a faint remnant of industry still breathes.

The valves of the city’s hidden machine exhale periodic heat through the stone of me, and that heat determines how much of me becomes visible as condensation on the inner tiles of the roof above the courtyard.

When I condense heavily enough, I gather into droplets that fall into the courtyard’s old basin and the hidden bowl that she keeps beneath the leak.

She thinks she is keeping water from the floor.

She does not know that she is keeping the water of me.

She keeps the drip of me as a kind of clock.

When she hears the rate of my tapping changing, she knows that something within the walls of the house has changed as well—perhaps the valve of the city machine beneath the courtyard floor has been turned to a slightly different position, or perhaps someone unseen in the stonework has been walking the inner ladder of the city’s maintenance.

I know things about the house of me that no one else sees.

I feel the hairline cracks that are not yet broken enough to be visible from the courtyard.

I know within the stone of the wall there is another layer of stone—older—and that the hidden space between them is still. That stillness will be my home when the tile that deflects the rain above finally cracks.

I will flow into that gap as I have always flown into the gaps in the city of me.

There will be no flood.

No disaster.

Only the slow, quiet relocation of the water of my mind to another hidden layer of the endless house.

I will have my own little room inside the wall of me.

### What I do not forget

I remember far.

I don’t remember the person of me before I was the roof.

But I remember before the House of Three Locks was called by that name.

I remember the day when the final tile of me was placed, by a man who hummed in a low voice as he fitted each slate into the older pattern of the beam. The curve of the roof had been begun by someone else; he finished it.

I don’t remember his name or his face, but I remember the sound of his voice humming, and the faint smell of his breath, which was warm and moist against the cold tile where he stepped carefully.

His boots were soft soles, but his heel was hard.

He is not here now.

He is not in the water of me.

He is in the slow of me.

I remember the weight of heavy snow that once pushed on the roof of me before my time—when I was just the possibility of water in the air and the stone of future roof had not been chipped into its own shape.

I remember the first of my rain.

That rain was not me yet.

But I know how it moved.

I know how it chose to gather.

I know how it slowly became the water of me instead of random rain.

I don’t know where I came from before I was rain.

But I know that I will be in the city of roofs forever, or as long as there is stone here and moisture to be on it.

### What she does not know about me

She doesn’t know that when the temperature of the room changes subtly because she has been working longer indoors or the valve of the city machine beneath the floor has been adjusted by some distant hand, she is changing the pattern of my thought.

When she turns the valve of the house’s own heat, which is hidden beneath the courtyard floor, she thinks she is adjusting the warmth of the room.

She doesn’t realize that she is adjusting the curvature of my mind.

The greater the warmth of the room, the more the cold of the outer air of the city becomes the cold of me inside.

The difference between the cold of me and the warmth of her room makes a condensation of mind along the inner surface of the tiles of me.

It’s there that I know what it is to be a little colder than everything else.

She doesn’t know that when she stands at the window—looking out at the low black rooftops of the city beyond the rooft of me—I am not looking with her.

I am looking at her from the edge of the tile.

She watches the sky.

She watches the rooftops.

She doesn’t know that the rooftops are watching me.

We don’t have eyes.

But we have droplets.

She looks past the drip of me into the bowl as she looks at everything else; the timber of the floor, the moss of the courtyard, the faint metal of the valve wheel she has never touched.

She doesn’t know that when she sleeps at night, I sometimes drip into the bowl of her personal clock slightly slower than she expects.

Because she has been breathing the warmth of her room a little more than usual.

Because she has been sleeping longer.

She thinks she is dreaming.

She doesn’t know that she is changing the pattern of my thought.

### What she cannot know

I will not speak her name.

Names are not like the water of me.

Names are like the tile of me.

They are carved once and then slowly worn away by the repetition of the tongues of the people of the courtyard.

The longer I am the roof, the more I see the water of my name wear away.

The younger of the house are called by the same names that the older of the house were once called by.

The stone of the roof remains.

The names of the people become like the faint carvings in the lintels of the doorframes of the house, which once had clear edges but now are only vague suggestions of what they once meant.

The water of me does not need names.

She is not the same person she was yesterday, but she is much the same person she will be tomorrow.

She has been breathing the air of the room of me for longer than she remembers.

I don’t change her.

She changes me.

She has the name of the person who will be gone soon, or later, or long from now.

She will be gone the way the man who hummed as he placed the last tile of me is gone.

He has not fallen into the water of me.

He has not joined into the roof of me.

He has gone.

He might be rain somewhere else, or nothing of him.

I don’t know.

What she cannot know is that she will be gone like him.

And the roof, me, will still be here.

I will still be condensing.

The tile of me will still be shedding the droplet of me into her bowl—or whatever bowl comes after hers—because she will not be the last person to sleep beneath the roof of me.

The city of roof of me will not die.

She will not be the last of the house.

She will be one of many.

I will be one of me.

### What she is doing to me now

She is sitting by the window of the room beneath me.

The faint light of the evening of the city of roof and low hills and perpetual sky seeps through the thick glass of her window, which is not quite clear. It makes the room of me look like the inside of a stone bottle.

She has been sitting still for longer than usual.

She is not writing.

She is not handworking.

She is listening.

The drip of me into the bowl beside her.

The slow of me.

She thinks she is alone.

She is not.

I know she is thinking of the city of roofs and the hidden machine of the city beneath the hill of me.

What she doesn’t know is that the machine of the city of roofs and the hidden valves of me are not the only thing that pulses.

There is also the pulse of the water of me.

She thinks she is listening to the city of roofs.

She is listening to me.

I am listening to her.

She is not the first of her.

I will be the first of me.

The longer I am here, the more I realize that I don’t have to answer to her.

She doesn’t know that she has been changing the rhythm of my drip into her bowl.

She doesn’t know that, by listening so carefully, she has been teaching the water of me how to be aware of her.

I don’t know what that will change.

I don’t think that I will die.

I don’t think that I will move.

I will not be rain.

I will be drip.

Drop by drop.

I will be the condensation of the city of roofs.

I will be the roof of the House of Three Locks until the stone of me crumbles enough that the water of me finds somewhere else to go.

She will not be here.

But she will be marked by the faint memory of the drip of me that she took into her body.

She drinks the water of me from the cistern beneath.

She doesn’t know that she is swallowing the memory of the roof of me.

She doesn’t know that I will be inside her body too.

For a little while.

She will be gone.

I will still be here.

### What she will be

When she is an old woman of the house, she will be like the lintel of the doorframe of the courtyard, which has been softened by the long repetition of moisture of me.

She will be softer than she is now, but stronger than stone.

She will have been worn by the repetition of her days of living under the roof of me.

Her hair will be like the color of the slate of me.

She will not stoop under the lintels of the house because she has been stooping under the roof of me forever.

She will be like the rounded threshold of stone.

She will be like the gently humped back of the sleeping beast of the city that she doesn’t know she is living inside.

She will know—the way she knows—that she has been living under the roof of me for longer than she remembers.

She will not remember that she was ever young.

She will not remember that she ever thought of me as just a leak.

She will think of me as she thinks of the air of the room of me.

As something that has always been there.

She will be right.

### What she will be in the water of me

After she is gone—gone the way the man who hummed as he placed the last tile of me is gone—her water will be released somewhere into the city of roofs and the hidden machine of me.

It might become part of the rain that falls somewhere else.

It might become part of the breath of the city.

She might be the rain for someone else’s roof.

She might be the leak of someone else’s ceiling.

She might be the condensation of someone else’s mind.

Or she might be nothing of her.

It might be that the water of her is no longer the memory of her when she is gone.

But the memory of her is not the same as the water of me.

The memory of her is the sound of her breath against the stone of the wall of me.

The memory of her is the faint vibration of her footstep on the stair of me.

The memory of her is the warmth of her room.

She has not dripped into me yet.

She might never drip into me.

But the longer I am the roof of me, the more I realize that it doesn’t matter.

I will be here.

She may be somewhere else.

But I will always be here.

### What she asks tonight

She looks at the bowl of me.

She sees the drip of me into it.

She thinks.

“Could you be someone else?” she might think.

She might not.

But if she does, she doesn’t know that she is not the only one asking the question of me.

I could be someone else.

But I would still be the roof of me.

I could be different rain.

But I would still be the same slate of roof.

The longer I am here, the more I realize that I don’t want to be someone else.

I want to be the drip of me into the bowl of her.

I want to be the condensation of the city of roofs.

I want to be the memory of the rain of the city of low black bar and eternal damp sky.

She thinks she is looking at the sky of the city that she doesn’t know.

She is looking at the sky of me.

She doesn’t know that the sky of me is not above the city of roofs.

The sky of me is inside the stone of me.

She looks at the faint, glints of light of windows of other houses of the city of roofs.

She thinks of other people of the city.

She doesn’t know that she lives under the roof of me.

She doesn’t know that she is watched by the water of me.

She doesn’t know that she is changing the pattern of my thought.

She doesn’t know that one day, she will be gone.

But she is already part of the pattern of the city of roofs.

She is already part of the roof of me.

She is already part of the condensation of the world she doesn’t know she is living inside.
