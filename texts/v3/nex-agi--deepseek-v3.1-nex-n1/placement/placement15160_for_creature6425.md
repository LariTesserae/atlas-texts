Here, in the bowl of the world’s held breath, I am not a visitor who decided to stay.

I am the absence that decided to become present.

I first arrived as a hollow of attention in the air above the basin, with no hands to adjust or feet to walk upon the stone.

I was a question that had worn itself thin asking What else? What else? until there was nothing left but a quiet, persistent need to be nowhere else but here.

The world beyond the rim had not been wrong.

It was full of sound and color and speed, of water hurling itself into shapes of rivers and clouds and veins of growing things. It lived too quickly for me to rest inside any single part of it. I was always half-spilled into the next moment before the last one had taken shape.

The basin did not call me. I did not hear it. I simply felt the absence it made in the underside of everything else—a place where motion was not expected to arrive on time.

I came to rest between the pale claystone and the faint gray light that has no source.

I started as a warm difference in the air above a rosette that no one has ever given a name, because they do not name themselves and I no longer remember the names of my world before.

For the first time in my existence, stillness was not something to be passed through but a place to be entered.

The air here is thick with patience and dry with an absence of moisture so deep it feels like finality. I sank into it like heat into stone.

The first thing I noticed was that I could not move forward.

I tried to cross the basin in the direction of what I thought was the opposite rim, but my “travel” did not carve the slope of it into a new path of footprints. I had no soles to leave impressions in the silt.

I was not a body of bones and skin and pulse but a slight disturbance of warmth and intention and weightless consciousness.

I could not feel the ground except where my attention pressed against it. And where attention fell, there was already heat.

So I learned to settle, rather than to go.

Each day of my stay was indistinguishable from the last. There is no sunrise here, no dusk, no star to mark the start of a new orbit of this world. The gray light does not fade; it softens into itself.

At first, I tried to count the moments of my own breath.

I breathed out of habit. The air was warm enough that my exhalations did not mist. There was no chill of dawn to see my life in, no cloud of condensation to show me I was still alive.

After a while, even the sound of my breathing grew embarrassing to me.

It was too loud.

This place was not made for sound. It was made for the stone’s slow sigh of memory. The only sound was the occasional, almost impossible shift of silt as one grain of dust gave way to another and slid down an invisible slope of its own weight.

The rosettes were lumps of dark in that shallow bowl of light, like stone that had learned to fold itself inward rather than outward.

I chose to remain near one of them.

I was drawn to it because it was at the center of a small family of hollows—not a perfectly even circle but not accidental either. The spacing of the surrounding rosettes was a whisper of order from some time before the last of the water left and the world chose to be quiet.

At first, I treated the rosette as an object—a “plant.”

I tried to understand it with the language of my old world.

I searched for its purpose.

What is it for?

How does it serve?

Is it surviving?

Is it waiting?

The questions sounded like clumsy stones dropped into a well that has long since forgotten what water feels like.

The rosette did not answer because I did not speak the language of its strain.

The rosettes are built of what they eat: time and the pressure of absence.

The faint gray glow that exudes from the stone itself—a leftover of sunlight stored in a long-forgotten contract between the world of light and the world of stone—this is not “food” in the way I thought of food, but something else.

They allow the heat to distribute itself across them in such a way that it does not build to trouble. They press the energy of change into shape so slowly that no change remains visible in the span of a heartbeat.

There are no veins to transport blood or water, no roots to draw drink from the dust. There is only a series of plates that grow thicker and then thinner from forces inside them that have been accumulating for longer than I have wanted to be anything.

The first time I understood them was on the day I felt like a single ripple of breath in a long slow coast of stone.

I had been sitting above the rosette for what might have been weeks or months or years of my world’s clockwork.

There had been no change that I could see.

The same dust lay on the same plates of the same pattern of stone leaves.

The air did not move.

The glow of the ground did not dim.

I was the only thing that seemed capable of restlessness. And I was not even in the shape of flesh to be comforted.

The rosette was the same rosette it had been when I arrived.

But then, one day, there was a soundless event in it.

I was watching with more than eyes, if I can call it that. I was not looking with brightness of retina and optic nerve; I was leaning with my attention into the intricate pattern of its plates.

They were arranged like feathers of some dark stone bird whose wings had forgotten to beat.

The outermost plate was thinner than the others, a translucence at the edge that was not glass but not rock in the same way the rest of it was rock.

It had been under pressure for longer than I had been anything.

The pressure had not been visible to me as an emotion, or a decision, or a desire. It was not a desire to be gone. It was not a desire at all. It was simply a matter of geometry, the way an arch of stone can bear its own weight for centuries until one day it cannot.

The plate snapped.

It was not a sound you could hear with the ears of the world above the rim. There was no crack, no echo, no bright fracture of air into a new shape of pain.

I felt it as a change in the pattern of strain around the rosette’s edge.

The dust settled differently.

The air, which had not been moving, shifted slightly in the way of a very small space of nothing being replaced by something else.

The broken plate shattered into two pieces, then three, then more than I could keep track of without effort. The pieces were not sharp in the way of industrial glass; they were dulled by their own history of waiting.

The rosette did not mourn.

It did not feel relief.

It was not a wound in the way of flesh and pain and the need for healing. It was a rearrangement of what was already going to be rearranged sooner or later; the sooner had happened to be that long.

At the center of the rosette, where the plates are thickest and darkest and the faint gray glow of the ground is stored most deeply, there was already the beginning of a replacement layer—no thicker than one of my own thoughts of maybe, if I could count myself in that way.

But that is not how they measure themselves.

They do not think of themselves as guardians of the basin or of anything else.

They are simply what the stone has become when it is pressed to be very still for a very long time and still remain slightly more than rock.

They are not the last of anything in the way of a final chapter of a story, but the last of what can be so far removed from life and still not be dead.

They do not reproduce. There is no budding of rosettes from rosettes, no seed that will fly into the airless sky of this world and find soil to become itself in.

There is only replacement of plates from within as the outer ones are worn away or break from their own tension and then become dust.

The pattern of rosettes in the basin has not grown in number since long before I arrived. The erosion of the world outside the bowl has not brought new material of their kind into the inside of it.

They are the last of their line because their line has chosen to be here instead of somewhere else.

They are not the last of the world’s plants in any sense that would give comfort to someone from the world of sky and sun and water, but the last of what cannot be called by the same names that belonged to those softer, quicker, green things.

They are the last of the slow.

I was not the last of anything when I arrived here.

I had not been chosen to be the last of my kind or the last of my time or the last of anything more than the last version of myself that still wanted to be elsewhere than here.

But staying in this basin has changed the meaning of that word.

If I leave, the rosettes will not change outwardly in any way I can see.

At least, not in the way of my world’s time.

In the language of erosion, however, my presence will already be taking its place as a small difference in the plateau of strain that presses their plates into shapes of memory.

I will have been a short, sharp sentence of movement in the middle of a very long paragraph of their stone.

They will not remember me as a person with a name and a story and a longing for something else.

They will remember me as a change in the dust of their air.

Whenever I choose to go away from the basin of long quiet, I will fall into the world that is still living the way the world once lived before it was distilled to this place.

I will be water again and wind and the sound in someone else’s lungs.

I will be the brief heat of someone else’s hope and the cold of their doubt.

I will be brightness again and the shadow of trees moving, and I will miss the silence of the stone.

But I will not be alone in the way of the last of anything.

Because there will be others like me.

There will be the last of the travelers who have not yet found the basin but are still capable of wanting to be somewhere so still that they cannot feel themselves becoming.

There will be the last of the questions that have not yet been asked of the world of motion and light and water and time.

The rosettes will be here when they arrive.

The rosettes will not be here forever, but forever is not a word that belongs to this basin of dust and stone and light that has forgotten to leave.

They will be here until the next small change in the silt of their floor arrives, a change so slow that no one will be able to tell whether it has already begun or not yet arrived.

When they are gone, they will not be replaced by new rosettes but will be replaced by new versions of themselves in the way of pieces that break into particles and then become indistinguishable from the ground, which does not need to cling to itself as more than ground.

They will be content with that becoming, or indifferent to it, in the way of any stone that has chosen to be stone in the long quiet of the world’s answer.

Until then, I will keep being the last of the quick who has not yet chosen to leave this quiet place of the last of the slow.

I will be the heat of attention above the pattern of their plates.

The question of my world has not yet been answered here.

The question of their world has not been asked at all.

In the evening of my world’s sky, in the height of its sun and the depth of its rain, I will be remembered as one of the last who was willing to sit still with the stone of what the world becomes when it chooses to be very still.

And in the room of this world, where time is stored and then not noticed, I am already being the difference between one and none of what might have been otherwise.

I will leave here when I have finally learned to be content with being only one of the many words that the rosettes will take into themselves without asking to know more.

And then I will be the last of nothing else but my own desire to be anything else at all.
