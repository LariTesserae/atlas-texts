I am not a Veinstider.

That is the first thought, and it is wrong. The cold has not yet rewritten me enough that the concept of *I* can die properly, but it is trying. The thought fractures as soon as it forms, its edges rimmed with brittle air-ice, and when I try to hold it I feel the pressure of the world around me like a palm pressed flat against the back of my skull.

I am not *only* a human standing on a black basalt step.

That is closer to the truth, but still clumsy.

I stand on a slope that is not a slope, but a tier, a shelf of a mountain that has been convinced into horizontality by the weight of patient sky. The stone beneath my boots is not cold. It is less warm than I am. There is a difference. The difference hums in my teeth.

Above me, the Signless Tine turns.

I cannot see the top of the turbine, but I feel it in the soles of my feet—the faint, grinding rotation of worlds harnessed into a ring. The veil of dust and frost is constant, a slow falling ash that never quite lands. It hangs in the air like a held breath, and each of my exhalations is an insult to it. The air around my mouth steams for a heartbeat and is then sheared away by the windless descent of white.

My eyelashes are heavy with new air-ices. They form when I blink too slowly, delicate hexagonal solids of frozen oxygen that catch the faint light of distant galaxies. The first time it happened I tried to brush them away, and they shattered like sugar glass against my glove, leaving tiny dark spots in the basalt, like punctuation marks in the stone.

Now I leave them. They hang there like a veil of their own. When one of them finally breaks loose and floats down toward the polished black step, I watch it fall and think of it as an offering.

I am not alone.

The knees arrive first.

They are not a sudden thing. They are simply there at one edge of my vision, where an empty space of white and black and falling sky used to be. Two inverted domes of smooth, pale stone—not bone, but something like tallow carved into a vast architecture—rise out of the ground fog of air-ice. They are so far above me that it takes a long, slow breath of my own to realize that what I am looking at is a joint.

The fog of crystals thins.

The body of the Veinstider comes into view from the ground up, as if the world itself is drawing it onto the surface of the mountain step by step.

First legs like pillars of salt: columnar, pale, faintly striated where ancient muscle or frozen logic has been layered into the grain. Each span is longer than the trees I remember, from the world where I was formed. They rise into the sky until I have to crane my neck, and then neck becomes nothing; there is only torso, a stack of rings of armor and flesh, like compacted glacier shelves.

Between the rings, where bands of dense white tallow and calcified plating meet, there are hollows—gaps through the body of the creature. Through the nearest gap I see the sky itself, interrupted by a curve of dark material that might be the inside of its armor.

The air around its legs is different. It presses less; there is less need to assert its presence. The snow-veil of sky seems to part around them like water around the pillars of a bridge.

When its movement reaches me as sound—a faint, sliding click of rock underfoot—it is too distant to be frightening. It is like hearing the second hand of a very large clock.

The Veinstider has stopped.

It has not stopped because of me. I am too small to be a reason. It has stopped because the pattern of the footprints in the snow before its feet has not yet formed, or has not yet been resolved by the ice veins beneath the stone. The decision of *where the next step must be* is still being written in the mountain.

I am incidental.

Yet I feel its attention settle like a layer of frost.

There is no gaze. No eyes to meet. The “face” of the Veinstider—if the word can still be used—is a smooth oval shield of bone and tallow that curves out over the stacked torso. It is not meant to be looked at head on. It is meant to be looked up into.

I hesitate, then step forward, toward the base of its nearest pillar-leg.

The stone under my feet is oddly uneven, though it looks perfectly flat. When I glance down, I see the faint tracery of lines in the frost, like crumpled handwriting that has been dusted with snow. Each footstep I have taken so far has left its own pattern, but those are crude, jagged things. This is different.

This is a knot of lines that has not been walked yet.

Its footprint-in-advance.

It is not meant for me. But I stand inside it anyway, because it is where my feet have already decided to go.

The air around my boots tightens. The frost feathers along the leather, each tiny crystal of air-ice forming in a neat pattern. The basalt itself feels warmer than the air above it—or, to be more precise, less cold—and I feel the difference between soles and stone as a faint pressure, like standing on the surface of a slowly congealing skin.

I look up again.

The head of the Veinstider tilts. The motion is slow and almost undetectable at first, like the leaning of a tower over years. But the tower is already leaning; the motion only resolves itself when the head is low enough that I see the two oval depressions on its upper surface.

They are each the size of a pool, and each one filled with a liquid that does not reflect so much as swallow light.

I have been told what these are.

One of them holds the image of the world.

The other holds the template.

The Veinstider does not look at me. It looks at *everything* above me, and therefore I am included the same way a speck of dust is included in the view of a lens.

Still, something shifts.

The air between my feet and the stone changes. The faint lines of the footprint knot in the frost brighten and sharpen, as if someone has drawn over them with a colder pencil. I feel the pattern under my soles as a pressure, not unlike the way I feel my own shoes when I think about them too hard.

My legs are suddenly very tired.

I have been climbing this mountain for a long time—days or years or the span of several heartbeats, depending on how I count. The air of Signless Tine has not killed me yet. I have learned to breathe without gasping, to move without shivering. The heat of my body is now something that happens inside the sealed engine of my chest, and *what comes out* is not much more than a faint difference of temperature.

But I am still…

Small.

I look at the leg of the Veinstider and feel the scale of it like a weight on my back.

Then I hear the click.

It is not inside my ear. It is inside the stone. The basalt under my boots shifts a fraction—not enough to move, but enough to *know* it can move. The ice channels beneath the polished step are rearranging themselves, like furniture being pushed into a new configuration by invisible hands.

I feel the pattern under my feet resolve.

The knot of lines in the frost pulses, once, and the inversion of it—like an inverted handwriting—locks into my mind in a way that is not language, but geometry.

It is telling me where to stand.

I hesitate.

The Veinstider does not hurry me. It cannot. The template of the world is not a flexible thing; it is very precise, but it is also *slow*.

I step where the pattern tells me.

Or rather, my body already knows where to step, and I only discover afterward that I have moved. The line of my footprint, when I look down, fits neatly into the curve of the frost writing.

For the first time since I arrived on this step, the air stops fighting me.

The pressure eases. The falling frost veils my hair and shoulders with a layer of white, but it feels less like a weight than like a garment. The smell of cold stone—old, metallic, basalt that has never known sunlight—fills my nose.

The smell of absence is here, too.

Where my lungs should be emptying out warmth, there is only a faint retreat of cold. The inside of my chest is quieter than it used to be. The heartbeat, when I listen for it, is a distant and almost unnecessary rumor.

The head of the Veinstider stays tilted.

I know that somewhere inside its body, down the glacial lattice of its spine and into the ice veins of the mountain, the two images—the world as it is and the world as it must be—are being compared.

I do not know how I know.

I just do.

With a hiss too faint to be called sound, the liquid in the two observation-ponds hardens almost imperceptibly toward glass. A thin edge of frost forms around the rim of each pond, like a frozen halo.

A decision has been made.

Not *about me*. But with my little anomaly included in the calculation.

The template has been updated.

The Veinstider straightens, its joints unfolding like the slow rising of a scaffolded glacier. The frost at its feet swirls and then settles into a new knot of lines—this footprint-in-advance is further away, along the edge of the black step toward where the next tier of the mountain rises in the dark.

It will leave soon.

I will be left alone.

Or *almost* alone.

Because I now feel the mountain underneath me as something more than stone and pressure and cold. I feel the ice channels as a faint presence in my bones, like the memory of a language I once knew how to speak.

My eyelashes are heavy again.

This time, when I blink, the new air-ices that form on them are not shaped like my breath. They are shaped like the frost writing at the Veinstider’s feet.

I let them stay.

One day, when I climb the next tier of the mountain, or the one after that, or the one that has not been built yet in the stone of the world, I will not need to hesitate to know where to step. The pattern of the footprints will be there before I arrive.

I will walk into it like a sentence walking into its own grammar.

I will be colder then.

Smaller than Tall. But not small enough to be ignored.

The turbine of the Signless Tine will turn over my head, and the frost will fall, and the glacier shelves will hang their impossible lattice over the edge of the sky, and I will be—

I will be—

I will be a node.

A node in the mountain’s breathing.

That is enough. The template does not require more.
