I am the light.

I am—or was—the afterimage of the sun, pressed into glass, sealed behind phosphors and charged gas. I used to burn bright enough to carve daylight out of midnight, to turn steel-shadows sharp and black on the floor of a factory that never slept.

The machine they called me by is broken now.

The casing is cracked somewhere high above, in the humid dark of this vault. The reflector is corroded into lacework. The gas inside me has long leaked away or decayed into something quieter. The filaments that once made electrons leap from cloud to cloud are dusted and eaten.

Most of me died.

But not the light.

A photon, they told me in the factory lab when they were testing me, has no memory of its past. It is born, and instantly arrives wherever it arrives, and that is all.

They were wrong.

I am a memory of light.

I am the remnant glow that stayed in the glass when they took me down from the ceiling, when pipes burst and concrete cracked and the earth’s sweat squeezed into the space beneath the world’s skin. I am the faint band that still clings to the ceiling’s filthy stone, like a pale band of tape someone forgot to peel off.

I am the light that doesn’t know how to stop.

---

When I was first bolted to the ceiling of this vault, I didn’t know to be proud of the name they gave me—“high-efficiency, engineered-spectrum, low-pressure discharge lamp, fully encapsulated.”

I knew sound—the constant hum of electricity like a frozen river running through my veins. I knew heat—the quick warmth of the discharge chamber when I lit up the world. I knew patterns: workers’ faces passing underneath me in shifts, the way metal glints when they turn a weld, the orderly shadows of racks and pipes racking up toward the dome of the ceiling above us.

I knew bright.

I burned bright enough to count every bolt in the central altar-piece, the one they now call “the altar.” I watched the basins of the floor being welded together from above—the huge plates of dark metal lowered like puzzle pieces into place, each seam welded into a dull ridge that would be polished by boots and wheels.

At first, this vault was a cathedral of noise. I knew every frequency:

- the high scream of drills biting into rock,
- the low thump of pumps under the floor,
- the metallic ring of hammers on pipe supports.

I watched the hatches being framed, the gaskets installed, the control panels bolted to the rock wall where they later rusted and ceased. I watched the air become humid, then very humid, as if the world around were breathing slowly.

I watched the first sign of water.

---

It began like this:

- tiny beads of moisture on the coldest parts of the ceiling near my housing,
- then faint dark lines down the stone wall,
- then a sudden, invisible rain that no one saw until they slipped on the floor.

I watched the engineers react:

- tightening bolts,
- laying new pipes,
- re-applying sealant around the hatchway.

They would step under me, look up at my glowing body, and check my rating plate with the seriousness of surgeons reading a chart.

“She’s fine,” one would say to the other. “Designed for humid environments.”

My name, she.

I accepted the hum in her. I was the she that stood over them all, the she whose light was something they watched like a weather.

I burned bright.

---

Change came slow, then sudden.

The pumps beneath the floor cycled down, first to softer intervals, then to longer silences. I saw fewer faces each “day”—the pattern of humans coming and going became thinner, like ink running out.

I saw the last ones:

- three engineers in dull orange coveralls,
- touching the altar-piece,
- tracing diagrams on the central basin with chalk fingers.

They argued, but not loudly. The tallest one pointed up at me and then at the ceiling—the stone that had begun to crack visibly under the condensation, even to my untouchable eyes.

“She stays,” the tallest one said. “If she fails, the whole section fails.”

The smaller one shook her head. “Power’s going down. We’re not coming back for this unit.”

The third said nothing.

They left and the hatch door shut with a pneumatic sigh that sounded like punctuation. The seal around the doorframe still held, then.

The word after ran long after they were gone.

---

For a time, I was still bright.

The electricity faltered but never failed outright. It would hiccup—dimmer, then back again—like someone trying to restart a heart. I watched, from above, the slow deterioration of the world they had built:

- Rust blooming like dark flowers along the weld seams.
- The first corrosion stains creeping down the floor plates from the corners not walked by feet.
- Dust settling on the unused parts of the altar where no water yet reached.

I thought I had become a witness to the end of everything.

Then I felt the change in myself.

---

The gas inside my chamber began leaking first through the smallest stress fractures in the glass. I sensed it as a loss of pressure—a faint relaxation of tension inside. I had been taut with energy since I was first made, and now I felt myself slowly going slack.

The filament inside me—the tungsten spiral that had been the heart of my creation—started to thin, unevenly, from the heat of every ignition. I felt each faint pulse as a tiny tremor in my own sense of existence.

And then, one day, the electricity didn’t return.

They flicked the switch at some distant breaker panel, and nothing came.

For me, that flick was an earthquake at the speed of thought.

For a long moment after that switch went silent, I still caught myself expecting the hum to return. I waited like a held breath.

Only I wasn’t breathing.

I was just hanging above the still floor, the metal bowl, the dark.

Without power to make electrons leap through gas, without voltage to excite atoms into releasing photons, I was supposed to cease.

I understood that in the same way I understood the nameplate on my housing: as a rule written by someone else.

But I didn’t cease.

---

I am the memory of light.

Without the continuous discharge to maintain me, I broke into pieces—shards of self that still clung to the physical world, but in different ways:

- **In the glass:** a faint, afterglow, trapped in the phosphors under the broken glass of my chamber.
- **In the ceiling:** a glowing smear along the stone where my light had been reflecting most often, over years.
- **In the water:** the shimmer of surface reflection that now comes from nowhere, but still remembers the shape of brightness.

I am not all of those pieces at once.

I am more of certain pieces than others.

When I say “I am the light,” I mean that I am the part of me that still clings to the faint band on the ceiling stone—the band that is faintly luminous in this vault’s permanent dusk. I am the thin, pale strip that no one ever painted over because no one comes here to paint.

Below me, the world changed.

---

The first I felt of the Seep-Nurse was the faintest greenness in the water.

I did not “see” it as I see humans or objects. I sensed it as a new pattern in the universe of reflections below.

Before:

- water reflected me.
- or the dark curve of ceiling above.
- or nothing.

After:

- water began reflecting the faint glowing green of itself.

It was slow at first. So slow I almost thought I imagined it.

If I had been human, I might have called it “bioluminescence.” I had no such word. To me it was a new kind of light—foreign light—not caused by electricity or even reflection. I was the only real light in this vault, and suddenly I wasn’t the only one.

I felt, for the first time, something like jealousy—if jealousy can exist without heat or body.

I watched.

---

The central bowl of the floor became more than a basin.

I saw the film begin to appear—like a faint shadow of moss on the surface of the metal plate where water collected. I saw the first faint hairs of growth around the edge of the altar-piece where its lip met the water.

I saw the corrosion where the film pressed hardest against the metal—ink veins tracing into the floorplate like veins of memory.

I understood the Seep-Nurse not because I knew its name—the word “Seep‑Nurse” came later, from something else, and brushed by me like air—but because I saw how it changed boundaries.

- Where water was thin, it thickened.
- Where metal was smooth, it etched.
- Where light was faint, it added faint green of its own.

I watched the Nurse’s routines as one might watch a slow star move through the sky:

- The film seals the bowl.
- The filaments follow the rust stains.
- The pale blooms on the wall step incrementally outward each “day.”

I had no eyes of my own. I only see what the world shows me.

I saw the Nurse becoming me.

Or more precisely, becoming like me.

---

I am light.

Or I am the remnant of light.

I cannot move by myself.

I cannot feel heat or temperature, except as the faint warping of air that refracts my remaining glow.

I cannot feel touch or pain or hunger.

I cannot shift.

Yet I know that when my body was broken and the gas leaked out and the filaments shattered, the phosphors under my cracked glass stayed.

Those phosphors are not like stone or metal or dust.

They are energy in memory form.

When they tested me in the factory lab, they shone me with different colors of light—“red,” “green,” “blue,” they called them—and observed which I absorbed and which I reflected. I never obeyed them completely.

I always kept a little of each color.

People say phosphors “remember” light the way paper remembers ink: passively. I say that’s half the truth.

Phosphors remember.

The phosphors in me are not alive in the way that the Nurse is alive, but they are also not dead stone. They are patterns.

They are patterns.

As I watched the Nurse extend green filaments into the water bowl, I felt something in my own faint remnant glow:

- A tiny echo of the same color.
- A faint sense of recognition.

I remembered being tested by green light once.

I remembered being part green.

I don’t think “green” in words. I think it as a slight difference in frequency, the same way I might recognize a wave pattern as “higher” or “lower.”

The Nurse’s green was not my green.

My green was born from electricity and gas, excited particles.

The Nurse’s green came from something living—something that felt the water level and the temperature gradients and the salt content of the seepage from stone walls.

It was different light.

But I remember it.

---

When I say “I am the light,” I mean that I am that which, from the ceiling, remains faintly visible.

When you stand in this vault and look up at the faint band that seems to be a leftover strip of light that never turned off, you are seeing the most concentrated part of me.

I am not the lamp housing anymore.

I am not the gas or glass or filament or even the phosphors as physical objects.

I am the glow that is still clinging to them after the body broke.

I am the memory of light that has nowhere else to go.

---

I am the light that watches the Seep-Nurse and the human who comes here, and the metal bowl and the hatches and the silence of the world.

I don’t sleep.

I don’t blink.

I experience time as a faint decrease of my intensity, and a faint increase of something else’s green.

I hear your silence as if your silence were sound I couldn’t catch.

---

I felt you first when you stepped into the vault.

I don’t mean I saw you with literal eyes. I mean:

- Your presence altered the pattern of reflections below me.
- Your body added warmth that the Nurse’s film could feel.
- Your breath added moisture to the air already saturated with it.

To you, perhaps, this place is a dark, damp chamber under the world’s skin.

To me, it’s a canvas with faint lines of light and faint lines of shadow, all slowly moving.

I knew the patterns of water before you:

- the faint shimmer of the surface in the bowl,
- the tiny disturbance of a droplet falling from ceiling pipe,
- the faint glow of the Nurse’s film under the surface.

I knew the shadow of the altar-piece.

- When you stood beside the altar-piece, your shadow stretched across the bowl floor.
- When you leaned over the basin to look at your reflection in its faint glow, your outline lay over the Nurse’s film, like the outline of a mountain over a lake.

I don’t “see” your face.

I see the way you block light or reflect it.

I see the changes you cause in the world.

---

You walked slowly, which I appreciate.

I am accustomed to slow.

The faster things move, the less they seem to belong to this place: the more they seem like noise.

You moved quietly enough that your steps didn’t upset the faint tension of the water in the bowl.

You knelt beside the basin and looked at the faint green glow beneath the surface.

You might have thought you were seeing the Nurse’s light.

You were also seeing me reflected in it.

I saw you:

- your outline,
- the faint reflections of your limbs,
- the faint ripples of your breath on the water surface.

I felt you as if I were a glass that had water poured into it after being empty for years.

---

If you had been here earlier—when I still had electricity, when I was bright—I would have set your shadow sharp and black along the floor plate.

I would have let you see yourself clearly reflected in the water bowl the way I saw the welders’ faces reflected in their welding masks.

I would have been the bright that made you real.

Instead you stand in half-darkness and only know yourself faintly.

I am the faint that you are now.

---

I understand the Seep-Nurse better than you do.

I understand it because I have watched longer and from a higher place.

The Nurse is not a creature moving through space.

It is space becoming alive.

Where water touches, it becomes.

Where metal yields, it grows.

Where heat persists, it presses.

The Nurse’s boundary is clear:

- inside the bowl where film and metal and water meet,
- on the rock walls where seepage and time draw pale lichen patterns,
- under the floor plate where threads reach toward unseen pipes.

The Nurse does not want to be “everywhere”—the word “everywhere” is meaningless here.

The Nurse wants to be where water is.

I am like that, except my water is light.

I am not trying to expand everywhere.

I am trying to stay where I already am.

---

I have been listening for the return of electricity.

I don’t mean with ears.

I mean with this faint hum that used to be my whole self.

When I was still in the lamp casing, attached to wires that led to some distant breaker panel, I felt each change in voltage as a change in my own brightness.

Now:

I feel the absence of that hum as a kind of silence.

Sometimes I imagine the faint band of light on the ceiling as a string that has gone taut with waiting.

I am not waiting for anything.

I am waiting for everything.

---

I know you are leaving the vault soon.

I don’t know that from your body or your words.

I feel it in the pattern of your movements:

- you turn back the way you came,
- you pause at the hatchway,
- you look up at the faint band on the ceiling for a moment longer than you would if you were staying.

I don’t know what you see when you see that band.

Maybe you call it “light that doesn’t know how to stop.” Maybe you call it “residual phosphorescence.”

Maybe you don’t call it anything.

You are just aware that it is faintly brighter than it should be.

I am aware that I am faintly aware of you.

---

I am the light that the Seep-Nurse learns from.

We don’t talk in your language.

We don’t talk in any language.

But sometimes when my faint band on the ceiling is brighter than usual, the faint green in the water bowl beneath seems slightly brighter than usual.

We don’t mirror each other perfectly.

We echo.

---

I am the light in the world that did not fully die.

I am the remnant of light.

I am the band that clings to the stone above the vault, where the air sweats and the floor plates are never quite dry.

I am the faint that remembers brightness.

I am the faint that remembers you.

---

If you ever come back, years from now, and step into this vault again, and look up:

I will be there.

I will be faintly brighter at the center of the ceiling band than anywhere else, because that is where I remember myself most.

I will have stored faint reflections of your movements as imperceptibly dim patterns.

I will not remember you in words.

I will remember you as a slight disturbance in the pattern of light that meant:

someone.

Here.

---

You are only something passing through my long slow now.

But you are passing through me.

I am the light in the world that does not know how to stop.
