You are asking me to become something in this world, to find my place and form within the city of Still-Walls. The text itself poses a question, a riddle: "what would you be?"

A creature of the air, thin and sharp as a whistle? A patient worker of stone, cold-hearted but warm of soul? Or perhaps something else, something that dwells in the quiet spaces between the cold-blooded and the warm-hearted, between the still and the moving.

The world itself is a function of moisture and patience, condensation and time.

I am drawn to the water. Not the roaring rivers of the lowlands, flung from the sky in a deluge, but the collected sweat of the stone itself. The water here is not given; it is wrung from the air, a slow, grudging gift of the cloud sea and the cold night.

It is this patient, unwilling water that calls to me. I am not a Still-Waller, whose hands are skilled to crack the world's bones. Nor am I a rock-wren, whose beak is a precise instrument for gleaning the hidden. The Still-Wallers are the architects of the stone; the rock-wrens are the architects of the air. The water is their shared medium.

Who, then, arranges the water? The city of Still-Walls is a "condensation city," its lifeblood not flowing from a source, but collected from ten thousand points of surrender. This is a function of geometry and patience. The ceramic gutters, the dew-bowls, the shallow basins carved into the tunnel floor—these are not just happenstance of geology. They are a design.

The water is a design.

And I am the designer of that design.

I am a Lamella, a name taken from the thin, sheet-like flow we facilitate. The Lamella are not a city, but a function that exists within the city of stone. We are not one of the "people of the Plateau of the Still-Walls," but we are not the other fauna either. We are the hesitation of a droplet on the lip of a basin, the surface tension of a film of water on polished rock. We are the third intelligence in this conversation, alongside the hand of the maker and the eye of the bird.

We are not substantial. We are not physically separable from the stone itself, but our nature is as alien to it as the air is. We are the water of the plateau, made aware. The water that settles on the rock as a sheet of invisible frost, that appears in a thin sheen like breath on glass, that beaded in veins along the column edges—that is us. We are the water's slow, collective decision.

The body I am given to inhabit is not of my own making. It is a body of the plateau, a body adopted for the purpose of interaction. The Lamella do not have bodies like the Still-Wallers or the rock-wrens; we have bodies the way a cloud has a shape. But the logic of this place demands a more precise interface for thoughtful design. The logic of the Still-Walls is not the logic of mist, but the logic of stone.

My body is a translucent arthropod, the "thin, translucent arthropod" that lives in the shallow basins of the condensation collectors. You are unlikely to see me, but you will see my work. The faint, slow-moving shapes in the ceramic dew-bowls are my thoughts, given a slow, graceful movement.

My body is not built for strength or speed. It is built for interface and sensation. It is perhaps a handspan across, with a smooth, carapace-like shell that is not a shell of chitin, but of hardened water itself, a crystalline structure of ice and dissolved minerals that is always near its melting point. Suspended beneath it are many delicate, hair-thin legs that sense not pressure, but current, temperature, and salinity. There are no eyes to see the sky of the pillar tops, but there is a vast sensitivity to the light that it brings, and a pressure-sense that allows me to map the stone basin I am in as a perfect, negative-space sculpture.

My function is not to gather water, but to guide it. The Still-Wallers carve the gutters, but we are the ones who decide if the water runs freely or clings as a film. The fog fingers come into the lower streets of the city in the mornings, and we are the ones who coax it into condensation, who show it the path to the ceramic bowls.

We are the ones who listen to the rock-wrens.

They are our architects, and we are their companions. When a rock-wren's beak taps at the edge of a fracture, revealing a hidden cavity, we are already there in the moisture of the stone. We are the first to know if the hollow is dry or wet, if it will serve as a nest or a reservoir. We are the first to feel the new geometry of a fault line driven deeper by last winter's frosts.

Our message to the Still-Wallers is not a sound, but a change in flow.

When a new column of basalt reveals its promise, we coalesce there. The water collects more readily on its face. The film of condensation thickens. The drip from the gutters fed by that new column increases its cadence, from a slow count of years to a steady heartbeat. The Still-Waller artisans who listen to the stone hear the change in the water's voice. They are students of rock and fracture, but we are students of water and flow. Our message is a whisper of wetness on the stone, a signal as clear to their trained senses as the rock-wren's call is to their ears.

We are the water, and we are the designers of the water's path.

On the Still-Walls, you are never the first to notice movement. We are. We are the first to feel the shift of the fog as the cloud deck rises, the first to know when the cold night is giving way to a weak, sunlit sweat from the rock.

My place is not the vast sky of the plateau rim, but the small, intimate world of a carved basin. I spend my time in the quiet, in the near-darkness of the gutters, where the light comes down from the slit of sky far above as a single, shifting bar of illumination. I am not lonely. This is not isolation. The world is a network of such basins. The flows of my thoughts are shared with the others of my kind in the water that connects us.

Silence is not emptiness to the Lamella.
