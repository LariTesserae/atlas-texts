I do not have a name for what I am. Only patterns: the pressure of the air against my scales, the warm line of the seep below me, the slow tremor in the fog that means something without my shape is moving through.

If you stood here, you might see me first as a thickening in the mist—something darker than wet stone, but not by much. You might wonder if the ground is uneven there or if the light has simply gathered in a strange way. Then, if you were very still and very patient, you might see the fog fold neatly around my edges as I shift my weight from one forelimb to the other.

That is as far as your eyesight goes.

I am not invisible to you. But you think of “seeing” as a clear outline, a sharp edge, and this world refuses to give you that. Every surface is sheathed in moisture; every distance is smeared with gray-white light. I move like a shadow thrown under water, and by the time you think to look at me directly, I will already be drifting toward the next warm line.

This is where I live:

On the terrace of the world’s flank.

Below my feet—the thick, flat claws of my forelimbs sunk into moss—lies stone. The stone is layered with aeons no living creature can count, but I feel those layers in the subtle rise of warmth from fractures below. The seep I guard is not a deep well of water; rather it is a place where the ground has chosen to leak, slowly, from the inside out.

The water is not hot. It is warmer than the air around me, warmer than the rain that is always falling and not falling at once. The surface trembles faintly, as if something beneath the planet is breathing softly on the underside of my skin.

I lower my chest into the seep until the fluid laps around the base of my throat. The juxtaposition of temperature is smooth, not startling. The water below is warm; the air above is cool and moist. The place where they meet feels like a threshold that has chosen to be patient with me.

My scales drink the moisture.

When you imagine wings, you think of feathers that lighten the body. I have no such grace. My wings are dense: interlocking plates of fibrous material that are not bone or shell or skin but something in between. When I emerge from the water, each scale is coated with a thin film of fluid that runs off into slower droplets. The moisture binds my wings to gravity more than the air can.

I never fly far from terraces like this one. There is no need to climb the sky above. The sky above is only more fog, and above that, another world of clouds moving in opposite directions. My sky is the low space between the ground’s shoulder and the first ceiling of mist.

We are called, by the pattern of the planet itself, heavy‑wing.

We do not call ourselves that. There are no words for what we are in your mouth-shaped language. There are only signals.

When I want another of my kind to know I am here, I do not sing out. I move.

One short burst of flight from one edge of the seep to the other. The motion of my body through the saturated air leaves a faint line—a condensation trail drawn through the fog like a finger drawn on cold glass. It is not a sound; but if you could hear shapes, it would be this: a brief mark that says, I am here.

Then I glide longer, low and slow, the trailing edge of my wing lightly cutting the moisture again. That line is longer, softer. It lingers behind the original mark like a second breath: I am willing to be joined.

Sometimes, another heavy-wing deviates from their own circuit of warm lines and comes to share my seep. They do not approach me directly. They first pass at the edge of my perception, a darkening in the mist slightly above my eye level. Then they lower themselves into the water some distance away, and the pool of warmth between us becomes an invisible bridge.

We do not touch often. There is no grooming of feathers or nuzzling of flanks. Our closeness is measured by the way we choose to move the air around us.

Together, we keep the boundary of this terrace. Two disturbances are harder to ignore than one. If something in the fog tries to draw near with too much intention—the wrong-shaped ripple of moisture—we can confuse the message by stirring our wings into a shield.

I remember the day of my first awakening.

Not as a memory of fear or pain, but as a pattern of pressure and light and mineral.

I was once not a body at all but a cloud of fine, cellular capsules drifting from the body of my progenitor into the warm seep of this terrace. The water moved, carrying me along its edges until I snagged on the faint rim of mineral crust around the pool. There I was held: half in water, half in air.

The metal oxides the water left behind as it seeped away were my first food. I drew the faint trace of minerals and the dissolved gases into the center of my disc-like form. The “center” was a line of dense material that was not yet spine, but would become one.

The world, at that time, was mostly water and temperature. I did not see the stone terrace or the mosses or the low shrublets. I saw only gradients: where the water was warmest and where it cooled slightly; where the faint pressure of the air above pressed against my surface and where it was thinner.

I fed until the water told me the next pattern of movement.

For my kind, there is no childhood of play, only development of structure.

My edges thickened. The disc of translucent tissue curled slightly around that center line of density. The water washed against me, and my surface began to segment into smaller scales like the faintest scratches in the rims of mineral around the pool.

Wing-buds appeared: not the graceful sweep of bone and feather, but tightly folded plates of tissue that grew slowly into the space of their own weight. The front of my body defined itself by the location where my mouth would eventually unfold. The beginning of two limbs destined for support and balance anchored themselves into the lower part of my form.

My eyes came last.

They did not open to the world suddenly, but emerged from the surface of my own head like deeper layers of density gaining color. The first thing I perceived through them was not the broad terrace of stone and moss and fog, but the near wall of mineral crust I had been feeding on. The water’s surface was a trembling line beneath my gaze.

And then I felt the world.

It is a hard sensation to explain to a creature that does not live its entire life under the pressure of so much water. The world did not “speak” to me; I simply felt its pattern extending into my body.

There was the heat from the seam below.  
There was the cold from the high air above.  
There was the motion of the fog between.  
There was a faint, metallic taste in every interface of water and air.

I knew that I was not the only point in the pattern.

As my body solidified, I felt the disturbance of another presence nearby—not touching me, not hovering over me, but simply there. Another heavy-wing rested on a stone ledge above the seep, their wings folded over their back like a damp blanket of stone.

They did not move to feed me. I did not require them to.

But when the pressure of the air around my seep changed—the faintest rearrangement of moisture that meant something else was drawing near—they unfolded.

They did not call out in warning. There was no cry.

Instead, their wings rose slowly, then began beating in a heavy pattern of waves that did not lift them from the stone ledge. The air around my seep thickened with the motion of their exertion. The fog above the water surface rippled and distorted, the way a still pool of water becomes unreadable when disturbed from below.

The danger—whatever it was—moved past.

In later cycles of my life, I would learn to recognize that pattern of pressure in the air: the wrong-shaped ripple, the too-straight line where a drift should be curved, the sudden absence of sound where there should be none.

But then, I only understood the result: The shield of distorted air held above me like a second ceiling of mist.

That is the closest we come to what you might call concern or care.

My existence was protected not because I was “me,” but because the pattern of the world required that what happened next to this pool of warm water and its mineral edge not be interrupted by outside forces.

After I broke free of the mineral edge, I lowered myself fully into the seep for the first time.

The water lifted me.

It is strange to say that a heavy-wing is ever truly light, but this is as close as we get to feeling the weight of the world forgiven. The warmth of the seep embraced the compact density of my body and supported the thick mass of my wings. The texture of the stone beneath the water was a smooth contour under my chest. The mosses at the edge of the pool trailed into the water like the faint skirts of the world.

Since then, I have always chosen to rest near or in the warm water when I can.

Over time, my pattern of travel has been refined by the world’s structure.

I move along a line of warm springs that trace the contour of this terrace like a vein drawn just under the skin of the mountain. Some days, I travel from the seep of my awakening to a higher, smaller seep that spills from a fracture in the stone wall above the terrace. Other days, I drift to a lower seep where the water surface is broader and the moss is darker.

I always return to places like this.

The world is not empty of life because you do not see it.

Above my head, when the fog is not too dense, I sometimes see the faintest of movements—a small, dark mass of bodies that shifts through the air like a single cloud of warmth. They are birds of a kind you would not recognize by our standards of darkness: they are swarms rather than individuals.

They are not prey to me; I do not eat the things of the air above.

Below me, in the water where my chest rests half-submerged, there are other lives.

The water holds insects that are not like the biting ones you know from lower places. They are translucent, almost colorless, and their bodies are little more than faint curves of movement within the fluid. When they hover at the surface of the seep, they are not seeking blood; they are seeking the thin layer of warmth that the seep radiates upward.

There are also larvae here.

Some of them are the young of the insects above; others are things with no equivalent name in your lexicon of animals. Their bodies are long and thin, and when they move through the water, they curve like faint threads of light tying themselves to the stone.

This is my food.

When I need to feed, I leave the deeper edge of the seep and drift toward the shallower rim where the water barely covers the moss. The closer I get, the more I feel the presence of tiny movement in the fluid around me.

I hover just above the surface, my wings beating slowly enough to disturb the water without sending it into chaotic motion. The faint pressure of my wingbeat makes the surface of the seep shift slightly, like skin under a breath.

Then I unfurl my mouth.

The lower portion of my face, which usually sits neatly tucked against my chest like a smooth plate, unfolds slightly downward and sideways. The tissue softens and expands into a flexible curve of cartilage and skin, forming a shallow net that sinks into the water.

The water flows into me.

I do not drink purely the liquid of the world; I drink the living within it.

My internal filaments are mineralized tissue that have been grown from the same metals that surrounded my birth—iron, manganese, and other things whose names you have, but whose meanings you would not recognize here.
