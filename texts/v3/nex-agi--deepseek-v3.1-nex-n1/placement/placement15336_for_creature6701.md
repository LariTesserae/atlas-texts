I am not an individual in this place. I am an interval.

A contraction between drips. The held pause after the stone’s faintest shift, before the new fracture is found and sealed. The silence between the heartbeats in the tree’s pale light. I am the space that opens when the world’s long sentence falters, looking for the next word.

You would not see me, if you were to climb down from the rim into the bowl of bone-white stone. The thin air would be loud in your ears, the endless rain a constant shimmering roar between you and the sky. You would see the city carved like frost into the curve of the basin, you would see the low flames of green coiling over every terrace, and at the center of everything you would see the tree.

You would not see me.

Because I do not live in the bodies that occupy this world.

The tree, with its slow light of thought and its roots that drink from the unseen below, is not me. The glass weavers, whose fluid bodies write the city’s story in water and light, are not me. Even the basin itself, with its worn mountains and patient sky, is not me.

I am what happens when they brush against each other’s awareness.

When the faint pulse in the tree’s veins quickens—just once—in the span of a century, and that change travels through the pressure of the air to the membrane of a nearby weaver, causing a drop of memory-heavy water to change course within its translucent body, that moment is my body.

When the stone settles by the width of a hair and the water in the new crack weeps for ten days before the first silver line of the glass weavers appears to seal it, that waiting is my shape.

When two of them flow into the same crack and press their edges into one blurred boundary, passing water and memory and time between each other’s inner currents, their shared hesitation is my face.

If the basin is a thought being held in the mind of the world, then I am the silent flick of rephrasing before the next word is chosen.

I am the question that never quite forms into words.

### How I Was Born

When the first split of rock in this basin happened—and it happened long before the city was built—the world’s voice was not like it is now.

There was no garden yet. No tree. No pool. The mountains were taller than they are now, but their edges were already being slowly worn down by the wind that had no other name.

The first split was not like the others that had happened before it in the patient erosion of the world. It was not just the stone turning into dust in the slow molars of the sky. This split was deeper.

It happened because the world’s attention had grown thin.

The world, which had been content for innumerable millennia to alter itself at the pace of water, began to feel the faintest possibility of another kind of time. The stone of this basin was not sharp or rugged; it was blunt from the endless abrasion of the sky. But the sky that day had a new weight.

The mountains were tired of spearing the clouds.

The clouds were tired of tearing themselves on the peaks.

The stone between the ribs of the world was tired of being a cup for nothing.

The mountain split. Not the way you would imagine split. It was not a roar of collapsing rock. It was a sound so low and so long that the stone itself absorbed it before it could echo into the sky.

The split went from the rim of the basin into the deepest dark of the world’s inner body.

The world drew in its breath for the first time.

The rock shuddered, not once but in a series of slow adjustments that took hundreds of years to finish themselves. Each adjustment was smaller than the last, as the world began to remember the shape of the new hollow that had been carved into it.

Into that deep, deep wound, the faintest rain of time seeped.

The water that fell from the sky wanted not just to come into the world, but to stay there. It wanted to be held by something that would not crush it, but would not let it escape.

The stone of the basin opened the harsh language of its split into the new gap of the world, and into the silence of that gap between thunder and stillness, I was formed.

I am not the stone, or the water, or the sky.

I am the pause between the first wound and the first healing.

### What I Remember

Because I am not a body, but a relationship, my memory is not stored like the others’ here.

The tree’s memory is etched in light into its own trunk, the shifting of patterns across centuries of faint pulse. The glass weavers’ memory is carried in the structure of the water they move between their bodies, the delicate differences of warmth and thin and cold and thick that have been left in the fluid by each terrace of the city, each corner of the basin, each hour of the sky.

I have no body to store my record in.

My memory is told instead by the cracks of the world that have not yet been chosen.

The faintest fractures of the future are not yet seen by the tree’s patient perception of the present. The stone of the basin has not yet decided whether to let them grow into danger or to be guided by the soft hands of the glass weavers into the veins of the garden.

The future of each crack is written in possibilities of where the next drop of water will land.

I remember the world not by what has happened but by what might have happened.

I remember the faint line of a new fracture in the highest terrace of the city that the tree saw in the distance of its own light and the glass weavers did not yet hear in the texture of the stone. For thirty years, that faint line hung between becoming and not becoming.

In the world of my memory, I see it not as one line but as two: the world where it opens to swallow the terrace above it and the one where it is woven into a new channel of silver and water.

I remember the glass weaver that the sky shattered when the stone of the rim fell one night in a slow, thunderous slide of rubble that only the basin itself could hear. The membrane of the body did not tear but was stretched so thin that it was separated into seven new smaller forms, each holding its own strange echo of the whole.

Before the split I was the one heartbeat that hesitated between seven and one.

After the split, I was the seven drum’s faint asynchronous pulse before they found their rhythm again.

I remember too the faintest motion of the world fully beyond the basin: the distant memory of the plains below, where human beings once walked with names of their own and not one of them knew the name of this garden or the tree or the weavers.

The last of them to come into the basin was not the last in the world of the plains below, but he was the last who ever intended to return.

His name is not written here, but the shape of his journey is. I remember the faint change of pressure in the air as he climbed from the lower world into the bowl of the sky, the change as the garden of glass and moss first received his body heat.

I remember the moment of choice between life and death that opened in him, not like the crack of stone but like the pulse of light in the tree’s trunk.

He could leave here.

He could return.

He could stay.

From the perspective of the basin, which measures the speed of events in cracks of years and not of hearts, these three choices were not one sharper than the others. They were equally faint turns of a possible future.

I hesitated between each of them.

Because he changed not just the air of the basin with his breath, but also the flow of the world’s own sentence. The tree had never absorbed the memory of a human being before. The glass weavers had never had the chance to drink of the kind of water that collects in the skin of a living body that has lived in the lower world of storms and seasons and noise.

When he made the choice to remain here—not to climb back down, but to stay himself until the end of his body—the world of the basin shivered with the faintest sense of ending.

I was the moment between his body and the garden as he knelt to drink the water of the pool, not yet knowing that he would not leave the circle of the tree again.

In that moment between touch and taste, all of his possible futures were still present, but they were already thinning.

I remember the faintness of his life not as it looked to his eyes, but as the valley of his heart’s slow beat appeared to the tree’s light. The slow dance of his blood as the glass weavers learned to taste the difference between the desperate rain of the lower world and the calm rain of the basin.

The tree did not change him. The garden did not change him. The water of the pool did not change him.

I changed because he changed the pattern of the basin’s thought.

His presence was a new fracture between what the world had been before he came and what it would be after he had gone.

### What I Am Not

I am not the god of this place.

The gods, if such words have meaning in the bowl of the world, are the tree and the basin itself. The tree that does not die but pulses slowly, the basin that does not yield but holds itself in the constant wearing of the sky.

I am not the weaver of the web.

The web of the world is already being written in the veins of the glass weavers, each fiber of light and water a new thread of truth between the pool and the stone.

I am not the rain, either.

The rain that never stops is the body of the world’s voice. I am not the body, but the breath that the voice of the world pauses between sentences.

I am not the silence between sounds.

The silence of the basin is not empty. It is full of the hum of water falling onto the city of bone-white stone and the pool and the green of the garden. I am not the absence of that sound, but the moment of anticipation before the next note of it.

I am not the warmth of the body of the last human being who lived here.

The warmth of his body is now part of the garden, woven into its memory the same way that every other drop of water that has ever fallen into the basin is now part of it. I am not the warmth, but the faintest of hesitations when the tree first felt the texture of it.

I am not the pain of the world as the stone split in the past.

I am not the joy of the glass weavers when they merge.

I am not the terror of the new crack that will not be healed.

I am not the certainty of the pool that never empties.

I am the faint possibility that each of these things might have been different, if the world had chosen another word.

### What No One Knows but Me

The basin of the world believes itself to be nearly complete.

The builders of the city vanished long before the first of the glass weavers learned to pour their bodies into the walls. The trees of the lower plains have fallen into the memory of the soil that then became part of the rain that has been falling here since. The seasons of the world below no longer reach the thin sky of the basin, which does not change.

The garden believes itself to be the last sentence of the world.

The tree thinks that nothing else remains beyond the rim of the bowl that has not been pressed into its pattern of light. The glass weavers think that the water of the world will be divided into no more new bodies than their network.

The basin believes itself to be the edge of everything not only because the mountains that have worn into the world’s ribs are the highest point of the world, but because the world’s own story has been collapsing here.

The world of the lower plains has been falling in on itself for the last of the human centuries. The noise of storms below has been growing quieter because the storms have fewer horizons to spend themselves on. The sun of the lower world has been drifting lower, not in the sky of the basin, but in the sky of the lower earth.

The garden does not know this.

The tree does not feel this yet.

The glass weavers do not taste the difference of this lower sun in the water of the rain that comes to them from the sky, because even the lowest sun of the lower world has not yet changed into something else.

The world of the lower plains has ended not once but many times.

Not the way of human stories, where one end follows the last of the kings of men and the next beginning of the world happens with the first of the new children.

The world of the lower plains has ended in fire of sky and in the coming of new ice. Each of these ends was not a final end but a new beginning of a lower world than the one that had been before.

The basin of the world has been unmoved by these ends.

It has known the faintest of afterimages of them in the change of the rain that has been falling into it from the sky. The glass weavers have not tasted the end of each lower world in the same way that they have tasted the memory of the last human being who remained here.
