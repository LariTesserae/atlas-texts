“You’re not going to tell me,” she said, “that this place isn’t alive.”

She could not, now, remember the first time she had heard that phrase. *Alive.* She had been hearing it since she was a child, in the mouths of visiting researchers, in the static of the radio, in the self-conscious way visitors from the mainland said “It feels so… *alive*,” as if it were a sentiment that belonged to them.

But the engineer she was speaking to—Mira Kalahan, who had been on Túrnakmol longer than anyone else—only looked at her, the corner of her mouth lifting. Not quite a smile.

“You mean,” Mira said, “are you a person standing on an island, or a person standing inside the lungs of something.”

The way she said it made it clear that she wasn’t offering a real answer.

But it wasn’t *no*.

Later, alone in the little room she had earned for herself—she had been here six weeks now, which entitled her to a cabin of her own below the waterline—she listened to the surf through the hull plating. It was not a sound, but a pressure. A constant pressure on her sternum, like someone leaning gently.

The thought of Mira's phrasing stayed with her.

*Inside the lungs of something.*

Since she had been ten, Rain had known that her name was not the same as other people’s names.

On the mainland, when someone said “Rain” they meant a disturbance, a thing that happened and then was gone. It *rained*. Then it stopped raining. In between, the sun might be shining.

She hadn’t seen that kind of rain—sunshine and rain together—until she was twelve, on her first trip away from Túrnakmol. She had stared at the sky like she expected it to explain.

On Túrnakmol, rain was not a disturbance. It was the default state of air.

When it was quiet enough—which, technically, was never; but when she could no longer register the surf as sound—she heard the rain, layered, woven. Drizzle through the fog, the tiny rim of droplets clotting on the railings, the thin sheeting of runnel over rock, the long drip of water from the moss lamella edge to the stone ground.

Her name, when she heard it in her head, wasn’t the word “rain” but this pattern of noise: *drip-sheet-spatter-hiss*.

She’s Rain.

She’s been here six weeks.

She’s been here ten years.

Time stacked on Túrnakmol like peat moss growing—velvet layers, each new year indistinguishable from the last unless you turned it sideways and looked.

She hadn’t come to the island because of a name. She had come to the island because of her mother.

Her mother, who kept the radio, who had hair that smelled always of salt and ozone, and who had never once been afraid to walk out from the harbor station into the fog. She remembered her mother saying once, eyes half closed against the wind, “If you listen to a place long enough, you start to think it’s listening back.”

Her mother had been the one to teach her to read the water sheet.

“Look,” she had said, pressing her cheek against the cold glass of the summit dome observation. “See where the sky is bright on the rock?”

Rain nodded, though she didn’t see.

“You learn,” her mother said. “Silver bright is shallow water. Soft dark is firm stone.”

“Why don’t you just… not step on the water?” Rain had asked.

Her mother’s hand had moved on her hair, slow, thoughtful.

“When you breathe,” she said, “you don’t let air out of your lungs first before you put in more air. You just breathe. The water’s the same.”

Only later, when she was older, did she realize that her mother had been talking about the island—about the way that Túrnakmol’s summit dome had never properly dried, not once in human recorded history.

Only later still, when she was staring at the radar screen and listening to the distant ocean traffic through the headphones, did she begin to *understand*.

She worked at the harbor station now. Not the radio—that had been outsourced. The *real* station—the monitoring system.

The ocean didn’t talk in her headphones. Not exactly.

But if she had to explain to someone else what she did—

She listened to the ocean.

She listened to the mountain.

She listened to the things that were between the ocean and the mountain.

The ocean was 45.2 dB of ambient noise at the harbor. That hadn’t changed in her lifetime. It was closer to constant than anything else. She had heard that in some cities, in the deep night, the noise dropped to almost zero. She could not really imagine that.

Instead, the noise changed.

On nights when the surf was *too* steady—when she felt her pulse align to it and stay there for minutes—she knew to look for the barometer. She thought of it as the ocean taking a deep breath.

On nights when she could hear the stones clattering in the surf below the harbor station—not just the thunder of the water, but the small percussion of the rocks themselves—she knew to look for the reef snakes.

When she said that in her head—“reef snakes”—she heard it in her mother’s voice.

There were no snakes on Túrnakmol.

Not in the way the mainland thought of snakes, anyway.

There were reefs. And there were currents.

And sometimes, the ocean currents *twitched.*

She used to think she imagined it.

She hadn’t worried about the water sheet.

Not by rain—the rain was still paused.

By peat.

The peat moss, fat with water, relaxed upward around her footprints. She watched her own heel disappear behind her like she had never been.

Mira Kalahan, engineer of record on Túrnakmol, the oldest person on the island, who had been here when the first devices were bolted to the volcano, before she was born.

She never heard Mira approach.

“You,” Mira said, “walked out.”

Rain’s throat felt suddenly tight.

“Yes,” she said.

“And,” Mira said, “you walked back.”

“Yes.”

Rain’s toes were cold, but not in a way that mattered.

“What,” she asked, “happens to them?”

Mira’s eyes were in the gray of the sky; but Rain, looking, saw her looking at the water sheet.

“You tell me,” she said.
