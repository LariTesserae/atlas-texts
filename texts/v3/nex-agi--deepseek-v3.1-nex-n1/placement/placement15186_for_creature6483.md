The air in your apartment never tastes quite right anymore.

It’s a still, sour taste, like the air itself has been sitting in the same room for days and has gone stale with the breath of people who aren’t there anymore. You notice it when you wake—before your eyes open, before you remember the layout of this room, the city outside your window, before you remember your own name. You lie on the mattress on the floor, the blanket stiff under your chin, and you taste the room like you’re testing a liquid.

It’s not the smell of dust or garbage or the sweat of your own body. That part you’re used to. This is something else: the air has grown thick, like water that’s been boiled too long and left to cool.

You’ve always been the one who notices.

That’s how you found the door the first time, or how it found you. You noticed the alleyway behind the old building was darker than the other alleys—darker than it should have been. There were no lights back there, no dumpsters, no homeless encampments. Just darkness, and a faint unevenness in the pavement where the concrete seemed to sag into some unseen mouth below the city.

At first, you didn’t tell the others. Not because you were hiding it, but because you didn’t know how to say what you sensed without sounding like a person whose edges had already gone soft.

But you told them.

You told them about the feeling of pressure under your feet when you stood there too long, like the pavement was breathing. You told them about the faint draft of warm air crawling up your legs from the cracks between the slabs. You said that when you crouched and touched the concrete, you could feel the hair on your arms lift—not with fear, but with something else.

“You’re imagining,” they said. “It’s just a cold spot.”

You’re tired of that word. “Just.” As though everything is just something else.

Still, you didn’t go right then. You waited days. You let yourself pretend to be in the same world they all think they’re in—the world of jobs and bills and crowded buses, the world where air tastes like dust and exhaust and nothing else.

But you kept dreaming of heat.

Simple heat: nothing burning, nothing torturous. Just the heat behind your ribs when you’re tired, the heat in your hands when you’re nervous, the heat of your own breath condensing in the small space between your cheek and the pillow. You woke with your skin clammy and dry at the same time, like you’d been sweating somewhere inside your body where sweat has never had to go.

Finally you went back, alone, at twilight. When you’d stepped far enough into the alley’s shadow that you couldn’t hear the traffic behind you anymore, you found the stairway.

It wasn’t hidden, exactly. There was no clever lock, no invisible door. There was a raw metal grate set into the pavement like someone had cut a hole in the city’s body and just covered it over.
