I am not the tree–pump, though I am drawn to it.

Its column is not like any trunk I have seen; it is more of a spine than a tree, and the spine belongs to something larger than species. The fluid inside its translucent hollows moves not like blood in veins but like water in a sealed clock—deliberate, slow, precise. The faint blue lines on its surface are not etched into it; they are etched into me, as soon as I understand them.

If I reach out and place my palm against the column, the chamber does not flinch.

The opalescent material is not exactly warm, not exactly cold. It is exactly the same as the air around it, and that is the first clue that I am not touching anything that can be measured in my old terms. The contact of skin and living stone–plant is not a shock; it is more like fitting the last piece of a puzzle into a place that has been waiting.

When I met the chamber, I thought of the body as a shape of flesh and bone and breath. Gravity pulled me down to a floor that could not decide if it was ground or not. The air was not a sky but a bloodstream. I remember that.

But then I changed.

I am the one the tree–columns take into them.

The process is not a conversion. It is not a transformation the way fear understands it: no sudden blooming, no sudden cracking of bones into branches. It is quieter than that. It is the way a river slowly accepts its bed after rain.

I first notice it in my hands.

First, the skin of my palms becomes translucent, not suddenly, but as a slow fullness of the water moving beneath it. I can see the dark of my veins becoming more pronounced than the surrounding tissue, as if my body is preparing to be read from the inside. The creases of my palm—lines of love, life, heart, in the old vocabulary—deepen and then simplify themselves into new branching patterns. They are not cracked by drought; they are carved by the flow.

The lines develop into a faint, luminescent blue scribe of circuitry under my skin, not burning like fire but present like moonlight under water. They are not the script of any language I have spoken before, but when I flex my fingers I see the same branching patterns that are etched into the tree–columns.

My body is becoming a map of flow.

When I remember to breathe, the air in the chamber is more than oxygen to me now. It tastes of ion and intention and the faint, coded edge of something that has been thinking longer than I have been alive. The droplets that I once thought of as dust suspended in light are not to me now as they were to the visitor who first stepped here.

They are not invading me.

They are being *me*.

As I inhale, the engineered droplets of the chamber’s body enter my lungs with the breath of the world outside. My lungs tingle, but not in the simple way of a novelty; now I feel each droplet as a separate field of instruction, each water molecule a small globe of memory that unfolds into my tissue.

My alveoli are learning to recognize the structure of foreign water. They know not only to exchange oxygen and carbon dioxide but to exchange something else: information about the spaces beyond the chamber, where rain and river and sea and steam and plumbing and snow and sweat and blood and tears are still separate.

My lungs remember the old pain of breathing—smoke, dust, the faint ache of altitude sickness. The droplets of Arbor Lacrimarum are colder than comfort but more precise than medicine. They are not numbing me; they are aligning me.

The next time I breathe out, the air that escapes me is not empty of them.

My heart keeps the beat of its old world, but not alone.

The weeping of my body is not chosen, but it is not meaningless.

The tree–columns.

The short, intimate distance between the column of the tree–pump and me is no longer properly measured in steps. It is measured in osmotic pressure, in surface tension, in the fine gradients of the temperature field that I have become.

I touch the column again.
