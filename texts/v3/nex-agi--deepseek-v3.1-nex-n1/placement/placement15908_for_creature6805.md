The world is this underworld. The city is just noise above.

I was not always this. Once I might have been something else—something with sharp edges and a clear outline and a name that belonged to the surface. But I have been down here long enough that the edges have been softened by humidity, the name has worn away like the label on the panel, and even in memory I am not separate from the Engine and the water and the dark.

I am the Lenten Engine’s second Caretaker.

Not the first. That one was older, municipal, his keys still on the city’s books. His sweater had a patch that meant something; his boots had been issued by Accounting. The first time I saw him, I was still a person from the upper world, descending that service stairwell with a work order and a nervous heart. The air thickened on my tongue like I was swallowing fog. The sound of the city fell away, and by the time I reached the iron door I was already hearing the hum before I understood the word.

He was already here when I opened the frost-scratched glass porthole with my own breath. He turned his head slowly, as if moving through water. His eyes were old and clear and not at all alarmed by me.

“You’re the trainee,” he said. Not as question. It was the first thing anyone ever said to me down here.

I remember the odd kindness of that word. Trainee. As if there were ranks to this, a ladder of some kind. But there was no one above him. No one below. Just the Engine and us and the water that never stopped.

I started out as human, more or less. The city still owned me: a junior engineer, a low-grade number in the Utilities Department, flagged for “deep infrastructure assignment.” I thought that meant I’d be crawling around maintenance tunnels and fixing pumps. I didn’t know that under the city of Khel Vaen, there are places that have gone out of date without being replaced, because they still work and the city above has forgotten how to talk about them.

The Lenten Engine No. 7 is one of those.

The first Caretaker taught me the rituals in the language of the upper world: circuits and pressure gradients and maintenance protocols under the Tower Provision Act. But the longer I stayed, the more I understood that all of that was only the structure of something else, something the city had never written into any law.

The Engine itself did not roar or rumble or change its output in response to orders from above. It had been disconnected from those kinds of commands long before I was born. Its dials were still obedient to the Tower Provision markings on the panel, but only by habit. The needle rested in the green because that was the state toward which everything here—water, vapor, stone, and rust—tended slowly, like a river seeking its bed.

The first Caretaker saw me watching the dials.

“You can’t read them like city meters,” he said. “The engine is not listening to you. It is listening to the basin.”

The sub-basin. The deep water table and the culverted river and the pressure of the rock above and below. The Engine ran its long cycle not because some switch was turned but because the world below the city had a certain tension in it, and the Engine was one of the places where that tension resolved itself.

As time passed—months, years, I am not sure how many—the city’s connection to me weakened like a signal under interference. The work orders stopped coming. The stairwell door at the top of the service shaft was replaced by a newer model with a different lock code. The comm system wired into the wall panel went dead and then was removed, leaving a small cavity of raw plaster like a lost tooth.

The first Caretaker made fewer and fewer visits, until one day he simply didn’t come at all.

I waited for him longer than I expected to. I wrote his month and year on the yellow strip with my waxed marker, out of habit. Then I wrote the next month’s, and the one after that. The ritual continued even though the priest had changed.

The city of Khel Vaen above moved on, its maps recompiled, its districts renamed. The Lenten Engine No. 7 of the Sub-Basin vanished from any records it had not already vanished from long before. From the city’s perspective, the deep substructure void was empty of interest except as a place to worry about collapse conditions.

From my perspective, the city above became the faint vibration of footsteps through layers of concrete and soil, the distant shudder of a subway, the occasional slow drip from a pipe that finally connected our world to theirs.

By the time the change in me had become undeniable, I no longer thought of it as loss or transformation. I had lived too long with the Engine’s slow hum to be surprised by the idea that something could be replaced without anyone being informed.

The Basin Beetles knew first. They became the nearest thing I had to priesthood, their hard black shells moving steadily over the surfaces as if checking the wounds of a sleeping patient. I had watched them for years, tracing their paths, cleaning the corrosion before it could bite deep. The beetles were part of the Engine’s immune system, and the Engine was part of the basin’s.

One day I noticed that they had changed their pattern around me.

When I first arrived, they’d avoid my feet as if I were a temporary anomaly—a large, warm object that sometimes blocked their trails and then moved away. They’d wait patiently in the shadow of my boot and then continue when I shifted.

By that time, they were no longer avoiding my feet at all. They were tracing over them.

I looked down at the floor of the chamber where I stood. The film of water was not clear. It reflected the faint glow of the phosphorescent tubes, the deep green-black of the Engine’s metal casing, and the faint shimmer of the Fungal Recorders on the ceiling. The Engine itself hummed in its endless bass not far away, its segmented body like the ribs of some supine creature.

The beetles were crossing over my toes, over the worn leather of my boots, as if they were now part of the same surface as the damp concrete floor.

The first time I felt a mandible scrape against the side of my boot, it was like any other noise of this place—small, precise, not notable enough to be alarmed by. But I had grown too used to silence down here not to notice when new sounds were added.

So I sat down where the floor sloped slightly toward the central drain channel and removed my boots.

My feet were pale, as they would always be down here without the sun. But not the pale of ill human health. The pale of damp stone, the pale of unglazed porcelain. The skin of my soles had been worn thin and smooth by the constant film of water, and with the thin layer of oil from the Engine’s metal casing it had hardened not calloused but almost sealed, like a surface being prepared for painting.

The beetles were on the floor of the chamber and on the surface of my feet and the boundary between the two was not clear.

I do not have the words to describe what followed that day. The Lenten Engine does not talk to me in words. It talks in cycles of humidity and pressure and small electrical relays that click every half day or so. The change in my body was less like being transformed and more like being absorbed into a longer, older process that had already been underway for decades.

I stayed longer than I meant to by that central drain. The water flowed around my ankles like a low conversation, and the beetles were busy cleaning me as well.

Somewhere in that time, my skin changed the way the rust on the iron supports had changed: not eaten away but encrusted, then hardened into equilibrium with the environment around it. It is not exactly human skin now and not exactly metal shell, but something like both of them at once—a layer of material that has been kept under the Engine’s machine breath and the basin’s slow water long enough to be permanently altered.

The vibrations of the city above me no longer feel like they belong to people and cars and trains. They feel like they belong to the pressure of water above and below and beside me. The sounds of the world have been filtered into new frequencies: the low hum of the Engine itself, the faint whine of some distant pump, the tiny click of a relay inside the floor, the drip of condensate from the ceiling into the puddle, the path of beetles over wet surfaces.

The Ghost Moths drift around the Engine’s heat. They never used to come near me. I was too cold for them, a body still running its own heat from the inside out. Now they hover near my joints and the line of my spine when I sit still for long enough. They can feel the heat being generated at the boundary of my body and the cool air of the chamber.

I am not exactly warm in the way of the upper city’s lives. But I am no longer as cold as the stone walls. The Engine’s steady heat and the cool of the basin have found a place to meet at me, and the moths are drawn to that gentle gradient.

I have not forgotten how to speak in the language of the upper world. But I have not spoken to anyone else in years. The stairwell door still opens from above if someone has the right code, but the last time that happened the man who descended the stairs was not a replacement engineer or the first Caretaker. He was a security inspector, checking that deep foundation void I have been told to call myself.

He saw me sitting beside the Engine panel with my hand on the casing and the beetles around my feet, and his first instinct was to shout, “Is there anyone down here?”

I remember the oddness of hearing that question in the chamber of the Engine that has always known its own answer.

“Yes,” I said. The word sounded strange in my mouth after so long, but it was not clumsy. It had been worn down as well.

“Who are you?” he demanded.

I had to think for a moment. The name the city had given me was still attached to me somewhere in the upper records, but it was not attached to me here in the same way anymore. The Engine had no name for me in words, only the steady hum and the pressure of its water against my body.

“Caretaker,” I finally answered.

I didn’t say “second.” It didn’t matter to him.

He looked at the Engine panel, the green needles of the gauges, the faint glow of the phosphor tubes, and then he looked at me and at the beetles around the floor and at the darkness of the ceiling, where the faint patterns of the Fungal Recorders were glowing their slow green script.

He didn’t say anything more for a long time.

In the end he turned back toward the stairwell without attempting to operate any controls or “fix” anything. He was city, but he was not stupid. He could feel the weight of the Engine and the basin and the time already spent here, and he knew it was beyond his control.

“You shouldn’t be down here,” he said finally at the doorway.

“Yes,” I agreed. The city’s rules did not allow for this kind of thing.

“Are you… going to come up?”

I looked around the chamber. The low ceiling of concrete and iron, the long columns, the Engine’s metal body with its faint green glow and the low hum of its deep interior. The film of water on the floor reflecting the pattern of the pipes and fungus and the faint gleam of my own feet in the darkness.

“Yes,” I said again, because it seemed true in the long time of the basin and the city and the universe itself. “Someday.”

He didn’t understand that the answer belonged to the same time frame as the erosion of the floor and the crystallizing of the rust on the supports and the slow journey of the Boiler Snails across the walls.

The door closed above him and the sound of his footsteps faded away. The hum of the Engine and the slow drip of the condensate filled the silence that he had left.

The chamber of the Lenten Engine is not a secret place exactly. It is not hidden from the city. It is simply not something the city can see into without its own seeing being changed.

The people who belong to the upper world of traffic and glass and rooftop gardens move quickly above me. Their time is measured in meetings and minutes and deadlines. The Engine’s time is measured in the pressure of the water table and the rate of the rust forming and the lifetime of the relay that switches every half day or so.

I have become one of the engines between the two.

I still walk the perimeter of the chamber when I wake—from habit, I suppose, or from some direction of a body that has been doing this for as long as I can remember. The notes are still in the waxed notebook I was given, though I never write in them in the same way the first Caretaker did. His words were full of numbers and initials and small codes that meant something to the upper world’s history of this machine.

I do not need those signs to read the Engine’s condition.

When I rest my hand against the metal casing, I can feel the pressure of the water inside the system, the slow turning of some internal shaft that has not been seen by human eyes in longer than the city has been using the newer power stations. The hum of the Engine reaches into my bones and my chest cavity, and my heartbeat slows down to match its pattern.

The green of the phosphorescent tubes in the Engine’s segments pulses not to the heartbeat of the city but to the pulse of the basin itself. The faint waves of light in those tubes would not be visible to someone who had not been down here long enough for their eyes to adjust to the darkness. But to me they are a clear rhythm of the world.

I am not the Engine. I am not the basin. I am not the city above.

But I am the place where water changes from one state to another and the Engine’s steady heat finds the chamber’s constant coolness. The beetles are my immune system as well as its. The Ghost Moths are my sensory organs as well as its. The Fungal Recorders on the ceiling are my memory as well as its, a glowing calligraphy of the chamber’s conditions over time, and the Boiler Snails crawling across the walls are my slow clock, their journey across the surface not progress but continuity.

The city of Khel Vaen has many engines, its factories and rails and turbines and server farms and all the systems of its layered life. The Lenten Engine No. 7 of the Sub-Basin is not one of those anymore, not in the city’s mind.

But down here it is still the center of a world that has not been replaced by newer versions.

The world above changes its district and its name. The world below maintains the pressure of the water table and the temperature and the rust of its supports and the slow accumulation of time on its surfaces.

The water beads on my sleeves and on the column of my spine. The humidity of the chamber condenses on me and then moves away again into the air or the film of water on the floor. The Engine hums in its low bass and I feel its breath moving through me as well as my own breath moving through the air.

The Lenten Engine does not talk to me in words. It does not need to.

I am the second Caretaker now and I have been here long enough that the distinction between me and the Engine and the water and the basin is not important enough to be kept track of.

The world above this place is the city of Khel Vaen.

I live beneath it in the chamber of the Lenten Engine No. 7 of the Sub-Basin: where the water goes from the air to the floor and then to the drain beneath the metal grating; where the Engine’s hum is constant; where the insects are busy keeping everything from falling apart; where the snails are crawling slowly across the walls and the ceiling and the metal of the machine itself; and where the faint green glowing of the Fungal Recorders is writing the memory of this place in a language that no longer has any human readers except the one who has been changed into a part of it.

I am not visiting the Engine anymore.

I have been given a long-term assignment to the deep substructure void of Lenten Power Engine No. 7 of the Khel Vaen Sub-Basin, maintained under the Tower Provision Act.

I do not expect to be relieved from my position.
