Here, in the low warmth before dawn, I am the salt-script.

You will not find me in the city’s crowded heart, where the walls lean close and the lanes coil like capillaries in flesh. I do not live in the hollowed rooms where the Jelibeth-dwellers sleep, or in the soft chambers where the mound-beasts shift their slow foundations. I am not one of the creatures that creep the walls like thoughts, or the low-slung beasts whose backs are plazas yet unborn.

I live on the outside edges of Jelibeth, where the city gums the horizon into itself.

The ground is not yet sure of itself out here. It lifts in faint hummocks, pale clay-and-chalk knuckles pushing up under the world’s skin, tentative as the first pressure of a new idea. The roads are not yet roads; they are the faint suggestion of where roads might someday be. The mounds of Jelibeth’s growth hump gently beneath the stone and lime and bone-root buildings, half-asleep, still deciding whether to become slopes or plains or something else entirely.

It is on this uncertain skin that I appear.

By night, when the air is thick but not heavy with any moisture we’d recognize as water, I am almost nothing. A coolness in the stone, a gathering of minute expectation on the surface of chalk walls and low parapets and the uneven edges of new foundations. The air gives itself to me slowly, the same faint breath that ruffles the edges of the Jelibeth people’s pale membranes, their garments only half cloth. The wind is not fierce enough to drive anything; it is more like a slow turning of the world’s lungs. In that sigh, when the temperature slips just so, I condense.

I form in thin, invisible films across the stone, the slightest film of moisture: not enough to drip, not enough to run. But enough to taste the stone itself. The chalk walls are faintly salty from the beginning of time—from whatever messy, unfinished chemistry of the world’s making left this place without river or rain. That salt is in me from the moment of my birth.

When dawn is still a suggestion, the horizon only a vague lightening of the milk haze, the city begins to breathe differently. The heat in the ground below and the cool air above shift just a little more apart from each other. The thin film I am made of starts to pull back into the air, leaving behind what it cannot carry away: the salt of the stone, the stain of its own faint presence.

Line by tentative line, I write myself.

The Jelibeth children see me first.

They are not tethered to inner rooms the way their adults are. Their bodies are smaller and lighter, the way everything new is smaller and lighter. The city has not yet fully decided how to hold them, so they go where the pressure is least—out to the edges, where the ground is still half-forming and the walls of the outer wards are still practicing their shape.

At dawn, they run to the low, humped shoulders of the infant hillsides, where the cobbles of the outer streets are not yet higher than the ground itself. The walls of Jelibeth rise out of the same flat surface as these rising mounds, as if the city and the earth are pushing up teeth together from the same jawbone. The children are the right size to play over these teeth without being broken by them.

They see me first because they are the first to look. The adults of Jelibeth do not look to the edges. They settle into quiet rooms and hollowed thresholds; they are part of a more interior pressure, the slow negotiation of where chambers can be carved without disturbing the mound-beasts beneath. The children are not yet burdened by that negotiation. They are still learning the language of absence and slackening hands.

And so, when I appear, faint and white as a secret half-scratched into the walls of the outer wards, it is the children who find me.

“Look,” one will murmur, not shouting, because shouting isn’t known here yet. “The salt is writing.”

They press small fingers to me, tracing a faint trail of white where moisture has been and gone. The line of salt follows a shallow seam of stone where a new wall was not quite perfectly joined to an old foundation. The children are quiet enough to hear the faintest of sounds beneath their touch—not water dripping, but stone settling, as the city chews itself into new shape.

I am not one word or one sign. I am a thousand broken scripts.

On one wall, I might follow the irregular gap of a poorly sealed joint where the plaster did not take to the chalk block. The line of salt is thin, precise as a nerve drawn in white chalk dust. It will be there for a few hours after the sun has risen, and then the day’s warmth—not harsh, but persistent—will loosen the bond of salt to stone and the air will take me back.

On another surface, an out-thrust parapet where the outer wall is still blunt and unfinished, I might be a faint halo of white stain around the stones where the night’s faint breath lingered longer. The circle of me is not quite perfect, because nothing is. It is merely where the air hesitated.

I am always being written and erased and written again.

The adults of Jelibeth, when they happen past me, do not stop to read me. They are not concerned with what the world writes on its walls. They are the quiet pressure gradients of the city’s balance, moving their bodies from one chamber to another as the mound-beasts beneath them decide where a new street or courtyard needs to unfold. When they glance at me, it is with the same vague attention they might give to the color of the sky before they walk away from the room they will soon leave behind.

I am not their story. I am the smallest of the city’s motions.

The mound-beasts ignore me entirely. Their senses are tuned to deeper pressures than the surface salts of walls. They are aware of the weight of stone above them, the slow shifting of sap under the canals of gelatinous green, the faint tension of where rooms are hollowed out and where they are not yet born. Their feet are not hooves or claws but broad, soft pads that leave no mark of tread on the cobbles they themselves wear down. The routes of the mound-beasts take them through the city like a slow pulse through a body, and their passing resets the script of me—sometimes by rubbing away the plaster on which I have written, sometimes by settling the stones of the wall back into stillness.

I am rewritten by every creature that touches the walls.

The smaller, fur-matted things that creep the sides of the houses like vines do not behave like animals in the old sense. They do not leave tracks of paws or claws in the dust because there is no dust to speak of. The ground of Jelibeth is not dry dust; it is the faint slick of sap and gel and stone, and their fur is not hair but a dense, mossy down that matches the walls in color. When they climb, they leave faint, sticky trails of themselves behind, not unlike some of the faint lines of my own staining.

The two of us sometimes cross: a faint white salt-scrip meeting a faint, darker, damp line of their passage. The result is not a message in any language that their children or the adults would understand; but the city feels the crossing of us, and it adjusts the pressure of its sap, the unfolding of its walls, by a minuscule amount none of them would ever be able to name.

At night, when the world cools back toward the low warmth in the ground, the rhythm of Jelibeth slows. The mound-beasts sleep standing still, their backs like flat plazas that do not yet know what they will be asked to carry. The creatures on the walls are quieter still, their fur darkening to match the shadowed plaster of the eaves. The Jelibeth people, in their inner rooms, murmur softly to each other in the low, clicking speech of their teeth, like chalk settling. The roof-mats of the city above them breathe softly: the fronds of pale green, the silvered undersides of leaves, exhaling the faintest of tastes into the air.

I am not present at night. Not exactly. I am waiting.

The air itself is waiting, too. It carries the faint scent of root and metal—not quite water, but something like it, locked in stone and sap and bone. I am part of that promise, the salt that has been drawn from the stone before and will be drawn again. When the temperature is exactly balanced between the ground’s remembering heat and the sky’s faint, distant cool, I fall once more onto the surfaces of the walls.

This time, perhaps, I will be different.

The city is not finished yet. The geology of this place is not wind and rain and river carving; it is the slow internal pressure of new chambers hollowed from within, of mound-beasts moving their roads into place, of children being lifted without harm by the infant hillsides that the city itself has raised. The stone of Jelibeth is still deciding how to be stone.

I am the salt in its throat.
