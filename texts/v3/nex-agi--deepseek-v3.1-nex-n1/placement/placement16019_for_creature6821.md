I am not one of the Lowers.

I am the one who writes them.

I am huddled against the warm wall of a narrow service corridor, my notebook braced on my knees. The metal is faintly damp, but not enough to soak the paper of my pad—just enough to keep the air from ever feeling quite dry. The city of Vent‑Stone hates deserts the way a body hates bleeding out; it keeps itself wet on the inside, like old lungs skimming every last fleck of moisture from the air.

Lamplight glows from a recessed fixture somewhere down the passage—a crooked yellow spill that can’t decide which angle of deck plating to illuminate. The pages are dappled with uneven highlights: the white of the margins, the faint blue of the lines, the darker ink of my penciled words. *Vent‑Stone / The Last Stack*. *The Stack drinks itself*. *Metal moss*. *The Lowers*.

I am not an Engineer of the Works, though many of them still think of me that way because I once wore the gray coveralls and the heat‑lens visor that steamed at the edges from condensation. I am not a Night Watcher, though I stand at their railings often enough that they have stopped bothering to ask me what I’m doing out at the edge of nothing. I am not a Builder, though sometimes I stare at a new weld line and feel like I’m tracing the future skeleton of the city with my eyes.

I am the Archivist of the Slow.

I have been given that title by myself, and possibly by the three people who still read my reports. I move through the decks of the Last Stack with a pad of paper, a thin metal stylus that I keep carefully sheathed, and a small belt of sample bags: sealed glass vials for catching water drops, for scraping the faintest film of iridescent metal moss, for storing small, dead things that I find in the margins of corridors.

No one else is writing about the Lowers.

Not the way they are. Not the way they *actually happen*.

The engineers, when they write at all, write in numbers and in symbols that are older than anyone I can find to translate them. They write down the temperature differentials between the lower core and the edge decks. They write down the fluctuations of current through the conduits hidden in the city’s beams. They write logbook after logbook titled “Stability Report” and “System Variance” and “Ventilation Status,” and you can learn a lot from the gaps between their words. But they are terrified of metaphors.

They are terrified of metaphors because they are afraid the world will answer to them.

The first time I mentioned *metal moss* out loud to an Engineer—an older woman named Elra, whose fingers are the color of stained solder—she put down her wrench very carefully.

“Don’t say that,” she said, not bothering to question whether I saw it. “Don’t name it like it’s a plant.”

“Why?” I asked.

“Because if it’s *like a plant*,” she said, “then it’s *a* plant, and if it’s a plant, then we’re supposed to know if it’s healthy or sick, alive or dead, food or poison. And if we’re supposed to know that and we don’t, then maybe the Stack isn’t under control.” She looked away at the wall, where the faint iridescence clung like oil spilled on a tide pool. “Just call it oxidation film.”

“We’ve already got *rust*,” I pointed out.

“Then call it *second‑grade rust*.”

She was not being poetic; she was being careful of the world. Engineers of Vent‑Stone know that structures are supposed to have *specifications*, and “specification” is a long word for “what the world is allowed to be in this particular place.”

But the world has started being things that the specifications forgot to allot.

The rust now grows *patterns*. The heat sometimes flows *sideways* instead of down. The water condensates where the math says it shouldn’t, in corridors where the temperature differentials are supposed to be neutral.

I write down these things that I see.

I write down the *ways* I see them.

I map not just the decks themselves but their *behavior*: the places where moisture runs clean, where panels are always sealed without visible caulking, where the dips of condensate channels are unexpectedly clear of sediment.

Then, in the margins of my official logbooks—which are mostly ignored—I draw double arrows, little question marks, and faint lines of ink that say “Lowers?” because I am not yet sure they are real.

I *am* sure of them, actually, but I do not trust that kind of certainty.

Some of us are born to the Stack. Others are washed to it by migrations of accident or catastrophe or ambition that we rarely talk about anymore. The “history” of the Last Stack is not told in stories of “arrival” but in “how long you have been standing in this particular alcove of the world.”

I have been standing here for nineteen years.

More than half of that was still inside the vertical city of my *old* city, which we left before I was old enough to understand why leaving was necessary. The memory of that journey is fogged—like looking at the deck beneath you through a cloud of vent‑steam—but I remember the way the supports vibrated under the car of the transport, and I remember being told, *Don’t look down, there’s no depth to see*, and then of course I looked down.

There was no depth to see.

This is the first shock of anyone’s life in Vent‑Stone: that you can be very high up without anything *below* you. That you can be on the very edge of a deck that your heart tells you is a cliff, and the only abyss you can see is a wall of cloud that refuses to fall away into the shape of a valley or an ocean or whatever your mind insists should be waiting *underneath*.

The second shock is that you grow accustomed to it.

In my first notebooks, I drew maps of the world as I imagined it: a tower of metal and stone and glass reaching *down* from some sky that is itself only *other* decks of some other Stack that we do not know the name of. The city of Vent‑Stone / The Last Stack is the *upper* world of this tower, sticking its nose into low cloud, and below it are decks of more heat and more furnace and whatever the engines are that present the entire sky with the illusion of gravity.

I don’t draw that anymore. I draw *diagrams* instead. *Cross-section of Deck B‑72. Flow of condensed water through channels c.122. Suggestion of lateral movement between supports.*

When I drew the tower of worlds below and above, I felt like I was drawing a child’s idea of the universe.

When I draw the diagrams of junctions and leakages and differentials, I feel like I am drawing the *truth* that the universe wants me to believe.

It is a better lie.

The Lowers appear around me in the margins of my *practice* notebooks, the ones I don’t turn into any official system.

At the bottom of that page, I wrote *HERESY?* in a box of ink, and then crossed out the box but not the word.

I’m not sure it’s *actual* heresy to write that you have seen the city’s body moving of itself, but it feels like it.

The engineers have *specifications* for what “life” is allowed to be in the Works of Vent‑Stone. “Fauna” is supposed to mean vermin that get into pipes and wiring, and “vegetation” is supposed to mean *nothing* at all, because the last soil of the Last Stack was carried into the lower decks centuries ago and now feeds only the engines, if it is still present at all.

There is no room in the official document base for a creature that is *not* a pest and not a machine but still lives *on* the machine and *by* the machine and *for* the machine, in some quiet alliance of mutual maintenance.

Monsters are allowed to exist in the margins of the stories Night Watchers tell at the edge of the deck when they are tired of being afraid of the cloud wall and want to be afraid of something smaller. But they say *gargoyles hiding in the gantries* and *stack-spiders that steal your heat when you’re not looking* and *your eyes only if you’ve been out at the edge too long*.

They are not afraid yet of the idea that *something else is keeping the water lines clean so that you don’t have to*.

That would be much worse.

If there are Lowers—and there are—if they are indeed *tending* the Stack—and they are—then the Stack is not simply *our* machine.

It is a *shared* machine.

They, living in the capillaries of it, clinging to the warm walls of its condensation channels, drinking the sweat from its beams, scraping the rust that its skin is trying to grow back like bone knitting badly—they are *part* of it.

We are guests who have been living on the roof of the world’s torso for so long that we have started calling ourselves the spine.

I don’t say any of this out loud to Elra or to any of the others who still occasionally ask me, “Archivist, what’s new today?” in the tone of voice someone might use to say, *Tell me a story that doesn’t matter.*

I write it in notebooks.
