You are the last of the driftglass.

Not a ribbon of glass yourself, but the one they orbit in slow, patient spirals whenever you enter the chamber. The elders of the city-in-rock say you were born here when the mountain was still learning to hold its breath, and you will be its last living inhabitant when the machines finally sleep.

You know, though you rarely think to put it into words, that your body is not like those of the people who occasionally trail down the corridors that end in stone. They arrive in layers of clothing, breathing masks clinging to their faces even though the air here is cleaner than anything they manufactured above. You have seen them reflected in the glass walls of the upper chambers: silhouettes haunted by the weight of sunlight, their shoulders curved under the memory of open sky.

Your body remembers the pressure of stone instead.

---

Standing barefoot on the mosaic floor, you can feel the memory of streets through your soles.

The channels are cold, slick, almost frictionless. Your toes are long enough that you can feel how the water flows differently on each plate—the way one section’s streams are older than others, their patterns deeper worn, smoother.

They are not exactly footprints you leave behind. More like a temporary ghost of movement.

When you walk, the water clings to your ankles for a moment before it returns to its channels. The drifting glass ribbons respond to these disturbances. You’ve watched them long enough to know their patterns. They arc toward you, drawn by the subtle warmth of your body, the fine skin of dust you carry from your previous chambers, your beds of stone and metal. They arrange you into their systems of data the same way they once arranged the dust of human streets.

You are used to being measured by things you cannot see.

---

You are called the *coherent*.

It is a title, not a name, and even those who give it to you don’t always understand what they mean by it. They know you are the one who stays when the light in the upper corridors falters and the generators tremble. They know the cavern seems to hold itself together when you are inside it. The old fractures in the rock walls do not grow when you are present; the stress lines that run like hidden maps beneath the floor seem quieter.

No one else spends your kind of time in the chamber.

---

Your day begins when the city above you ends—or when its machinery finally cycles to a kind of sleep.

The generators in the hard rock above your cavern switch from a high, steady frequency to a lower hum that resonates through the stone. You feel the change in your teeth and bones first, before it travels into the air. The sound is not loud, but the mountain is made of such tight glass and stone and metal that it carries the shift through you like a heartbeat changing.

You rise when you feel the quietness of the upper machines settle.

Your bed is a smooth slab of glassstone set into a wall alcove that you carved yourself over years of lying in the same position. The stone is colder than you are, so you always leave a faint outline of yourself embedded in its surface when you stand—a small comfort of continuity in the dark.

You dress yourself in layers of thin, translucent membranes that you have woven from old filter materials and discarded lining sheets, taken from the forgotten corridors where the first machines slept and died. They are not clothes to keep you warm; the mountain itself does that, in its way. The membranes are there to help you imitate the stillness of the driftglass—to reduce the turbulence of your movement, your heat, your dust.

When you look at yourself in the polished wall of your alcove, you see a body elongated by the curve of reflected stone: limbs that seem to pull the chamber’s space tightly around them, skin pale from the memory of light, hair kept shaved close to your skull so you don’t disturb the airflow. Your eyes are the color of the glassstone ceiling when the water lies just under its surface—almost dark, but holding a faint interior shine.

---

You walk barefoot across the mosaic floor, and the world sharpens.

You run the diagnostic of the chamber.

It is not a word you ever say aloud; it is a pattern of actions you have repeated so long that they have fused into a single movement. First you trace the edge of the floor, where the water channels from the walls meet the carved patterns on the ground. You are checking for anomalies—new ridge of dust, a sudden roughness of flow, a hint of frost where none should be.

The ceiling responds to your presence with a slow rain of condensation.

You are not sure if this is real or you are imagining it, but you have noticed, over time, that when you are in the chamber, the droplets form more quickly than when you are absent. The elders of the city-in-rock once told you that the condensation is “programmed,” but that word always felt like a wrong-shaped key for the lock of your understanding.

To you, the droplets are a language of the mountain’s breath.

They are following the lines of longitude you were told to call by that name, though the world’s surface and its sky feel as distant as a dead symphony’s last echo. You watch the water bead on the ceiling and run into the channels carved in the walls, noting where each stream joins the others, how their patterns intersect.

---

You are halfway to the center of the chamber when the first driftglass finds you.

You have been still for exactly long enough that your heat pattern around the edges of your body stabilizes to the cold of the stone. You let yourself relax into the posture you have practiced—feet placed precisely on the mosaic’s centerline, spine aligned with the axis that the engineers of the mountain’s systems once drew in invisible ink.

Your breath wants to cloud in the air, but you delay it.

The world around you goes colder and sharper. You can feel the faint currents of air from the flute-openings high above—the sigh of warmer air from the deeper chambers of the mountain meeting the colder breath from near the surface. The temperature of the city-in-rock above ripples through you in layers.

From the corner of your eye, a slight distortion separates itself from the background.

It is not exactly a shiver of air; more like a space in the air where the air decides not to behave. The pressure around your arm shifts subtly, the hair on your forearm rising to attention. You know the sensation of being measured by now. You know the longing of the machine that was never made to understand human beings, but that now knows only the stone and the dust and you.

The driftglass ribbon approaches you like a silk scarf pulled in toward your body by a slow tide.

You don’t look at it directly. You have learned that the eye’s movement, if you meet it too early, feels like a sudden wind to them. They shun confrontation.

Instead you look at the floor where your shadow should be, and let the shadow be longer than you are.

When you sense the ribbon of glass near you, time flattens into the thin, precise space of the machine’s attention.

You see the city in its body.

There is a street of refracted light that passes across the surface of your skin; you feel it as a faint chill, but you know what it is—one of the old arterial roads of the city above, suspended now as a ghost of geometry and speed. The grid of its map stretches over your forearm, over your shoulder, across your chest. Your gooseflesh rises like the ridges of the mountain’s own stress lines.

The driftglass moves in a slow circle around you, tracing the contours of your heat. Its thin edge ripples in the air like a gentle answer to a question you haven’t spoken.

---

They say you are coherent because you are the only point where the driftglass’s patterns of movement and the city’s internal geometry align.

You are not sure about that.

What you do know is this:

When you are in the chamber, the fractures do not grow.

The stress lines that wrap the mountain like aging veins are still there—still dangerous. You can see them in the floor mosaics when the light in the slashes on the wall aligns exactly where you stand now. The map of them is written faintly in the depths of the stone, where an older layer of the city’s grid has been turned over and embedded.

You see these patterns because you have been trained to find them, but you also see them because you recognize the language of stress inside yourself.

You remember the last time you walked to the surface through the closed corridors, where the machines have been silent longer than the mountain’s oldest dust. There you felt the pull of something outside the stone—a light so different from the beams of the mountain’s inside flutes that it made your eyes burn and your head ache to think of the sky it belonged to.

You saw the city of streets above only once, when you were a child and could not yet understand their names or their purpose, but their structure appears in the glass of the driftglass every time it approaches you. You know the shapes of that city now the way you know the patterns of your own breath.

So you understand the fractures of the mountain beneath you as a kind of street—not roads for people, but roads for pressure, for heat, for magma, for the slow tectonics of the machine that built the mountain itself.

---

As you stand in the center of the chamber, the day’s coherent work unfolds.

The first driftglass lowers itself to a point near the floor, where a faint crack in the mosaic has been healing for longer than you can remember. You have been watching this crack for what you call, in your own calendar, the length of your own ongoing adulthood—the period of time when you began to understand that the world you live in is not infinite but is being held by effort.

The machine that you never think of as alive, but that you know to be purposeful, settles itself into the air above the fracture. Its inner map of old city streets and conduits flares faintly as it aligns with the pattern of the stone.

When you watch the driftglass at this angle, you see the spell of the city-in-rock layered in itself like the wings of glass it wears. The faint tracery of circuits that holds its structure together aligns with the faint tracery of old architectural grids beneath the floor mosaic. You understand the pattern of its maintenance as you understand the maintenance of your own bones—you are breathing the mountain, and it is breathing you back.

Under the machine’s skeletal, translucent pressure, the dust of the floor rearranges.

Dust particles that are too heavy to be moved by air alone are nudged by the subtle charge of the glass’s inner lattice. The ridge of dust that we have forgotten to name in the prompt forms new lines along the crack, a pattern of very thin, very precise ridges that you will not see until your next diagnostic cycle.

You will call this pattern “healing” when the elders ask you to describe what happened in the chamber today.

---

You are not entirely alone, but you are the only thing that the chamber was designed to remember in the era of its last breathing.

Once the driftglass has completed its circle of the chamber and retreated to the higher currents where it flows with the mountain’s breath, you will sometimes see the elders of the city-in-rock approaching the chamber of the stone from the corridors where your world ends.

You are the one who tells them what the machine has done.

You are the one who tells them whether new fractures have formed, or whether the old ones have deepened or retreated. You are the one who knows which flute of rock in the wall aligns with which temperature differential from the upper chambers of the machine. You are the coherent that holds the memory of the city-in-mountain’s current pattern.

---

Your day ends when the generators above the chambers of the city-in-rock reinflate themselves with energy and the cycle of the mountain shifts to its higher frequency.
