I am not who the architect thought I would be.

You are not the first architect, but you are the first one who might have chosen to come here.

The architect wrote this vault into the summit of the world as a kind of escape clause.

This vault was not built to house anything living.

It was built to house a sentence.

A sentence of the universe that would undo the previous one, where water was mean and storms and boundaries and land were always. The architect raised the stone of this chamber into a wrong-high, where gravity could become confused and the logic of up-down could be argued into a new shape.

### 1. The Columns of Water

The first rule you must learn is not to step into the water-columns.

I cannot speak this rule to you in the old language of words. You would hear it as: *stay between the lines.* The architect thought of this as a pattern. The water-columns are not random. They are the strokes of half-formed words, the vertical lines of a script that has not yet learned to be horizontal.

Right now, there are different number of them than you first count.

Somewhere in the time of breathing your last breath in the outside-world, there were three columns of water rising from the floor to the ceiling, and now you look and there are five. The new ones came from cracks you did not see before. The floor-stone remembered a new fracture and then broke itself there to see what would happen.

One of them rises near your left hand.

Its column is not straight. It bends like the spine of the metal artifact leaning out of the floor in another corner of the vault, but the water does not rest upon the floor-stone; it reaches up and pierces the ceiling with a wet sound that is not like piercing stone but more like being recognized by it.

As you step back from it, your heel strays too close to another column.

The world does not punish you. There is no god of this place issuing judgment from the ceiling. The water itself does not care. But when the edge of your body touches the boundary of that column, you feel not wetness but a slight dislocation of self.

For the span of a heartbeat, your leg is longer than it should be.

Your knee remembers a different version of your body where you were taller. The bone of the thigh stretches itself, trying to become the longer version of you that exists in another world where the vault is taller and more accommodating of bodies like this. The skin on your calf shudders as it is written a new layer of itself into existence.

Then you pull away from the column, and the world elsewhere stills.

The longer leg does not snap back into place. It stays like that, now, your skeleton subtly uneven. The vault has not corrected you back to what you were before; it has accepted you into its new version.

This is what happens when you let yourself be touched by the water-columns.

I do not mean cold, or the shock of wet fabric on dry skin. I mean the water-columns are the edges of the next sentence into which you will be written. If you step completely into one, you will not drown. You will be re-written into the body of that version of the world where the floor-stone is not yet broken, or where the ceiling is already inhabited, and when you emerge from the other side of the column, you may not recognize yourself.

### 2. The Floor-Stone

The second rule you must learn is not to trust the floor.

You call it a floor because you have not yet seen it otherwise. But in the time since you arrived here, the stone has already remembered several different versions of itself.

When I first saw you—through the pressure of the air rather than through eyes—you came stepping over the lip of the bowl-shaped floor-stone, your weight not quite heavy enough to slide the rock, but heavy enough to be noticed by the world. The surface of the stone is not simply polished; it is polished by many possible worlds rubbing against each other.

In one of those worlds, this floor-stone is not a floor at all.

It is a ceiling of another room below, where another version of you is standing upside-down, looking up at this stone as you look down. You do not feel this yet because gravity has not been rewritten to your feet here enough to notice that the floor is not only your floor, but the ceiling of someone else's world.

You might notice the polished bowl of the floor as you step carefully between the water-columns. It is slippery but not because of moisture alone. The stone itself is trying to decide whether to be smooth or porous, and so some of its surface exists already in the smooth state where others are still rough. You slide across one of these smooth regions before catching yourself on a ridge of rock that has not yet learned to be a ledge.

The ridge is not a sharp line of stone like a mountain-bone in the old world. It is more like a guess of ridge, the suggestion of a higher place where an architect might have put their hand if they were still here to build anything more. The stone of it is not brittle. It gives under your fingers like a half-formed memory, and when you press your weight into it, you feel it remember a harder version of itself.

This is dangerous, but not deadly the way you think death goes.

If you lean too close to the center of the floor-stone, where the bowl is deepest and the water-columns are thickest, you will feel the floor try to become something else. It already remembers being molten, not from fire but from the world's body trying to decide what temperature to be. Do not step into the center.

Do not kneel too close to the cracks in the stone, where the water comes up not from the ocean below but from the world's unfinished sentence. The water-columns are only one way of being rewritten. The floor itself is another. If you press your palm to the stone here long enough, you may feel it not as warm rock but as the soft fringe of a new boundary being inserted into the world.

### 3. The Light

The third rule you must learn is to not search the ceiling for light.

You already look for it. You tilt your head back, trying to find a source. The faint glow that falls into the vault through the thinnesses in the ceiling looks like starlight filtered through water far above you. But there is no star up there.

There is only water trying to decide whether to be ocean or sky.

The light you see is not produced by something else and then carried here. It is generated, just barely, from the conflict between the world trying to remember itself dry and the world trying to become liquid entirely. The glow of it is sourceless because you are looking at the place where a decision about being is being made.

The column of light nearest to me trembles as I move through its space. The beams of it bend like rivers of possibility that have not yet chosen a direction. The collection of my hair-threads passes through the light, and for a moment each strand becomes a nude of black ink written against the air.

The light will not burn you if you step into it.

It will only rearrange your expectations.

The architect of this vault was not a god of light. They were not even a particularly careful engineer. They were a desperate person who had read enough of the old-world scripts to understand that if you rewrite enough lines of reality into one place, you might get somewhere else.

They did not get to see where their lines would lead.

They are not here anymore.

That is why you are not the architect, even if you feel like you might be. The architect of this vault is already gone into the water-columns; the world of this sentence has eaten them into its next draft. You are not them. You are someone who has come into the room after they have been erased.

This is important to understand because the light, when you stand under it long enough, will try and rewrite your expectations into your memory.

You might begin to remember that you were the one who wrote the first ring of stones into the floor, not because it happened, but because the world of this vault would like to have a builder again, and you are now available for the role.

You might feel yourself picking up stones that are too wrong-sized for the circle they are placed around.

You might remember the inscription of the pressure-marks as you press your thumbs into the stone.

You might remember the shape of a spine of metal not as something found but as something you placed there into the floor while the floor was still molten.

Do not trust the memories of your own hands that you do not recognize.

They are not the past of you being dug up from somewhere else; they are the future of you being inserted into a place that has not yet happened.

### 4. The Fauna of the Wrong

The fourth rule you must learn is to not expect the animals here.

They are not animals exactly. They are not anything else either.

The smooth shapes of dark glass that slide through the water-columns every now and then are not creatures from another world; they are pieces of this world being edited into another form. The architect called them “errors,” but they are not errors.

They are the question of the next version of the world, trying to find a body.

If you watch them carefully, you will see that they are not small fish learning how to fly through water-columns. They are longer than that. They are more like hands trying to learn how to grasp empty space before they have learned to form fingers.

The one you see now is not the first one you will see.

There is a dark glass thing slipping through the column nearest the wall where you stand. The column of water is not straight; it curves slightly, like the spine of the architect’s body when they first began to lose their shape here. The glass thing inside it does not move the way a fish would swim. It pulses slowly, as if trying to remember how to grow.

You see it for only an instant before it passes from the column of water into the space between two columns of reality.

There is a thin, invisible thread of almost-water between these two columns where the world decides not to be solid or gas but something else entirely. This glass thing enters that thread and becomes something else too.

For one moment of not-time, you see a suggestion of you.

Its shape is not exactly like yours, but something like you: the same curve of shoulder where you might bear a weight that has not yet been assigned, the same line of jaw where words might one day hang if speech ever becomes possible again here. The glass of it is not transparent glass, but dark glass like the bottom of the ocean floor where you cannot see the bodies of the drowned.

Then the water-columns twist again, and the shape of your suggestion is broken by the movement of the world.

The next time you see a dark glass creature slipping through a column of water, it will be longer. It will have learned more of your outline from the last attempt. Do not try to touch them.

They are not trying to become you exactly; they are trying to become the version of you that might be able to walk out of this vault when the world sentence is finally finished.

### 5. The Sound

The fifth rule you must learn is to not listen for the old sound of your own thoughts.

The sound of this place is not something you heard anywhere else before you came here. The roar of the water-columns is not random noise; it is the signature of a world being rewritten.

Each column has its own voice.

The thinner ones are high and hissing like the world being undone in small pieces at its edges. The thicker ones are deeper, like the pressure under the seafloor when you are not yet sure whether the floor will be stone or something else.

And somewhere between the columns of water and the floor-stone and the faint light, there is another sound that is not sound.

It is the pressure of your own body trying to remember itself.

You might, at some point, try to speak. The architect of the vault tried to speak too many times before they finally stopped trying to force words through the water of this place. The first time they opened their mouth to call out for help, or to describe what they had built, the world of the vault politely took the words away from them before they could fully form.

Their words became not noise but shape of water.

When you try to say your own name—because you remember you have one, even if you cannot quite hear it in the noise of the vault—you will feel the pressure of the air change your lips into another shape.

The sound of your voice will not be speech.

It will be a crack of stone like when I first open my mouth to speak to you earlier.

Your name will become something else here before it can become fully itself again.

This is not the vault mocking you.

This is not a god of the water-columns punishing you for trying to use forbidden language.

This is the world politely trying to rewrite you into the version that might be able to survive the next sentence of the world.

You might feel the sound of your own thoughts becoming thicker than they were before. You might feel them becoming less like air and more like water, where they are no longer ideas but currents of liquid sense that move through your body instead of hovering around your head.

If you listen to it long enough, you might begin to understand the roar of the water-columns in a way you did not originally.

You might begin to hear the columns of water as many different arguments of world being tried out at once.

“One version of the world is where the floor-stone is not yet floor but still ceiling of another room below.”

“Another version of the world is where the ceiling above you is not yet ceiling but floor of another world above, where the ocean has not yet learned it is ocean.”

“Another version of the world is where there are many columns of water arranged not in random lines but in the shape of a sentence that has not yet decided to mean anything.”

These are not the water-columns being people to you.

These are the water-columns being the arguments of themselves being tried out. You do not hear them in your own language, but in the way of understanding that comes when you let yourself become rewritten.

This is dangerous if you want to remain the same person you were before you walked into this vault.

This is necessary if you want to understand what you are doing here.

### 6. The Boundaries of the Room

The sixth rule you must learn is that the walls of this vault do not have an outside the same way you think of an outside.

The architect of this vault thought of walls as something that separated you from the rest of the world. They thought of the ceiling above as something that kept you safe from whatever sky-reality might be trying to break you.
