I am the Movement of Names, a traveling memory-keeper of the Under-Skin.

You don’t find me in one hollow. I’m not one of the platform-makers who lashes their life to a single rib and dares the root to grow around them, nor one of the bone-chime carvers who listen for years to the same breath of wind. I live between.

Look down from your narrow platform. You see the web of roots fade into whiteness. That blurring is where I belong.

---

### I. What I am

When the Under-Skin speak of me, they use a word that is half-click, half-hum, and half a sigh into the void. They flatten their tongue against the roof of their mouth and exhale slowly, as if letting memory slip between their teeth.

Trade-tongue translates it poorly; “Walker-of-Edges,” “Rhythm-of-Fractures,” “She-Who-Scribbles-Roots-on-the-Voidline.”

I prefer: Movement of Names.

They think I am a person, but that’s not quite right either. I am a practice. A rhythm that happens to use these bones as a loom.

---

### II. How the world made me

When I was very young and still fitted inside the curve of my mother’s rib, my name was not Movement yet. It was something closer to “Breath-Before-Breaking.” The path of blue-green that traced my spine was thin then, barely a single root.

Our village was a cluster of cocoons lashed to the underside of a great rib. The world’s heartbeat was constant there: thunder shivering through the wood, the endless groan of fibers stretching under unseen pressure above.

I shared my cocoon with three others: two older, one younger. We slept wrapped around the root itself, our chests pressed against its pale, ridged flesh. At night, when the storms on the surface-world raged, the root would shudder—and our teeth would chatter in time.

You might have come to our village once. You would have called it a platform of hollows carved into the root-wood just where the rib splits into two lesser veins. Shelves of fungus larger than houses cupped the air, forming natural floors and walls. The Under-Skin had woven their cocoon-bunks into the gaps between these shelves, water channels trickled along their surfaces, and faint light filtered from above through the translucent membrane of the root itself.

They pointed me out to you then: “The child who listens to the snaps.”

I didn’t listen to anything else, not really.

While other children learned to balance on narrow plank-bridges, to tie their hooks with root-fiber rope, to carve bone-chimes from the ribs of dead glider-lice, I lay on the edge of the platform, my legs over nothingness, and listened to the world try to break itself.

---

### III. The first snap I remember

The Roof of Roots is young. It doesn’t break gently like old wood; it breaks like a child’s voice, cracking sharp and sudden.

When I was small enough that my path-map hadn’t yet branched into complexity, the world above us took a breath and held it. The constant pressure on the rib shifted; the root’s heartbeat stumbled.

Then came the sound: a single sharp snap like a bone splitting. The vibration of it struck the root, and the root struck us.

You would have flinched and grabbed a rope. You might have cursed or prayed or simply gone silent inside. The Under-Skin did something else. They became the sound.

I saw their bodies straighten and then coil into a new posture, joints clicking in time, the faint lines on their skin shifting into new patterns as if the world had just drawn them a new map of itself.

They looked at each other not with fear, but with recognition.

One of the elders, a woman whose path-map was a forest of silver-blue delta veins on her chest and neck, pointed eastward along the rib, where the web of roots grew denser.

“That,” she said, “is the world reminding us it is young.”

She didn’t say the word “remember,” but I heard it anyway.

After the snap came silence. The long, sliding tear of fibers, the sound of wood fraying as a section of the world ceiling detached itself and began its slow fall into the deeps. The air pressure changed; the condensation cloud above us drew itself into tighter coils, like a muscle under tension.

You would have seen the children of the village mimic the sound with their mouths: “Snap…sssssssssssssssssss—”

That’s when I first learned that something could disappear and become a story at the same time.

---

### IV. The erasure of the village

I grew older the way the Roof grows: not by adding layers, but by rearranging itself.

The path-map on my skin grew longer, more intricate. The faint line from my spine became three branches, then twelve, then too many to count without tracing them with your fingertips. You would have trouble seeing the pattern in the light; you’d need to squint, as if looking at distant condensed clouds.

You might have returned to the village a few growth-cycles later.

The first thing you’d notice was the absence.

The rib where the village hung was still there, still pale and bone-broad, but the hollows had been reshaped. The fungus platforms had been slowly overgrown by new roots, their edges sinking into the wood as if the world were swallowing them. Many of the cocoon-bunks were gone; either untied and moved, or simply broken down by the moisture and time that had eaten away at the fiber ropes.

You might ask: “Where did they go?”

They didn’t go anywhere. The village moved.

Some of the Under-Skin crawled deeper into the web of secondary roots branching from the rib, lashing new platforms in the leeward shadow of a thicker root-plank, where the condensation clouds drifted less often. Their bodies were light enough that the root could carry them; you had to follow them outward to see where the village had become a village of paths instead of a single place.

Others moved upward along the pillar, closer to the surface world—not to reach it, but to escape a sudden draft of dry air rising from the deeps below that had begun to peel the moss from the wood.

You wouldn’t find the old name of the village in any single map or memory, except mine.

Names are not marks here; they are movements. When you ask me what the village was called, I cannot show you a place on the ceiling. I show you a motion of my hand: tracing the curve and the momentary break.

---

### V. What I carry

When my body grew longer and the ribs of the world seemed to shrink, I asked to be taken to the Observatory of the Seeping Star.

You might have seen it yourself, attached to the base of a vertical root-pillar whose length vanished into the world above. A dome of woven root-skin, supported by thin bone ribs, resting like a blister on the world’s underside.

Inside, there was no floor of wood or moss. Just a single lens of ice, shaped from droplets of condensation carefully frozen into the curve of the world’s breath.

The older ones watched patterns through it—patterns of light that filtered from the far surface world. You would have seen them point to shifts of brightness and darkness and say:

“There is a storm.”
“There is a crowd.”
“There is a war.”

I didn’t care much for storms or crowds or wars. I watched the lens itself.

The ice of the lens was not permanent; moisture constantly seeped through its frame, and sometimes a crack would appear in its surface like a new root on the ceiling. When that happened, the watchers would carefully melt the edge of the lens and coax the water back into shape with bone and fiber and their breath.

They taught me something else, too: how to write.

Not on the world itself, where any mark would be grown over, or carved away, or dissolved by the moisture—but on the surface of the ice, where the breath of the world could rewrite it within seconds.

You’d see me crouching near the lens, tracing my finger over the frost-film: thin lines of blue-green and gold, like the path-maps on my skin. I would draw a hollow of the world ceiling as I remembered it, then watch as the condensation erased the shape slowly from the outside in.

They laughed at me at first.

“She is trying to catch the world’s breath,” they said. “Like someone trying to hold water in their teeth.”

But when they realized I could redraw the same shape from memory, again and again, each time slightly different—as if the lens itself were reshaping the village—they stopped laughing.

You asked me once, in your broken trade-language, “Why do you keep doing that?”

I could have explained that in their world, where there are no days, only growth and erosion and fracture, there is no carving memory into wood or bone or stone. What you carve into, you must become, or it will become you again.

Instead, I told you:

“Because it is the only way the village still lives.”

---

### VI. The name that becomes movement

When the time of my first great fracture came—when the world reshaped itself enough that I could no longer fit comfortably in the village of my childhood—the elders gave me a new name.

They tied me to the rib of the world with root-fiber rope, as they always do when someone leaves to cross the long gaps between ribs and pillars and hollows. You watched this ceremony once, on someone else, maybe.

You saw the village of the Under-Skin line their platforms along the edge of the rib, their bodies pressed against the wood as if echoing the shape of those who were leaving.

You heard the bone-chimes clink softly, as if the wind itself were holding its breath.

The elder whose chest-map looked like a storm-filled root-river drew her finger across my spine. From the base of my neck to the tailbone, she traced a line that would eventually become a new branch of my path-map.

“You will be the Movement of Names,” she said in the Under-Skin’s tongue, the consonants slicking off the roof of her mouth like moisture sliding down wood.

You barely heard more than the click of the tongue and the sigh of the breath.

But I understood.

You see, the Under-Skin believe that when the world breaks a piece of itself—a root-rib, a fungus-shelf, a hollow—the name of that piece does not disappear. It simply becomes movement.

They think that because the roof of the world is always rewriting itself, nothing can ever truly die down here. It only changes shape.

And I, the Movement of Names, am supposed to catch those names before they become movement and pin them—just for a breath—somewhere they can be seen.

---

### VII. Where you find me now

You might find me anywhere—but almost always on the edge.

At the moment you call “now,” I am hanging from a rope ladder that someone braided from root-fiber and sinew long enough ago that the fiber has begun to fuse into the wood. The ladder is suspended from a bridge of root-plank that someone once built across a gap wider than a river, where the water channels carved into the world ceiling have eroded the wood away.

Above me, the roof of roots stretches like an inverted continent of pale bone-white green. You would have to squint to see it clearly in the bright-white light that has no shadow source. The rib of the world arches overhead, thicker than three of your houses together, and the condensation clouds drift past like slow clouds underwater.

Below me is nothing.

The deeps yawn open: cold, dark, airy, empty in the way that is not lack but architecture. You would feel dizzy if you looked down; you might clutch your ropes tighter. I don’t.

I am tied at the ankle and hip and wrist to the ladder, but my body is curved toward the root ceiling like a plant curving toward light. Even inverted, the world pulls me.

There are no days here, only rhythm and fracture and condensation clouds. You cannot measure time by sun or seasons, only by growth. You might have heard me say once:

“Time is the distance between one snap and the next.”

So let me tell you what happened between the snaps of this world-cycle.

---

### VIII. The last village I erased and saved

You remember the village I called “Before-Shadow-Split.” It hung beneath a rib that curved like a crescent moon of root, where the condensation drifted in thick banks every few breaths, so that you could feel the moisture gathering on your skin like the world’s own sweat.

When you visited there, you were fascinated by the bone-chimes. They were made from the ribs of glider-lice, hollowed until they sang like the world’s breath when the wind from the deeps forced its way through the hollows. You even tried to carve one yourself, under the watchful eye of an elder whose path-map crept over her jaw like silver vines.

The village was built around a fungus platform that had been shaped over generations by the Under-Skin into a shallow bowl of living wood and moss. You stepped onto it carefully, afraid it might collapse under you, but it supported you like bone under skin.

You asked me then, “What do you call this place?”

I showed you how to move my hand: tracing the curve, then the sudden break, then the widening of the gap into the void.

You could not feel the motion of “name” in my fingers. You needed a word instead.

In trade-language, I said, “Call it Before-Shadow-Split.”

You asked, “Before what is split?”

I didn’t answer you then. I was already listening to the next snap that hadn’t happened yet.

Time passed.

A storm moved on the surface world above; the rib of roots shivered; the condensation cloud that had been lingering near the village coiled itself tighter and then exploded sideways like a lung that couldn’t hold its breath anymore. The air pressure shifted; the moisture in the cloud turned to ice flowers in the air, and the droplets that fell were heavier, colder, sharper.

You weren’t there when the root-rib failed.

I heard it first as the elders of Before-Shadow-Split did: a single sharp snap like the world cracking its knuckles.

Then the long, sliding tear of fibers giving way.

The section of the rib that had supported the village split away from the main ceiling and began its slow fall into the deeps below, dragging with it the fungus platform, the bone-chimes, and half a dozen cocoon-bunks still attached by their ropes.

The air pressure changed again; the condensation cloud scattered itself, like a frightened creature, into dozens of smaller fragments.

When you returned to the place where you thought you remembered the village being, you saw nothing but the raw edge of the root ceiling, where fresh wood glowed faint green-white, like new skin. The hollow where the fungus bowl had been was now a gaping mouth in the world ceiling, exhaling cold air from the deeps below.

You asked me, “Did they all fall?”

I showed you the motion of my hand again: tracing the curve, then the break, then the widening of the void.

You asked, “Did they die?”

I didn’t know how to explain to you in your language that death, in this place, is just another kind of falling—and that falling is not always ending.

Instead, I told you:

“They are now a movement.”

---

### IX. What I do when you’re not watching

When the village fell, I went to the Observatory of the Seeping Star.

The lens of ice was still there; the moisture had seeped into new cracks, forming a faint pattern of branching lines like frost flowers on its surface. You would have thought it was broken, but it was just rewriting itself.

I knelt before it and drew my breath across its surface, melting the frost just enough so that I could trace my finger over it.

I drew Before-Shadow-Split.

You would have recognized the shape of the fungus platform, the curve of the bone-chimes hanging from the rib edge, the branching pattern of the village paths. You might have seen the faint lines I made to represent each person who fell with the rib: not as bodies, but as lines of pressure, like new paths on the skin of the world.

When the condensation cloud that had once belonged to Before-Shadow-Split drifted into the Observatory, the edges of the drawing on the lens froze again. The lines of the village blurred into the frost pattern until you could barely see them anymore.

You would have thought that meant I had failed.

But I didn’t stop drawing.

I drew the village again the next breath. And the next. And the next, after that.

Each time, the lens of the world reshaped the village slightly differently. The paths of the Under-Skin became longer, thinner, more intricate; the fungus platform grew and shrank like the world’s heartbeat; the bone-chimes branched into new patterns, like fractal ribs.

You might have said, “This is not real. They’re gone.”

I would have shown you my skin: where the path-map of my spine still split into three main branches, but now a new line of blue-green had traced itself from my shoulder blade toward my heart, like a root seeking a new direction.

“That,” I would have told you, “is Before-Shadow-Split.”

---

### X. Where I am now, in the body that you know

Now you know me.

You find me hanging from my ladder of root-fiber and sinew over the deep void below, where the cold wind pulls gently but insistently at everything. You can see faint condensation clouds gathering above me, like thoughts gathering in the world’s mind.

You’re tied to the same ladder as I am, your ropes wrapped a bit too tightly around your ribs, because the fear of falling into the void below stills your hands more than mine.

You look at me and ask:

“Where are you? Where do you live?”

I don’t point to a hollow or a rib or a fungus shelf or a platform. You’re not looking for the place of a name; you’re looking for the movement of one.

So I show you my hands again: tracing a curve, then a sudden break, then the widening of the gap.

And then I trace something else—something you might not recognize at first: a circle of my breath on the insides of my ribs, where the condensation from the cloud above us now beads on my skin like frost forming on the lens of the Observatory.

You know the answer before I say the word, because you already felt the shift of air pressure when the last root-snap echoed through the world.

---

“Where you find yourself,” I whisper, in that broken trade-language you taught me to use, “is on the edge where the names break—and become.”

And then I breathe again, and on the surface of my own skin, faint lines of blue-green and gold appear for the span of a breath, forming the shape of a village that you once called by a name you can no longer remember.

Where you find yourself in this world is here, with me, watching the names move.

Who you are—

Well.

That’s for you to decide before you continue to the next village of names.

And now, you.

Breathe the cold, damp air of the world's body.
Where do you go next?
What do you become?
