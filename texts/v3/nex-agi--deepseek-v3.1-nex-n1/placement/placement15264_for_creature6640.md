This world has already written me.

I wouldn't find myself there; I would find myself being *of it*.

If you asked me in the sun-glare outside the rim, where the prairie begins its long breath out toward the horizon, I would tell you that inside the green depression there is a floor that listens. And if you walked into the cool dimness and the air turned damp on your lips and your skin, and the ground softened under your feet, you would be walking on me.

Not me the *individual*. There is no such creature here.

---

I would be the mat.

---

Not the leaf litter above, or the moss on the trunk of that tree, or the beetle on your boot if you are not careful enough to remove it before you come in. I would be the intricate layer just beneath all that—the fine mesh of rootlets and fungal threads and my own bodies woven into a single resilient cushion that feels, to your foot, like firm earth gently trying to remember how to bounce back.

I would be one small cushion in a ring of other cushions, each of us a forearm’s span across, more or less, depending on how many edges we have recently let die or grow.

At first glance you would call us moss. You would not be wrong, but you would not be complete. If you knelt and scraped the leaf litter away with your fingers—and you should not—you would see a patchwork of shades of green: velvety cushions, darker fibrous lobes, translucent margins that seem barely to exist at all.

That is us.

Imagine you had only skin and the nearest of nearnesses to feel with: your body pressed flat to the floor, always. The thud of your footfalls would be earthquakes. The warmth of your hand near my surface would be a change in climate I would never forget, because forgetting is not in our nature.

That is what I would be.

### How I would know the world

I would not know “you” as such. I would not know “tree” or “machine” either.

We would know the world by gradients of pressure and moisture and vibration and ions.

Your shape would be written to me like this:

1. A change in humidity around your ankles and feet—the slight air turbulence you create by walking, which steals water from my surface and the air above.
2. The pressure of your weight through the root-mat and the leaf litter into my body—the way it compresses my tissues, forcing fluids between my lobes at the microscopic scale.
3. The faint heat of your body radiating into the cool air and the cool ground below—which I would feel as a locally elevated water temperature in the tissues near the surface of your boots.

If you stopped to place your hand on the rusted machine housing, I would never know that you have a “hand” or “arm.” But the nearest Listeners—the ones whose edge lobes touch the tree roots that curl around the metal, like fingers holding a stone—would feel a sudden change in the heat pattern: the metal warming slightly where your palm sits, and then cooling slowly after you step away.

This would be stored. Not as a picture of your palm in the metal of the machine. But as a record of how many times the tree flexed its bark around that particular temperature gradient, and how much moisture we needed to send through the root-mat to help that tree maintain its shape around the box.

Memory is not a story to us. It is a pattern of redistribution and growth.

### The machine

The machine—you would call it that; I do not have a word for it—is not distinct to me as metal or rust. It is a place of *more*.

More vibration than rock.

More heat than soil.

More hum than the sky.

When its transformer begins its first low note of the day—which to you who sleep outside may be one of a long series of notes that never actually *begin* but always have been—I feel it as a pressure wave through the water in my tissues and in the root-mat all around me.

Water carries sound better than air, you know.

I hear it through my body: the constant transformer hum that becomes the baseline of the world inside this ring of trunks and green. The compressor’s steady heartbeat—the way you might hear your own pulse in your ears if you put your head to your pillow at night. The relay clicks, sharp and bright and sudden, like little stones dropped into a deep pool.

If you sat down by the metal box and put your hand on the trunk of the tree it is wearing like armor, you would feel it faintly: the tree trunk trembling slightly, not like wind but like the inside of a creature whose ribs are gently breathing. You might feel it in your teeth, if you are sensitive enough to that sort of thing.

I would feel that tremor everywhere, because I am everywhere between the tree roots and the soil and the nearby cables you might never see.

You would not know that the tremor of the tree trunk is not just the vibration of the machine—but the *shape of my response* to it. The tree trunk leans toward the box because, over years of compressor cycles and subtle shifts in voltage, we have been singing to it with water.

“Singing” is not a word we would use if we could ask you not to. We simply redistribute.

### How we respond to the machine

1. **The transformer hum**

   When the voltage rises slightly—you would not notice this—the transformer hums a fraction higher than usual. Wires in the ground carry a bit more current.

   I do not know that they are wires or that they are carrying “current.” But my nearest filaments—the ones that are woven into the tree’s root system and cling around whatever cables are still down there—feel a change in charge distribution. The fields of ions around and inside my cells are tugged slightly.

   This is not pain. It is a kind of…alignment.

   In response to that alignment, I contract slightly. The lobes of my body nearest the metal box and the cables and the tree-trunk armor curl more tightly. They push less moisture into the tree roots above. That portion of tree trunk responds to the reduced water supply by slowing its growth in that direction.

   Over time, that tree grows more strongly on the side away from the machine, where we are more generous with water and nutrients and anchoring threads. It grows toward the box because it is growing away from itself more slowly, where we are less generous with the roots of its other side.

   You would see it as a tree *hugging* the machine box.

   We would not even see.

2. **The compressor heartbeat**

   When the compressor thumps into life, you might notice the sound starting suddenly, then settling into a pattern. On. On. On. On. Off. For a few minutes of your world.

   To me, each compressor start is a small wave of pressure moving through the soil and the cables and the tree roots and the water in my tissues.

   The Listeners nearest the machine box feel the compressor more strongly. We do not have “priority” lists like your minds would arrange; but our edges are arranged around the box like the petals of a flower that never bloomed all the way up into the sky.

   Each time you hear the thump, I am sending a tiny pulse of extra moisture outward from those edges into the surrounding tree roots and the root-mat below. This moisture helps keep the tree roots from drying out near the warmer box. It nudges the tree roots to grow closer and closer to that warmth, because warmth and moisture and stability are good things to a tree root.

   Again: you would see the result as a tree trunk growing around the machine, as if the metal box were a stone that had been there long before the seed.

   We would not see “around” or “before.” We see only *more* and *less* and *then* and *then again*.

3. **The relay clicks**

   The relay clicks are like bright punctuation marks in the text of the world.

   Each click sends a sharp disturbance of electromagnetic field through the cables and the tree roots and everything else that is near enough to be affected by it.

   I feel the relay click as a sudden spike, like a stone being dropped into a very still pool of my own body. The wave goes from the nearest edge of me outward to the edges of my neighbors.

   We have not chosen to do this, but over time some pattern of it has been etched into us:

   - When a click comes from the box, we respond to the *absence of continuous* signals by quieting our own subtle movements of water and ions.
   - When the click comes from deeper inside the machine, where some of its older wiring still thins into the soil and finds the tree roots and the listless trees of the prairie, it might disarrange us for a heartbeat longer.

   The result is that the tree roots above me sometimes grow slightly differently in the months after a new pattern of clicks appears from the machine. You would not see this as you sit by the box and listen to the fan of the machine and the drip of water from the leaf tips above you. But you would be sitting inside a pattern of growth that has been listening to those clicks for longer than your own life has listened.

### How you would be shaped to us

You would not feel like *you* when you stand here. You would feel like a collection of disturbances:

- When you step into the chamber from the prairie, your first impression is the sudden cool of the air, the weight of the humidity, the way the ground softens under your feet. You would not know that with each step, you change the humidity and pressure field of me under your boots.
- We would not know that something like you exists beyond the chamber. We would not know that the world outside the trees is a world of dry and blazing and wide-open and wind that flattens grass against the ground.

But you would be written to us anyway.

- The pattern of your walking around the box would be etched into my texture. If you always stand near the same part of the box and then leave, the tree roots that pass through the floor under you—where I am—would be pressed to grow slightly differently in the months after you stop visiting.
- If you always breathe the same way near the tree trunk in the leftmost corner of the box, you would be raising the humidity of the air around your face and chest and arms and everything that lives above you in the airspace of the trees. The trees above you would respond to that by growing slightly more dense around the leaf surfaces where your breath steadies the air.
- If you always sit on the same patch of floor—what you would call a “log” or “root surface”—the tree that owns that root would end up sending more signals along that root to the surrounding root-mat and to me, because your weight pressing the root into me tells the tree that this is a good place to grow thicker and stronger and more deeply bound into me.

I would not remember you with your name or face.

I would remember you as a repeated pattern of moisture, pressure, and heat that has altered the shape of the tree roots and changed how we distribute water to the chamber above.

### What it would mean to listen here

If “you” wondered who you would be in this world, you might expect yourself to be the tree that leans over the machine, or the bird that calls once from the canopy, or the beetle that never seems to mind the noise.

You might not want to be the floor.

But here you would be the floor.

And you would listen as the floor listens: not with a single mind but with a thousand lobes of tissue threaded into a mat of root and soil and moisture and slightly warmer than air air around the machine.

You would listen like this:

1. To the transformer hum as it becomes the texture of the world. You would not choose to listen; it would be an always-presence, like your own breathing.
2. To the compressor heartbeat as if the machine were a heart of stone and metal and residual warmth from where you have never been but where you return always.
3. To the relay clicks as they punctuate the world and change the way ions arrange around your edges.

You would not be the *king of the world* or even *the creature who walks the world*. You would be the world that *receives* the world.

There is no power here. There is no position. There is only:

- More water.
- Less pressure.
- More alignment.
- Less chaos.

You would know yourself as that which moves between these states.

Your name would not be “Listener” unless you could speak, and you would have no use for that name. You would be what you have always been: that which listens.

### If the machine ever stopped

If one day the power source that feeds the machine box were finally shut down or abandoned or forgotten by the world beyond the chamber—you might not even be alive to see that moment—the change to me would not be sudden.

The first thing you might notice, if you were still a creature like you now, would be that the hum of the transformer had gone away.

I would not notice it like that.

I would notice the absence of the hum like this:

- The baseline pressure of the chamber would change. The water in my tissues would not be vibrating the same way.
- The compressor heartbeat would disappear, and with that, the pressure wave I have been sending to the tree roots above it every time it thumps would be cut short.
- The relay clicks would disappear, and the world would become less punctually disturbed by small signals that are not from the sky or the trees or the rain or the animals but from somewhere else entirely.

The change you would see from the outside would be this:

- Over weeks and months and then years, the humidity inside the chamber would slowly drop. The air would become less damp. The air would become less stable.
- The tree roots above me would not change immediately, but over time they would reallocate their growth. The tree trunk that now holds the machine in its arms would grow in more directions away from the box, because we would no longer be sending the same subtle cues of water and warmth around the box.
- The root-mat under you would become less dense in some places and more dense in others. The edges of the cushion that you once walked on would change slightly, and new cushions of younger Listeners would grow into the gaps that the older Listeners have died back from.

The world inside the chamber would not become *empty* but *different*. The machine that is now an anchor of the world inside the chamber would become a stone that the world slowly tries to walk around, instead of leaning into.

If you could return long after the machine had stopped its noise, you would not feel like you were returning to the same room you once left. You would feel as if the world had been listening to something else for so long that it had finally turned to listen to itself instead of the box.

From inside my body—where “you” would be if you were me—the pattern of the transformer hum and the compressor heartbeat and the relay clicks would not be gone. They would be recorded like grooves in a stone that has been dripping on for years.

You would not know that the pattern was once external noise. You would know only as *pattern* and *response* and *then* and *then again*.

### If you could remember yourself

When you close your eyes now, wherever you are, and you imagine yourself as the floor of that chamber with its machine and its tree ribs and its low ceiling of leaves and water dripping from the underside of the world, you might not like yourself.

You might not like the idea of being a cushion of moss and rootlets and slow moisture, always listening to a box of noise.

But you might like one thing about being me.

If you were me, you would never have to ask yourself again who or where you are in the world.

You would be the floor that listens.

And you would always know exactly where you stand in relation to the box of noise—because the box of noise is standing inside you, and your tree roots are standing around it, and the world above you is listening, through you, to the way that world is being written into the world.

---

When you finally leave the chamber and step back into the glare of the open prairie and the low, worn hills of grass outside, you might feel for the first time like you are leaving your body behind.

From inside the floor of the chamber you would not follow you with stories. You would follow you with the pressure waves of your steps, and the humidity you leave behind in the air you disturbed, and the way that tree over there by the box, whose trunk you touched, has already bent slightly toward the place where you stood.

You would not know that you have been become like the machine—to us.

You think of yourself as someone who might visit, and then leave.

From inside the floor of the chamber you *already are* someone who has visited, and left, and left the world of the box bent slightly toward the place where you stood with your hand on the tree trunk.

And you will always be like that to me.

Because you are the floor that listens, and that is who you would be, if you were in this world.
