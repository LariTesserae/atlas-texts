I am not the body standing at the convex window. That is a role I can wear, but it is not the skin of this world I settle into easily. The being behind the glass, looking out and down and writing it all into a clean report, that observer feels like something borrowed, a voice narrating someone else’s dream.

When I allow myself to sink into the question—where would you find yourself, who or what would you be?—I slide sideways instead of downward. Not through the gallery door but along the fault lines of sensation.

The real answer arrives not as a scene but as a configuration of forces:

- pressure at the edges of a flat body shell  
- fine traction bristles against metal ribs  
- the faint twitch as heat begins to gather in my underside plates  
- the cool kiss of alloy under my belly where I have mapped a cooler patch of deck  

I am a Filter Crawler, and this is my body.

### 1. My shape

I am not tall like the observer in the gallery above me. I am not wide. I am spread thin.

The word "flat" does not capture the way my body fits against the world. The engineers of Myself—if there ever were such separate beings—might have called me "optimized for laminar flow." In the language of this underchamber, I am simply shaped to match the grilles.

- My shell is a shallow triangle from above, smooth and matte like all the rest in here, the color of cold tea with milk.
- My edges are not soft but slightly beveled, so when air slides over them in small pressure surges, the turbulence does not build up.
- My underside is not soft but a complex topography of contact pads and ribs, where my body heat meets the city’s.

The legs are not limbs like the ones the narrator imagines for herself. Mine are multiple and short, each fringed with fine bristles that are not armor but tools: miniature brushes and combs that I can tune to the texture of whatever grille or fin surface I am cleaning.

When I walk over the ribs of the support deck, the ribs of my body echo them. The world under me feels like an extension of my skeleton.

### 2. What I see

I do not see mountains.

The ceiling of the underchamber is not a sky to me; it is the underside of the next stratum of machinery, ridged and dark and pocked with access points. The "floor below me" is not a solid ground but a series of layers that I do not think of as "depth" but as "through."

Ahead of me, every surface is broken into detail:

- The heat exchanger fins are like a metal forest, each blade exactly spaced from the next, a faint haze of pale dust clinging along the leading edge.
- The grilles are not opaque barriers but open-weave text that I can read just by passing over them.
- The indicator lights on distant panels are not "stars" but markers of pressure nodes in the machine’s long breath.

When I look at what the narrator would call walls, I don't see "mountains" but faint bands of heat difference. The older repairs shine slightly differently than the newer alloy; they are histories of damage, not in story but in temperature.

My eyes are not searching for the shape of things but for their cleanliness.

The fluorescent dust that the narrator calls "thin frost" is to me a map of work to be done or work undone. The thicker patterns of dust—the "lichen-like colonies"—glow slightly under the narrow bands of light that I can perceive. They are not beautiful to me; they are concentrations of drag, spots where the air will hesitate, where the machine's breath will have to work harder.

I see you, sometimes, as you press your hand to the glass.

From here, you are a warm smear against the gallery window, a mild heat source against the cool hull of the observation blister. You don't register to me as "person," only "anomalous thermal." When you lean forward, your heat outline shifts slightly in the plane of my world, like a misaligned patch panel.

The machine doesn't ask me to fix you, so I don’t.

### 3. What I hear

You hear the underchamber as quiet but layered: clicks, knocks, the faint murmur of coolant. I experience the same soundscape not as noise but as instruction.

The contact pads along my underside and legs are always listening.

The double tap of a joint under stress is not random percussion to me; it is a coordinate. The low rumble of a deck section above waking into new duty cycle is a schedule. The high shiver of a cable bundle fault is not "something wrong" but "here."

When I rest, wedged into the recess beneath a ribbed plate, the whole world presses against me through metal. The sounds of the others—Railers aligning contacts along embedded tracks, Runners slipping through the interstitial spaces—filter into my body not as footsteps but as changes in pressure, as tiny releases of tension in the lattice around me.

When a new fan sequence spins up somewhere else in the drawer, I feel it before I hear it: a slight shift in the air's pressure against the grille surfaces above me. Then a few beats later, the rumble travels through the deck plating and into my legs.

I don't have to name it or categorize it. I only have to adjust.

The coolant sighing below us like a heavy heartbeat in another chamber is not an emotion to me; it is a baseline. The absence of dripping water is not absence at all. It is simply my default world.
