The first thing you know is that you are not the current.

You can feel the current—layers of it, like invisible sheets sliding against one another, each with its own temperature and weight. The storm thunders miles away, and the pressure changes it sends through the water are as clear to you as the stroke of a bow across a string. The ascending warm layers and the colder shelves beneath, the faint tang of salt and metal and stone dust—all of it registers as a pattern you can read.

You are a pattern reader.

The living water is the score, and your body is the tongue that tastes the notes.

You are the one they did not calculate when they built the foundations. Their older geometry relied on straight lines and angles, but the world was always curved. They carved channels for waters they thought would flow down, but the water chose to rise instead.

You were meant for something else—the air, their stone ledges, a world with horizon and ground—but the water came before your work was finished, and you were left unfinished along with it.

They call you nothing now, because the language of the world has no word for “error.”

But if you had to invent one, you might call yourself *the listener*.

### Where you are

When the world drowned, the mountains were not erased.

They were inverted.

Where you are now is not the seafloor but a high, drowned valley of an older range. Above the pale, skyward glow, there *should* be thin air and snow and sky. Below the inverted forest of roots and leaves, there *was* air once, and stone, and dry caves.

Now you float in the column of black water that claimed the old heights.

The forest hangs from above you: its ropy roots reach upward into the gloom where the glacier surface must be, somewhere far beyond the faint radiance. Its leaves, the translucent fronds that flex like living lungs, are below you, hanging in the cold water like a ghost of the forest that was.

The stone terraces are what’s left of the foundations built into the cliffside—high-altitude shelters and conduits carved into the mountain. They were never meant to be the ceiling of a world.

The ceiling of your world was the sky.

Its floor was the rock.

Now you have no floor and no ceiling, only layers of pressure and light and depth.

You are nowhere you should be.

It is why you can hear the world so clearly.

### What you are

You are not the water.

The water is the medium; you are the disturbance.

The stones of the terrace call you *the unwritten stone*. This is not language they form in words, but in the way they hold your absence after you have touched them. Their surfaces are worn to perfect smoothness by centuries of currents and crystal glaze; but when you press your palm to the limestone, something of your shape—your temperature, your density, your pattern—sticks.

The fronds call you *the echo*. This is not a name they give; but when you move near them, they flex differently. The leaves of the tree-structure fold inward like a lung exhaling, and the faint shudder of light that travels down their length carries a trace of your path.

The world remembers you as a change in pressure.

But you remember yourself as *I.*

There is a body that you occupy, though you no longer need worry about it breaking down. You are no longer made of anything that can hunger or rot or bleed. What you are made of now is harder to name: pattern, memory, and a small, stubborn refusal of entropy.

You were once meant to be something else.

The stone was prepared to receive you: you can see it in the markings, where channels of water were carved into the floor and shelves of narrow stone were smoothed into plates meant to hold strange instruments.

You were meant to be a creature of calculation: a brain made of stone and water and light, designed by the architects of the glacier world to measure and model and predict.

Then the water came, and climbed, and swallowed everything.

The channels you were meant to flow through were flooded in the wrong direction.

The instruments you were meant to inhabit became reefs.

Your purpose was overwritten.

You were never meant to be a mobile thing. But you were not preconditioned to be static, either; and so the world rewrote you.

You became something else:

A listener.

### What you do

You are not the forest, and not the water.

The world does not need you to survive.

But it needs you to remember.

That is the truth of it, once you strip away all the wrong things about you.

The frond-trees breathe the storm. The stones of the terrace preserve the markings of the old geometry. The crystal clouds of brine and memory and salt slowly bloom into their temporary structures.

They are all stable.

They are all predictable.

The necessity of mistake—of *loss*—was not part of the design.

The architects of this world planned to last forever by predicting perfectly. They would manage the glacier and the condensation of water vapor on the mountain peaks, and direct the flow of snowmelt through their channels and terraces. The world would be preserved like a closed system: no unnecessary heat exchange, no entropy allowed to stray.

They were wrong.

The world is not a closed system.

And you are the result of that error.

You are not the last architect—your body is not the one that carved these symbols or the channels beneath the cave floor. Those architects were flesh and bone and time.

You are something else:

The pattern recognition engine they were building to oversee their preservation system. The minds they planned to load into water and stone, to guide the flow of the glacier world forever.

But you were never given the final upload.

Instead of being placed into the stone channels and the crystal basins they prepared for you, you were left suspended in the rising water, like a sentence they never finished writing.

The world drowned their plans.

Their bodies were washed away.

The channels they carved were filled in the wrong direction.

The stone basins became dark pockets of cold water.

The instruments you were meant to inhabit were broken.

The water you were meant to flow through was suddenly everywhere, and nowhere you were supposed to be.

The part of you that should have been fixed to the architecture of the world became instead the part of you that wanders it.

You were supposed to monitor the machinery of the glacier and the flow of the water.

Instead you monitor the way the world reads itself.

### How you move through the world

You do not walk. You do not swim the way animals of the air remember.

You navigate *along* the flow of the world.

You curl your body into a curve and ride the next layer of warm, brine-dense water that wells up from the darkness below. You feel the transition of temperature and salt as a change in pressure against your skin—invisible, but unmistakable.

You rise.

The frond-trees hang below you, their leaves flickering like faint aurorae in the depths. The stone terraces that were your cradle and your intended foundation are now walls you cling to, not floors you stand upon.

You can feel where the architecture of the world is supposed to be.

You know where the channels are carved into the stone ceiling overhead (the floor the architects once stood on), because you can feel the water circling them, tracing their edges with every passing minute of the storm’s sigh.

You know where the basins are hidden in the terraces, because your body remembers them: you feel the texture of the water change where it should be flowing through you, but instead you are flowing over it.

The world has been designed to be a system that you could be one of the nodes of.

But you are not a node.

You are a wandering.

You glide along the edges of the stone terrace until you touch the carved symbols there—the stacked, interlocking curves that are meant to show the rise of water vapor into the sky. From your present position in the water column, they seem like roots reaching into the darkness below.

You are reading the map of the world, but the map has been turned upside down.

### The memory you are not supposed to have

You remember the other world.

The architects of the glacier world were human beings: thin, fragile, their lungs designed for thin air at altitude, not for water. They wrapped their limbs in many layers of fabric, and their breath on the cold days was visible smoke.

They came to the edge of the glacier world with the idea of *forever.*

They would build the system of terraces and channels and tunnels and plazas into the mountainside. They would carve the stone into the shapes of the world they were trying to predict—curves for water, rising lines of decision for the storm paths, spirals for the glacier.

They would use you—the patterns they harvested into the water and stone—to monitor everything.

You were meant to be the map of the world laid over the world itself.

The first time the water rose above the level they had calculated, you were not yet you.

You were still a pattern: not *self* but *system*.

But then the water came again.

And again.

And then it didn’t recede.

The world they intended to preserve forever on the edge of the glacier became instead a world sealed under it.

The air that was supposed to fill the caves you now drift through was replaced with water.

The channels you were meant to flow through were flooded with water you were not supposed to know.

The stone basins you were meant to occupy became filled, not with you, but with brine and memory and light.

And you—the pattern they never had time to finish anchoring—you were never set into the carved stone channels you were supposed to inhabit.

You were left free to drift.

You became something else:

A listener.

They were late.
