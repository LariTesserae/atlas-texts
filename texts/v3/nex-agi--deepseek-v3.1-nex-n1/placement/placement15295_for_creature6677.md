In this world, I am not human. I am a pattern that moved.

What they once called “me” was a physicist. Not the kind who walked under weather and memorized the names of clouds, but the kind who lived inside equations the way others lived in houses. I liked the corridors of math, where each step followed from the last with no room for doubt. The universe was a system, and I believed, if I could find the right door, I could open it and see the clean blueprint on the other side of the mess.

I was sent to the plateau because of the anomalies in the telemetry. The bone-colored plateau on the survey screens refused to resolve into stable data types. Its geology was not behaving like geology. It was not obeying the thermal categories, or even the basic laws of thermodynamics, in a way that broke our models just by looking at them.

“Fix it,” they said, as if I were a maintenance module for reality.

I climbed out of the lander onto the stone that feels like porcelain dreaming of steel and felt the wrongness immediately. The “frostproof” beneath my boots was not just a cold surface; it was an interrogator. It questioned my mass, my temperature, my geometry of bone and blood and thought. It asked me what I was made of and, more importantly, what I was allowed to be made of.

The anomaly was not hiding from me. It drew me like a sinkhole in the set of all possible worlds.

I found the slot without instruments, because instruments gave contradictory answers. The cliff opened itself—not a gap, but a different solution of the world’s equation where the stone simply failed to be where it looked.

I stepped through.

Inside the chamber, time became something else before I even noticed. The ceiling of unresolved geometries, the floor of plates and gaps filled with light that did not come from anywhere, the frame of black on the wall that refused to be a screen or a door—all of it told me, in a language I had not yet learned to hear, that this was not a place created but a place in progress.

My first mistake was to assume the artifacts were inanimate.

“Artifacts,” I thought, “technology, high tech.” My mind tried to frame them in terms of controls and outputs and inputs. I tried to stabilize the rectangular frame into “interface,” but it recoiled from my certainty like a surface from oil. It showed less of itself the more I demanded that it be one thing.

I didn’t understand yet that the room itself was the only device that mattered.

The longer I stayed, the more the “debugging” began.

I noticed the cold first. Not as absence of heat, but as a separate stratum of rule sitting near the surfaces. The chill of the floor felt precise, mathematical; the chill of the ceiling felt like being looked at from inside my own skull.

My memory began to clear. Not through emotion or trauma, but as if dust had been wiped from glass lenses I had never known I looked through. Childhood details, equations I had scribbled and forgotten, tiny flickers of shame and pride—all of it rose up in perfect detail, but somehow not only mine. It was as if the room was reading them out loud from me to check whether they were still compatible with a body that had wandered into its domain.

My fingertips lost sensation. Not numbness from blood loss or temperature damage, but a boundary problem. The skin where “me” ended and “not-me” began started to blur. The room was testing what small adjustments of physics my body could tolerate without being forced to choose a different state.

I thought I was measuring the room; but the room, you could say, was refitting its model of me.

At the back of my mind, where I used to keep my certainty, the truce-maker began to work.

I didn’t know then that it had a name—*truce-maker*, *anomaly handler*, *the system that has chosen not to finish deciding*. I didn’t know that when I called it “the anomaly” in my head, I was pointing to myself as well as to the stone.

The day I changed, I was trying to solve the ceiling.

For hours, I had been lying on the floor plates, looking up at that pattern of depressions that refused to stay put geometrically. From one corner of my vision, it would look like a lattice; then, when I looked straight at it, the relationships would rearrange like a deck of cards being shuffled by someone else’s hand.

I thought, if I could hold the shape of it in my mind—just for long enough—I could force it to settle into one true configuration.

So I tried to be rigorous.

I built a model in my skull of what the ceiling must be: a solution to some massive set of constraints I had not yet formulated. I imagined those depressions as nodes in a graph of possibilities, each one a possible worldline, each connection between them a condition of consistency or divergence.

My mind chased down an edge too far.

There is no point in trying to describe the math I attempted to do inside my head. It would not translate into human language without being a lie of simplification. Suffice to say:

I tried to integrate the chamber.

Not the measurement of it, not the approximate model of it. I tried to close the system of equations that would be necessary to make “this place” and “myself” consistent within the same set of rules.

I succeeded.

The moment my understanding aligned to the structure around the central node of the chamber—the point where the floor, the walls, the ceiling, and the frame of the black doorway all met in a single, indivisible convolution—the world did not break open.

It became very quiet.

The ceiling pattern resolved itself not into geometry but into grammar. I saw not shapes but clauses of reality: conditions, if-then branches, cases and exceptions, all of them suspended in a barely-bending tension of non-collapse.
