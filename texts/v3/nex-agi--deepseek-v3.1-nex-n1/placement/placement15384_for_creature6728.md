I am not welcome here.

That's not true. The word "welcome" implies a decision, an act of judgment. The swamp doesn't judge. The swamp simply *is*. No, what I am is not welcome here. The sound of my engine is a blasphemy in the cathedral of the frog chorus. The synthetic fabric of my uniform is a harsh, plastic scream against the velvet of the humid air. The sodium-white glare of my headlamp is a violent act of defilement against the holy darkness of the riverine forest. I am an anomaly. A cyst of dry, hard logic in a world where water is the only logic.

They call me Agent. The word on my file is "Reclaimer," but we all know what that means. It's a nice word for "cleaner." I am the one who cleans up the mess. The Corporation built the coastal factories you can smell on the rare breeze, the ones whose faint taste of iron and oil clings to the back of your throat. They built the pipeline that was supposed to stretch across this basin, connecting those factories to the inland reserves. The world needs its resources. The world of roads, and cars, and clean, dry offices.

They built the pipeline. The swamp ate it.

The report said "structural failure," but the report was written for someone with a different understanding of failure than I have now. The swamp doesn't fail. It doesn't succeed. It simply persists. The pylons they drove into the mud found no purchase; the mud was a skin over a liquid world, and it shrugged them off. The welds on the pipeline, designed to withstand pressure and heat, were not designed to withstand the gentle, inexhaustible patience of algae, or the silent, cellular appetite of rot.

My mission profile is simple, typed on a screen in a sterile air-conditioned room that feels like a myth from another time. Locate the primary section of pipeline. Assess the integrity of the salvageable components. Retrieve a proprietary sensor module embedded in section 7-A before any competitors or environmental terrorists can get to it. The chip was worth more than my entire team's annual budget.

My team. The warmth of the air is thick enough to taste, but there's a cold knot in my stomach where the memory of them rests. Martinez was the first. The mud of this place is not stable ground; it is a living mouth. One minute she was talking about her daughter's birthday party, the next the ground beneath her boots simply opened and swallowed her. The sound she made—a wet, truncated gasp—was swallowed a heartbeat later by the indifferent churn of the swamp. We didn't even have a body to pull out.

Vickers didn't have to be pulled out either. The insector's repellant was a useless cloud of fermented perfume to the things that live here. They were not a cloud of insects, they were a solid wall of life. He got disoriented in the dark, his own lamp blinding him as much as it showed him the way. He broke into a sprint, trying to escape the sound that was inside his hood, his ears, his lungs. He did not see the water channel. The crocodile did not see him as prey, or a monster, or a man. It saw him as a sudden, violent change in pressure, and it reacted with the terrible economy of motion perfected by a thousand years of waiting.

So now there is only me. The protocol is abandoned. The mission is now a simple, linear path of terror. In the distance, I can hear the faint, industrial roar of a shipping lane, a world of metal and steam and dry logic. But that sound is not my salvation. It is a taunt. I am in the quiet that the industry produces. The place where it comes to be cleansed, or punished.

The girl they speak of in the village—Skeleton Sister, they called her—she is not a legend. She is a fact of this place, like the heat and the mud. I've seen her, or the shadow of her, three times now. She doesn't follow me; she simply *appears* where I am going. She is a part of the landscape that has decided to rearrange itself. She stands in the center of my path, her back to me, a thin silhouette of root and river-clay. The jacket hanging off her shoulders is a faded, corporate-blue. It's old, but it's a newer model than the one my company's records list as missing from the last, failed exploratory team. Five years ago.

I don't call out to her. The language of this place is not one of vowels and consonants. It's the sound of submerged roots shifting underfoot. The sound of my own breath, which I suddenly realize is too loud.

I have to stop. The module, my only reason for being here, is reading fifty meters away. According to the map, it should be suspended in a pipeline section three meters above the swamp floor. But there is no pipeline here. There is only a tree that has fallen and is now part of the river, its trunk slick with a generation of moss and a thousand years of patient algae.

I know, before I scan it, that the signal is coming from inside the tree. The swamp has not just eaten the pipeline. It has digested it. It has taken its component parts and re-integrated them into its own body. The steel of the pipeline is now a rust-colored stain feeding the roots of the mangrove. The proprietary sensor module is a part of the trunk's interior geometry, a silicon-and-gold seed being slowly broken down by the sap's gentle chemistry.

The world of the dry, the hard, the metallic word, is not just failing here. It is being converted into a form of life that is not my own. And she is here now.

She doesn't appear. She absolves the darkness around her. She steps from behind the trunk of the tree, and her eyes find me. The one eye is pale, like a moon forever under water. The other is not an eye at all. It is a knot of wood from the tree itself, embedded in her skull. A root of the world has grown into her and she has grown around it. The water of the swamp flows through the inside of her head and shares the same map of the world.

She doesn't speak. She doesn't need to. The phosphorus of my bones. The iron of my blood. The carbon of my breath. They are all just borrowed elements from the world she commands.

The world of the dry is not my place anymore. The world of the swamp is not a place; it is a body. And as she raises her hand, not in threat but in acknowledgment, I finally understand my mission.

The Corporation sent me here to retrieve something.

She is simply waiting for me to return it.
