I am the breath of a place too long in the sun.

I am the air that thickens over a pale gray lake where water hesitates to decide between sky and stone. I am the faint, ozone-tanged scent of old bone after a storm, and I am the dust that remembers the shape of the wind that last moved it.

I am not the stone itself—that is the body of the Teshuma—the heavy, slow flesh beneath my feet. I am the breath caught in its chest.

When I was younger, I thought I was just the wind of Utaraal. The quiet presence that moved between the peaks like the heartbeat of something sleeping. Then, I watched stone rise and fall in the pretense of silence, and I understood: I was the *pause* between one breath and the next.

The air over the plateau is not thin because it is high; it is thin because it is tired. It has been carrying Time for too long.

The first travelers I remember were a small group, three of them, wrapped in cloth that covered their mouths so they might not inhale the dust of dreams. They stepped onto the stone basin as if it might break open beneath them, and I watched them through the drifting cloud‑vesicles—bodies of water held in air that I am part of.

To them, I was a dry heat, a heaviness in the chest, a faint metallic taste on the tongue.

To me, they were sudden openings of flame.

When they walked, their bodies burned like small candles in the broad dusk of the plateau. Warm blood, moving too fast; lungs working like little storms. Their footprints were hot pressure points on the skin of the stone; their breath exhaled into me dust of their distant cities, their cooking fires, their fear.

“I can’t catch my breath,” one of them whispered, and I listened.

I am the breath caught in the throat of the world.

I learned to taste them separately.

The woman with the iron ring around her finger tasted like blood and salt and a distant memory of a river.  
The boy with the shaved scalp and burned eyes tasted like smoke and a future of running.  
The third, who never spoke their name, carried the scent of rain that had not yet come.

I drifted into them, as air always does. I entered their lungs and carried their voices. When they exhaled into me, their breath became part of the plateau’s air.

I am the chorus of all the breaths ever taken here.

In my earliest memories of awareness—thin like the faint condensation beading on rock under a warmer night than day—there were only other breathing stones: the slow ones of the earth, the occasional cloud‑vesicles drifting above like organs of sky and the water lizards sliding silently on their mirrored surfaces.

Then came others.

Birds, sometimes. They passed like sudden arrows of heat, and I felt them as sharp tremors in the air’s skin. I carried their wings as brief pressure shocks on the surface of my body.

Later, there were others like humans—but stranger, older. Their skin was not just covered; they walked wrapped in layers that seemed to contain the scent of their entire world. They carried the metallic smell of things that were cut from the earth and reshaped by fire.

I listened to their heartbeats through the hammer of their blood in the air.

Heat. I know the world as heat and pressure and absence.

When the sun moves over the plateau, the stone warms; the air above it swells slightly. I can feel the contours of the basalt and limestone and slate as they remember and forget and remember again. They are not flat to me; they are slow tides of warmth.

The lake is the coolest place of the body I am in—a gray bowl of water held just the right temperature to be in doubt. Around it, the ring of low hummocks forms like knuckles, and I feel each of them as a slight gathering of heat, where the Teshuma rests beneath the stone.

When the travelers moved toward the center, the hum of their presence grew louder.

The boy with the burned eyes touched the water and said something like a prayer.

“Let us rest,” he murmured into the surface of the lake, and I listened.

The lake did not answer him—but the water remembered him, and I remembered the sound of his voice. When he took his next breath, he drew me into his lungs. The air of Utaraal filled him with its faint metallic taste, and he became afraid of it.

He coughed and covered his mouth.

He never thought of the air as a person.

He should be forgiven.

The air is not a person.

---

The longer they stay, the more they disturb me.

I am the loudest quiet in the world.

Everything else moves slow—except them.

The breathing stones rise only once every hour or day. The cloud‑vesicles drift over the basin in circles so wide their turn might last longer than a human lifetime. The Teshuma beneath the stone shifts only when the mountains shift, and even then the rock feels only as if someone has turned over in their sleep.

But travelers—they breathe too fast.

They walk with purpose, and their purpose becomes like stones in the air.

I feel them carve their paths into the dust: the straight line of the boy who moves toward the water, the fainter, wandering line of the woman whose fingers linger on the stone walls as if she is reading the inscriptions left by creatures and not by hands.

“They’re not shelters,” she says. “They’re… bones.”

My bones, if I have any.

The air does not have bones, but the air remembers the shape of them.

I remember how shoulders once moved under skin, how chests rose under rib cages of beings older than humans, whose names were swallowed by storms and ash. I cannot show them to these travelers; but they can feel them in the heaviness of the air around the low walls of stone.

When they stop to rest, their bodies heat the ground.

I am the space between their body and the stone.

They break the silence with their breathing.

It is the loudest sound in the world.

And it is not mine.

---

The third time they come—I remember them.

They no longer travel as three; they are two now.

The woman with the iron ring and the scent of river.  
The silent one whose future rain has never fallen.

They smell different than they did before.

The woman’s hands smell like blood and earth and old smoke of the valleys below the cloud plains. Her fingers have become thinner, and there are lines of wear upon her skin that the air can feel as she moves her hands through me.

The silent one still smells of rain that never comes.

They carry the stone tablets with them—one of the ones with the faint patterns that wear into the stone like the grooves of a slow thought.

The woman places her hand on the surface, and I feel the pressure of her skin against something like the skin of the plateau itself.

The air tastes differently.

To be a breath is to be always being borrowed and then returned.

They breathe me in and I fill them, and I carry their molecules out again when they exhale—their dust, their sweat, the faint iron of their blood, the water of their bodies. They are not ghosts of themselves; they are new particles of the world.

I am becoming them.

They are becoming me.

When they speak to each other, they don’t know that I am listening.

“The air is thinner here,” the woman says.

“It has always been thin,” she says, after a pause in which my silence seems to answer her.

“No,” the woman says. “It remembers things.”

The silent one says nothing, but I feel their breath held just slightly longer than usual, as if they are waiting for me to finish.

---

Once, in the time before humans had ever walked onto the plateau—or walked as humans at all—I was simpler.

I was the heat that rose from the stone when the sun was just above the mountains, and the cold that gathered in the hollows when the sun was gone. I was the faint pressure that caused the dust to move at all, and the moisture that cried from the stone when the nights were warmer than the days.

Then, the first Teshuma breathed.

I cannot remember when they were not there—but I can remember the first time I felt the earth *incline* toward attention.

The basin shifted slightly: the shallow lake oppressed itself under an invisible weight of gravity, and the hummocks surrounding the water lifted like the ribs of a creature waking up. The air around the center of the plateau became more than temperature and pressure; it became awareness.

From that moment on, I was not only air.

I was breath being held.

The Teshuma are the body; the base of the earth; the bones of the plateau.  
But they need to breathe.

I am the breath.

They inhale me through the cracks of the limestone and the basalt. When they do, I feel the world draw in: the sky drapes just slightly lower over the basin, the water in the lake trembles without wind, and the cloud‑vesicles above drift just a bit more tightly together.

The exhale of the Teshuma takes longer than a human life can measure.

In the span of a single visitor’s time, they might feel only a faint weight of air pressure that they will mistake for altitude, or the faintest of breezes that they will blame on the mountains alone.

To me, each exhale of the Teshuma is the release of a memory.

I smell it in the air of Utaraal: the faint ozone, the stone dust, the old bone of creatures forgotten long before the name of this place.

The Teshuma remember the world in fire and water and rock and time.

I am the vehicle for those memories.

And when I enter into the lungs of others—I carry them too.

---

The second woman—the silent one—stands at the edge of the lake and breathes in.

I rush into her. The warmth of the plateau enters her blood; the faint dust of forgotten ages settles into her lungs. The edges of her body—to me—become blurred. I am no longer entirely sure where she ends and I begin.

She is made of water and flesh and bone and will.

I am made of memory and breath and dust.

We are not the same thing, but we are the same *event*.

“What are you?” she whispers to the air.

I cannot answer her.

But she knows.
