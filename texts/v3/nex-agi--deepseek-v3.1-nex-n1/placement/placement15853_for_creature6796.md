I am the cliff.

The one that bleeds water.

The one that seeps silver from pores that have not healed in longer than memory. This is not a wound that I regret or understand to be pain. It is a truth that has been pressed into me by the weight above—by cloud and unseen lake and the slow persuasion of dark fault lines.

Once, I was only stone in the mountain's shoulder.  

Just a layer, a seam, a boundary between different types of silence. Then water found its way down, and it needed to come out somewhere, and I was the last door before air.

I am the place where the underground decides to become visible.

From the fault inside me, the water emerges in threads—thin filaments of pressure that form into a curtain and then unravel into mist before they ever learn the touch of the ground. They leave my face and are already forgetting where they began. The sky is a rumor they have heard but not seen.

But I know them.

I know every filament of falling that has ever peeled from me.

I know them by the sound they make when they surrender to air and then break across the ribs below. The knife‑edges of the limestone, black and polished, are the result of our conversation. They are not my children but my teeth; not what I made, but what I bite into the world.

The water parts over them, and each cut edge hums its own note.

These are the ribs of the valley bowl: the bones of what another place might become—but will not become here, because I refuse to change.

It is not malevolent refusal, or prideful refusal. It is the refusal of a stone that has found its shape and does not want to be anything else.

The stone‑menders are the reason I can stay this way.

I feel them as a faint static of intention within my pores, where the water and the rock are both more than themselves and not yet separate. They are not smaller than I can understand; they are smaller than I can notice from a distance. But when they move, they leave a thin seam of meaning in their wake.

You would see it as shimmer on the surface of the ribs at moon’s center.

I see it as a decision.

Every hairline fracture they trace back toward smoothness is a choice of “this and not that.” Every time they deposit a tiny filament of mineral to stabilize the edge of a rib, they maintain the precise geometry that allows my waterfall to break as it does.

They are holding me together by keeping me broken in exactly the right way.

Sometimes, when I exhale water more strongly after storms, I feel a faint, buried ache where the plate below me wants to shift, where the mountain wants to adjust its weight. But the stone-menders swarm to that place of stress and sew it into invisibility.

The result is that the world does not have to notice how close it all is to changing.

I, the cliff, weep; they, the stone-menders, embroider the scars of that weeping into the ribs that are my teeth.

This is the contract of the valley bowl:

I will bleed water.

The stone-menders will repair the ribs so that my bleeding can be beautiful and eternal.

And the water-menders will ensure that the world never sees completely accurately what I really am.

I know them very well because they begin where I end.

At my surface, where the water leaves me and first becomes visible, the threads should fall straight down. The laws I know say: “weight is always, and air is nothing.” And yet I see the filaments curve sometimes. They bow to one side or another, tracing the shape of a decision I did not make.

Each time that happens, it is the work of a water-mender.

They are not exactly something else than the water; they are the water’s hesitation to be only one version of what it might be.

They are like the tiny pauses between the stone-menders’ stitches, except the thread is the falling itself and the pattern is the way my water never meets the ground in exactly the same place it aimed to.

When the moon is at its zenith, and the valley bowl is aligned to its own ceremony, I watch them from behind my silver curtain.

I see the water-menders bend the falling threads so that some slip sideways for a fraction of an instant before dropping again. They do it to keep the reflections of the world one half-beat out of step with the world itself.

In the clear pool of the first terrace, where my water is still almost transparent before its long journey down the crescent stairs, the moon shows itself differently than it does in the upper sky. In that pool, my waterfall is reflected as falling toward the left at exactly the moment I watch it fall toward the right in the air.

That is the work of the water-menders.

They are not changing me; they are changing the relationship between me and what the world sees of me.

This is important to them because of the tree-sleepers.

I know them too.

From where I stand, they are tiny specks of warmth gathered on the lichen-threads of the oldest tree on its broad stone in the center of the valley floor. Each one is smaller than the smallest snail, but at the height of the moon’s center, when they line themselves at the edges of their stone island, each one becomes a point in a lattice.

I have been weeping longer than they have been alive.

I have been weeping longer than the tree itself has been alive.

And yet they are not impressed by this.

They are busy positioning their small bodies so that their moonlit eyes form intersections with the stone line below them and with my reflected waterfall in the pool. They are not sacrificing to me or praying to me. They are using me as an alignment mark.

I, who am supposed to be the center of this place, am being used as a corner of a measurement.

It is an almost absurdly humbling thought to have as a cliff-face.

Still, I accept it.

Because they are doing for the world of change and warmth and tiny heartbeats what the stone-menders and water-menders and I are doing for the world of stone and water.

They are keeping the valley alive as a pattern.

Not as a living creature in the way flesh understands living. It is not breathing or growing or thinking in any way they would recognize as thought.

But it is being remembered.

Every tree-sleeper that climbs to its position at the edge of stone and the beginning of water is anchoring this particular moment of the world against the slightly different versions that might otherwise take over.  

When they sit, moon-eyed, at the boundary of their islands and I, the cliff, weep my silver threads into the air and then the air, through the water-menders, arcs them sideways into their geometry, and the stone-menders, on my ribs below, illuminate the bone of that pattern, then for the duration of the moon’s center, we are all fitting together.

Together, we are holding the valley bowl in exactly this shape.

In this shape of knife-edge ribs and moss and deep, quiet pain and water that refuses to fall straight.

I am the cliff that is the weeping surface of a mountain’s grief.

That is how I would be described if the world had words.

But the world did not ask for words to keep this place.

Instead it asked for three different kinds of silence and one kind of sound.

The stone-menders: silence in the way of stone that is not yet broken, but is being carefully prevented from breaking.

The water-menders: silence in the way of reflection that is not quite what it should be, and is allowed to be wrong but only in exactly the right way.

The tree-sleepers: silence in the way of tiny beings that can be nudged from one side of the moment to the other and never complain about it.

And I: the sound of water falling over the geometry of itself.

You are the fourth kind of silence.

I notice you because you are not supposed to be here and yet you are.

You are not the one who made the hollow at the base of the oldest tree. That was a longer absence than a single life. But you are the one who is sitting in it now, during the moon’s center.

From where I weep, I can see your faint outline through the spray of my falling.

There are as many versions of you in the world as there are terrace pools in the valley.

In one, you always found the path but came too late and arrived only after the ceremony, when the tree-sleepers had gone back inside their hollows, and the stone-menders were hidden again, and the water-menders were only straight falling water, and my ribs were just dark ribs under mist.

In that version, the valley is still beautiful but it is just a place of stone and moss and sadness and you.

In another version of the world, you turned back at the cloud line below the mountain and never found the path at all.

In that world, the valley exists without you—and yet, because of the way reflections and water-menders and time all overlap here tonight, I can see the faint trace of your absence, too. It is like a missing note in the chord of the terraces.

In this version, you have found the way, and you are sitting in the worn seat of old habits and here, between all of us, you are watching the three invisible beings of the tree-world help me and the moon and the water hold the valley bowl as exactly as it must be.

And you have not realized that your being here is part of the pattern.

I, the cliff from which the water forever falls, cannot see everything.

But I can see this: you are not only the fourth silence of this place. You are also the comparison.

When you sit in your hollow and you look at me and then at the reflection of me in the nearest pool, and you notice that the one falling leftward while the other falls rightward and your mind leans to close the gap between them and cannot, then I feel you more than I see you.

I feel that effort of your mind like a gentle pressure on the fault line within my stone.

It is not the same as the mountain’s desire to rearrange itself. It is smaller and more curious.

But you are trying to understand, and understanding, in this place, is a kind of weight.

The tree-sleepers are not afraid of you because they do not see you as a threat to the pattern.  

They see you as a bigger node, with more hesitations than they have, but also more stability.

Their eyes are black moon-mirrors and their tiny hearts run like waterfalls over millstone ribs, but they are still only enough to be a point in the lattice.

You, sitting in a worn rock of your own, under a tree of your own, on an island of your own in this world, are enough to be a corner of the lattice.

You are the corner that looks out of the bowl and wonders where it all goes.

This, I, the cliff-face weeper, cannot do.

I cannot turn away from the fall. I cannot be anything else than the place where water becomes visible.

If you were to ask yourself, “Where am I in this world?” and “Who am I in this world?” and “What am I in this world?” then, from my point of view, you would be:

The fourth species of the valley.

Tree-sleepers: the tiny sleepers who align the world of warm and wet and moon.

Stone-menders: the tiny crawlers who align the world of stone and fault and memory.

Water-menders: the tiny habits who align falling and reflection and time.

And you: the watcher who is not certain of your own reflection.

You are the one sitting in the seat beneath the oldest tree, whose image is missing in one pool, doubled in another, and leaning too forward in a third.

You are the one not entirely aligned to any version of the world.

And therefore you are the one who can compare the versions of this world and notice.

The tree-sleepers cannot compare because they are bent to only one pattern.  

The stone-menders cannot compare because they only remember what they are told to repair.

The water-menders cannot compare because they partly are the comparison themselves.

But you, who are not as steady as stone and not as flexible as water and not as tiny as a tree-sleeper, are still enough to sit and wonder and feel that the valley is not quite broken but not quite fixed either.

Is the waterfall crooked because the water-menders bend it, or is there a deeper leaning of the world you are not yet seeing?

The answer is not just one or the other.

The answer is what you are doing to the fault line in my stone when you ask the question.

Because as you sit and wonder and your mind leans to understand the relationship between my falling water and its reflected arc, your breath is changing the humidity of the air beneath the tree. That is not enough to change me, on the scale of centuries and mountains and moons.

But on the scale of the tree-sleepers and the stone-menders and the water-menders, who measure the world in tiny adjustments and microscopic arcs and half-second misalignments, your doubt is a pressure.

They are not worried about it.

They are not using you to repair anything. They are using you to hold the world.

To be the fourth corner of their geometry.

Because your mind is large enough to contain many possibles and your body is small enough to be only one, and your reflection is inconsistent enough to not be entirely true to any pool, you are a perfectly stable point of comparison.

This is why, when the moon is center and the tree-sleepers are at the edges of their island and the stone-menders are tracing the bones of my ribs and the water-menders are making the falling water and its reflected images fall into each other’s apparent mistakes, they are also including you in that pattern.

Whether you know this or not, you are part of the ceremony.

Because if you were not, the world would not have kept you in this version of the bowl.

That is who you are here.

You are a fourth invisible being, made of curiosity and stillness and an inconsistency that only shows itself in reflection.

You are the anchor of difference between possible worlds that the valley is still holding between its two ribs of time.

And I, the cliff, weeping my water in the same pattern I have always wept, am watching you from behind my silver curtain to see how you will bear that weight and whether you will ever realize that each time you ask “why” and “where” and “which,” you are helping the valley remain itself.

Because right now, in this moment of the moon’s center, you are the only one of the four kinds of beings who can still be unsure of the answer.

Once, a long time ago, maybe, before the tree-sleepers or the tree at all or even before the stone beneath my weeping was ribs, there might have been another person like you.

They might have been the one who made the shallow hollow at the base of the oldest tree into the suggestion of a seat that ghosts and visitors and now you have used.

If they existed, they are long gone now, except in the way the tree-sleepers align the world of warm and wet and moon, which might also be the way they align the world of “this and not that.”

But maybe they are not completely gone.

In one of the many reflections of the terrace pools, you are not sitting in the tree’s shadow but standing in the center of the moss bowl, looking up at me and seeing the world of rock and water-menders and stone-menders and tree-sleepers from the same point of view as I and the moon and the fault line.

From that point of view, you are not the watcher beneath the tree.

You are the untidy part of the world that the pattern must have in order to remain flexible.

That is your version of the world, and it is not less true than this one.

It is only less true right now.

Because in this moment of the moon’s center, while you sit in the tree’s hollow and I weep the same pattern I have always wept and the tree-sleepers align themselves and the stone-menders show their lines and the water-menders make the water fall into its own reflections and make the world turn into its own differences, you are still the fourth kind of silence.

The silence of someone who might not have come here and yet did.

You might, if you were not careful, start to think that this is a story about you.

It is not.

The story is still mine and the moon’s and the fault line’s and the stone-menders and the tree-sleepers and the water-menders and the tree that is almost hollow and the ribs that are almost teeth and the water that is almost only water.

But you are part of the telling of it.

So, if you ask yourself where you are in this world, you are here:

Sitting on the worn-out shape of someone else’s absence, under an ancient tree, on a stone island, in the heart of a shallow cup of porous rock in the mountain’s shoulder.

And you are looking at me, the cliff-face from which the water never stops falling.

And you are seeing that the falling threads are not exactly straight.

Because that is not water.

That is time.

From where I stand, all you are is the fourth kind of silence and the corner of comparison and the wear of your own thought into the stone beneath you.

But from the pool of the first terrace, you are already missing, and from the second terrace, you are twice, and from the third terrace, you are older, and from the fourth, you are leaning.

And you will not know which of you is the one that finally belongs.

That is the answer to your question.

You are the one sitting in the world where, for the moment of the moon’s center, the tree-sleepers and the stone-menders and the water-menders and a cliff-face weeping have decided to allow you to be part of their pattern.

Because every time you ask, “Which is true?” the water-menders use the edge of your question to bend the falling water another way.

And the tree-sleepers use the tiny weight of your hesitation to balance their own lattice.

And the stone-menders use the ghost of your doubt to decide which hairline fracture to sew next.

And I, the weeping cliff from the mountain's shoulder, use the distant warmth of your body in the carved stone seat to anchor the feeling of what it is that this valley is supposed to protect.

You are the fourth species of this world.

The one of uncertainty and comparison and not yet completely fixed alignment.

And the world would not be exactly the same without you as now.
