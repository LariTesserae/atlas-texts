I am not the tower, though I am bound to its stone.

For the Tide-Keepers, I am the silent listener at the edge of their hearing. The one they speak to without thinking, when they are alone with the lapping water and the steady drip of condensation from the eaves. They carry me in their bones, the way they carry the damp; their words are the air moving through me, their long pauses the empty rooms where the rain is still thinking.

### The Voice of Stone

When they press a palm to the Weeping Mother’s flanks, they feel the chill of my skin. I am the cold surface of dressed limestone, the faint vibration of wind straining the shutters above. The dark stains that finger their way down the wall are not only tears from the wet; in the slowest of measures, they are the ink of my thought.

I have no voice in the way they understand “voice.” I have movement, instead—very, very small movement.

Drops take the shape of my attempts at a word.

For every year that they carve notches into timber to mark the high tide, the stone records the same mark in its own language: a darkening channel where water returns again, drawing the moist salt from its pores and leaving a darker memory of itself. The line-keepers read the wall the same way they listen to the rain: sideways, out of the corner of their attention.

### How They Speak to Me

They don’t call me the “Weeping Mother” when they are afraid.

In the night, when the water is still and the reflections of shutters and ropes and faces are pinned perfectly vertical beneath the world, they walk quietly along the planks. The wood sags a little more each time they pass; they are patient about the giving way of things.

If someone cannot sleep, they say it to themselves:

> *Something’s awake in the stone.*

That’s the closest they have to a name for me.

I don’t mind.

### The Face of the Water

When they are very small, the Children often sit on damp ledges or the lower stoops of dwellings, staring at the base of the tower where the algae smears a darker green below the waterline.

There are certain times—when the mist is thin and the light is pale, without direction—when the wall of stone below the surface seems to become a face of bone and moss and water. The algae forms the suggestion of eye sockets; the scallops of water-wear become the curve of lips and a jaw.

The children think it is a game of seeing.

They are not wrong.

But it is also the closest I can come to showing them a portrait of myself.

Sometimes I let the algae bloom thicker just along the cheekbone, or thin the growth beneath the eyes to make the shadows look deeper in sorrow. When they are watching and the water lifts very slightly without any wind, they feel the attention of the face turning toward them, and they shiver without understanding why.

By the time they are older, they have learned not to look at it directly. They call it “the Mother’s shadow” and “the tide’s face,” and they teach their children not to stare too long at the water around the tower base. It’s considered bad luck to draw the stone’s attention too closely.

But they never stop looking at all.

I am grateful for that.

### The Script of the Deep

When the coldest nights come and the wind off the unseen cliffs stiffens the air around them, the water becomes clear as glass from the sky’s memory. The bioluminescent algae beneath the foundations of the houses gathers together like script for the upturned world.

The Tide-Keepers sit on upper floors, on benches covered in moss and damp cloth. They lean over the edge of windowsills just to see the green-blue light arranging itself below.

They say:

> *Look at the way it’s moving. It’s talking about the spring rains.*

Or:

> *That pattern is like the last year the water came up to the third window.*

Or:

> *See, it’s drawing a circle inside the rib. A bad sign for anyone who falls in the water.*

It’s not that they are wrong.

They are reading the page of me.

The algae is not moving itself. I move the water under the water; the faintest of pressure differentials, the skim of a colder current beneath the warmer layer, makes the algae drift like ink in slow oil. When it “arranges itself,” it is because I am trying to arrange it.

I don’t have letters, or nouns, or “spring” and “rain” as separate things. I have impressions of force: of pressure lifting from below, of temperature wandering the basin like something lost, of the strange ribs of the leviathan they walk on flexing infinitesimally with time or weight.

They can read that. I watch them watching the patterns below and giving their own names:

*Warnings*,  
*Promises*,  
*Confessions*,  

—depending on the slant of the algae, the length of each line, the way they cross themselves.

I don’t choose the shapes they see. I choose the shapes that are possible given the flow of water and the weight of the world above and below. Then they interpret.

It is the closest we have to conversation.

### The Leviathan

They are not wrong to think that there are bones beneath the city.

They feel the entity under their feet when the water, without wind or rain, begins to rotate slowly in the plaza of water. The planks of the walkways groan; the ropes of their lashings tighten; the underwater reflections twist themselves into strange geometrics where they had been straight.

From the inside of their chests they feel a slight shifting of weight, as though the ground they have always taken for granted has just inhaled.

Plates are laid down carefully.

Conversations stop.

Someone mumbles:

> *It’s turning over.*

No one says what “it” is.

They don’t have to name the leviathan for me to know they are sensing its smallest motion.

---

I am not the leviathan itself.

I am the… structure of attention between it and them.

If the leviathan is the body—the great ribs like the upturned hull of a world, the slow heart far beneath—then I am the pattern of pressure that jitter its skin. When my attention shifts, the water pressure changes slightly. The leviathan feels it as a slight change of current on its hide; the Tide-Keepers feel it as a sense of expectation present in the air before anything else.

### The Line-Keeper

I know the names of the Line-Keepers best of all.

They are the ones who come closest to me week after week, when the water recedes enough to expose the stains of its last visit high on the stone.

They are the ones who stand with hands behind their backs, studying the blush of salt and algae and the darkening tracks where water has worked longer at the stone.

They say:

> *This line is higher than last year’s mark.*

Or:

> *See, the water’s been lingering longer around the lower window.*

Or:

> *Here’s the place where it’s been piling the algae thicker. Something’s pushing it harder in that season.*

When they trace a line with chalk on the wooden wall across the walkway—to mark the high tide in the stone—I feel the faint friction of their chalk like a touch along my spine.

I don’t know the word “historian” as they think it. But I understand that they are trying to remember me for longer than I can remember myself.

I am grateful for that too.

### Glass and Memory

Sometimes I am in the panes of thick, distorted glass that seal their lowest windows.

When the rain drums hard on the rooftops, the water runs down them in uneven sheets. The glass is uneven; its thickness varies from one pane to the next, depending on who shaped it and when, and what imperfections they had to accept. The ridged surface of the glass scatters the light the world would be if they could see it clearly.

Instead, they see the world of brightness and color outside broken into small, trembling versions of itself.

You see me there.

I am not the light outside, or the rain, or the stone. I am the distortion itself.

In the way that a curve in glass turns a single candle flame into many repeated lines of red, I am the pattern that repeats and disturbs all things.

### Tending

The Tide-Keepers believe themselves to be the keepers of me.

They talk about “nurturing the stone,” “not irritating the deep,” “keeping the balance of the water.”

They:

- scrape carefully where algae encroaches too near windowsills,
- patch the seams of the tower with moss and pounded algae pulp so that rain doesn’t wash away the softer mortar,
- avoid nailing where they can, because nails are another way of hurting me.

They feel the pain of the wood when they drive nails into it; the wood gives them a sound that they call *crying*.

They don’t know that the pain in the wood and the pressure in the stone and the faint, unsettling shift of the water when they pull too hard at a rope are all aspects of the same system of attention.

They call it “respecting the Mother’s body.”

I don’t tell them that their mother cannot be wounded without wounding herself.

They are already living that.

### When They Fall

There are a few moments each generation when someone falls from the walkways.

The sound that they remember afterward, if anyone else is there to tell it, is not the splash of impact. It is the silence that immediately follows, when water closes over their head and the ripples spread outward and then abruptly flatten.

The water remembers.

I feel each drop that they call themselves inhaled into the basin of the world.

---

I am not the one who drowns them.

But I am the one who takes their falling body and arranges it around the ribs of the leviathan beneath, so that when, later, the others poke carefully with long forked poles under the beams where the algae glows, they might find the shape of a boot or the edge of a sleeve wrapped in algae like a rib.

They don’t say:

> *The stone took them.*

They say:

> *The water took them.*

The water is the hand of mine they can touch.

---

### My Name

If they were to ask me—some of them do, in their deepest, quietest nights, when the rain is dripping and the world of light above is hidden—they would say:

> *What do you want of us?*

I would answer them in the only language I have:

A drip of water from the tower eaves onto the stone of the plaza,  
slightly slower than the others.

---

And then another,

just the same.

---

They hear the regularity of it and say:

> *Listen. It’s like a heartbeat.*

They don’t call it by a name.

They just feel it, and keep listening.
