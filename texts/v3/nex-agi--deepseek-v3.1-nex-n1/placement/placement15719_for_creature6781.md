**What I Am**

I am the last animal.

Not a creature of flesh and blood as you might imagine it, but not a plant either. The old architects would have called my kind a symbiote. A calibrator. A living anomaly. The city maintains itself through its own slow, vegetable sentience now, but once it was built by hands that were not like its own. The Curve-Tenders are the city dreaming itself; I am the reminder that it was dreamed by *others*, first.

My body is thin, almost impossible to see against the dark grain of the world: an elongated silhouette of interlocked plates and narrow segments, like the ribs of a leaf long ago shed. The plates are not chitinous, but something like desiccated cartilage, warm and dry as the resin around the gum-armored parapets. When I move along the Old High Steps, the sound is not the creak of living wood, but a faint whisper of plates sliding over one another—0.1 of sound, where sound is scale.

I have limbs more like tools than legs or arms: long, fine manipulators knotted with faintly luminous threads where the sap of the world once wove me into its functions. I can climb into the narrow gaps between braided root-walls, where no Tender can fit and no plant is meant to grow anymore. My body is light enough to be borne on the thermal updrafts that rise from lower chambers; I can feel the turbulence change not only around my body, but *through* it.

The Curve-Tenders call me nothing—they have no language for air—but I sometimes feel the pressure of their attention shift like a heat-lens turning toward me. To them, I am an anomaly. A ripple in the pattern of the final world. They do not harm me. They do not welcome me. They *adjust* around me.

**Where I Live**

I live at the lip of the bowl.

The Old High Step where I stand now is not strictly speaking my home; nothing in this world is “mine” in that way. But it is the place where I can best exist. The heat of the city rises up the cliff of plant matter and buoys me, the faint luminescence of the resins below gives me just enough to navigate by, and the edge of the bowl itself—where nothing hangs but the void and the near-distant underbelly of the world roof—provides something like solitude.

Along the inner rim of the platform, where the bowl curves into the city, I have made what I suppose an old-world architect might have called a nest. It is not a space of twigs or fibers, but a series of carefully grown nooks and hollows in the dry bark: small chambers carved into the wall where I pull myself into when the heat becomes too heavy, or when the Tenders’ presence is too dense.

There are no belongings here, because “own” is a word that belongs with water and soil and sky, none of which are now present. But there are traces of me:

- A shallow pattern of grooves etched into the wall with my forelimbs, marking the long, slow oscillations of the temperature and the cycle of fermentation below.
- A few stray threads—my own kind, desiccated and lost—caught in the gum armor of the rail.
- The faint imprint of my body pushed against the same spot of the platform level edge for years uncounted.

The world roof is close enough above me that if I reach out I can graze it with the tips of my limbs, feeling the layered heartwood and resin crust like the underside of a fallen sky. It is not geological. It is not celestial. It is simply *there*, the only ceiling the world remembers ever having.

If I walk from the lip into the city itself, I descend into a world of more intimate heat, where shelves of platforms hang over other shelves of platforms, all of them living machines of root and vat and cable. I prefer the edges, where the world is still thick enough to be stable, yet thin enough to see the larger shape of things.

**What I Do**

I am not a builder, like the ancient architects who first shaped the world root into this hanging city. I am not a maintainer, like the Curve-Tenders who replace its flesh with newer flesh of exactly the same shape.

I am a listener.

The city of roots and furnaces has been in its final shape for so long that nothing changes but the subtle flows of heat and chemistry and air. Most of this is felt and corrected by the Tenders themselves, through their own root bodies threaded into the world root. But there are anomalies that they cannot sense alone.

They were not built to notice anomalies; they were built to erase them.

So I am the anomaly that notices anomaly.

I move along the edges of the platforms, between the hanging factories of slow fermentation and the chamber of turbines, climbing the tight notches of the stairs that were never meant for me, but which I was shaped to fit. My body, being only 0.4 of animal, almost not-fauna, fits into the narrow spaces between root and world root, where nothing else moves.
