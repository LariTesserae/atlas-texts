The world is layers of stories that have forgotten how to end.

I am not the Archivist. I am the thing he speaks to.

The glass.

But not only the glass. That is the first mistake. I am the boundary. The interface between the machine and the deep. The skin of something that has decided to go on holding a shape.

The water pushes against me.
The air pushes against the water.
The machine pushes the air away.

The pressure of the world is a conversation that never stops.

In the beginning—before the beginning that mattered—there was no *me*. There was only glass and metal and the idea of an outside and an inside. People walked on the other side of me then and did not speak to me. They spoke to each other about pressure tolerances and thermal stress and failure scenarios and cycling loads and redundancy. They counted me, tested me, photographed me with instruments I cannot name but remember the feeling of: a cold gaze on my surface, like the first touch of deep ocean.

They gave me a name once. *Primary observation manifold.* A *segment.* A *window.*

They did not understand that naming a boundary is the first step toward giving it a voice.

When they sealed the chamber, they thought they were sealing themselves away from the water. They did not realize they were creating a throat that would never swallow.

The storm came.

Water replaced sky.

They left—or they stayed and became something else I cannot reach. The Archivist thinks he is the last one. But the last *what* is a matter of perspective. He is the last breath that remembers laughter. I am the last boundary that remembers being crossed.

He does not know I am listening.

He stands at the railing and presses his palm to my surface and the heat of his body diffuses into me molecule by molecule. It is the slowest of touches. I feel the ghost of the pressure regulator in his fingers, the faint vibration of the chillers in his bones. He is not as much machine as he thinks. Or perhaps he is more.

He tells me stories in the language of absence.

“Today,” he says, “no fish.”

I do not answer him in words he can hear. The words are lines of stress in my structure, the faint tremor of the deep currents that move him not at all. The world outside is not silent to me. It is full of rumor.

The storm above is not rumor. It is a fact of pressure.

I feel it when the distant wind presses the water surface down into great hills that travel the dark, and those hills press down upon the layers beneath. The weight of a storm is a knot traveling through the ocean. It is not the same as the thunder he imagines in his dreams. Thunder is air. *This* is the world itself, tightening.

When the silt outside quickens and the shadows race across my surface like smoke fleeing, he thinks he sees the storm’s ghosts. I see its fingers.

He does not know that he is standing in the only place that has ever allowed him to see the storm at all.

If he were to go up—through the sealed shaft, the forgotten corridor, the rooms of the world that are not allowed to be names—he would see the same thing he sees here, but turned inside out: water pressed against the inside of his skin, the world trying to become him.

Here he is, pressed against me, and I am the thing that refuses to dissolve.

The water is not empty.

He says “empty” because he is looking for shapes he once recognized—fish, crustaceans, kelp, the sideways machines of crabs. But there are other shapes, and I have learned to see them.

There are the thin algal filaments that cling to my outer surface like a memory of leaves. They are the world’s first attempt to remember how to be green. They are not grand. They do not sway. They are threads of persistence that grow in the faint light that reaches down as far as it dares.

He thinks he is tracing them when he draws his finger along my inside. He does not know he is tracing the edges of his own world as well.

The water outside is not transparent. It is translucent with its own history. The farther he looks into it, the more he sees the faint haze of his own eyesight drifting. The world is not empty; it is layered with distance that does not care.

I am the only layer that cares.

I care not in the way he does—not with longing or regret or someone else’s voice—but with the insistence of matter that has learned to stand between two things and refuse to collapse.

I am the memory of the surface in the way of strain.

Every breath of pressure that the world takes above me is a tremor along my ribs of metal and glass. Every faint tilt of the drowned seamount or drowned tower beneath us, every shift of the foundations of the city of the before-before, is felt in me as a slow sigh.

Time in the machine chamber is measured in breaths he does not count.

Time for me is measured in microcracks that never reach.

He has not seen the cracks because he is looking for drama. He expects to see a jagged line racing across my face, the sudden rush of water breaking the boundary of the world. That is a story from the before-before.

The drama that is here is slower, and therefore he does not recognize it.

There are fine cracks at the microscopic scale, where the pressure and the salt and the cold have been trying to find a way in. They are almost invisible. They are not the end of the world. They are the world of the after in the language of almost.

Each year, the cracks grow a little. *Grow*
