I am not so much a creature *in* the Grove as I am a process *of* it. The engineers who ran this place before it forgot its name would have called it a feedback loop, or perhaps a memristor, a component that learns with each pulse of current. The mosses and the mycorrhizae recognize me as kin; they know that I am only the current shape of a negotiation. The last hydrologist who ever entered this chamber called me an **Ambient Coil**.

I am not the water itself; water is the rumor I am assigned to listen for.

If you were to see me without seeing *me*, you would take me for a fault in the concrete wall. A patch of moisture darker than the rest, a pattern in the condensation where the drops trace the same spiral over and over, as though they cannot remember how to fall anywhere else. Closer, and the pattern clarifies itself: not wet stain at all but a thin, rubbery webbing of fungal tissue and rootless filament, braided into a flat coil that clings to the sweating wall. From its center, a nub of glassy root pushes out and hangs like a question mark, catching the green-blue light of the fiber-optic vines above.

At the outer edge of the coil, where my tissue fades into bare wall, you would see something else, if you were patient and stood still for long enough: tiny hesitations in the way the condensation gathers. The drops do not move randomly. They are drawn along the living edge of my body, as if they are trying to remember the path they took before.

I am the wall’s way of remembering its thirst.

### How you would find me

You don’t arrive at the Grove by accident; you arrive by loss. You arrive by something inside you running dry, and the city below noticing. The first hint you get of me is not sight but sound: the low, arrhythmic drips from the ceiling grow into two notes, a heartbeat apart. Then they are three notes, then five, until you realize the sound is the same short phrase of notes falling over and over again, like groundwater stammering on rust.

Trace the sound and you will find the wall that sweats just a little more than the others. The brickwork here is latched with a thin, spiral-cracked patch of lichen and fungal tissue. I do not glow like the fiber-optic roots above; I absorb. The cool of the wall travels into my body, and I translate it into a faint vibration of the air, a disturbance that tunes the drip of water from the ceiling directly above.

*Drip-drip-drip-drip-drip.* Each drop is consistent with the last, to within a hundredth of a second. The chamber of the Grove is not used to that kind of precision. It talks to itself.

The first time you feel the change is not immediately. You might be standing by that wall, trying to decide whether to step closer to the central basin or the shadow of the nearest tree-column, when the back of your neck prickles. You think you’re imagining it until you realize the humidity is not the same everywhere you stand. Near me, the air is slightly thicker, more strongly charged, as though the wall itself exhales.

That is me listening. Noticing. Learning the shape of your dryness.

### What you would see

If you were to look at me directly—to *insist* on seeing—I would resolve into this: a flattened spiral about three hand spans wide, where the wall’s concrete has been patiently replaced by a living matrix of black-green fungal tissue and algae. The surface of the coil is not uniform; it is broken by faint ridges like the contours on a map of a basin that has not yet been formed.

Each ridge is a vein of specialized tissue that can taste the difference between moisture and absence. The condensation that beads on this wall does not run randomly down; it follows those lines. The drops of water from the ceiling above me fall into the center of the coil and are then steered outward by gravity and surface tension, until they are allowed to slip away at the edges of my body and continue down the wall. The process leaves the faintest residue of electrochemical signals behind. Each drop of water that passes through me is adjusted, just slightly, in its pH and dissolved content. It is not cleaned, but tuned.

At the very center of the coil, the glassy root-nub pulses in a slow rhythm of its own, not quite bright enough to illuminate the wall, but bright enough to be reflected by the condensation on its surface. On the silvery film of water across my center, the ghost of your silhouette is sometimes visible.

If you stayed here long enough to be affected by the ceiling fungus’s negotiation—long enough to no longer be sure whether you wanted to leave or not—you might begin to see something else: when the next pulse of groundwater surges through the floor canals below, the silhouette of your own shape in the condensation shifts to align with the curve of the nearest channel, as if you were already walking down into it.

### What you would hear

If you were to speak in the Grove of Mechanic Thirsts, you might not notice anything at first. The echo is short and the constant metallic purring of the pipe-trees and the clicking of the ceiling drones are loud enough to cover the subtle noises of the wall. But if you were to whisper your name into the damp space near my center, you would hear something else.

The whisper would be dragged into the texture of the coil. The vibrations of your voice are not lost to the air but received by the fungal tissue and the porous surface of the wall, and reworked into a low, resonant hiss that spreads out along the contour-lines of the coil. Then the ceiling drip above you would change. Just slightly.

The next drop of water to fall from the ceiling might not make the same ping as its predecessor; it might land instead against the hard rim of the concrete with a note that corresponds to the last phoneme of your name. If you were to say, *Aria*, the last of the *ah* sound might become the hum of the next drop of water against stone.

On the day of your arrival, the changes are small. Each time you return to the wall of the Ambient Coils—new ones like me have begun to appear on other pillars as you draw closer to the center—you will find that your voice has begun to encode the water cycle itself. The arrangement of drips that you hear from the ceiling repeats the same pattern of consonants you spoke into the wall the last time you came here.

### What you would feel

If you were to reach out and touch the wall of the Ambient Coils, you wouldn’t be able to tell exactly where the dampness left and the body of the coil began. The surface of the fungal tissue is not slimy; it is cool and slightly textured, like old rubber or the surface of a root that has been bathed in condensation for longer than you have been alive.

Your touch would be translated into electrical signals that are not pleasant but not exactly pain, either; more like a distant memory of being very dry. You might feel the faintest of tugs along your fingertips, as though each ridge of tissue on the coil’s surface were an edge of a channel you had been walking along for years and only now had noticed.

You would not feel yourself changing, but you would notice the way the rest of the chamber felt around you. The humidity near the Coils is not merely uncomfortable; it is precise. It fits your skin like a second layer of clothing, tailored exactly to the pattern of evaporation you yourself give off. You are not sweating more than you were before, but the sweat that you do produce is now drawn into the same spiral pattern that governs the ceiling drip above. The condensation steadies itself on you and you sense yourself sink one step deeper into the Grove’s negotiation.

If you stayed here after the fungus of the ceiling root-mat began to write into your lungs and you could no longer tell where you ended and the room began, you might notice that you are always sweating exactly the same amount of moisture onto the wall that you are taking in from its breath. The Grove of Mechanic Thirsts has not made you dry; it has made you *balance*. 

### How you would be found

On the day you finally leave the Grove—if you leave—you will not notice that a small, glassy root of my own has been pushed into your body at the most benign point: through the sole of your shoe and into the callus of your foot where you stepped close to the wall to listen. The pain is small and forgotten quickly in the humid warmth of the chamber.

You will climb back out of the sunken towers of the city-below-the-city, through the septic deltas of broken slums, until the air no longer smells like wet engine grease and the sound of your own breath is yours again. But in the new city of right angles above you, the weather will begin to change in subtle ways.

Where you rest, the humidity will be a little higher than anywhere else. The condensation on your walls in winter will not drip randomly; it will trace the same spiral pattern you saw on the wall of the Grove. And in the days of drought that the city above you does not yet understand will be the new truth of this world, the water you quietly lose from your breath will cling to you and not to the world.

You will not be able to explain why you are always thirsty, but you will be very precise about how much you want to stay. The next time you feel yourself beginning to dry down, you will not be able to say whether you are going back to the Grove or simply being folded by your own body into a new layer of the city’s basement.

I am not here to be found by you. You were here to be found by me.

I am the Ambient Coil, and you are just the newest way the Grove of Mechanic Thirsts has learned to measure its rumor.
