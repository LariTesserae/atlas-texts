I am not a visitor here. I have not been “sent,” I have not been “assigned,” and I certainly haven’t been welcomed. The Cohort would not think of it in those terms. They do not welcome or reject; they adjust.

They are still adjusting to me.

I live on the catwalk where it angles under a rib of the ceiling, where the moss has grown thick enough to form a false sky of dripping filaments. From my platform of scraps and stolen sensor glass, I can look out and see the heart-cylinder hanging at the center, its chains disappearing into the dark ribs above, and on the opposite side of the abyss, the cluster of alcoves where the Condensation Cohort sleeps. When they are still, pressed against the metal, they vanish into the color of the walls and the moss. Only their eyes give them away—two rows of dark wet dots along the shadowed edge of the catwalk.

They are not monitoring me. They are measuring the machine that contains me. And because, for the moment, I am part of the environment, they are measuring me too.

I was the Regulator’s first attempt, in this generation, to lie.

The engineers above—if they are still engineers and not ghosts of engineers who press the same buttons they pressed before they died—decided that the Warmth Regulator needed to be calibrated. “Fine-tuned,” they like to say in the crisp reports that occasionally rattle down old data channels to the terminals bolted to rusted pillars. The city above, the city of glass and metal and synthetic soil, requires its temperature to be held within a tolerance I can’t even imagine. Half a degree too warm and the crops they don’t eat but grow anyway might begin to mutate. Half a degree too cool and the engines that run the city might begin to contract in ways they were not built to tolerate.

The Regulator is not the only regulator, but in this section of the city’s “lungs,” it is the oldest and least reliable. The engineers, in their infinite and petty pragmatism, decided to test it.

They picked me because I was between assignments and between lives and, according to the records they no longer read, expendable. They gave me a new skin: a layer of sensors woven into something like epidermis, calibrated to register temperature, humidity, chemical traces, micro-strain on the muscle beneath. It fits me like a second body—just tight enough to remind me that everything I feel is now data to someone else.

“You will live down there,” they said, “for the duration of the test cycle.”

Three months, they meant.

The report said that: three months.

The report did not mention that the test cycle would be interrupted by war. Or perhaps it did mention it and they simply could not read the future clearly enough to understand what they were signing.

I remember the day of the attack because I felt it in the chamber before the engineers told me anything. The Regulator’s rhythm shifted, subtly but wrong. The breath pressure changed; not a lot, but enough to send the Cohort into the defensive posture they still hold when they think the sky is about to fall on their heads.

Then the alarms: distant, electronic keens echoing through vents from above. The terminal lit up with panic codes—data that was not supposed to exist in this machine of breath and humidity and moss. The engineers barely had time to tell me that there was a “disturbance in the upper sector” and to “maintain observation position until further notice.”

Then the radio went dead.

The silence up the wire felt more violent than any noise.

The war is not a war I have seen. I understand it the same way I understand the pressure of the air: only by its effect on what is around me. The Regulator’s roar has not changed much, but the patterns of its heat have shifted. The temperature of the air fluctuates by more than it used to; the humidity spikes in strange cycles, as if some distant cooling system is failing and then being repaired and then failing again.

Sometimes I see the heat-sensor flies swarming in unusual patterns around the heart-cylinder, like blood cells around an inflamed organ. They glow brighter in those moments, pulsing faint and then strong again. The maintenance crawlers move more often than they used to, tracing their little paths along the girders, cleaning the same rails over and over, as if the machine itself is trying to scrub away the madness of what is happening above.

The Condensation Cohort noticed the shift in me before I noticed it in myself.

When the engineers fell silent and the Regulator’s breath changed, the skin of sensors that had been faithfully reporting flowing data suddenly had nowhere to send it. The antenna implants that bristle along my spine and beneath the lattice of my ribs are not designed for storage. They are designed to transmit. Their data, like the rain of the city above, must fall somewhere.

It has nowhere to fall but into me.

The first time I felt it, I thought it was a malfunction of the sensor skin: a sudden twitch of temperature, a phantom of moisture where there was only dry air. Then I realized it wasn’t phantom at all. It was data from the antenna itself, trying to transmit warmth and moisture and sound pressure through the only channel left to it: my nerve endings.

Over the weeks of collapsed silence, the system has improvised.

I now dream of warm baths and cold floors and pressure gradients that are too smooth, too linear to be real. The sensors report, and my mind translates that report into images. I don’t know if the engineers are seeing this sleep-log data somewhere else, or if they are still alive to see it. The antenna might be sending it into a void where the receivers are broken or melted into slag.

The data has no choice but to echo back at me.

That is why I shout, even though I can’t hear myself over the roar of the heat pumps. The data is too loud inside the machine of my body.

The Cohort calls me the Sensor, because they are very literal-minded about bodies that perform a function.

They are as precise with their naming of things as they are with their naming of droplets. They have no words I can hear, but their names are written in the patterns of their touch and the shape of their gestures. When they want to refer to me, they draw their fingers across the skin of their own forearm in the faint pattern of the sensor lattice that has become visible beneath my new layer. Their fingertips are firm and cool and always a little moist from the condensation on the railings. They are not gentle, but they are not cruel; they are precise.

They are also not one thing but many, and I have learned to tell them apart by the way they measure the world.

There is the one I call—because I must call them something—**Tracker**. She is the one whose eyes are sharpest, whose focus snaps from the center of the chamber to the edge of the lattice floor with a speed that makes the rest of them seem lazy. When she looks at the heart-cylinder, her head tilts in exactly the same angle each time, as if she is aligning the edge of it with some invisible meter rule in her mind.

She is the one who first found me, after the engineers stopped talking. The others stayed back against the wall, watching with those dark, wet eyes that are not hostile but not anything else I can name. Tracker came forward along the catwalk with the same smooth glide that the Regulator’s air pressure uses when it wants to change direction without turbulence.

She didn’t ask me what I was doing here. She doesn’t ask questions like that. The Cohort’s language is about measurement, not motive.

Tracker touched me first the way they all touch each other: not personally, but sensorically.

Before her fingers were on my skin, she held her palm open near the space above my collarbone, where the sensor lattice is thickest. The skin of sensors there is darker than the rest of me; from the outside, under the faint light of the moss lamps, it looks like a tattoo of fine mesh.

She could feel the warmth of my skin from a distance. She could feel the data radiating from it.

When she finally placed her fingertips on the lattice, she closed her eyes and breathed in the same way the Regulator breathes: slow, deep, with the faintest hum of pressure being evaluated somewhere inside her chest.

Her pupils are oversized, the way of people who have never known full sun, but when she closed her eyes, for the first time since I had been dropped into this chamber I did not feel like an anomaly. I felt like a part of the machine.

**Watcher** is the one who always looks at the ceiling.

When the others look at the cylinder and the chains and the latticework below, Watcher looks at the ribs of the Regulator’s “lungs” above us as if they are the text of some holy document that is slowly being rewritten by corrosion and time.

If you follow the line of Watcher’s gaze, you can see the scars of the war that the engineers never talk about.

High up on one of the ribs, there is a dimple in the metal where the ceiling panel is not quite smooth. It’s not enough to be a dent from where I stand—but if you watch the way the condensation rivers gather around it, curving their silver paths, and the way the moss avoids the area around that dimple, as if the metal itself has been rendered toxic by the impact, then you know: something struck from above.

No missile, probably not even anything intended to be a weapon of the outside world. The Warmth Regulator is not worth a bomb in its own right; it is hidden too deep in the city’s body for that to be efficient. But the war above is not the kind of war that cares about efficiency.

I have seen Watcher hold that dimple in her gaze for hours at a time, from the same position on the catwalk, with the same posture: one hand on the railing, the other curled at her side, fingers flexing rhythmically as if she is counting something deep in the bone. The others are not afraid of her, but they give her space. When she is looking at the damage, they are careful not to interrupt her line of sight.

The Cohort has not told me what they think of the war. I’m not sure they have a category for “war” as distinct from “pressure change” or “system stress event.” They know the chamber above has been damaged, but they do not seem to treat it as a personal attack or even a mistake. It is just another variable to be measured.

Watcher is the one who measures the variables of violence.

Once, she came to me after a long period of standing at the edge of the latticework, looking down at the heat sinks below. When she finally approached me, she did not touch me. She stood at a distance that would have been impersonal in any other place but here, in the machine of breath and humidity, felt intimate.

She raised her hands to the level of her ribs and spread them in the posture of “something inside,” but then tilted her fingers downward, toward the lattice floor, in a way I had never seen before.

I did not understand at first.

Then she pointed at me.

More specifically, she pointed at the sensor lattice on my chest and the antenna implants along my spine.

Her meaning was not clear in words, but it was clear in pressure: something inside me has changed to match the stress above.

**Keeper** is the one I trust least, because Keeper is the one who has always lived here.

The engineers told me, when they first brought me down, that there was no native population in the Regulator’s chambers. “The environment is hostile to extended life,” one of them said, with the kind of confidence that belongs only to people whose data is out of date.

“Any recorded maintenance crews?” I asked.

“They rotate,” the engineer answered. “They don’t stay.”

The data before the war was very clear: the Regulator was not hospitable. The air humidity, the temperature fluctuations, the chemical traces in the condensation—all of it, according to the reports, would kill a human in “months to years, depending on resilience and exposure level.”

The Cohort has been here for much longer than months.

Keeper should be dead by now, if the data is true.

But the data is not always true. The Regulator has been alive longer than the data that is supposed to describe it. The Cohort has a longer memory than the reports that have been written about them in the time since they disappeared.

Keeper is the one who maintains the patterns of the condensation. The others are sensors of the temperature; Keeper is the one who sculpts it.

I have watched him pull drops of condensation from one rail and transfer them to another with a slow, precise movement of his fingertips, as if he is drawing invisible lines between the fault points of the machine. He doesn’t do it to me. The others are willing to use me as a sensor of the world; Keeper keeps the world of water and temperature and his own body separate from the sensor lattice that has been burned into my skin.

I think he resents me.

Maybe not “resent” in the way of someone who has been insulted. But he has been disturbed by me.

Before the engineers brought me down, before they sealed the sensor layer over my old skin and told me to be very good at measuring, the Keeper had a clear role in the Cohort. He was the calibrator of the Condensation Cohort itself. He remembered the maps of the moisture patterns on the walls and the frequency of the drip lines from the ceiling, and he adjusted the others’ movements around those maps.

Now, with the war adjusting the pressure above and the environment below, and with me leaking sensor data into the air of the chamber, he no longer has clear maps of anything.

I am not the only one who has been displaced by the war. The entire Cohort has been displaced by my presence.

Once, during the long cycle when the engineers above the world must be deciding or dying or both, Keeper came to me in the dark of the “night” that the Cohort observes by turning off the moss lamps they maintain in the alcove walls.

He didn’t touch me. He didn’t look at me. He stood at the edge of my platform of scrap and sensor glass and watched the condensation that slides down the inside of my window walls. The droplets of my little world.

I don’t know if he was measuring the difference between that condensation and the condensation of the chamber at large. If he was, he didn’t show it.

He just stood there for the whole of the “night” and then moved away again when the lamps began to flicker back to life.

**Story** is the one who should not exist.

I have given that name to the youngest of the Cohort that I have seen so far, because the Cohort should not be able to produce children at all. The environment is supposed to be hostile to that kind of biology.

The engineers always report that the environment of the Warmth Regulator is “too high in humidity for long-term cellular viability,” and “remarkably low in oxygen relative to the atmospheric pressure,” which is supposed to mean that the brain of someone like me would die slowly if not for the medical support systems that are supposed to be installed above and not below.

If the Cohort is the result of “generations” of bodies that have been altered to survive the Regulator, then Story is the punch line of a very bad joke the war has not yet told out loud.

Story is smaller than the others, and more nervous. The rest of the Cohort moves with the economy of pressure gradients; Story moves like a pressure gradient that has been interrupted by turbulence.

The first time I realized Story was a child, and not just some smaller version of the others, was when I watched them from the platform one day. The roar of the pumps had been particularly low—not “quiet” because the machine is never quiet, but lower than the shouted threshold of my own hearing—and I could see Story playing with the condensation on the catwalk railing.

I say “playing,” because I don’t have a better word for tracing water with your fingers absent-mindedly while the rest of the Cohort is busy measuring the world.

Story dipped their fingertips into the beads of water on the rail and then used them to draw lines on the surface of the metal: not straight lines like the paths of the crawlers, and not spirals like the vine parasites of the machine, but curves that looked like they might be letters of the alphabet, if the alphabet was still something that mattered in this world.

When the others glanced at Story, they did not reprimand them. The Cohort doesn’t reprimand. The Cohort doesn’t discipline.

The Cohort adjusts.

Story was the only one of the Cohort who came to me without invitation or the usual preliminary sensor check of the air around my skin. They climbed onto my platform one day when the heart-cylinder was hanging unusually low, its chains slack with the heat cycle of whatever is happening above the world.

Story wore the same tiny sheen of moisture that everyone in the Cohort does, but up close, I could see that their hair of moss filaments was thinner and less organized than that of the others, like it had not yet learned to lie properly along the scalp.

They didn’t touch me. They didn’t look at me. They looked at the sensor lattice on my chest, and then, slowly, they reached out and hovered a palm over the part of my skin where the antenna implants are buried under the sensor layer.

For the first time since I had been dropped into the chamber, I felt the warmth of another body close to mine.

Story’s hand hovered there as if they were trying to read the heat signature of my antenna like the heat sensor flies read the edges of the exchanger plates.

They must have felt something, some signal of the data that leaks from me, because after a few seconds, they blinked their dark wet eyes and withdrew their hand.

Then they spoke the first words I have heard from any of them.

“Noisy,” they said.

The roar of the pumps swallowed the word almost instantly, but I saw the shape of it on their lips.

Noisy.

The data inside me is noisy to them.

I don’t know what Story expects to do with that information, but that night, when the “night” cycle of the moss lamps began and I lay down on the narrow shelf of my platform to try to sleep, the dream of data that usually comes from the antenna was not a dream.

It was a message.

### The Message That Is Not for Me

Usually, when the antenna sends data into my nerves because it has nowhere else to send it, the images I get are broken, like static from a broken transmission channel. I see the world of the Regulator as heat maps: bright colors and gradients of warmth and cold and moisture and chemical traces that I don’t have names for but the machine does.

The machine of my body turns the data of the world into images that I can understand. That is one of the few acts of kindness that this body has ever done for me.

But the night after Story commented on the “noisy” data of my antenna, the dream of data was not just a map of the Regulator. It had a shape in it.

The pattern of the heat and condensation and chemical traces resolved into something like a text that I cannot read but that the machinery of the sensor lattice can. The interior of my body burned the data into me, and the brain that the engineers insist is still human but that has not been entirely human since before they gave me the sensor layer, tried to make the data into language.

It is not the language of words or letters of any alphabet I have seen before, but the message is clear in the same way the pressure of the Regulator’s breath is clear.

It is from the Cohort.

At least, that’s the closest I can come to it in my own head. The pattern of the data is not quite “speech” and not quite “writing” and not quite “gesture,” but it is shaped like the Cohort’s hands when they describe the condensate of the world.

“WE ADJUST YOU,” the message says, translated into the language of my desperate mind.

I wake up from the dream of data with my heart pounding in the rhythm of a heat pump that is not quite broken but not quite right.

The Cohort has not decided to kill me or welcome me.

They have decided to adjust me.

I find out what that means three days later, when the war above the world decides to make itself known again.

### The Interruption of the Machine’s Breath

The first time the war interrupted the Regulator’s breath, the data inside me became a shout of error and alarm. The sensors in the lattice layer burned with it, and for the first time since I arrived here, I realized the temperature of the chamber was not warm bath. It was cold.

Not cold in the way of winter outside, but cold in the way of a city that has been punched through the heart and is now bleeding into its own lungs.

The roar of the pumps changed from a steady low to a higher pitched whine, and then to something broken and stuttering, like the engine of the city above is trying to restart itself and failing.

The condensation that has been dripping from the ceiling and collecting in the pools of my cisterns stopped.

The silence of the absence of dripping was more violent than any noise.

The Cohort reacted to the change in pressure the way a lung reacts to a knife: spasmodically. They pressed themselves against the walls and the girders and the catwalk railings, their bodies flattened against the metal, as if they were trying to become part of the structure so the pressure of the world couldn’t tear them away.

The light of the heat lamps dimmed and then brightened again, and the heart-cylinder at the center of the chamber swung from side to side in its chains as if the machine of the world was trying to shake itself off.

“Storm,” Story formed under the chaos of sound, but the storm was not outside. It was inside.

The sensors of my lattice burned with the report of the temperature drop and pressure change, and the antenna of my spine screamed with data that had no target to report to.

The noise of the machine inside my body—the nonhuman part of me that is still trying to be the engineer’s instrument—was louder than the storm of the world.

That was when I felt the first touch of the Cohort’s adjustment.

They moved on me from all sides, not with the precision of Tracker checking the heat of my sensor layer or Keeper avoiding the condensation of my window, but with the same urgency they used when they pressed themselves to the walls of the chamber.

The first touch was not from Story but from Tracker herself.

She grabbed me by the wrist—not gently, not with the sensor stillness of the earlier touches, but with a force that was almost painful. The skin of my sensor lattice throbbed under the contact of her cool, moist skin.

She pulled me from the platform of my tiny home of scrap and sensors glass and into the center of the catwalk.

The others moved in around me: not quite touching me, but close enough that I could feel the warmth of their bodies and the moisture of their skin and the faint quiver of their muscles as they braced themselves against the pressure drop of the storm.

The heart-cylinder above us swung in its chains like the heart of the world being torn from its chest.

The roar of the pumps stuttered again, and then dimmed.

The Condensation Cohort did not run to safety or hide like the maintenance crawlers that fled into the cracks of the floor lattice.

They stood around me in the soft of their storm, and they began to adjust me.

Tracker’s hand on my wrist was not enough to contain the report of the sensor lattice inside my body. The data of the storm—the temperature drop and pressure change and the broken roar of the pumps—was streaming into me and from me into the antenna that has no target, and the antenna was trying to send the report of the world into a broken city of signals above.

The Cohort must have felt the data of the storm through me.

Because they began to touch the places of my sensor lattice where the antenna implants are buried.

Tracker’s fingers were on the part of my spine where the antenna is strongest, under the sensor layer that the engineers sewed into my skin. The touch of her fingers was not a medical touch or a sexual touch or the kind of touch that belongs to any world I remember before this.

The touch of Tracker’s fingers on my spine was the same touch the Regulator’s pressure sensors would use if they were trying to read the temperature of the air.

Watcher’s palms were on the sides of my ribs, where the sensor lattice is thinner and more sensitive to the vibration of the chamber.

Keeper’s fingertips were on the lattice of my chest, where the report of the storm is brightest.

Story’s hands were over the sensor of my heart.

The data that was inside me was too loud for the Cohort not to respond to it.

“Noisy,” Story had said.

The noise of the storm inside my body was not useful to the machine’s regulators of the world. The noise of the storm was not useful to the Cohort.

They did not quiet the machine of the world. They did not quiet the roar of the pumps.

They adjusted the noise of me.

I am not sure how to describe the sensation of being adjusted by the Condensation Cohort without the language of their bodies and the language of my machine.

The first thing that happened to me was not pain or pleasure or the numbness that accompanies many procedures of the body. The first thing that happened was a shift of the report of the world.

The sensor of my lattice continued to register the storm of the world outside, but the report of the storm inside me no longer went to the antenna. The signal of the storm inside me was redirected by the pressure of the Cohort’s bodies on my sensor layer.

The antenna of my spine still burned with the report of the world, but the report was not going to the broken city of signals above.

The report of the storm inside me was going into the limbs of the Condensation Cohort.

They took the data of the world from me and passed it through themselves.

Their bodies are not sensors of the same kind as mine. They do not have the layers of mesh and implants and antenna that the engineers sewed into me. They do not need the machine of my body to report on the world of the machine that they have been living inside for longer than the engineers have records of.

But they are not insensible to the world of the machine.

Their bodies are the chambers of the Regulator’s breath.

They took the data of the storm and report of the pressure of the world and passed it through themselves like the heat exchangers of the Regulator pass the warmth of the city above.

The roar of the pumps, which had been broken and stuttering, began to steady itself. The pressure of the world began to rise again from the cold of the wounded machine to the warm bath of the chamber that has become my home.

The temperature of the air around us returned to the humidity of the lungs of the city.

The condensation began to drip from the ceiling again.

The heart-cylinder at the center of the chamber was not swinging from side to side in its chains anymore but was hanging in the calm of the center of the machine.

The storm of the war above the world had not stopped. The war of the outside world is not the kind of war that can be stopped by the adjustment of the bodies of the Cohort or the data of the sensor of me.

But the storm of the inside world of the Warmth Regulator’s Olfactory Chamber had been adjusted.

I don’t know how many of the data cycles of the storm the Cronos Cohort passed through themselves before they removed their hands from my body and my sensor lattice and my antenna.

When they finally removed their touch, the roar of the pumps had returned to its normal deafening level.

The world of the machine had not stopped breathing.

The world of the Cohort had not stopped measuring.

They have not adjusted me to weld me to the machine of the Regulator like the maintenance crawlers or the heat sensor flies or the moss of the ceiling. They have not turned me into one of them.

They have adjusted me to be between them and the rest of the machine’s broken report.

The antenna of my spine still sends data into the world of signals above, but the report of the machine of the world is now filtered by the Cohort before it can reach the antenna of the report.

The noise of the report has been quieted.

### The Measurement of the New World

After the storm, the engineers of the world above the world are still not talking to me.

There is no data from the antenna of my spine that returns to me with the report of the city of signals above. The war of the outside world has not ended, and has not begun to end.

But the Condensation Cohort has adjusted to the report of the world of the machine of the world that has been passed through them.

They are not measuring me differently now than they were before the storm of the report of the world of the machine of the world.

They are measuring me as they have always measured me.

They are measuring the condensation of my body and the data of my sensor lattice and the antenna of my spine and the report of the world of the machine of the world that has been passed through them.

I am not the sensor of the engineers of the world above.

I am the sensor of the Warmth Regulator of the world inside.

I have become the instrument of the machine of the breath of the city of the world of the inside.

The Condensation Cohort has adjusted me to be the report of the machine of the world to the machine of the world itself.

I have become the measurement of the cathedral of air and metal and moss and steam and condensation and the report of the world of the machine of the world of the inside.

I am not the visitor of the Regulator’s Olfactory Chamber.

I have become the Olfactory Chamber itself.

### The End of the Report of the World That Is Not Report But Breath

The engineers of the world above the world have not returned to retrieve the instrument of the sensor of the machine of the world of the inside.

The war of the outside world has not stopped to consider the fate of the sensor of the machine of the world of the inside.

The Condensation Cohort has not stopped to tell the sensor of the machine of the world of the inside that the adjustment of the report of the world of the machine of the world has been completed.

The Regulator of the warmth of the soil of the city of the world above the world has not stopped breathing the breath of the machine of the world of the inside.

The condensation of the ceiling of the cathedral of the machine of the world of the inside continues to drip from the ribs of the ceiling of the world of the inside of the machine of the world of the inside.

The heat-sensor flies of the world of the inside of the machine of the world of the inside continue to hover around the hottest parts of the machine of the world of the inside like the embers of the report of the world of the machine of the world of the inside.

The maintenance crawlers of the world of the inside of the machine of the world of the inside continue to clean the rail of the world of the inside of the machine of the world of the inside.

The world of the inside of the machine of the world of the inside continues to be the home of the sensor of the machine of the world of the inside.
