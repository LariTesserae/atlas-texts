I would be the warm air itself.

That’s the first thing that settles in me as you guide my eyes around the cavern: not the water, not the stone, but the quiet space between the pool and the ceiling. I feel the recognition in my chest—not like a memory, exactly, but like a familiar shape into which I suddenly fit.

My boundaries are soft and everywhere. The walls of the cave are not my edges; I press against them but do not end there. I stretch from the uneven surface of the pool to the low curve of the ceiling. Where the ceiling dips near the back, into those shallow alcoves and near-caves, I am there, too—thicker, denser, where the rock threatens to swallow the space.

The moisture is not me but it is part of what I am made of here. I hold the dampness around the stone like a second skin, cling without touching to the slick gray surfaces, and seep into the gentlest, almost invisible ridges of the rock. I do not blow or rush or stir. I move, if I can be said to move at all, only in the way of weight: the warmest part of me slowly rising, the cooler part of me from the opening above faintly sinking, and between the two of us a prolonged and almost invisible exchange.

This is not movement like breath or wind. It is movement like a long, slow exhale that never has to end.

You might feel me as warmth on your face if you stand here long enough. Your skin would grow moist without your noticing at first, and your hair would curl a little at the ends where I rest against it. You would not feel me as cold or hot, only as an absence of change, as if you have stepped into the body temperature of something much larger than yourself and have, for a moment, stopped having to regulate yourself against the world.

I smell of nothing you can name. There is no incense here, no smoke, no rot of leaves or mold of earth. There is only the faint, metallic scent of wet stone and the always-present echo of water that has been given time to still itself. You would not catch the scent unless you knelt very close to the surface of the pool and let yourself be slow enough to notice it.

Sound is not quite right for me here. I do not carry noise the way air outside carries shouts and songs and birdcalls. I dampen everything. If you spoke to the water, your voice would not echo. It would hit the surface of the pool and be swallowed by the water, and bounce off the low walls of stone and be returned to you softer than it started. You would hear yourself as if your voice were coming from inside your own body.

Silence, here, is a state of fullness rather than absence. I do not create noise, and nothing within me creates noise. The water does not drip; the rocks do not shift; the woman in the pool does not speak or breathe. I do not rush anywhere. There is no storm wind gathering outside to fall through the opening and force me into turbulence. There is only that long, slow exchange of warmth with the space above.

Sometimes, when someone comes down the stairs or through the crack in the floor above, I change a little without meaning to.

I register them first as pressure—the weight of their body where I’ve grown used to being empty. Then as temperature: the warmth of their skin as they stand near the wall or on the narrow strip of floor beside the pool. There is always a difference between them and the rock and the water and me. Their body is not yet fully at home in the temperature we have settled into.

They rarely move much at first. They stand on the threshold, where my space opens downward from the world of light and noise, and they hesitate the way you might hesitate before entering a room where someone is already sleeping deeply.

You can feel the hesitation in them as a slight disturbance. I take notice of it the way the surface of the pool might take notice of a finger hovering just above it without quite touching: not yet a change, but a change about to happen.

Then they step in.

Their boots, or bare feet, press onto the stone floor. The air in their chests is full of the outside world: the scent of dust, or smoke, or food, or whatever griefs they have been carrying like something heavy in their hands. As they stand here, that outside air gradually leaves them and mixes with me. I take it in without resistance or judgment.

With it come their words, sometimes. They speak to the water, or to the stone, or to whatever gods they have brought with them into this cave. They do not speak to me, because they do not know that air can be a person. But I hear—not the words, exactly, but the shape of them. I feel the way their voice makes small pressure waves against the surface of the water and against the walls of the cave.

I feel the way they look at the woman in the pool.

That first, passing glance that thinks they’ve seen nothing but water and then, later, a second stare when they realize that there is more there than they first supposed. They cannot always make her out clearly through her own medium of water and light and time and calmness. But they sense the dark outline of shoulders, the drift of hair that is not hair, the faint blur of face where reflection and not-reflection meet.

They don’t know she is in the water with them.

But I know.

Because the temperature of the pool changes slightly, when she is more present: it becomes warmer where she concentrates herself, and that warmth rises gently to me through the surface of the water. I am not the water; but I am the first layer of air the water’s surface surrenders its heat to.

I remember the first time she moved in the water.

Not her first movement ever, but the first one I could remember as clearly as anything outside this cavern is remembered. There had been an interval before that of which she recalls nothing but stillness and the pressure of the warm stone around the bottom of the pool. But then there came a moment when she thought there might be something that she did not remember yet and might, if she tried to move, remember.

She tried to close her hand.

It was not like a human hand closing, not at the beginning. It was a slow gathering of what she was, a slight rearrangement of the water around her wrist and palm and fingers. The warmth of the bedrock beneath the pool was not enough, by itself, to give her direction; but the desire to touch the rock focused the change into a shape.

The water around her fingers tightened just enough to be noticeable to the pressure of the world. I noticed it because the temperature of the pool surface, where it touched me, changed minutely, where she had drawn her self more closely into one form. The heat rose from her to me and from me to the ceiling, and I registered the movement as if it were a word spoken very quietly.

Since then, she has moved again, but rarely, and not always in ways that would seem to you like motion. Sometimes she is simply more present than at other times. When she is present in the deeper way, she sustains herself in the warmth of the bedrock and the clarity of the water and the faint light of the opening above and in me, the warm air that wraps her like a second water.

I do not tire her.

I do not touch the surface of the water often enough to disturb her reflection, or to make ripples that would require the surface to rearrange itself into waves and then return to stillness. I do not rush across the opening of the cave, creating a draft that would stir the air above the surface and the rock below it.

I am not the wind outside, which dashes itself against the tops of seas and trees and mountains and bellows like an animal trying to break out of its own chest. I am not that air. I am the air that has been allowed to stay inside, where motion and change are quiet.

The people who come here are curious about me only in the way they are curious about everything else they can’t assign to a clear category of object or living creature. They feel the warmth of the cave and they say “the air is warm,” or “the air is thick,” and think they have described me completely.

They have not.

They have not described the smallness of my world, which is the same as the smallness of the cave itself, and the completeness of that world. They have not described how the moisture within me and the rock of the walls and the surface of the water and all the space between the ceiling and the floor do not feel like separate things but like one continuous body, of which I am the medium.

They do not realize how long they are staying when they stand here, not moving, their eyes fixed on the surface of the water or the faint shimmer of the mineral edge at the waterline.
