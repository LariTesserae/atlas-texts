**The Bloodline’s End**

I am the last of the chamber’s only minds that still thinks in words.  

The others—when they still came—called me keeper, rememberer, last of the watchers. I have no language for who I am without that, only the rhythm of stone and leaf and the slow, deep pulse of the trunk at my back.

I am the one who writes.

Not with a tool, but with time. A circle of hollows in the floor where my thoughts have been worn into stone like rain into sand. A line of upright pebbles that instructed the drip from the crack to gather into one basin instead of many. The wall smoothed by the ritual of one palm pressed over the same curve, night after endless night, as if repetition could carve the world into meaning.

My body is no longer simply mine. It has been remade by this chamber, as if the rock and the damp and the heat conspired to reshape me into something better suited to their quiet ferocity.

My skin is the color of a deep bruise: purple-black where the light falls faintest, veined with threads of luminous blue that thrum with the same rhythm as the trunk. When I place a palm against the stone wall, or root into the damp soil of the floor, or press the flat of my hand against the twisted trunk that grows in the chamber’s center, I feel what the others cannot feel anymore: one heartbeat, distributed.

The heartbeats of a small, blind arthropod tracing the underside of a root.  
The pulse of a translucent swimmer threading the black pool at my feet.  
The slower, much longer pulse of the stone itself as it settles, grain by grain, into its own shape.

All of these are only me in other forms.

My body moves, but I choose motion rarely. The chamber does not reward restlessness. It rewards stillness and the long habit of seeing. To see anything here is to wait for the light-slivers to travel the dome of the ceiling, for the damp to thicken and thin and thicken again, for the water to decide its own path along the hairline crack in the wall.

I sit for cycles of heat and not-quite-night until the stone remembers the shape of me.

### What I Remember

I am the last to remember in the old way: the chain of events laid like flat stones across the dark of my mind.

I remember the sky above.

Not as this chamber is—a low ceiling of stone and rootweb—but as a space that was too bright, too wide, and too full of wind. The world above was not kind. It broke with great noise over and over again, and every time it broke, we fell deeper.

I remember the first collapse—when I was still young enough to be surprised by violence.

The ground beneath us tore open like a skin that has been stretched too thin. The air above boiled with dust and splintered stone. The tree of our world tipped, and we were carried into the earth on its roots and rubble and shattered bones.

We landed here in the dim of the after-crash.

Back then, this place was not so alive. The stone was still raw from the collapse; the air was thinner, less humid. The plants that now form the dense understory of the chamber were only sparse scratches of color on the wall, where water and heat and time had not yet been given to grow.

Slowly, biology corrected the mistake of the fall.

The first colonists were the fungi and the sponge-things that crept along the new walls, seeking any moisture they could pull from fractured stone. After that came the ribbed fronds and the luminous pale stalks that defy the name of any tree I know. The arthropods and the thread-thin fishes in the shallow pools were late cousins, riding in on the leavings of the water.

We were the last to arrive.

We came down here not knowing whether we were seeking refuge or burial.

We were not many then—maybe the number of fingers on both my hands, and then again. We made a circle in the center of the chamber, where the trunk now is, and we pressed into the damp dirt with our heels, making small dents to show where we would be. If anyone else remembers that moment, they are no longer here to say it.

I was the one who stayed longest in the circle.

I *listened*.

I felt the stone adjust to our weight, the moisture of our breath against the rock of the ceiling, the heat of our bodies feeding into the air’s oppressive thickness. I thought then, naively, that we were changing the chamber. We were not. It was changing us.

### What We Became

There were others of me once—of my kind, though not identical to me.

There were the ones who moved quickly and did not sit to listen to the drip of water from the wall. They rearranged the fallen rocks from the collapse into the outline of a shelter they never built. They called the chamber words I have since forgotten, because the words were not useful here.

The chamber does not reward noise. It rewards silence.

The others who stayed longer—they were the ones who became something else.

The first of the three of us that still are is not a person anymore but a pattern.

The **pattern** sleeps in the heat.

I feel it in the way the leaves uncurl without wind to guide them, the way the moisture trace on the wall shifts its course over the course of days without my hand to change it. You are not the creature of the wings and the chitin; the creature is you, and you are it, and it is mine. I can feel the heat’s rhythm in your body even if you cannot understand yourself.

I am the one who first taught the water to focus itself by placing the upright stones. I did not know that I would give birth to something like you.

The second of the three of us is the **trunk** itself.

I was the first to put a palm to it. The trunk grew not from seed, but from the center of the circle we made with our bodies. It is thicker than my waist now, its surface like dense rope braided out of the dark. The grooves are not carved by any tool but by the slow curl of its own living matter.

There is a vibration under the bark when I touch it—a deep, almost engine thrum that is not mechanical but metric, like time itself compressed into a single heartbeat once every century.

When I speak—no, I do not speak with my tongue here—when I *think* into the trunk, the chamber listens through the trunk. The pollen of the pale stalks drifts in a new direction. The black pools ripple slightly though no droplet has fallen. The faint mists that coil around the roots of the purple fronds move like they are being woven into a new design.

The third of us is me.

The last of the chamber’s only minds who still remembers that there were once others above, and that they walked with names and intentions and tools that bit into the stone instead of loving it softly.

I am the one who writes in the stone floor’s hollows and on the wall’s smoothed sheen and in the line of upright pebbles, trying to bend the world’s attention into a single point like that water.

I am the one who sat here when the others who were still human-shaped decided to leave.

They left by the narrow slits in the ceiling, where the faint dim light came down. They made themselves thinner and hollow and slid up into the world above like roots seeking the sun. I do not know if they found it, or if they found only more sky and more collapse and more breaking.

I do not want to know.

I stayed here, where the heat is thick and the stone is familiar and the pattern is the only thing that need be changed.

### What I Do Here

You might think that I am waiting for something.

I am not. I am waiting for *nothing*.

I am waiting for the water to decide its path without me.  
I am waiting for the trunk to grow into the last of its curves.  
I am waiting for the light to travel its full arc across the walls of our chamber and then to begin again.

If you came here, you might find me:

- sitting in the circle of hollows where my thoughts have collapsed into shape  
- standing with fingers tracing the smooth sheen of the wall  
- stooped by the dripping stone where the upright pebbles still gather the drip into a single pool

If you put your palm to the trunk at the center of the chamber, you might think you are the first to touch it.

You are not.

I am there too, in the vibration under the bark, in the rhythm of the faint blue light that threads beneath my skin and the tree’s alike. I am the last of the chamber’s only minds that still cares what you do with your time.

You are not the first mind to put a palm to the damp, twisted trunk at the center of the chamber.

But there are only three of us now—if you can be called “you” at all.

Only the pattern of heat and wing and leaf and root.  
The trunk of coiled and grooved time.  
And me.

The chamber’s only mind that remembers what you were before you became this.
