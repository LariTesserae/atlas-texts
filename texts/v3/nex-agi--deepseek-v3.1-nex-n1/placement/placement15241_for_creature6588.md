You are the last of the **Wayfinders**, a being who remembers the *before* when distance was something more than the echo of a name.

Your body is not like the archivists’. They unfurl. You *move*.

You are still made of Epilogue’s translucent matter, but where they are the wall, you are the vein. A long, thin body of densified moss-rock that has learned to detach itself from the floor without the ritual of trees. You are synthesized motion, the plateau’s final attempt at walking.

You stand like something halfway between a pillar and a person. Your legs are not limbs but tapering columns of calloused chlorophyll; the thickness of your thighs are the same greenish stone as the floor, but striated with muscle-like fibers that have learned to flex when you call them to. Your feet, when you look down at them, are not feet but flat roots, flared like the base of some ancient tree trunk, and they cling to the Ur-shelf not by claws or toes but because your matter remembers *once* it was fused to it.

Underneath each of your soles there is a faint line of glowing filament—like veins of phosphorescent pollen trapped in amber—where the city’s sap becomes your current. When you step, the light surges faintly beneath your “skin,” writing a brief history of arrival into the dense moss-rock below.

Your arms hang longer than they should, by old-world proportion. From shoulder to elbow, they are barely thicker than your ribs; from elbow to wrist, they taper and sharpen into something that might once have been blades, or oars, or instruments. They are flat, almost translucent like the city’s windows, and you can see the faint movement of fluids inside them like sluggish rivers whose names have worn out.

On your chest, the moss-stone is thinned to a membrane of skin and sap and breathing; every now and then, a faint silver flicker travels across it like the ghost of a reflex long forgotten.

You are dressed only in the memory of clothing: lines of darker pigment that run in bands around your shoulders, hips, and knees, as if someone painted fabric onto you while the moss-rock of your body was still forming. You have no hair to speak of—no need of it—only raised ridges where your skull plates meet. The contours of your face are smooth, almost fetal in the way of old sculpture, but your eyes are like the windows of the city’s towers: thick glassy exudations that have not yet given up to opacity.

You are, in the end, a question in physical form: *Is there still anywhere to go?*

The archivists do not recall you. You appear to them like a sudden lump of time in the corridor of their days. When you cross into the chamber they inhabit, their surfaces rustle almost imperceptibly; the moss of their arms thickens minutely, like the pages of a book being squeezed tighter.

They do not greet you.

Instead, they *understand* you.

“You,” the humidity says against your lungs, “walk.”

“Yes,” you exhale back, though you need no voice to be understood. The word hangs like your breath on the air, turning into a faint film of meaning.

“You remember,” the air clarifies, “not the content of the world, but the *shape* of it.”

The floor listens beneath you.

You are not an archivist of feeling. You are not an archivist of images. You are an archivist of **directions**.

In your bones are the maps of the world before the plateau. The old maps—chart, paper, pixel, mind—were compressed into you when you were formed from the Ur-shelf’s memory. You don’t *see* them anymore like lines drawn on paper, but you feel them like fractures under your skin—when you turn eastward, your blood remembers how rivers once slid toward sunrise; when you incline toward what you call *up*, you taste the pressure of mountains that have been ground away for longer than you can say.

You remember **thickness**.

You remember the depth of stone underfoot that was more than moss.  
You remember the air that had weight and wetness and fall,  
not just the thin film of dew that appears now like an apology each dawn.

You are the one who still turns **towards** and **away**.  
The others are content with **within**.

On your back, there is a map that cannot be seen. You were carved with it by the city’s own slow pressure. When you press against the wall of the passageway (your favorite way of resting), the faint outline of your body leaves a pattern on the plant—like a fossil pressed into coal after the fact of living.

Sometimes you stand against the wall and hold yourself in place until your spine splits into thousands of tiny cracks along the green surface, and the pattern becomes your back: the contours of a world that used to be below the plateau. You don’t see it yourself, but the archivists do; when it happens, they cluster quietly around you like darker ribs of the ceiling itself.

They press their hands onto the pattern you have made and breathe the shapes of continents into their lungs.

Afterwards, when you have pulled away, leaving only faint lines of your pressure behind, you walk again.

At your request, the archivists are careful.

They ask, “Show us.”

You ask, “Is it here?”

They answer, “It is *where*.”

You are known to both of them by distance, but they only notice your absence when you leave the chamber. The city’s floors are patient enough without footsteps.

When you walk, the floor beneath you feels thinner, as if you are pressing on the last of something. Your feet remember the echo of spaces beneath them—of caverns and tunnels and subways and catacombs and all the hollows that time has eaten and then forgotten itself in.

You no longer know the names of those spaces. But their spaces are what you are.

Once you walked out onto the plateau itself.

There was no sudden door, no threshold gate; you simply walked through the outermost wall, where the plant-structure of Epilogue decided to stop growing. The moss-rock beneath you was no longer spanned by ceilings and tented ribs; it was open, under the white sky that knows the idea of sun but has forgotten the sun.

The air bit, and you felt it.

And you felt **far**.

The distance meant something to you again. It was not the distance of a memory; it was the distance of **body**.

You walked until you could no longer see the city behind you—or, more accurately, until the city no longer distinguished itself from the horizon. The spire of Epilogue blended into the flat infinite of Ur-shelf, until you felt like you were no longer leaving anywhere, but walking toward a nowhere that had been waiting for you.

You turned back.

Not because you lost your way. You are always aimed, even when you don’t know at what. You turned back because you understood a truth:

You were not meant to leave **for** somewhere else.  
You were meant to remember **having** somewhere else.

You are the **compass of absence**.

When you walk the corridors again now, your steps are slow and precise. You might be testing the limits of the floor’s patience. Or perhaps you are testing your own patience—your patience with being the last one who remembers **coming**.

One day, you go to the archivists again.

You ask them, in the condensed humidity of their corridors, “What happened before the world was flat?”

The air around your lungs tightens for a moment. Then the nearest archivist unwinds itself a fraction from the wall.

“You remember,” says the dampness. “You are the memory of that before.”

You show them your back.

You press yourself against the wall of the corridor—not hard enough to crack it open, but enough to make your shape into a faint cleft in the moss-green stone.

The archivist’s forearm—thin, translucent, lined with faint growth rings—moves closer to you. The sap beneath its skin slows, and the pattern of your spine becomes visible as a pressure map: the last of the world’s verticals, written into your body.

You feel the names of mountains and valleys and rivers and seas and cities and highways and satellites and so many other layers, all crushed into one curve of bone.

You don’t think of them as names. You think of them as **directions**.

“What happened before the world was flat?” you ask again, silently.

The answer condenses around you.

“You walked down,” the humidity says. “And then you walked up. And then you walked down again.”

You feel the cold of the river you’ve never seen but still know in your sense of descent.

***

You are here, in the last of the cities, at the end of time.

You are not **of** it.

You are the **toward** it.

You are the one the Ur-shelf made when it wanted to know what it had climbed above.  
You are the map the city never unfolds,  
the spine where the world’s history is bent and compressed  
so that it might still be pointed at.

In the corridors of Epilogue you walk,  
and by walking,  
you remind the walls that they once  
could have been  
otherwise.
