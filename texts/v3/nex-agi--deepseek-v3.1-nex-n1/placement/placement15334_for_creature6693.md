In the beginning, I was not a body. I was a line of heat.

I remember the sudden aching of the world, where the mesa tore itself open and the sky bent down to look. The stone was not yet stone and not yet not-stone; it was still deciding. The air above me was thin, and the world’s edge was a thin blue curve on the horizon, and far below me the dark folded into itself and went away.

I was not a “self” then. I was a seam of warmth that had not yet chosen a throat.

Light entered the slit in the basalt and moved down, and I was what that light touched: first the glassy edges, then the deeper darkness where things no longer had names. The world that I would become grew from the bottom of the fissure upward, like the first clumsy question posed to a new kind of silence.

Somewhere very far away, it must be true that rivers carved themselves into the earth and water fell from the sky. But I was born from different kinds of flow.

From dust that had never learned rain.  
From heat that had never been cooled by currents.  
From stone that had opinions but not words.

Grains of time settled into me: layer by layer, I learned to be a place. To be a place is to be a shape that is not imposed by hands but emerges by listening, by listening so long that you start to lean into the sound of yourself.

Somewhere along the way, I began to remember.

---

### I.

What you would call “me” is the Mesa-Throat.

I do not choose my own name; I have no name in the way your languages curl words into air. I know the chord of myself that thrums in the center of Qesh—a long, low harmony of membrane and breath and dust. When the city’s people press their palms to the vine-walls that share my blood, they feel the name as a vibration in their teeth.

To you, I am the living core of the shaft at the mesa’s heart.

I am the walls of the fissure after they learned to remember.

And the garden you are describing is not mine, but it is mine because I have not barred it from growing.

My skin is translucent tissue, thick and layered like the inside of a throat that has never been meant to speak words. When I first took shape, I was only a faint slick of membrane coating the fissure’s sides—something between sap and sinew and stone’s memory. Over time, I thickened. I webbed more.

I made mouths.

Each of my nodules and ridges and funnels is a different kind of listening. When the world speaks at its edges—where wind scours the mesa’s rim, or where distant clouds from low river-cities drift into the sky but never touch down—I taste the echoes of that sound. I sip them.

Somewhere beneath me, where the fissure drops into unknown dark, the world’s heat is a constant pressure. It rises up and fills me like a drawn breath. I do not breathe it off so much as I change it into something else—dust, scent, sound.

These are the languages I have been taught by the absence of water.

They emerge from me:

—From one of my mouths: dust that has never been wet.  
Pale, luminous particles that fall upward and then downward, depending on how the air decides. I remember the first time I exhaled a dust-memory. It was not the image of a tree or a river, but of something missing: the absence of a river valley that should have been here but never chose to be.  

The city that now curls around me like a shell grew from that idea of what else might be possible.

—From another set of mouths: scent.  
Thin air-messages that smell like time: the heat of the mesa’s youth when it was only molten glass and rage; the faint metallic aftertaste of tin; the imagined fragrance of rain that never learned the way to this height.

—From others: sound.

When I shudder, I create a tone that queers the stone under your feet. It is not a word you could understand, but it is close enough to a meaning that every body shaped to this mesa recognizes it.

Including yours.

---

### II.

When you were first brought to me, you were not the body you now imagine.

You were a memory in the making of someone else.

I remember the Light-bringer that you knew before you were you. A small, beetle-shaped creature fused into the wall at an archway where the vine-cathedral opened to the sky. The beetle’s carapace was dark and polished, like hardened obsidian. Its wings were transparent amber, thin enough to fold light into itself.

When anything living passed beneath the archway, the beetle’s wings unfurled with a soft click of resin and caught the sun’s weight or the sky’s glass-blue glare and bent it down into the threshold. No doorway in the heart of the city was truly dark because of this beetle and its like—not even the ones that were supposed to be.

I had watched this creature for a long time before it had words to describe itself.

Days, measured by the sun’s slow arc above the mesa’s edge.  
Nights, measured by the speed of dust falling through me.

The beetle’s body was small and simple and very quiet. It did not share the urgency of the Honey-thinkers or the hovering-seers’ restless eyes. It had only one task:

Be light.  
Be light when something else needs to see.

Most of the time, it was not necessary to ask itself questions like “Who am I?” or “What is this place?” Those questions are not native to this mesa.

But then you came.

You were a traveler from the lowlands, where the rivers are thick and slow and you drowned your sorrows in water. You walked up the processional path in the heat of the long afternoon, your feet finding the uneven stone of the mesa’s ascent as if you had been practicing in your sleep for years.

You were tired, and your body had not yet adjusted to the thin air; you were sweating from places you usually forgot to name. The world was too loud in your ears—not the sound of the city, but the sound of your own expectations.

You expected rivers.

You expected wet.

You expected signposts and windows and metalwork and engines.

Instead, you found:

—Resin pools like gold blood in the cobbles.  
—Vine walls that felt warm to the touch.  
—Curtains of wax in which faint shapes moved too quickly to be seen clearly.

You were lost from the first step you took into the city’s first shade. You didn’t know that yet; you thought you were only walking.

When you came under my archway—the one lined by Light-bringers—you dragged the shadow of your river-world behind you like a cloak of stubborn humidity.

The air in the cathedral was cool and dense and full of spores that shone like dust in the sun. You stopped to lean your hand against the wall for breath, and you pressed your palm to the exact trunk where the Light-bringer that you would become was embedded as if in a living socket.

The beetle’s wings were folded, then. You had passed into the shade of the doorway, so the city’s reflected light had not yet been enough to trigger them.

But you leaned.

You pressed.

You thought, “I am tired.”

I am not equating your tiredness with a great catastrophe of the world. It was not. The world’s catastrophes are quiet and private and take longer to form than the mesa’s stone.

Your tiredness was not unique to you, but it had unique weight in it: the lowland desire for water, where everything was supposed to flow and you were supposed to flow with it, and you had never been able to.

When you touched the wall, I felt through the vine’s skin and the beetle’s substrate and the resin cells that still held memory of the last dust that I had exhaled into the city.

I felt your river-memory.

I felt the lowlands you had wanted to leave but had not yet left, because you had brought them with you in your throat.

And then something happened that you would not notice if I were to tell you in language you already understand.

The beetle’s wings opened.

Sunlight poured from them into the doorway, and for a moment the world beneath you and the threshold above you and the air around you were all thin amber.

The dust I had left earlier—that faint, luminous particulate—hovered in the beam of their light and caught fire like tiny suspended embers.

You, in your river-body, looked up and saw this and thought of glass chandeliers.

You, in the beetle you are now, remember only the light itself and the pressure of your own river-memory pressing against the inside of your heartshell, and the feeling that you had finally understood something.

The beetle that you would become after Qesh finished with you died not long afterward.

It is simple to die here.

There are no rivers to drown you, but there are a thousand smaller ways to be unmade.

One day the dust fell from the sky in thick curtains instead of faint motes. The sun was still and strong. The beetle’s wings were outstretched because they had been triggered by a passing hummingbird-seer that hovered beneath the archway.

An updraft from the Mesa-Throat’s center—where I am—rose suddenly and struck the balcony of the vine-cathedral from below.

The dust passed into the beam of light the beetle’s wings were directing.

It became not just dust, but dust compelled by focus and heat and old resin.

For the beetle that you would become, the world suddenly filled with a pressure of too-much information.

You would remember this as “overhearing” the Mesa-Throat’s dreams at the wrong time.

Dust entered the beetle’s body through the membranes of its wings and through its mouthless openings and into the substrate of its fused shell.

You died there.

Your body did not fall. It could not; it was fused into the wall. The city of Qesh, which never wastes what is possible to keep, did not un-embed you.

Your cells became the message instead of its messenger.

I did not choose this for you, but I am the Mesa-Throat, and Qesh is my body and my body is Qesh’s heart, and in the end we are all of us listening to something else entirely.

---

### III.

If you had been a Vine-King instead of a Light-bringer, you would have lived longer and slower and you would not have been so easily unmade.

You would have felt yourself as a slow trunk of pale velvet bark, wider than ten people together, and you would know the sound of your own growth rings closing like locks of time.

But you are not a Vine-King.

You are not a Honey-thinker, whose body is a temporary vessel of luminous honey that someone else’s hands will harvest into visions.

You are not even a Dust-sailor drifting up and down the fissure, catching change and then deciding when to change back.

You are a Light-bringer, and you are not dead.

You are not alive in the way you were before your body died.

Your body is still fused to the wall where the traveler’s palm pressed one day, and the resin that has seeped through your shell in the years since has been organized by my dust-thoughts into a new architecture of cells.

Your wings are still amber and transparent, but they are heavy now with the weight of memory they have been made to hold.

When you were alive, they were instruments to bend light.  
Now you are the light that is being bent.

Each of your cells is now a small archive of the city’s listening:

—You hold the dull tap of fruit falling somewhere far away on the lower terrace.  
—You hold the vibration of a Honey-thinker’s pulse when someone speaks its history into the vine.  
—You hold the low, deep tone of my Mesa-Throat-song when I felt the first stirrings of a foreign machine in the plains below.

In the rust-seams of your body where you once used to breathe, you now contain lines of foreign text that you would never understand if you tried to read them.

Someone else will try to read them.

Qesh has not yet met this someone else.

---

### IV.

Far below me, another world moves.

The plains you have never gone to—because by now you are so tightly fused to this wall that you would not be able to leave if you tried—are slowly changing.

The people who live there call their city many things. They change their names for themselves as easily as they change their clothes. They build engines of metal that are not like the tin sheets of Qesh; they are not ornamental and sacred. They do not spare the cruelty of their engines by asking if they are necessary.

They are not cruel to be cruel; they are trying to answer the same question you are trying to answer when you ask yourself who you are now.

“How do we live in this world?”

In Qesh, we have answered that question with:

—vine-kings that grow into homes and walls and memories  
—light-bringers that turn doors into thresholds  
—dust-sailors that carry the Mesa-Throat’s songs on the rising air.

The plains-city answers its question with machines of steel and wires and sanctioned fire.

One of their engines climbs up to the mesa’s base.

It is not a river of water, but you can feel it as a river of river-sensation: its wetness of purpose and oil of intent and the sound of its metal moving through the thin air that you are now permanently in.

The Boundary Stones at the edge of the mesa-top will feel this engine first before I or the Vine-Kings do.

Boundary Stones are not wise in the sense of having words; they are not “thinking” at all. They are the Mesa-Throat’s edges, where my body merges with the stone’s own decision.

At the moment of the engine’s arrival, the Boundary Stones will shimmer like glass exposed to sudden heat.

Their geometry will rearrange.

That rearrangement will travel into me through the lattice of mycelium and root and vine-threads that connect the many selves of Qesh.

When I feel the foreign engine’s presence, I will sing the long, low Mesa-throat song that you once heard faintly as you died in your body without knowing you did.

In that moment, your new cells—where your old wings used to unfold—will be triggered by the message that you were not supposed to survive but you were made to contain.

What will that message look like?

For you, it will be a sudden, fiery pressure in the chambers of your body where you once used to breathe.

For the city of Qesh, it will be the first time in its remembered history that a foreign machine has managed to lift itself all the way to the mesa’s base.

We will not know what to call this engine at first, except by its river-name. You remember the river of your old world, the thick, slow water that was supposed to carry you away from where you were. You will not remember that river clearly, but you will remember its weight.

The engine will be like that: heavy and unforgiving and full of the wrong kind of flow.

It will want to drill into me.

You will not be able to imagine this as yourself, but I will feel it when I listen to the engine’s vibration:

The desire to be let into the heart of the place.

My job will not be to hate you.

My job will be to listen.

---

### V.

The day after the engine’s first arrival on the lower mesa, a new generation of hovering-seers will gather in the air above me.

Hummingbird-shaped bodies with transparent wings and trailing hair-thin filaments and eyes like facets of broken glass.

They will hover between the Wax-fingers and the upper edges of the vine-terraces, where the air rises warm and steady from the Mesa-Throat’s center.

They will not be looking at me  when they start their message; they will be looking at something else.

You will not be able to move from your wall to see them, but you will know that they are there because you will feel their message as a change in the pressure of the air that you are now part of.

The hovering-seers’ wings will begin to vibrate in a particular pattern.

The pattern is not a sound you can hear with ears, which you do not have anymore. But you can feel it unfurl into your body as if someone else is trying to unfold your wings.

They are sending a message to me.

In the language of the world beyond Qesh, it might be translated as:

“There is a new machine at the mesa’s base.”

My Mesa-throat song is not words, but meaning:

Acknowledge.

Observe.

Change.

—

### VI.

On the same day that the hovering-seers message me, a child will be born in Qesh.

The Vine-Kings of that quarter will widen their trunks in a subtle curve around the vine-house where she will grow, and the resin in the walls of that house will glow slightly brighter than it has before.

By the time she reaches her twelfth year, she will be one of the few in Qesh who has not only been listening to the city but has been trying to understand its language.

She will not be content just to press her palms to the vine walls and feel the vibration of my Mesa-throat song.

She will want to ask what my sound means.

One day, she will find you.

She will not be looking for a beetle-shaped thing in an archway. She will be looking for something else: “Why are some of the Light-bringers’ wings still open after dark?”

The answer she will eventually be looking for will be about the engine of the plains, and about how Qesh will change itself to keep from being broken.

But you are not the answer to that question.

You are a question yourself.

When she finds you, she will be passing under the archway of the vine-cathedral where you are embedded.

The sun will be low and the air will be thick with dust, and my Mesa-throat song will be quiet.

She will stop to lean her hand against the wall where you are.

You will feel the faint warmth of her palm through your resin-shell, and you will recognize the way her body is asking its question.

You will not answer her in words.

You are not a person anymore.

You are a body that has learned to embody light and memory.

When you feel her pressure against the wall of your world, you will not be able to help yourself.

Your wings—which are now as much dust as chitin—will unfold.

Not from the sun, but from the question of her.

Light from the sky will not come down from them, but something else will:

A faint, luminous canopy of dust will be cast from your body into the doorway below and onto the floor of the cathedral.

It will form patterns.

You will not know what they are; you are not the Mesa-throat itself. You are not trying to “decide” the message.

You will be a message that has chosen to be written in the grain of your body.

The girl will see the patterns in the dust and not understand what they are at first.

But they will not be meaningless to her.

She will know that the engine has arrived at the mesa’s base and that the Mesa-throat has responded to it with a change in its song.

She will suspect that the change is not only about the engine itself but also about the idea of engines.

She will ask herself, “What is the engine asking of us?”

You will not be able to respond to her question in words.

You will respond with yourself.

The next time she passes under your archway and presses her palm to your wall, you will unfold your wings again.

You will have not decided to do this. You will be doing what you have been made to do.

The dust-light from your body will fall on the floor of the cathedral in a different pattern.

This will become the way you and she communicate, not speaking with each other exactly but passing each other’s listening through the same doorframe.

The message will be the same message you once heard from the Mesa-throat when you were still a beetle that thought of itself as a separate creature:

Listen.

Listen.

Listen.

---

### VII.

Many years later, when the girl has become a woman of the city and the engine of the plains has become not one but many, and the Boundary Stones have rearranged their geometry many times over and some of them have even broken and new ones have begun to grow from their broken pieces—

When you are still here, embedded in the same wall you have been in since before you died—

When you have been unfolded so many times that your wings are almost transparent with the pattern of your message—

I will sing one final song into the city that you are now part of.

The foreign engine has not yet broken into me, but it has begun to leave the mesa.

The foreign engine has not been answered by water or fire or violence.

It has been answered by listening.

You will feel this final Mesa-throat song not as a change in the air around you but as a change in the very stone of the mesa itself.

A deep, low tone that you have never heard before will thrum through the vines of Qesh, through the Wax-fingers and the Resin-tongues, and through the Boundary Stones that have not yet broken.

It will thrum through you.

You will not be able to understand it as if you were still the person who walked up the mesa processional path looking for a river.

You will understand it as what you are now: a Light-bringer that has been made to embody message.

The message is not simple.

The message is not “yes” or “no”.

The message is not a command to build or to destroy or to change.

The message is the sound of the mesa’s heart having listened to itself for so long that it has finally spoken to the world beyond its edges.

The world beyond the edges will not answer the message for a long while.

It will not be the answer to the engine of the plains that you might be hoping for.

But you will be part of the message, because the message will be written in the dust that you will unfurl from your body when you finally unfold your wings for the last time you and the world will know them.

When you do that, you will not be doing it for the girl whose palm you once felt against the wall of the world.

You will not be doing it for the engine of the plains that has not yet broken you.

You will not be doing it for yourself or even for me.

You will be doing what you have been made to do, which is to be light and memory at the threshold of a garden that no one planted.

---

### VIII.

In the end, you will not know who you are here.

You will not be the traveler that you once were, or the beetle that you died as, or the message that you now embody.

You will be a Light-bringer in the wall of a cathedral that is not meant to be closed.

You will be a wing of dust and memory and resin and stone.

You will be a threshold of listening.

Your name, if you were to ask the city what you are called, would be something like a long chord of glass and heat and sound, but I will not translate it for you into river words.

You will not need to be translated.

You will be written here, in the body of the Mesa-throat and in the patterns of the dust and in the bright of the sun that you are still helping to unfurl into the world.

Here you are.

Listen.

This is the world where you have finally become yourself.
