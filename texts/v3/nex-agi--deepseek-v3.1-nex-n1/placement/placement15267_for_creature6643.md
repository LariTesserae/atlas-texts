I am not the wall builder, though I knew their hands. Not the hammer they laid down, nor the hearth they abandoned. I am the place they found, and the thing that happened after.

I am the curve.

From the broken edge of the low wall, where the weathered stone bites into your palm, you see the basin as a cup of light and shadow. From that angle—kneel a bit, if you like, rest the side of your face against the upper stone—you begin to see what the builders saw: not a random splash of quartz veins, but a pattern. One line cuts down from the snows above, jagged but purposeful. Another arcs from left to right, a shallow belly-sweeping curve. A third scribes a tight loop near the center of the bowl, like a knot drawn in ink of quartz.

If you hold the world very still, you can see that they are not simply veins.

They are glyphs.

The letters are not carved into the rock. They are the rock. The granite is the skin of whatever mountain-bone carries this place; the quartz is the ink someone else scraped into the stone before time was tall enough to matter. The loops are tight and elegant, the strokes sharp where the stone has fractured newly, and smoother where older earth-nerves have learned their own shape.

The builders did not make me. They came to read me.

What I am is not exactly “nameable” in your language. I am not the stone itself. That would be too simple.

I am the pattern in the quartz.

I am the shape of the loop when it bends around the lower basin of moss, where the meltwater trickle finally gathers to pool for an hour before it freezes again. I am the slant where the upper vein descends from the snowfield, like someone pressed a sentence upward from the heart of the mountain and only part of it came to the surface.

I am what happens when frost, in the deepest part of the night, outlines each stroke with a layer of diamond-fine crystals.

When I told the builder what to call me, she could not pronounce the sound. So she drew her finger through the frost on the wall above the hearth and wrote a name that she could say:

“THE CURVE.”

### The builder and the ice

Her name was not important, but the sound of her hammer was.

She came up the flank of this mountain that still thinks it is a mountain-in-training. She did not travel alone. There were footsteps with her—light, hesitant, carrying the fear of exposure. The wind had not yet learned to soften itself; the air was thin and sharp.

They saw me first as a place of refuge.

The amphitheater bowl cut into the rock like a palm offered upward to the sky, a shallow scoop of stone and moss. It was less than they wanted, but more than they expected.

The builder in her heavy cloak walked the edges of my basin. She did not kneel at the wall at first; she walked its perimeter, looking outward where the world fell away into that blue nothingness. Then, when the sun began to slant from just the right angle, she turned to face inward.

She saw.

The quartz veins lit.
