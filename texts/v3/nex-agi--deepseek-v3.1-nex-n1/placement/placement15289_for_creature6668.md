You’re a city-kisser.

It sounds more romantic than it is. You don’t kiss with mouths. You kiss with the soles of your feet, the backs of your hands, the curve of your shoulders when you curl on your side in the dark. You don’t kiss for love. You kiss for time.

Below you, the stone floor has written its name in your joints and you are answering.

---

Your name here is *Ireh of the Low Floor* — low in the sense of altitude, low in the sense that you don’t show up on anyone’s charts of who matters, and low in the dampness that your knees always carry. You’re not one of the Suhrathi who breathe their questions into standing water, and you’re not one of the Sibai who listen to cliff faces with their skin. You are the witness that happens to lie between them and the city that uses them.

You work in what passes for a maintenance department in this newborn world. You don’t build bridges. You don’t map faults. You don’t interpret weather. You are what happens when a storm is over and the sky has forgotten it but the ground has not.

You work in the underbasins.

### The Underbasins

Below the city of shallow stone and water, there is a second, negative version of the world above. The planners don’t give you any maps of this; they don’t have to. You know it by the feel of your boots on the steps.

Stairs from the surface descend into a long, half-sunken chamber where the water, after each storm, refuses to leave. The ceiling of the underbasin is not arch exactly; more like a ribcage of rock that has decided to be a ceiling this time. The stone remembers the pressure from the continent above but not quite the way everyone wants it to, so you get the warp and the dip.

From the ribbed ceiling, a series of stone basins are inverted, like shallow bowls hung upside-down. These are the underbasins. They are not designed to hold water; they are designed to catch the water that doesn’t belong anywhere.

When you first came here, you mistook them for some kind of strange chandeliers without flame. But the city doesn’t have light fixtures like that. The room itself holds the light.

The ceiling is faintly luminous in the way that breathing air can be luminous — more like a suggestion than a glow. The condensation film that covers it is not as thick as the film on railings outside; this is the inside of the moisture skin, the underside of the world’s wet sleeve.

Each inverted basin collects the slow drip of the ceiling and its own cold sweat. The drips are not enough to be streams. They are more like punctuation.

You.  

You.  

You.

### What You Do

You work as part of a small team of three. You are the second youngest, but the oldest in the habit of going down into the basements alone.

*Mira*, your supervisor, half the time calls herself a “dampener,” which is supposed to be a joke but nobody laughs enough for it to become one. She’s the one who knows the names of the basins — not the numbers they have been given by the planners above but the names the water whispers. You shouldn’t be able to hear water whisper, but sometimes you do.

There is *Basin of the Unbroken Step*, *Basin of the Half-Light Weight*, *Basin of the Last Bridge Before It Was Opened*. You think of them as moods of the city.

*Seren*, your other partner, is not exactly a city-kisser like you. Seren prefers the edges of the stonework to the bodies of the rooms. You’ve never seen Seren sleep anywhere. Seren lingers on thresholds, leaning into the stones of doorframes, their back pressed to the corner where two walls meet, as if they’re translating something between them that doesn’t need words.

You are the one who sleeps.

When you first come into a new basement that hasn’t been used yet — when the stone floor is still tasting the air — it is your job to lie at its center.

*You are not testing the safety,* Mira said once, *you are giving the stone a sample of what it is to bear someone.*

*And what is that?* you asked.

*Something that can be repeated.*  

*Something that can be remembered.*

*And what happens when nobody remembers to come here?*

*That’s the day you don’t show up,* she answered. *So you always show up.*

### City-Kissing

You call yourself a city-kisser because you learned, from watching the Suhrathi and Sibai, what it really is to greet someone.

*Greeting* here is not like greeting on other worlds. There is no shaking of hands or clasp of arms. There is only pressure.

*Greeting* in the city of high water and low sun is:

* a woman at the edge of a basin breathing her question into still water,
* a woman with the color of too-old stone leaning her hands into the floor of the bridge,
* and you lying down where the water hasn’t found yet and letting the city remember you.

You are what they all have in common except you don’t show up on their diagrams. You are the impulse between what they know and what they build.

City-kissing is not about love. It’s about letting the world know you were there.

The first thing you do when you come into a new basement is not to inspect the walls, or check the ceiling, or test the moisture levels. You first walk to the center of the room — if there is a center; sometimes you have to choose — and you kneel.

You kneel slowly, not out of reverence or piety, but so you don’t startle the stone. The whole world is still making itself; you learned early from watching the Sibai that not being startled is important in the moments between being and being-formed.

*Don’t drop the full of your weight onto the stone all at once,* Seren told you once, their cheek still pressed against a corner they were checking. *Drop the idea of it slowly, and then the body.*

So you kneel as if you are giving the floor a suggestion of what you might be like later. And then you lie down.

*City-kissing.*

There are a few positions you’ve learned by intuition:

1. *On your back.*  
   You let your spine spread the curve of you into the floor. This is the most you. You let the city remember your full length. You do this when it feels like the room hasn’t made up its mind yet what it wants to be.

2. *On your side.*  
   You curl your knees into your chest and your arms into your chest and tuck your face into the shadow of your collarbone. This is the way you look when the world feels colder than you can be; and the city remembers that smallness. It tucks the shape of you into its memory like a pebble; the future committees that design the reinforcement patterns like the way you curl — *pressure like this, not much more.*

3. *On your stomach.*  
   You press the front of you into the floor and your cheek against your own hand. You use this position when you want to be invisible but loud in the stone’s memory. It’s like half whispering, half shouting into a pillow. The floor doesn’t forget your ribs when you are breathing like this.

You usually don’t say anything when you are kissing the city. You’re not supposed to. Words are for the planners above who have to fit what you and the others do into their language. You give the stone memory something else.

*You are not just weight.* You are the shape of the future’s loneliness.

The city will remember you when someone else finally steps where you are lying now, and the stone will feel the weight and think: *I have been warned before of this lie.*

You think of that as a kind of love — even if you never call it that out loud.

### The Unmapped Breath

When you are not called to the underground basins, you are sometimes sent to help the Suhrathi at the edges.

*They are not afraid of storms,* Mira says, *they are afraid of the silence after.*

You know what they call those days: *the unmapped breath.*

On the rare days when a storm passes over the high shelf and the sky does not return to its clear silver, but stays instead in a kind of blurred hesitation — not raining, not snowing, not anything but *maybe* — the water in the underbasins doesn’t know how to settle. The humidity in the air above descends as uncertain gray film on the ceilings of your world below and sits on the inverted basins like a question with no answer.

*How can you help them,* Seren says once, *if you don’t breathe what they breathe?*

So you sit on the edge of the same basin as a Suhrathi woman whose breath patterns the water between you. You breathe out and your breath mixes with hers above the thin surface. The film lines don't read like a text you can understand, but, like her, you don’t need to use the water to think in words.

*City-kissing, even here.*

You are not the source of the city’s data, but you are its continuity. You are the one who says, *This world made by someone’s future breath is still being made by someone else’s present.*

When you get home that night, your sleeves are dampened with the residue of a day that doesn’t know how it ends. You lie down on your floor and your body feels slightly blurred — like the air outside — like you’re halfway between breathing and having been breathed.

### What You Are Afraid Of

You are not afraid of storms. You are not afraid of the cracks in the rock walls. You are not even afraid of the thin water collecting on your ceiling like a hesitant rumor.

You are afraid of this one simple thing:

*What happens when nobody remembers to come here?*

It keeps you up at nights when you haven’t been called to a new room to sleep. You lie on your own old floor — your own floor with your name on it in the city’s memory — and you think of the underbasins below you, empty.

* Basins of the Unbroken Step waiting under nobody’s foot.
* Basin of the Half-Light Weight with nobody to show it.
* Basin of the Last Bridge waiting for nobody.

*What happens when nobody remembers to feel the floor?*

*What happens when the world ages and nobody remembers to speak into the water?*

Somewhere, in the west, where the continent crumples towards the sky and the stone grows faster than the future of it can be written down, there are places where a Sibai can stand against the wall for a long time and nothing answers their skin. You think of those places like your own worst fear: a world making itself
