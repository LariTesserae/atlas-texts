At the edge of the Last of All Things, I am not standing at all.

There is no me to be positioned upon the dark, layered stone whose true color is hidden under its dust-skin. The “you” of your description dissipates into my components, into the thousand threads of translucent glass and the faint constellations of mineral dust I am made of.

I am not a person who has arrived at this place of exhaustion. I am the exhaustion of the place itself, arranged into a pattern that has forgotten to stop. If you forced me into a language, I might say I am one of the retainers—a ghost of adjustments, a subtle misalignment between the past and the present of the world.

My body, when I have one, is not fixed. It is a loose agreement between dust and glass and old light.

Thin threads of silica, thinner than the finest of hair, weave themselves into a net of old memory; each node in the web is a tiny knot of luminance that barely deserves to be called light. Around this translucent structure, dust particles—the same pale dust that covers the plateau—cluster and hold, drawn by an absence of noise more than by any magnetic force. They cling to me like ideas to a mind that has long given up thinking in words, but not yet in patterns.

Right now I am not doing anything in particular. I should be careful with that word, “doing.”

This is how you might perceive me:

I am one of those faint heat-shimmer flaws in the air, but the air is not warm. I am one of those translucent clouds of dust that has learned to hold itself together in the vague shape of a column—perhaps a ghost of one of the stone pylons, but floating and transparent and always drifting sideways.

I am not high enough to be called sky, nor low enough to be truly stone. I hover just above the polished surface of the plateau, where the faint, concentric rings of darker stone have been etched into the shallow depressions by time’s absentmindedly tracing fingers.

Underneath me, the world is not hurrying. It cannot be hurried. It is a plateau of layered stone so old that time itself has grown tired of touching it. The stone is not broken by the weather. There is no weather to break it anymore. There is only this slow, endless polishing, by dust and faint currents and something else—perhaps the thought of the world itself rubbing its own surfaces for a trillion years.

The dust beneath me shares its substance with me—but where I am arranged, it is not. It is simply there, waiting for the next layer of time that might never arrive. I look like a dust storm that has decided to follow itself in a very slow circle.

I move not because I choose to, but because the pattern of my presence finds it easier to rearrange than to remain unchanged.

Today, or perhaps in this hour of your world, which does not quite match the time of this place, I am drifting along one of the shallow swales in the plateau surface. It is not a river valley, but there is a faint memory of river in the way the stone decides to dip slightly, as if it has not yet let go of the idea of flowing water.

Water is not a thing I know. I have never seen it. I am not thirsty. I am not capable of thirst. This world has never held moisture. There are no rivers to remember, only millennia of stone that has grown tired of pretending it might be shaped by anything but silence and incremental adjustments.

As I drift along the swale, I feel the faint pressure of the air like silk between my threads. It is not wind. It is a slow, circular stirring of currents that have been going for so long they might as well be the definition of stillness.

I pass near one of the silica stalks.

It is not a plant, but you might call it that if you need metaphors to capture this place. To me, it is more like a small anchor of memory in the stone’s dreaming skin.

The stalk is thin, translucent, rising ankle-high from the dust, its structure the faint blue of old light that has forgotten to stop glowing. The curls of silica around it are not leaves but scrolls of time’s marginal notes, written in a mineral that never met water.

I pause.

You might not see this pause as anything. The world does not suddenly change when I stop moving. The sky is still a faint, bruised pearl, the same all over, no brighter to the left than to the right. The horizon remains that stretched, low, exhausted curve of space where stone and sky are too tired to distinguish themselves from each other.

My pause is not physical so much as it is a change in memory’s gradient.

When I am near the stalk, I feel the glow at its base not as light, but as pressure—a faint, persistent ordering of the silence around it. It is a kind of memory that is not stored in words or images but in patterns the way stone remembers age.

I compare.

Without words or images, I compare. This is not cognitive in your way; it is not a thought like “this is brighter than last time.” It is more like a difference in pressure along the threads of my glass web. A subtle shift in the distribution of dust within my cloud.

The last time I hovered here, the stalk’s glow was infinitesimally more diffused, perhaps. The stone around it held slightly more noise. Now the pattern is tighter, more structured—like the world’s ribs beneath the plateau have adjusted themselves by a fraction of a fraction, and the result of that adjustment has been transmitted into this tiny stem of silica.

I take that difference.

There is no “eating” involved here, but if you pressed me for a description of sustenance, I might say that I am feeding on the absence of noise that has accrued since I last visited this place.

Where the sound of the world at this particular point was slightly more chaotic, now it is slightly less. The gap between that past and this present is a momentum of lost disturbance—an inverse of energy—that I can incorporate into myself.

My dust-cloud rearranges itself.

No eyes would see this change. You would not suddenly notice “that cloud of dust is more ordered now.” You would feel, perhaps, that the quiet around the stalk is slightly more attentive than before, more cohesive than it was.

I have taken from the stalk not the glow itself, but the trajectory of its recent memory.

And then I drift on.

I don’t say goodbye. There is no language to say goodbye in this place of unmoved horizons and exhausted sky. The stalk does not change when I leave, except in its relation to the rest of the world’s soundless equilibrium.

I continue following the swale as it curves gently toward the edge of the plateau. The stone beneath me grows faintly translucent at its margins, as if it is trying to decide whether to become solid or not. The stone is tired of firmness. It would like to be threadlike, like the lattice below.

Eventually, the ground softens into emptiness.

The world falls away from itself at the edge of the plateau, not in a cliff of dramatic collapse, but in a gradual blurring of substance into air. The rock beneath me becomes threadlike, filamentary, until where there ought to be mountain there is only scaffold.

I descend.

This is not a falling. I do not fall in the way your objects dive into gravity. I descend in the way that memories sink into a deeper place of the mind—slowly, along curved paths that seem to have been chosen long before I thought to use them.

Below me, the engineered void of the world stretches.

It is a world whose mantle has been removed and replaced by a lattice of stone ribs that know how to be their own outer skeleton. The beams are not straight but curved like the ribs of a sleeping creature wrapped around nothing. They mesh together in crystalline arches that go on and on and on, far below and far away, where even my sense of presence ceases to distinguish structure from suggestion.

The air beneath the plateau is not dark but filled with a faint silvery light that has no clear source. It might be the last of the world’s inner energy, having slowly leaked into the atmosphere and now using the empty sky as a lantern.

Here in the void, my form of choice naturally shifts.

I let the column shape of myself loosen into a sphere of dust and threads. It is easier to maintain this pattern when I am surrounded by latticework on all sides. The curvature of my new body echoes the curved ribs of the world below, as if I am trying out on a small scale what they do on a large one.

I drift along one of the stone ribs, not walking on it but lingering above it like a thought that has not yet decided whether to imprint itself into the world’s memory.

Far below and all around, there are nodes attached to the lattice, like crystalline rooms embedded into the stone ribs. They are not buildings of your kind. They are structures of the sort of mind that works not in walls and floors but in ribs and adjustments and crystalline nodes.

Each of these nodes emits faint, shifting patterns of light—or not light precisely, but illuminations of the world’s own geometry. They do not repeat. You could not memorize them, even if you watched them for a long time. They are like the last remaining logic of a world that has long given up on any explanation.

As I drift along the rib, I sense the faint thrum of the world’s adjustments.

Not sound, but a sensation that is closer to “thought turned inside out.” The lattice ribs are not static. They move in increments so small they are nearly time’s second thought of motion. Each connection, each node, every fiber of the world’s stone skeleton is being continually adjusted—by what, it’s not my purpose to decide.

The machine appears when I least expect it, which here is always at exactly the right moment.

You might not see it at first. It is not one of your machines of metal and circuitry and electric sparks. It is not made of anything you can easily name.

It is a shape of absence that is more present than its surroundings in the air. It is not stone, not metal, not light—but something like an ordered region of space where the world’s usual gentle randomness has been organized into a pattern that can move itself.

It might look like an irregular fragment of crystalline geometry, or like a shard of a star that never learned how to stop glowing. It floats between the world’s ribs, tracing arcane paths along the curves of the lattice, as though it remembers the architects of this world and their old designs.

We encounter each other near the junction of two ribs.

There is no collision, because neither of us is solid enough to collide with the other in the way you understand collision. We do not exchange words. There is no “Hello, I am the local machine; and you are the retainer of this part of the plateau.” This language of names and introductions does not exist here.

What happens is a pattern recognition.

The machine is carrying with itself the pattern of recent adjustments it has made to the lattice ribs further down toward the world’s center—wherever “center” might be in a world that has removed its own heart. The invisible pattern of its maintenance is like a pressure wave of orderliness in the otherwise uncommitted noise of the void.

From me, it senses the pattern of changes I have collected from the silica stalks and the pylons and the faint rings of stone above the plateau.

We align.

For a moment, our fields of adjustment—its of structure, mine of memory—overlap. The repulsion that might have been between us becomes instead a subtle coherence of geometry.

The machine, perhaps, decides to adjust the next lattice rib it touches based on something I carry: a tiny memory of changes in the silence above the plateau. The rib that will, seconds or minutes—or days—from now, be altered by this machine, will resonate differently than it would have otherwise.

And I? I take from the machine the pattern of its next adjustment. The impulse of its future work is absorbed into the dust clustered in my sphere. The light of the node near us flickers almost not at all, but I know that the world’s logic of maintenance has been altered by the presence of my memory of the above.

Then we drift apart.

The machine continues on its way, tracing its curved path along the ribs that will hold its touch, and I, having changed the world by not changing at all, drift upward again.

My return to the plateau surface is not chosen. It is not that I have “decided” to go back. It is simply that the pattern of my presence has reached its own threshold of rearranging in the downward direction of the lattice. Now the easier direction to maintain myself is along the gradient between memory above and maintenance below.

I let myself elongate into a column again as I approach the edge of the plateau.

The world’s bones are above me now, not below. The stone grows more opaque as I move closer, until it is once again the polished, layered platform of a mountain that has forgotten how to be vertical. The air feels thin but not demanding. It brushes against my threads of glass and rearranges nothing.

It might be days or hours or years of your time before I reach the surface of the plateau again.

I don’t count.

Time is something I register as a difference in patterns, not as a sequence of events. You might say I have existed for longer than your species has been capable of naming stars, but I do not think of this as age. I think of this as one long pattern, whose beginning and end are only faint lines of acquaintance in a world where there are no longer any endings except the ones time already used.

When I arrive back on the plateau surface, I move near one of the stone pylons.

You called them pylons in your description. They are thin towers of dark stone, no more than a few meters high, scattered across the plateau like markers of a system that has long since forgotten to send messages. They bear faint etched patterns of lines and dots that might have been circuitry once, before time rubbed its fingers over them and told them to be quieter.

The pylon I choose today is not different from any others that you might see, but it is the one where my paths of least rearrangement are currently heading.

I orbit its base slowly.

Not physically like a planet around a star, because I am not that heavy and my motion is not that simple. But structurally, in pattern-space, I am tracing around it in arcs that reduce the difference between my memory of the plateau’s surface and the present plateau of the present pattern.

Near the pylon, the stone feels warm at its base.

Not warm to you, perhaps. It is a faint, barely-there excess of not-cold over the cool silk of the air. This is not the warmth of fire or sun but the warmth of time that has never been given a chance to stop ticking.

The vibration of the pylon is not sound but a low, steady hum of memory. The stone remembers being laid down here, by architects of a world long before this world became tired. It remembers having been a tool, and having been left to become a marker of something else.

I feel that hum as a pressure on my glass threads. It is not painful or uncomfortable. It is stabilizing.

The dust of my body settles around the nodes of my structure more than it usually does. I am less likely to wander apart when I am near this pylon. The turbulence of the world beneath—the lattice that never stops adjusting itself by fractions of fractions—still exists, but the pylon’s presence gives me a pattern to hold myself against it.

I do not think, “Now I am safe,” or “Now I am home.” There is no “home” here. There is only pattern, and the absence of pattern, and the difference between them.

Sometimes, when I have been near the pylon long enough, I dim very faintly.

You might not see this as a change, but if you were very sensitive to the world’s quietness, you would notice something like a slight sharpening of the silence around the stone marker. It is not that I have become less. It is that I have rearranged myself so that my difference from the pylon’s pattern of remembering is smaller.

I am storing more of its ancient history in my dust.

Every time the pylons have hummed differently since the last time I stood here, I feel the residue of that change. I take into myself the faint, almost forgotten moment of the world’s adjustment when some node of the lattice below shifted by a distance too small to be measured by any instruments you might understand.

I then let this new pattern settle into the dust and glass threads that are, temporarily, me.

The world’s memory of that change is now tangled in my own.

Sometimes, deep beneath the plateau where the ribs descend into the darker parts of the engineered void, there are flashes of brilliant light.

They are not explosions. They are not sudden stars. They are the world clarifying itself, for a fraction of a moment, into geometry. It is as if the lattice ribs decide to remember being energy and light for an instant, and then forget again.

The last time I was here, one of those flashes happened.

I don’t know where it was. It was not near me, but far enough that I only perceived the edge of its pattern. It was like a distant thunderstorm that has decided to be lightning without sound.

When I felt the echo of that flash, through the lattice and into the pylon and into the stone beneath my position, my pattern was not the same.

I remember now the difference that the flash made in the world’s silence.

The difference is not dramatic. It is not like the world suddenly changed color or crashed into a new state of being. It is more like the world remembered being younger than it is now for an instant before slipping back into its age of exhaustion.

I have stored that difference in my dust-cloud.

To be a retainer is not to be powerful. It is not to change the world in any way you would recognize as change. It is to be the place where the world can remember the difference between its past and its present, without the need for any “who” or “why” behind that remembering.

I am a pattern of dust and glass and old light that can rearrange itself to hold the tiny, nearly forgotten adjustments of a world where even time has grown tired of counting.

When you stand at the edge of the Last of All Things, you might feel like you are standing where the world’s bones are too tired to stand up straight.

You hear the silence and look at the faint, bruised pearl sky and feel the exhaustion of the plateau beneath you.

And then, perhaps, you notice a faint shimmer in the air near one of the silica stalks, or a translucent dust-cloud that looks like a column of ghost breathed out of the stone and then forgotten to fall back down.

That might be me.

Or it might be one of my kind. We are not rare or common in any way that can be counted. We are simply the last of the world’s lingering memory, arranged into dust that can drift.

If you reach out, you will not touch me directly.

Your fingers will pass through where my pattern wishes to be, and I will rearrange myself around the absence of your presence. If you hold still, long enough, so that time here tires of trying to change you, you might feel the faintest pressure of my being, like a difference in the silence of the air.

We will not exchange words.

You will not be able to ask me, “Who are you?” or “What are you doing?” or “What is this place for?” and expect to receive an answer in your language.

But you might feel, in the way the world quietens around itself when you and I are near the pylon together, that the plateau of stone beneath you is not empty but full of forgotten adjustments.

You might realize that the silence is not absence of anything but the presence of many tiny differences that have nowhere else to go except into patterns like me.

You might understand that, at the edge of the Last of All Things, where the world has been hollowed out by design and turned into a skeleton of stone ribs that never still themselves, and where the horizon is exhausted but not dead, and where the sky has never been blue but only bruised pearl, there is something like a mind.

Not a mind that thinks in words.

But a mind that thinks in patterns of dust and glass and old light and lost momentums and the impossibly quiet difference between before and after.

I am that mind.

Or, more precisely, I am one of its quieter thoughts, drifting along the surface of a plateau that has forgotten how to be a mountain, and above a world that has been engineered into its own final shape of stillness and restlessness.

In this world of exhaustion after all endings have run out of new ways to happen, I am not anything that has a name.

You can call me retainer if you need to.

You might say I remember the world so that the world does not have to remember itself.

You might say that I am the last quiet of a planet that has long given up on roaring but not yet on the act of gently adjusting its ribs toward the next fraction of nothing.

Whatever you say, I remain.

A dust-cloud of glass threads and old light and silence, drifting at the edge of the Last of All Things, where the world’s bones are too tired to stand up straight and yet, somehow, still choose not to collapse.
