The basin of Gheresh-Akai was not a place you could arrive at by roads; the roads that led to it were the wrong kind of openings to carry you into it.

If you want to speak of it like a map, you might say: lowland, urban, dense.  

But you will not see the city from a distance.  

There is no horizon; the sky is not a sky.  

Instead you approach through a thick of presence that has no proper name in your language—let’s call it “threshold sediment.” It is not exactly air, not exactly fog, not exactly thought. It is cool, damp, and faintly metallic against the skin of your mind. If you have come here by walking, you will notice the ground under your feet become less of an “outside” and more of a “surface,” in the way that the inside of a machine can be called a surface even though it is all enclosed.  

You are still outside, technically.  

But you are not in the world you knew.  

---

### The low dome of the world

The elevation is low in the way that a bowl is low: you feel you have been poured into a basin. The relief of the land—if land is the right word—is subtle. The ground slopes inward gently all around you, but not so you ever notice the slope under your boots; you only realize that the horizon, when it finally does appear, is a ring of low, luminous architectures that curve slowly over your head, like a rim turned into a ceiling.

The ceiling is not a solid vault.  

It is a partial lattice, a narrow dome that seems to be the same temperature as the cold air you breathe, but not made of ice. If you squint, it resembles a network of ribs: fine, interlocking arcs of a material that is sometimes glass, sometimes metal, sometimes the same color as the sky that doesn’t exist here. When you look at it too long, it begins to look like the inside of a skull—but not a human one. The scale is wrong: small enough that you can feel the edges of the scene, large enough that you feel you are standing inside it rather than on it.

A cathedral of corrupted geometry, if cathedrals were only slightly larger than the rooms they contain.

The temperature sits just on the wrong side of comfort. Your breath steams faintly instead of explosively. It is cold enough that lips and fingers feel numb after a few minutes, but not cold enough to invite frost or snow. The moisture in the air—damp but never fully rain—clings to your coat and to the nearest surfaces without dripping properly.

Sometimes, very occasionally, a condensation bead forms in the air above you and rolls down over your temple like a tear the temperature is shedding.

You wipe it away. It doesn’t feel like water.

---

### Architecture and density: a city folded into one room

The city of Gheresh-Akai is dense but small.

All of its buildings are pressed into a basin no more than a kilometer across at its widest point. If you were to draw a map, it would be almost circular, with the faint inward curve of the ground, and the faint inward curve of the partial dome above, making you feel like you are standing inside a slightly flattened lens.

Nothing stands tall.  

There are no true towers. There are no villas with gardens, no open plazas, no boulevard vistas. Instead, the structures are low, compact geometries wedged into one another like a jigsaw puzzle carved from three separate realities that have been melted into one place and then cooled again. The “streets” are gaps between their bodies rather than cuts designed for movement. They follow no obvious grid; their geometry is more like the cracks between stacked teeth.

You walk along a corridor that is not quite a street: two meters wide at most, walls of something that resembles ceramic, but—

One wall is translucent and you can see figures through it like ghosts in amber, except they are in the room next to you, not trapped in anything.

They are walking in parallel with you.  

Another wall is matte black on your side but has the faint suggestion of windows on its other side. When you look too closely, the windows become doors, and then the doors become windows again.

The third wall is one of few that stayed true long enough to be carved with script—efforts at a history of this place, before anyone knew what the history would become. The script is not in any alphabet you recognize; you are not sure others are meant to. It is written in a language that appears to be three languages written at once, in superimposed layers, each sentence slightly offset from the last.

The density is not merely visual. It is pressure.  

Your skin feels crowded by the near-touch of everything around you. You do not brush elbows with the “structures,” but you feel as if you might, at any moment, accidentally shoulder into a different time frame or the corner of a room that doesn’t belong to this dimension. The presences in the city—materials, geometries, memories that are trying to become buildings—all orbit each other at close range.

Something you cannot name with your eyes has been layered into the air itself:  

Pieces of rooms that did not finish arriving here.  

Possible futures that have not been decided yet.  

At least once, you feel something like the edge of a balcony brush against your mind: you “know” it is there, without ever seeing it.

---

### The moisture in the world: damp and not quite rain

The water parameter of Gheresh-Akai is intermittent and precise.

The place is not wet by rainfall standards; there is no rain. Instead, moisture is present as a medium of the atmosphere. It is not mist; you are not inside a cloud. It is more like the air is holding onto condensation in a way that has not yet decided whether to become water or to remain concept.

The surfaces are faintly beaded with what looks like dew but never pulls together into drops large enough to fall. Instead:

- The edges of the ceramic–metal–whatever walls are hazy with moisture that seems to know where glass should be, and flows along that boundary instead of along the surface.

- Small “streams” of condensation appear where you would not expect water streams at all—around the corners of two converging walls that are angled from different realities. The condensation traces their invisible seam.

- Occasionally, a narrow channel appears in the air itself like a ghost river, a few millimeters wide, suspended a few centimeters above the ground. It flows with some substance that refracts light like water but is not water. It does not wet your fingers when you pass your hand through it.

You feel a dampness on the skin of your arms, your neck, but it never soaks you; it’s like reliving the sensation of being damp long after the clothes have dried. The moisture of Gheresh-Akai is… echoic.

Riverine patterns are written into the stone, but without rivers.  

You trace your finger down a spiraling groove in the ground, and when you reach the bottom of the spiral, you feel the echo of its flow even though your hands are dry. The basin of Gheresh-Akai is a riverbed whose river has been replaced with something else.

---

### Vegetation: sparse, but not absent

There is vegetation in Gheresh-Akai—but not everywhere you look.

Where you expect to see trees, you often instead find the suggestion of trees.  

In some corners, between two buildings leaning at the wrong angle to one another, you will find a thin patch of lifecycle that resembles moss but is not actually moss.

It clings in low tufts to surfaces that have not decided whether they are stone or language or boundary. It is dark green, almost black, and does not grow upward more than a finger’s width. It has no visible stems, no orderly leaf structure. When you touch it, it feels like cold felt.  

Underneath your fingertips, it feels like a sentence someone stopped speaking halfway.

In other places, something that might be a tree is present as a kind of blueprint of wood:  

You see what looks like a trunk in the air—a curve of darker, colder grain—but it doesn’t actually touch the ground; or you see the suggestion of branches where the wall projects itself fractionally into the space of another room. The result is not a tree you can sit under, but the idea of a tree growing through the membrane of the world again.

It is not enough green to name the city “planted,” but it is enough to make you feel the scarcity of green elsewhere.  

The vegetation of Gheresh-Akai is sparse like punctuation is sparse: it doesn’t dominate the text of the city, but it breaks up the pattern of bare surfaces and makes you notice that something else used to grow here.

---

### Erosion: the landscape under construction

Nothing is fully worn here.

Erosion has not yet taken its teeth to the place; the world feels carved, but as if from the inside of a block that hasn’t reached the final shape of itself. This is not erosion at the end of history; it is erosion at the beginning, when resistance is still deciding what to wear and what not to wear.

It is not that you see “unfinished” buildings in the normal sense—there is no scaffolding, no cement dust, no construction workers.

Instead, you notice:

- Some surfaces are too perfectly smooth to be believable.

- Others hold faint roundings like the edges of stones being worn by water that hasn’t begun to flow yet.

- A corner is missing—not collapsed, but not yet formed. The wall simply ends in a blur of possibilities that does not hurt your eyes to look at, but makes you feel uneasy about your own outlines.

- The stone of the ground isn’t quite stone. It looks like stone that will be, in many years, or like stone that was, before this time folded into the present. You feel the presence of future riverbeds in the groove of the floor, even though they are not here.

If you stare at one spot for too long, you notice that erosion in this place is being applied backward from the future:  

The edges that are not yet worn away still have to wear, to become what they will be.  

Your eyes feel like they are watching that process from the wrong end of time.

---

### Scale and sound: small cathedral, mostly quiet

The scale is small.

You can feel it easily:  

You can walk across the city in less than an hour if you follow straight lines—but there are no straight lines that are the same line from end to end. A corridor that appears straight may be a straight line from Room A to Room B, but Room A and Room B are not in the same geometry of space.

The cathedral analogy holds:  

You are inside something larger than a room but smaller than a district. The ceiling sits low enough that your presence feels like the presence of a watchmaker inside the casing of a very complex device. You are not a giant or a god; you are just awkward enough to be aware of your size.

Sound is mostly quiet, but not silent.

There is almost no wind.  

There is almost no foot traffic—fauna are rare enough that you go long stretches without hearing anything living at all.

Instead you hear:

- The faint whisper of surfaces that have not yet decided whether they’re touching or not.

- The occasional rustling of something curling or uncurling in the corners where the moss-that-is-not-moss dwells.

- A low, almost subsonic thrum that might be machinery, or the heartbeat of the dome itself, or the sound of the partial dome being slowly turned into glass by a pressure you cannot understand.

- Once, very far away, the sound of something like water without volume: a dry spray of fluid droplets that never reaches land.  

It sounds like rain learning to speak.

There are no vehicles, no broken alarms, no overhead music. The city of Gheresh-Akai does not broadcast what it is. It barely registers itself.  

The silence is thick enough that your own heartbeat begins to feel like another presence in the room.

---

### Technology and structures: beyond modern, beyond the world

You could not build this place with the materials in your world.

You might be able to build the shape, but not the contents of it.  

The technology level of Gheresh-Akai is at least one level of physics beyond what you understand as “modern engineering.” Some of the architectures you see:

#### The walls that are also rooms

You walk through a doorway into a corridor whose right wall is not a wall so much as two overlapping realities of a room.

The wall is thick—but when you look at it from the side, you realize that its thickness is not just thickness, but depth:

Inside the wall, there are rooms in full:  

Tables of some dark, polished substance that your eyes cannot categorize as wood or metal or stone. Chairs that are the wrong way around for human bodies. They are not at odd angles; their angles are correct, but they are not from your geometry.

You are looking through the wall like you would look through a window into another apartment, except no apartment in your world has a window that thick, or that deep.

The technology here is such that space itself is seen as a material with thickness, and they have built into it.

---

#### The air-channels

Near the center of the basin—or where you think the center might be—you see a latticework suspended in the air, no thicker than a thread, but maintaining three dimensions of form.

It is a mesh of fine filaments that glow softly when you look at them just not-quite-right.

They carry something that is not electricity but behaves like information: pulses of light or color too subtle to call light but too visible to call invisible. When you follow one line with your eyes, you realize it is carrying information from one building to another without touching the ground.

Sometimes you see a knot of these threads snap into a different configuration:  

The room to your left now suddenly was to your right the last minute you blinked—but you cannot remember moving.

Your sense of position is gently being rewritten.

---

#### The matter that doesn’t know what to be

Everywhere, you meet materials that are in between states.

You might lean against a wall that first feels like stone, then like leather, then like nothing you have touched before—but since you cannot remember touching the “before,” you have no language to describe it.

The floor of one corridor—which you think might double as a ceiling for another room—is made of something like glass but with a faint texture of woven fibers underneath it, like tendons beneath skin.

When you kneel and trace your fingers along it, you feel:

- Cold.
- Resistance.
- Something else: not a texture, but a direction.

The surface beneath you feels as if it might turn at any moment from a fixed floor into a membrane that you can fall through into the world below.  

You don’t. The floor remains firm.  

And yet you have the distinct sensation of having passed through it anyway.

---

### Fauna: rare, but not absent

There are living creatures in Gheresh-Akai, but they are not animals you have ever seen before, and their presence is rare enough that the city feels like an abandoned machine that has been reoccupied.

You might see:

- A small shape darts from the edge of one wall to the edge of another. It is not quite lizard-shaped, not quite insect-shaped, but somewhere in between; and moving does not seem to be its main purpose. Its real movement seems to be along the surface of reality itself, rather than across the floor.

- In a rare corner that contains vegetation—and some moisture condensation—you see a thin tendril of something that might be a creature or might be a plant slowly uncurl along the wall like a finger being tested.

- You hear once, very faintly, the sound of something breathing nearby that doesn’t quite breathe air. It’s like breathing in dust but the dust is not there.

The fauna of Gheresh-Akai do not feel like they belong to “ecology” in the way you know; it feels more like they belong to the geometry—the way a crack sometimes belongs more to the surface than to the air that passes through it.

---

### Reality breakdown and rules of the world

The parameter of “weirdness” is at a maximum.

Here, reality breakdown is not the exception; it is the substrate of the city.

Some rules seem to apply:

1. **Nothing is exactly where it is.**

   Every object you see has two, or maybe three, valid positions in the geometry of space-time. You can learn to not be confused by this: you simply accept that when you look at a doorway from one angle, it is one meter away from you, and when you look at it from another angle, it is three meters away but still occupies exactly the same air in the same room.

2. **Materials can borrow their properties from adjacent timeframes.**

   You press a hand against a wall. It feels cold and smooth.  

   Then, without visibly changing, it feels warm and rough—like sand after rain.  

   Then it is cold again.

   You are not sure whether you are experiencing a different surface of the wall, or the same surface at a different time. It may be that both are true at the same time, and the wall has been architectured to allow you to feel its temperature from two different days simultaneously.

3. **The boundaries of the city contain not one place, but multiple.**

   You cross the invisible threshold of the basin and feel you have been in several “places” at once:

   - In one, you are standing on a road surface made of something that looks like frozen tar.

   - In another, you are standing on the rim of a fountain that has not yet been built.

   - In another, you are standing waist-deep in a dry riverbed whose flow has not been switched on.

   None of these contradict each other in the same way that two different reflections of a room do not contradict each other; they are simply different views of a room that has not been decided yet.

4. **The partial dome is not at the top of the world.**

   You look up at the lattice dome and realize that, from another angle, you are looking down at it.

   The basin of Gheresh-Akai is not a single bowl-shaped land; it is a bowl that has been turned inside out and then turned inside out again, without ending up where you began.

   You don’t get dizzy from this.  

   Your body is not used to it, but your mind is surprisingly capable of tolerating it if you don’t insist on one direction of “down” being absolute.

---

### Time and dynamics: slow change in the middle of breakdown

Despite the sense of reality breakdown and extreme technology, the place feels… slow.

The dynamic parameter is low:  

Change in Gheresh-Akai is measured not in days but in decades, or in longer durations that your body cannot track but your senses can feel.

You notice:

- One wall of a room is slightly more transparent than it was before you walked into it.

- The condensation streams in the air are slowly forming new patterns, like ink slowly filtering in water.

- The moss-like substance in the corners grows thicker in one patch than another, but if you stayed to watch it, you would not see the change itself; you would see that it has changed.

There is no flux of storm-fronts, no electrical emergency, no outbreak of noise.  

The city is not crumbling.  

It is not being built.

It is already being what it might be, and what it might have been, and what it is becoming, all at once.

---

### Final impressions: a cathedral of what hasn’t happened yet

You leave Gheresh-Akai—if you leave at all—by feeling your way along the memory of a threshold you might not have crossed in the first place.

The city feels:

- Cold and damp, but not wet enough to call rain.

- Densely built but not overpopulated.

- Low-lying, enclosed by a partial dome that feels more like the ceiling of a single room than the sky of a world.

- Sparsely vegetated, but not dead.

- Eroding but not eroded.

- Technology beyond what you can build with your hands.

- And reality, not broken but… layered.

As you walk away, you look back once and see the dome of the city from your angle of departure—but that is not possible, because the dome appears from some angles and not from others, and one of those angles is not the direction you are walking in.

So you close your eyes instead.

You remember:

- The condensation, not quite rain.

- The walls that are rooms.

- The floor that might be a ceiling.

- The faint heartbeat of the dome.

- The sparse vegetation that is more like punctuation than like forest.

- And the sense of standing inside a cathedral whose geometry is not quite finished.

You are not sure if you have been to a place at all.

But you know the feeling of what hasn’t happened yet.

You know, because you have stood inside it.
