In the high place of perpetual weeping, everything is made of water that is trying to become stone and stone that is trying to become sound.

You are standing on the rim of a small bowl of mountainside, perhaps the size of a basilica or a small hamlet, no bigger than the reach of a shout. The sky is a low ceiling of congealed mist that never lifts or moves, but only breathes faintly in the same rhythm over and over again, like the lungs of a sleeping machine. The air is cool enough that you can feel it in your teeth, but damp enough that your clothes are already growing heavy at the cuffs.

The place is not raining; the place is being thought.

Water weeps from every surface. It is not like rain and not like streams, but something in between. The entire bowl is a single weeping face: rock walls weep down into themselves, narrow crystal shelves weep along their edges in thin silver curtains, and the ground weeps in small circular tears that never run into each other, but rise and then fall, like glass being blown into tiny bubbles and then dissolving into air.

The ground is not mud and not stone, but a kind of hardened dry water, a bluish material that rings like porcelain under your heel but still deforms slightly where you step, as if you are leaving footprints in the memory of ground. The elevation of the basin is high, but the world around you is not jagged mountains, only this single cathedral bowl cut into the side of something larger and invisible, like a small chapel carved from the flank of a sleeping god.

⇨ terrain & materials  

The basin walls curve gently upward to meet a rim of dark glassy stone that looks like basalt forced to remember its liquid form. It is not fractured by age or erosion; there are no cracks, only ambiguities. The rock is too young, still forming its habits from cold water and pressure and time. The cliff faces do not look like they have been broken; they look like they are still deciding what shape to be.

Rivulets course down the walls in thin, perfectly straight lines, as if they were drawn by rule instead of gravity. They glint in the faint light that never comes from any source you can see, but is always the same: a low, pewter-colored illumination that seems to soak from the air itself rather than fall onto it. There is no sun or sky or movement of clouds above, only that same soft dimness held like breath held in a lung.

The ground under the weeping water is not flat but subtly arranged in low, perfectly circular ridges and concentric rings like ripples that have frozen halfway into permanence. Each ring is the color of wet steel under a thin film of clear water, and at the center of each ring sits a small gear of stone.

They are not exactly gears, but they are not exactly not gears either. They are teeth where the stone has been convinced into rationality. Each of these small stone rings has a coin of darker metal at its center, from which water oozes upward in a thin, perfectly vertical column, then falls over the teeth in a continuous curtain. The columns are the height of your wrist, maybe less, so they don’t break when you step near them, only ripple in your passing shadow as if responding to something else.

When you are not moving, they are entirely still.

Along the curves of the basin floor are what you might call trees, if trees were things made of water and memory. They are not wood. They are not leaves. They are upright structures of clear, glassy water that has thickened itself into standing forms, several meters tall, each one slender and composed primarily of negative space. The trunk of each tree is a hollow column of water with no inside and no outside, just a surface that has convinced itself to be solid. The branches are forests of concentric rings that rise from the trunk like levels of a cathedral, each ring periodically collapsing into droplets that then reassemble their orbits into higher rings, as if the water is climbing itself.

The leaves are not leaves but partial pages of translucent text that partway exists, written in a shimmering angular script that you cannot read but that feels like instructions. When you try to look directly at it, the words migrate into glassy, geometric squints and then return to illegibility. They fall like garlands from the water-rings, but they never hit the ground; halfway down they become merely rain again and rejoin the weeping.

Throughout the basin are great flat sheets of water suspended like windows in the air, not fully attached to anything, but not exactly hanging from anything either. They have been left here to be looked at. They stand like glass panels, each one slightly angled at odds with the others. When you pass them, their surfaces are not perfectly reflective; you do not see your face clearly. Instead, you see the same place, but from other times of the same eternity that never happens. They show you the basin roofed with a different sky of glowing metal, or the basin covered with low, factory-colored mist that roils but never moves, or the basin reflected from below itself, as if you are standing on the inverted ceiling of a machine world and looking up at this bowl of stone.

Everything is wet to the touch, but nothing is truly cold. The temperature is that of a deep cellar or a cave where you have been waiting long enough to be part of its temperature. Your breath does not steam; the air is not cold enough to freeze your airway, only cool enough to make you very aware of breathing. The water feels like silk that has learned to be temperature instead of fabric.

⇨ erosion & youth  

Because the place is young, there is no wear to be found. “Young” here does not mean new in the sense of not having existed yet; it means new in the sense of a shape that is still forming its tendencies from the options of physics and reflection and machine. The edges are not sharp yet. They are not round yet. They are still in between.

There are no valleys or channels carved into the ground. There are no patterns of ages where water has repeatedly chosen the same path over millennia. The water seems almost surprised to find itself in the same place twice. It falls from above, rises from below, runs from inside to outside and outside to inside, but it does not remember its previous journeys. There is no erosion of grain by grain, only erosion of idea by idea.

The ground has not yet been persuaded to be anything else than what it is, and because of that, it is obedient to only a few rules at once. When you watch it long enough, you realize that the surface of the ground is not still water, but a very slow motion of water moving toward the nearest stone ring, where it is lifted into the column, then returned to the basin floor again from the weeping walls above. It is not erosion but adolescent water-cycle: a basin recycling its own wetness into itself before it has learned to leave any tracks behind.

⇨ scale & sparsity  

The space is small enough that you could see someone else standing here with you if you were not alone. The crown of the weeping walls forms a circle not much larger than a choir hall or a small village square, just enough space to hold a single story of stone and water and not enough to be lost in. There is nowhere else inside this bowl to go; all other directions are the rim, and beyond the rim you do not see anything recognizable as world, only a suggestion of more basin like this one, or arms like this one, or mouths like this one, depending on how you look at it.

The scale is human but not comfortable. You could almost imagine a small community of craftspeople or carvers of crystal water-trees here, except that there is no evidence of anyone else having ever stood here. There are no paths worn into the bending floor. No footmarks persist except your own, and as soon as you look away and then back again, your footprints are no longer shapes but only suggestions.

The density is low. There is not much of anything here, except that there is too much of what it is. The water-trees stand at irregular intervals, each one separated from the next by several strides of empty ground. The flat suspended windows of water are placed like punctuation in a long sentence that never ends, but the sentence itself is made of pauses between words. Each feature is alone: the tree, the ring-gear of stone, the window-screen, the weeping wall. Each is surrounded by enough emptiness to be considered, and that is the point.

The presence of this place is in the absence of any other place. The world beyond the rim is not present, except as a uniform color of not-black and not-white, like a painted wall behind which something else is being constructed.

⇨ light & dim  

The light is not dark enough to be night, but not bright enough to be day. It is always like late afternoon in a room where the curtains have been drawn over the only windows. There is no source of illumination to speak of, no filament, no burning sun, no glowing machine. You can see clearly enough to read if there were anything legible to read, but there is not.

The color of the air is that of bone under dust, or of old paper under water. The light does not change direction as the nonexistent day passes because there is no day to pass.
