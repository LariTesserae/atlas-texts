High on the thermal plateau, the air itself has been turned into a tool the city never asked to use.

You know you’re approaching Thaumos before you see its walls. The skyline first shows itself as a trembling of heat above the horizon—then, as you climb, the terraces of condensate-collectors and the faint, shimmering outlines of the towers behind them. The old plateau road is a worn spine of black stone, its edges smoothed into curves by centuries of steam and wind and the weight of countless crossings.

The elevation is deceptive. You feel breathless at first glance—not from the altitude, though the air is thin and sharp, but from the way heat and pressure conspire against you. The plateau is a highland of trapped heat, where the air that comes up from the lowlands can’t escape easily and lingers, boiling over itself like water in a pot that will never cool. The sun is close to overhead and white-bright, but you notice the light more than you feel its warmth; the warmth is already everywhere else.

Your feet are walking on a world made of water.

Everywhere there is stone, there is also water. The ground under your boots is not dust but a faintly damp, dark-gray pavement that has been manufactured by steam and time and the pressure of the city above it. The plateau itself is a vast slab of basalt and older crystals, but its surface is not raw: it is polished by millennia of condensation and evaporation cycles, until the rock glows faintly when you drag your heel across it and the trace of your step is momentarily wet, then dry again.

The road itself weeps.

You notice the sound of Thaumos before you see it clearly: almost silence. The manual says the sound is 0.1, but that doesn’t quite mean what you expect. There *is* sound—you just don’t hear it until the city forces you to listen.

Along the road, there are no plants. No green leaves, no brittle desert scrub, no stubborn moss. You will not find a single blade of anything you would recognize as vegetation. This is not a world of plants. It is a world of tears and tools and living things that are not plants.

Water seeps out of the rock faces along the road in thin sheets, like sweat. The engineers of Thaumos have long ago carved this weeping into a pattern. You see the evidence of their work in the shallow channels and bridges that run beside you on the road, each carrying its own slow ribbon of clear water down the hill. The water shines faintly with the light of the high sun, but there is no chatter of brooks, no splash of rapids. The water is thick and silent, moving in a low, slow crawl that seems to resist the pull of gravity.

The temperature is high enough to be wrong: you can feel it in the way your clothes cling to your skin even though there’s no visible sweat, and in the faint sheen of condensing vapor that appears on the cooler side of boulders and then vanishes in the same breath. The air itself changes temperature as you walk through it—cooler where you cross a shadow cast by a condensate-collector tower, then hotter again as you move into the open.

The elevation shows itself in a pressure that you feel in your teeth more than in your lungs. Your ears don’t pop; you only notice a strange tugging, as if the weight of the city above you is trying to draw something out of your head. The plateau itself feels like a table-top set up above the world, and Thaumos is built on that tabletop with the confident permanence of something that has had time to learn its own shape.

The city’s buildings are not high towers, but wide, low structures of dark glass and stone and metal, arranged in layers that step down the slope. The outer ring of Thaumos is made of condensate-collectors—great flat platforms of metallic glass held up by slender columns, each tilted at precise angles to catch the rising air and its burden of water vapor. The edges of these platforms are lined with faintly glowing lines that you realize are not paint but a kind of frozen water that has been arrested into solid patterns, like frost entombed in glass.

The interior of the city is visible through gaps between the buildings: narrow streets laid out in geometric patterns that follow the old thermal pathways of the plateau rather than any human idea of a grid. The streets are almost empty of people, but they are full of other things.

You come closer, and the world clarifies itself in steps.

First you smell Thaumos: not rot, not smoke, but something like hot metal cooling in water, and behind that, a faint ozone slickness that tells you there is electricity here, but quietly hidden.

Next you notice the fauna.

The creatures of this plateau have no interest in resemblance to anything you know. They move like liquid but they are not made of water. They are not made of anything that looks like animal, either.

There are no plants here, but the world is not barren at all.

The road surface itself begins to show its life. You look down and see a pattern of faint, glowing veins that spread beneath the transparent layer of manufactured stone. You realize you are walking on a kind of skin, and beneath that skin are creatures that follow the water and heat channels of the plateau like they are veins of blood.

They are not large, most of them. You see the first ones as you approach the outer edge of the city: thin translucent forms like glass threads, flowing just beneath the surface of the road, each only a few centimeters long but gathered together in bands that might be kilometers in length. They move with a slow, pulse-like rhythm. When you stop to watch, they stop when you stop; when you walk again they resume their flow, as if they are waiting for you to decide which direction to be alive in.

You test the temperature of the road with your hand: it is warmer than your body, but not painful. The material of the road is not one stone but many, pressed together into a smooth finish that feels like the inside of a bath warmed and then allowed to cool almost all the way to comfortable.

Above you, the condensate platforms collect water from the air.

This is a world with little audible rain, but constant rain-in-reverse.

The hot air of the plateau rises up the slopes, drawn by the cooler pressure of the heights above it. As it rises, it passes over the metallic platforms and their blades of stenciled frost-patterns. Vapor condenses into the channels of these frozen shapes, and then the water clings to the surface, not dripping down but rather creeping along the lines that have been laid out for it like the tracks of a factory.

The water moves along those edges and then runs down thin columns toward the city below, entering into a system of pipes and channels that are themselves visible only as dark lines inlaid into the face of each building. Each path of water is directed by contours of art and engineering, into tanks that are not visibly hidden but carefully exposed, like jewels on display.

You see how the water nourishes the city. You also see how it nourishes the world.

The fauna here are abundant, but they are sparse of presence.

This is one of the rules of Thaumos: the world is full of creatures, but you cannot see them all at once.

There are the glass-thread roads beneath your feet, which are not roads but highways of communal organisms living in the mineral substrate.

There are the air swimmers that you begin to notice only after you stand still for longer than you expect to be comfortable. You look at the sky and then at the towers of the collectors and you think there is nothing there but the heat-haze at the edges of your vision. Then you realize some of that haze is not air at all.

Above you, near the platforms of the collectors, there are creatures that are only visible when they pass through a beam of light or a condensation shadow. They are long thin things like gas threads, or like smoke that has learned to swim in circles. They move with a slow grace that matches the temperature of the air and the pace of the water’s crawl along the collector edges.

When you watch long enough you see that they are not alone. There are others that are not even gas-shaped but more like pressure waves: disturbances you only notice when you notice that the air over there is thicker than you thought it should be.

They are predators of a kind you cannot understand without watching them for longer than you’ve been allowed to stay.

You see a moment of violence: one of the gas-thread swimmers pauses in its track around the collector, and the air above it shimmers, then hardens in a curved way that is not physically possible in your memory of air. Invisible jaws, or perhaps just pressure differentials made into mouths, close around it and the disturbance in the air becomes briefly visible as a pattern of colors—like the oil film on cooling water, but without the water.

Then the air is smooth again.

You are not sure if you saw death or a change of state.

Thaumos itself is both city and machine.

The buildings you walk into, at the edge, are not arranged like houses you would recognize. They have no front doors that you can see, but seams of the city’s skin where a pressure plate can be opened by the correct gait of boots or wave of hand or perhaps simply by the way you allow your lungs to breathe in and out.

You gain your first entry by accident. You lean against what you thought was a wall, and you realize it is not exactly a wall at all; it is a layer of compressed air that has been scripted into shape. You feel your weight against it and it yields, then pushes you back with a force that is both exactly equal and not exactly from you. You understand that this is a threshold: you have been given permission to notice that you are not the same pressure you were before.

You walk through.

The world inside the city is quieter even than the road.

You step into a long chamber that has a floor made of the same road-threads as outside, but they are thicker here, more visible. You see the threads of creatures beneath the floor but they are not the same as the ones you were walking over before; these are smaller and paler, and they pulse in time with a beat you can feel more than hear.

It is not sound, but vibration.

The temperature in the chamber is higher than outside, and you feel the moisture of the air fall onto you as you walk into the center. You did not expect it, but you realize the condensation in your lungs is less than you expected for the heat of this place. The air has been engineered, too: not to breathe for you, but to breathe *you* into something else.

You look at the walls and you see that they are not entirely stone.

They are made of layers of compressed time and water and glass and metal and something else you cannot name but which you know from the way it looks at you.

You don’t mean that metaphorically.

The walls look at you.

It is subtle enough that you’re not sure you are seeing it at first. You turn your head to examine the room and you notice that the pattern in the wall nearest you has shifted slightly. You look back at the door you came through and then you glance at the same wall again and you realize the pattern has not gone back to where it was before.

You are being observed by the building itself, or by the world that is wearing the building as a mask.

This is not shocking. You knew from the beginning that this world would be strange (1.5/3), but you did not expect how calmly that strangeness would be integrated into the everyday.

The industrial dimension of Thaumos is not obvious in the way you think of industry—no smoke, no roar—but it is present everywhere like the heat of the plateau itself. You see the marks of it in the way that the water from the condensate collectors is channeled not into the ground but into manifolds of shining metal and glass that you gradually realize are not just storage but engines of another kind.

They are engines that don’t move, but that *shape*.

You see a worker, or what you take to be a worker. You’re not sure what they are, because they are not exactly human.

You stand in the corridor of the chamber and you see a figure step out of a wall that you would not have been able to name as a door until it opened. It does not walk like a person but more like a pressure change that has been given limbs and decided to use them.

The body is covered in a suit of something that looks like glass but moves like cloth. The surface of the suit is not perfectly smooth but textured like the surface of the condensate-collector platforms, except that each raised pattern is faintly glowing with the same color as oil-on-water that you saw before, when the predator took the gas-thread swimmer outside.

You realize that the suit and the outside of the collector platforms are made of the same class of thing.

You are not looking at a person wearing gear. You are looking at a living system that has been invited to wear a person.

The worker moves past you, not with the sound of footsteps but with a series of soft sounds that are not quite sound: more like the uncovering of one pressure from another. You feel the space around you change as they pass, and you realize that the fauna of Thaumos are not limited to the outside world.

The creature that you thought was human is not only human.

The suit itself contains colonies of the same thread-things that run beneath the floor, except these are arranged differently, more densely packed, and threading through the other person’s body in ways you can only imagine by the way you do not want to imagine.

Their eyes meet yours and you see something else you are not prepared for.

The eyes are not only eyes.

You are looking at condensate lenses.
