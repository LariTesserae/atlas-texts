At first glance the plateau is a city of stone.

Not the distant city of a human scale, but something closer and immanent. The towers are not of brick or mortar but of rock itself. The ground is dense towers of basalt and greywacke, the columns of ancient lava flows and seabed stacked up and then tilted by an age I’ve never been to, but can feel pushing up from below the soles of my feet. The air tastes cold and thin, sharp at the back of the throat. Each breath is work.

Wherever the bedrock surfaces, it wears a faint glisten. The plateau is a high, cold table of stone, cut from its neighbors by high fault lines and fault scarps, and in the night air moisture from the cloud layer below settles on the rock as a sheet of invisible frost. When the sun comes up, the rock sweats. It doesn’t run in rivers. It appears in a thin sheen, like breath on glass, in beaded veins along the column edges, and in the carved gutters of the city itself. The plateau is vast enough to have its own small weather: the lower cloud deck wraps the plateau’s rim like a skirt, and the upper sky comes down clear and cold at the top.

From the east edge, where the trail ends, you look down and see white cloud boiling very slowly against the cliffs. No surf-noise. No river roar. Just the faint, whispery rustle of condensation sliding down stone channels and the occasional call of a distant bird. The sound is thin enough that you can hear your own heartbeat along the inside of your ribs.

---

The city is built into the rock itself. Not a human city, but a city nevertheless.

The basalt columns are irregular, like fractured pillars pushed up at the dawn of time and then left to be scoured. The people who live here—the people of the Plateau of the Still-Walls—have not built so much as they have uncovered. The stone is young enough, still, that the walls of these columns are often hollow in the center: bubbles of gas trapped as the lava cooled, now revealed as erosion opens the outer faces. The city’s makers followed the cracks where the rock was already weak, and, with stone tools and steady hands, pried the slabs free.

What you see now is not a single composition of architecture, not a palace of polished geometry, but a dense, layered settlement: the rock column into the skyline, hollows within the stone hollows, and all the spaces between filled with time and patching and reuse.

At the bases of the towers, where the frost seeps through the cracks and the night gathers cold, the rock once showed soft green and grey and brown stains of lichen and moss. The city-makers have chipped those away. The channels of the city are now lined with the hard, dark stone of the column interiors, the surface polished smooth by centuries of slow flow and hands and knees.

The city’s “streets” are mostly watercourses: low galleries carved into the rock where condensation and the rare melt of distant snows run downslope toward the edge of the plateau. Some of these channels are deep, notched by step-forms and small, locally-made water wheels of dark wood and bone. The rims of each channel show the faint sheen of water that’s not quite enough to run free but stays as thin film and beadlets, clinging to the rock.

The air is saturated with the presence of stone. You walk between verticals that are taller than any tree, and the habitation is dense enough that you pass through perpetual shade. The daylight doesn’t fall horizontally; it comes down from a slit of sky far above. The city’s “road” system is a narrow, branching network of cutaways into the column faces: low stone tunnels with no roof but the sky, where the sky is a ribbon of pale light between the tops of the pillars.

---

If you stand at a central point, where the main channels converge and a broader platform has been carved out of the rock, you can see the scale of the place.

From the southern edge of the plateau to the northern scarp, the city of Still-Walls stretches across a distance that feels continental in its gentle curvature. The horizon seems to rise away around you, not as a paraboloid of sky, but as a wide ring of stone columns. The base of the plateau is buried below the cloud deck, swallowed by mist and the slow-moving cold, so the city doesn’t appear to be set on the ground at all; it hangs like a broken continent of stone above the sky.

The weather of the plateau itself is small but precise. The cloud layer below the scarp moves up or down depending on the hour of the day: the fog fingers come into the lower streets of the city in the mornings and then retreat at mid-day, leaving the stone channels slick with moisture. Almost no rain falls from the sky above; almost all the water is the sweat of the rock itself and the slow capture of that cloud deck.

If you stand at the edge when the cloud deck is rising, you can see how the city’s layout answers the fog. The lower streets—the ones that vanish into the cloudbank—are built as narrow, closed formwork, with overhangs and shallow basins carved into the tunnel floor. The inhabitants of Still-Walls have tens of thousands of small ceramic bowls and hollows set into the rock, where the fog condenses into drip after drip after drip. That’s their primary water source, not rivers or rain.

Vegetation is sparse and stunted. The morning fog brings spores of lichen and moss and the occasional seed of a high-altitude, low-growing plant, but none of it grows where the city-makers want clear stone. The vegetation appears in the cracks that the makers haven’t yet cleaned, or at the tops of the pillars where the stone tools cannot reach. The roofs of the world, here, are green with minute gardens of cold-resistant mosses and thin, wiry grasses that live in the thin dust of broken rock.

---

This is not a city of humans, but of someone else.

The people who call Still-Walls home are bipedal but smaller than a human, and their bodies show the stresses of a cold and high, thin environment. Their skin tones are pale and grey-blue, like the sky at the edge of the cloud-deck. The bone of their faces is thick, especially around the nose and mouth, where frosts would have bitten most easily. You can see the evidence of those colds in the way they move: the economy of action, the absence of sudden gestures.

Economically, Still-Walls is a condensation city. The tools are primitive but highly specialized. The workshops are timbered lofts within the hollow columns, where they have set in stone anvils and carved benches. The forges are nearly cold by the standards of any other people’s smithies: the heat is not for melting metal but for softening the bone and hard-shale of the plateau’s surface materials. They produce small tools—fine needles of bone, scrapers of edge-flaked stone, wooden handles carved with long-term patience.

The architecture of the city is dense but not the density of a human slum. It’s the density of a colony of barnacles or a coral reef: the repetition of a small set of shapes over a huge substrate. The stone column is the gift of the world; the hollows within the stone are the gift of the geology’s youth; the people have made only the final cut of the stone and the arrangement of the tools.

The dwellings are not rooms in the human sense, but cavelets and hollows within the column walls, with shallow thresholds carved so that seal-forms can be engaged to keep fog out. The passages are narrow enough for two of the inhabitants to squeeze past one another with murmured breath and no more than that. The ceilings are low, to preserve heat.

They are not cold in the way of people who freeze to death in minutes. They are cold in the way of people who cannot feel a warm day in a year and whose body has never been made to run warm at the level of organs or blood. The inhabitants of Still-Walls have a slow metabolism: they are not cold-blooded, but they are cold-hearted.

They are not cold of heart.

---

The fauna of Still-Walls is abundant and subtle.

You can hear them when the fog is rising. The calls of the high-altitude birds that nest above the cloud line and hunt below it in the early hours of the day. The birds are not large, but their voices carry thin and clear in the thin air, and they sound fewer than they are, because the sound stretches.

You see the smaller things: the cold-furred rodents that are many of them but each of them small, and in the color of rock and lichen. You learn the quality of their presence not by direct sight but by the way they disappear into the stone—the way they move between the cracks as if they were the rock’s own nerves.

The water of the plateau supports not fish but something else: thin, translucent arthropods and small, soft creatures that live in the shallow basins of the condensation collectors. They are rare to see, but you will notice faint, slow-moving shapes in the ceramic dew-bowls if you crouch down and look at the surface with the sky of the pillar tops above. The dew of Still-Walls is alive with its own quiet ecosystem.

The larger fauna are not dangerous beasts. The environment of Still-Walls is too cold, the food supply too sparse to support predators of any size. The most dangerous animals are the ones that tunnel the rock: the cold-adapted creatures whose jaws work like stone tools and whose teeth leave the same gouge-marks in the basalt as the city’s artisans.

Some of the city’s people are hunters of those tunnels. They listen to the rock with the bone of their jaw against the stone column and hear the faint shavings of stone teeth from below.

---

At the level of fabric, the city of Still-Walls is almost without cloth.

The fibers here are thin and rare: tall, cold grasses that grow near the upper rims of the pillars only where the snow never stays long enough to crush the shoots and where the frosts are not so deep that they blight the seed. The city’s few textiles are made from those grasses and from the hair of the cold-furred rodents and the down of the birds of the rim. The clothing of the people of the city is not a garment in the sense of human clothing; it is more of a compact layer of insulation carefully wrapped and sewn in tight patterns around the body.

The rest of their body armor is time itself: the slow adaptation of generations to the cold, the high, thin air, the sparse calories of a condensation city.

The tools of the city are stone tools, but they are not simple. Their edgework is sophisticated and meticulous. The artisans of Still-Walls have learned to read the internal structure of the rock as well as any geologist, and they know which column of basalt will yield to a tool with a fracture that runs like a silk thread and which will resist to the end. The sounds of the city’s craftwork are not the loud banging of metal hammers but the faint clicking of stone against stone and the occasional pop of a column face releasing a slab under the focused pressure of a wooden wedge.

Above the city, the air of the plateau is thin but clear. The sky is so deep and blue that it almost seems to be its own kind of stone, and the stars are visible from the moment of sunset until the sun rises again later the same day. The cold of the night is not the cold of frost alone but also the cold of absence: the absence of water, the absence of green, the absence of warmth. The absence of noise.

The sounds of the city at night are the faint drips of condensation collecting from the ceramic bowls and channels of the upper galleries, the occasional cough of a small animal in the rock, and the soft brushing of feet against stone as someone pads the long tunnel routes from one side of the pillar-city to another.

There is no rush. There is no bustle. There is only the slow patience of a cold people who have learned to live in the stone of a young, cold, high, and dense continent of rock above the clouds.

---

If you were to come to the plateau at the end of one year and stay until the beginning of the next, you would not see much change.

The change is not in the movement of the people or the shifting of the walls. The change of Still-Walls is in the water of the rock itself and in the movement of the cloud deck below the scarp edge. The change of Still-Walls is in the slow erosion of the column surfaces, where a frost-wedge from one year carves a new line and then doesn’t change for a decade. The change of Still-Walls is in the way that the tools of the inhabitants slowly wear a new set of edges into the ceramic gutters, where a new threshold of stone is cracked to let more fog into the condensers next year.

The city of Still-Walls is not a city of rapid change. It is a city of slow cycles and patient processes. It is a city where the life of the inhabitants is measured against the life of the rock in the same way that the life of lichen is measured against the life of mountains.

At the end of the day, when the sun goes down in the thin air of the plateau, the stone of the city assumes the color of the cloud deck below the horizon edge: the white of the cloud becomes the blue of the cold stone and the sky above the pillars becomes a deep void that you could almost fall into.

Somewhere far below the cloud base, there is another world, where the rivers flow and the forests thick and warm and humid spread out across the lowlands, and where the evenings are loud with night insects.

The inhabitants of Still-Walls have never seen that world. They only know the rock of their plateau city and the cold thin air and the quiet condensation water that falls from the ceramic gutters of the towers of stone, and they have no need to see anything else to be content.
