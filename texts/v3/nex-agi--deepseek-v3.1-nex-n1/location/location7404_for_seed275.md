Down here is not sunlight.

That’s the first thing you notice. Not “absence of” but “not.” The ceiling over the world is a dense mat of pale, root-like filaments that glows softly in the same spectrum as noon, but without heat. It hangs sixty meters above the irregular floor of the cavern—flat, even, unbroken across the entire vault of the city—and its illumination is constant, unchanging, planetary.

You stand on a rib of stone that still shows raw tool marks. The floor is carved; this is not a cave that grew by water. The space is a single enormous chamber, maybe two kilometers long and half as wide, a hollow in the planet’s crust. The ribs rise in arches and terraces, forming stepped levels. Roads and rails and beltways curl around these ribs. The entire city is carved into the rock of one chamber, but the rock is only the skeleton. The flesh is wood.

Vegetation has won.

---

### Form and Architecture

You are looking at a forest that has swallowed a manufactory.

The lower ribs are still bare stone, rough and freshly cut. In places, drill cores lie stacked like forgotten logs, waiting for removal that never came. Faint dust lines show the original layout of foundations—here a warehouse block, there a power station. But now great trunks have grown up through those floors.

They are white.

Not white like birch or paper, but white like bone, or like porcelain under pressure—smooth and dense and cool to the touch. They grow from deep shafts where factory machinery used to sit. They’ve split the concrete retaining rings, widened the shafts into their own trunks, and now climb, straight and precise, toward the luminous ceiling.

The branches are not random. They fork in a way that follows elaborate engineering diagrams—angles that are too regular to be natural, but too graceful to be mere steel girders. Some trunks are fused with the remnants of old industrial structures: pipes, conduits, catwalks. They have not merely crushed them; they’ve incorporated them.

Closer to the center, the trunks grow thicker, twisting into columns that support tier after tier of suspended platforms. These platforms are not made of metal decking but of living wood—boards of a pale, almost glassy hardwood that grows outward horizontally. You can see concentric rings if you look at the edges: new growth rings, laid down by the year, but the planks are still perfectly flat and even.

The city entire is built from these platforms.

Residential blocks are stacked in the branches of a single colossal tree, whose crown rises like a cathedral in the middle of the chamber. Office spires are grown from the trunks of others—multi-story chambers carved directly into the wood, hollowed but not killed. Industrial decks are arranged around the trunks like spokes, each level hosting a different function.

The wood is not passive. You can see faint slow movement, if you watch long enough: the floors of a suspended plaza shifting slightly as they expand and contract under the weight of people; stairs re-forming their steps; a balcony cantilever lengthening like a slow inhalation. You could design a neighborhood here, and ten years later, it would have grown into your plans.

Every structure is still a plant.

---

### Air and Atmosphere

The air is cool, steady, never uncomfortably cold or hot. It feels like a cellar in spring—ground-chilled, neutral. You breathe it easily, but there is no humidity. No dampness. No metallic smell of water running.

In fact, you cannot smell water at all. No river smell, no storm smell, no moss smell. There is a faint scent, like charcoal dust and polished resin, and underneath that something like refined starch—smooth, not food-like but chemical. You could spend years here and never see your breath fog.

The ceiling forest of roots provides the light. It also provides the air.

If you look at the ceiling long enough—and you are not the first, everyone does—you see that the luminous root mat is layered into strata. The lowest layer is simple white; above that are faint color variations—pale greens, yellows, blues—like a polarized sky. You know that those roots are scrubbers and filters and heat sinks; you know that the atmosphere of this entire closed world is manufactured by the functioning of that plant ceiling.

That’s where the oxygen comes from.

Somewhere past the hard stone above you—some kilometers up—is the surface world, where water and sun and seasons are something else entirely. That’s irrelevant. The world relevant to you now is the tree city in the stone chamber.

Up there, the world may be drowning in rain, or scoured by salt winds, or flooded by seas. Down here, water is a resource.

---

### Water: Scarce, Altered, and Controlled

You notice it first in the small things.

The ceramic basins at street corners aren’t fountains. They are shallow, wide bowls. They are empty.

The sewers are dry channels, not dark wet tunnels. You can see them down between the living ribs of the city floor: little channels, neatly carved and lined with glazed ceramic tile, now filling with faint dust and tiny leaves instead of sluicing water.

No water runs.

The water here is not liquid at all.

From the ceiling of the world, a steady rain of very fine powder descends—not quite snow, not dust, but something like powdered sugar. You can hold out your hand and let it settle: white, crystalline, dry, but smelling faintly of hydrogen and structures.

This is a water world.

The water is not in oceans. It’s in the crystals.

Ceiling roots are specialized to split atomic bonds—bounding oxygen into gas, and hydrogen into stored lattice. You don’t know the chemistry; you only know that you can breathe, and that you never see rain.

The water you need is collected from ceramic grids at the top of the highest tree spires. These grids are grown to be precise; they are part of a trunk—a branching trunk whose crown is flattened dish shape—and the crystals fall into them. They do not melt. They are collected as dry white powder, then…

Transportation.

---

This part of the world is still being formed.

On the lower tier, near the stone shell of the cavern, “construction” is happening. There is no building site in the old sense—no cement mixers, no flatbed trucks. Instead, you see rows of what look like hand-carved stone troughs arranged in precise arcs.

Men and women in work clothes—coolant suits, you call them—scatter fine crystalline powder from shoulder-mounted hoppers. They do not rush. They wear masks and goggles, even though the air is safe; they know that if you breathe the crystals too deeply, you will feel your lungs begin to stick.

The powder is mixed.

The “mix” is not water. It’s not liquid. It’s not anything.

You watch a worker pour a measured amount of the white crystalline dust from a ceramic container into a stone trough. Another worker adds a darker powder—a mineral, ground from crushed stone—and a third, a tiny careful quantity of another catalyst: a metallic-green glitter like copper filings.

They stir the powders together.

At the center of the trough, they press a seed—a pearl-shaped node of bone-white wood—into the mixture. They cover it.

Then they wait.

You watch the measured sparseness of the scene—powders, stone troughs, six workers in open space. You’d expect a warehouse, a hall, a factory floor. It’s not. It’s open—just ribs of stone, space between the presences, and low grown-rail lines that the workers’ tiny carts move on.

Minutes or hours—it’s hard to say—pass.

They leave.

When you return to that same trough later, or someone else does, a trunk has begun.

It’s a low ring of dense white wood, maybe knee height, now perfectly joined to the stone trough floor. On its top surface, a network of very fine channels—like leaf veins—have etched themselves into the wood’s surface. When you look at it from the side, you realize that it’s already hollowing at the center. Chemical channels have appeared.

The black powder, the metallic-green glitter, and the crystalline water—they’ve moved.

The trunk has already integrated them.

This is “erosion.”

You are living at the beginning. Most geology is just—geology—stone and move and settle. But here, stone is carved and then taken over. The ribs are only scaffolding; what will endure is the network.

---

### Movement: Urban Machinery

Across the ribs of the stone skeleton, a grown transport system snakes.

Most rails are embedded—metal tracks fused right into the trunks, lines of silver in the porcelain-white. The trains that run on them are small, automated, with carriages like elongated ceramic barrels—no windows, only sliding doors.

When you ride, you feel no vibration: the track is damped by the wood. The train accelerates smoothly, silently, along a curve that spirals up around a trunk the thickness of a tower. You glance to the side—through the mesh—and you see lights of smaller districts below: platforms, plazas, neighborhoods.

You notice one thing: the city has no rooftops.

Every roof is a branch.

This is not forest above streets—it’s forest as streets.

Smaller platforms grow out as branches—living, slowly—from the trunks like bookshelf brackets. On these brackets, you have residential blocks, studies, shops, laboratories. The ceilings above their internals are thick canopy of leaves and branches—so dense that you don’t see the ceiling of the world from below; just another layer, another floor.

On some levels, the industrial branches have thicker bark—shielded, armored—where you see armatures, crane systems, and material conveyors integrated into the trunk. Some machinery is still steel—factories suspended from tree branches—where you hear faint hisses and mechanical grindings. Some stations have internal engines: silent fusion, or compact induction. Their roofs are part of the same trunk.

So this is industrial.

But the industry is not the primary object.

The industry hums quietly—a faint vibration—buzzing deep inside the largest trunks, working with them. It’s no longer obvious that anyone owns it; it’s more like a nervous system.

Above the industrial tiers, the residential branches are thinner, more airy—more light passing through them—and there are openings. You can step onto open balconies that are themselves growing; the railings are sprigs of new wood, left in place.

You can look out and see.

---

### Openness and Sparse Presence

Despite the city—and despite the vegetation filling—the space does not feel dense.

You see:

- voids  
- empty air between trunk and trunk  
- a wide clearance below the lowest branches  
- arteries where you can walk straight for hundreds of meters without interruption  

The ribs of the stone floor are spaced wide enough that you can feel the distance. When you stand on a central platform—grown from the side of a trunk—and you look down at the floor below, you see sparse placement. One block here—one block way over there—connected by long fan bridges of thin bone-white wood.

You notice pedestrians.

Not dense crowds. There’s no sense of rush hour—maybe there never is. People walk in small clusters or alone. They’re dressed lightly—no heavy coats needed—just layered jackets or thin coolant suits, hands exposed.

They move quietly.

The sound here is not overwhelming. The machinery is distant—buried, insulated—and the trains run with barely any noise. There’s no wind to whistle; no nearby water to roar. No air conditioning.

What you hear are:

- soft scuffs of boots on wooden surfaces  
- the occasional click of a ceramic door latch  
- faint thrum from somewhere inside the largest trunk—and  
- if you listen very closely, a prolonged, low sliding—like branches shifting—from overhead  

This is “mostly quiet.”

And the shapes—they are strange.

---

### Strangeness: A Plant-Governed World

The city grows—but not exactly to the plan of anyone living.

Architects don’t draw blueprints on paper anymore. They cultivate patterns.

You see structures for which there is no obvious metaphor—neither “leaves” nor “windows” nor “houses” exactly:

- Boxes of polished wood suspended in midair by trunks that have thickened behind them like hands  
- Stairs that are a single snake-shaped branch—steps formed by knots along its length—  
- Circular platforms where floors have grown into latticework—open gaps—through which you can see many levels down  

And above—higher than all—the ceiling of roots.

That’s where you feel the weirdness most strongly.

At night, if you call it that, the ceiling grows slightly dimmer; not dark, but shifted down in intensity. The patterns of light change—like a faint flicker of aurora, or the glitching of a screen—and you realize that some regions of the root ceiling are brighter—others dim.

You notice:

- some regions of the ceiling root forest are “young,”  
- some regions are “old.”

In the young regions, the root network is thinner, more synthetic—blue light irregularities—as if the roots are growing out of control into patterns.

In the old regions, you see gaps—patches where the roots have been pruned, or burnt away, or died.

Each city below—on this continent—must have its own ceiling.

You imagine other chambers like this one, under other mountains or plains or deserts—each city a tree in a stone bowl, with its own luminous root sky.

That’s what you think.

But then—when you find the records—you see references.

You see:

- a schematic of a cross-section of the world—  
- each layer marked  
- each “root” system  
- each “trunk”  
- and underneath that drawing  
- another drawing  

The labels say:

> intra-crustal  
> forest  
> urban  
> layer

Underneath that:

> down  
> found  
> city  
> tree

You see a child—curling up—on the floor of a wooden library—with no ink, no paper—drawing on the surface of the branch itself, with fingers—watching the branch grow slightly darker around her traced lines.

This is normal.

### Fauna: Living Amid and On the Plants

Birds exist—not birds like feathers, but birds like wings.

Most of the animals you see—you call them animals—are integrated.

You notice:

- tiny insect-like creatures that clings to the surfaces of branches—hard shell, many jointed legs—grazing on mineral dusts or structural resins of the wood  
- larger “rodents” that are half—chitinous carapace, half—fur, exploring the underside of platforms like tree hollows  

The predators are—shy.

They rarely show themselves.

The predators—if you can call them that—are not sharp teeth. They are—hooks.

Some branches have specialized “growths” that are not branches—and not animals either—but hybrids: curling tendrils that can suddenly close around a small creature—like insectivorous plants—except never with teeth, just with—infolding. You notice that when something—living—gets too close, a branch might grow a little thicker—a fan—that—velcro—catches.

You see some of these—plants—holding—

You don’t like to think about it.

You keep your hands to yourself when you walk near unprotected edges.
