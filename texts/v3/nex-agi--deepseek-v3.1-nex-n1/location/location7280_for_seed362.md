You stand on the edge of a nonexistent sea.

The floor, which is not a floor but the skin of a world, is a pale off-white, like bone left too long in the sun. About it there is nothing of sand or salt or dust—only a faint graininess, like the surface tension of solidified milk. It is not cold to the touch, but it gives back no warmth either; it holds the temperature of a body that died hours ago.

You are not sure, when you think about it, whether your eyes tell you what is *below* or *above*. The surface curves up gently, rising away from you, and at the same time it seems to fall down into unfathomable depth. The distant horizon is not a clean line but a suggestion—where the white fades into a blankness that has no color, because it has swallowed all of them.

This is the first reality of the place: there is no water.

No water, and yet there is no dryness. No moisture, and yet there is no thirst. Your lips are not chapped. Your skin does not crack. The air is not empty, but what it carries instead of water is not something you can name. It tastes like static before the storm, except there is never any storm.

---

The second reality is that everything living is a plant.

Or at least, that is the closest word you have.

It rises out of the white substrate like a city dreaming of being a forest. You cannot tell where one organism ends and another begins. Stems are also columns; leaves are also walls. Canopies knit overhead into ceilings that are not so much roofs as they are the upper level of a single continuous body.

It is very dense.

In every direction you look, there are forms upon forms upon forms. Surfaces interlock with surfaces, surfaces become surfaces for other things. Nothing is quite perpendicular to anything else; every edge leans slightly, as if the whole place remembers being a seed that has never finished opening. You thread your way along corridors that are not exactly corridors but spaces between the bodies of plants that have forgotten to be afraid of each other.

The color palette is wrong in a way you feel before you understand. The dominant tone is a deep, bruised indigo. The leaves of the lower tier are almost black at the stem, shading into a deep ultramarine as they broaden. Higher up, the color lightens: a clear blue fading into cyan at the tips. There are no true greens. No chlorophyll waiting to be exposed. No yellows or reds that are not the faint, bruised hints of a piling-on of blues.

These are not plants that have stolen sunlight; they have stolen something else, and they have had a long time to learn.

---

The third reality is that it is cold.

Not freezing cold—not the brittle cold that breaks things—but a steady cool that has been here so long it has become part of the structure. The great plant bodies hum quietly with it. You feel the faint chill rising through the soles of your boots, through the white floor that is neither stone nor soil but something else, and you realize the chill is not primarily thermal.

It is a species of distance.

Everything you see is very close and very far away at the same time. The curve of a leaf at your shoulder is only inches from your cheek, but you feel as if you are looking at it through a telescope. The field of depth is wrong. The air between you and it is not empty but is filled with a faint insistence of … presence.

The fourth reality is that this is a built world.

Or at least, it was once.

As you move deeper into the plant-city’s interior, the floor thickens with an understory of petal-platelets that have never fully fallen, but which have fused into the floor like a persistent carpet. You see the suggestion of lines beneath them—rectangles that are too straight to be organic, but which are not exactly straight anymore.

The further you go, the more the plants give way to structures that are not plants but machines made to resemble plants in order to survive.

Columns are fluted like stems, but inside you can hear, very softly, the faint thrum of a circulatory system that is not entirely biological. Ducts are disguised as vines; valves pretend to be nodules; translucent panels along the high “canopies” are not exactly leaves but not exactly solar collectors either. They glow faintly from within, shedding the almost painful light that has no color you can name.

The fifth reality is that this place is vast.

You cannot see the limits of it.

You walk through galleries of vegetation that open into chambers of vegetation that lead into halls of vegetation that reveal sky of vegetation. There is weather here.

Packets of air circulate through ducts like blood in a heart, but they are lazy and slow. They generate a soft wind that never quite becomes a breeze. It carries the occasional scent-fragment of something like ozone and something like wet paper. You feel the suggestion of fronts passing—changes of pressure that make your eardrums pop, though you hear nothing.

The ceiling-far-above is not an opaque surface but a layer of translucent material that flexes. When “pressure” builds, it dimples outward like a membrane about to burst. Then it relaxes again.

The storms are very quiet.

When they come, the air grows thick and slow. You feel as if you are trying to breathe through glass. The blue foliage above pulses slightly. The light that was bright before becomes brighter, but it is a brightness that has been lifted into a higher-frequency band of the eye. You feel the light more than you see it.

The sixth reality is that there are almost no animals.

You are not sure if the absence of animal sounds is a true sound-absence or the result of your ears failing to be tuned correctly.

You have heard no rustle of movement through the understory. No wings. No distant calls. No buzzing of small things. Every once in a while, you feel something like a presence watching you, but when you turn, there is only a wedge of blue leaf that might have been slightly more closed than before.

Eventually you see a creature.

It is a small, folded thing tucked into the junction where a “branch” meets the wall of a chamber. It is shaped like a seed pod with limbs. Its limbs are not quite jointed correctly.

You watch it for a long time.

It does not breathe. You think perhaps it does not need to. If you look at it directly, it is perfectly still. If you look away, you occasionally feel a faint slippage in your peripheral vision, as if the world has just taken a small step sideways.

When you finally force yourself to see it for what it is, you realize that its face is not exactly a face, but a pattern of lines that suggests where a face might be drawn if you were to map it onto some other surface. Its eyes are flat, polished places where the light stops.

It does not move to eat. You never see it eat. You are not sure if it eats at all, or if it lives off some other kind of transaction.

The seventh reality is that everything is wrong in a way that feels like it is trying to be understandable.

The geometry of the plant-city is not consistent from one area to the next.

You step through a thick arch of leaves and into a chamber where the “floor” is now slightly above where the last floor was. You step again, and it is below. You cannot tell if you have gone up or down. The curvature of the world does not align correctly with your inner sense of gravity. The light you followed into this chamber comes from the side, but it feels like it is coming from above.

If you retrace your steps exactly, you will not exit the same way you came in.

You are not sure if the world has rearranged itself or if you have rearranged your memory of it.

The eighth reality is that sound has been peeled away.

There is noise, but very little of it.

The machinery—the “plant-machines” that are the engines of this place—moves, but it moves without friction. You never hear the shriek of metal or the grind of gears. There is no rush of water through pipes. No air vents whistle; no fans whir.

The quiet you live inside is like the inside of a glass.

When you speak, even in a whisper, your voice stands out clean and sharp and then vanishes too quickly. It is not echoed back. It is not absorbed. It is simply taken somewhere else.

Occasionally, you hear a faint sound like a sheet of paper being torn at very great distance. It comes from nowhere in particular. When you try to locate it, it becomes the direction you are looking in. It stops when you stop.

The ninth reality is that everything is still happening.

Processes continue.

The air moves. The “pressure” changes. The lights pulse faintly in response to some deep rhythm of the world. You see the soft movement of fluids within translucent stems, not quite blood, not sap, not coolant, but something like all of them.

Pieces of the structure above you occasionally unfurl. They stretch like fingers opening after a long sleep, then resettle into slightly different configurations. The walls are always adjusting themselves.

There are patterns of motion that you feel more than see.

When you look at a section of the wall directly, it is perfectly still. When you glance away, you see the afterimage of it having moved slightly. You begin to wonder if you are the one who is flickering, and the wall is steady.

---

You are not sure how long you have been here.

You have not eaten. You have not drunk water. You have not slept.

On one level, you are aware that this should not be possible; on another level, your body has not complained yet. It is as if the world itself is holding you together, keeping you from falling apart according to the rules of a different physics.

You find a place where the floor is a shallow bowl of blue-white ribs that curve around you. They rise like the ribs of a giant whose skeleton has been turned into a room.

In the center of the bowl is a small object.

It is not metal. It is not ceramic. It is not plant.

It is a small, polished shape of no color you can name, but which you label “silver” only because language has left you small other options. It rests perfectly still on the surface of the white floor, without marking it.

You reach out and touch it.

Your fingers are cooler than it is.

In the moment of contact, the world tilts.

---

You are standing on the edge of a nonexistent sea.

The floor is a pale off-white, like bone left too long in the sun.

In front of you stretches the city of blue plants that has merged into a machine that has merged into a world that has merged into a single organism.

You are not sure if you have been here before, or if you are arriving for the first time, or if you are arriving again after having left.

Your memory is not broken.

It is just that some of it is now on the wrong side of the world.

You begin walking again, into the cool, bright, quiet heart of a plant-continent that has never been anything else, and which has long ago settled into its established shape, weathering its own strange storms, under a light that has learned to be a sun.
