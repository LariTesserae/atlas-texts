High on the plateau, the world is still being made.

The air itself is water—cool, heavy, saturated. You taste the sky before you see it. Everything you look at wears a faint, luminous blur. A constant condensation rains down from the clouds that press low over the plateau, forming millions of tiny droplets on the thick, living canopy of plants below until the leaves themselves seem to weep, and every surface glows.

---

### Terrain and geology

This plateau rests like a table above the rest of the world.

The rock itself feels young: rough, unfinished, unweathered. The stone hasn't yet worked into the familiar shapes of valleys and gorges. Instead, there are broad swells and shallow depressions, like a skin of cooled lava slowly hardening into permanence. There are no deep ravines, no rust-colored cliffs carved by centuries of water. Instead, water appears everywhere as a thin film, a steady sheen on stone, a constant trickle beneath the surface.

The plateau itself is built of dark stone that seems vaguely glassy—a porous, black basalt mixed with some lighter, crystalline intrusion that catches the light like veins of chrome. Underfoot it feels solid but uneven, like the floor of a workshop still cluttered by half-carved blocks.

Where water and stone meet, the stone is still undecided about what form it will take. Small channels are beginning to appear—not yet deep, but persistent. They lead across the plateau in shallow, braided patterns that gather closer and closer together until they finally plunge over the edge in a series of thin waterfalls that disappear almost immediately into mist. The plateau itself has no sharp edge; rather, it fades away into a cloud sea that boils around its knees. The world beyond is invisible, lost in white.

---

### Vegetation as substrate and substance

The vegetation doesn't grow on the plateau; it is the plateau.

Almost everywhere you look—except for scattered patches of black stone—the surface is one continuous architecture of plants. Trunks, branches, leaves, and roots blur together into a single mass. The ground isn't soil; it's layers of compressed plant matter—living, dead, and half-living—woven into a thick mat. You walk on something that feels like a cross between wood and cork, dense but resilient.

There are no solitary trees in the sense of “individuals.” Each trunk splits into others, branches root into neighboring trunks, roots tangle and merge, forming fused arches and hollows beneath. The canopy rises quite evenly across the plateau—no tall peaks of forest, just a rolling green ceiling that stretches over the low stone hillocks.

The trees themselves are strange in their lack of strangeness: they have leaves, needles, bark, but nothing feels like familiar species. The bark is smooth and pale, like the skin of a young mushroom. The leaves are small, stiff, and glossy, arranged in tight spirals around every twig. They all angle toward the light, which falls everywhere at once.

Because the plants themselves form the ground and the air around them, they create their own micro-landscape. Hollows form where trunks arch inward and roots meet higher overhead, creating caves beneath the living canopy. Spines of thicker growth run in long lines across the plateau, like natural walls, and in between them are flatter glades where the living roof is thinner and brighter.

---

### Water in the living world

Water is everywhere, but hidden.

On the surface, you see it as condensation: droplets forming on every leaf edge until they drip in steady, irregular rhythms. Where the tree trunks arch over each other, water runs down in thin sheets that glisten, then vanish into the porous plant matter below.

There are no true rivers or lakes here—only temporary pools and shallow streams that appear briefly wherever the land dips low enough. The plateau itself is spongelike. Water arrives as rain and mist, is absorbed into the complex mass of roots and trunks, and travels slowly through the living system before emerging farther along in some other place.

The ground itself is damp, not muddy. The plants absorb most of the water before it can accumulate. The largest pools you might find are no wider than a few steps across, shallow mirrors of sky reflecting back the bright clouds above. They last only hours or days before being absorbed again.

Where the plateau meets the cloud sea below, water truly dominates. The edge is marked by a ring of dense vegetation that grows low and thick over black stone. Water pours off the leaves there like a constant shower, loud enough to drown out voices, and everywhere you step there is a faint squelch beneath your feet. The rock itself is slick, covered in thin films of algae and moss that look like smeared oil paint.

### Atmosphere and light

The temperature feels cool but not cold—like early morning on a plateau in springtime.

The air is thick with moisture, so it feels cooler than it actually is. There's no wind in the everyday sense; only slow, heavy currents of air that slide along the living canopy like water over stone. The sky itself is rarely seen directly. Instead, there is a luminous ceiling: clouds that hang low over the plateau, glowing.

The light is bright, but diffuse. It comes from everywhere at once—reflected off the clouds and the wet leaves, so shadows have soft edges. There is no sun glare, no point source. Instead, the landscape itself produces light indirectly through sheer reflection: every glossy leaf, every polished stone, every trembling droplet adds brightness. The result is that everything glows softly, as if seen through translucent paper.

The air smells clean but sharp, like crushed needles and damp stone. There is no rot smell, despite the wetness—the vegetation breathes water in and out so quickly that decay never has time to assert itself. The plateau feels like a place still too young to smell of death.

---

### Sound

The dominant sound is water.

The voices of waterfalls are always nearby—one distant and steady, like a low white roar at the edge of the world, and many closer, smaller ones that fall from leaf to leaf, from branch to branch, until every drop contributes its voice to a chorus of rustles and splashes.

Over the plateau itself, the sound is less thunderous than oceanic. It is a constant rush of droplets falling onto leaves, onto stone, into water. The noise blankets the landscape like a second atmosphere. Speech becomes difficult; you must move close to hear each other or raise your voice above the endless murmur.

There are other sounds beneath that water-voice, but rare and brief:

- The sudden crack of a branch—not the breaking of wood, but the sharp, wet snap of a plant element shifting under the weight of accumulated water.
- The infrequent cry of some unseen creature—a single high note carried once across the plateau and then vanishing into the noise.
- The occasional low groan of metal, far off.

The silence that might be expected at this altitude and scale does not exist. Instead of silence, there is a single, continuous soundscape, like surf inside a shell.

---

### Ecology: life in balance

The vegetation is the primary environment.

Most animals here are small and narrow-bodied, adapted to moving through the tight spaces within the living mat. They are rarely seen: quick glimpses of something pale and thin slipping between roots, or the faint impression of something moving beneath the translucent bark of a young trunk. There are no flocks of birds overhead, no herds of animals grazing across the glades. Instead, there are occasional, solitary presences—a movement at the corner of your eye, a brief disturbance in the patterns of water dripping from leaves.

The plants themselves support most of the visible life. Everything else lives on the margins of that dominant life-form. There are flying insects that appear only rarely, hovering briefly over the pools before vanishing back into the vegetation. There is something like small, pale lizards that drink from the edges of leaves. There might be larger creatures somewhere deeper in the plateau, but they are rare enough that they might go unseen for years.

Life itself is not the weird thing here. What's strange is the ratio between living and non-living: the list of animals is short, and the list of plants is essentially one—massive, integrated, and everywhere. The plateau feels almost like a single organism whose organs are otherwise uninhabited.

---

### Human presence: scattered, faint, industrial

The plateau does not feel "uninhabited," although it largely is.

There are a few structures scattered across the plateau—small buildings, low and angular, made from metal and stone, not wood. They sit on the patches of bare rock that poke through the plant mat, like islands in a green sea. Their contours are simple and functional: rectangular shapes with flat roofs, no windows. The doorways are small and sealed with heavy hatches. The walls are painted with pale, reflective coatings that have begun to peel, revealing layers of rusty metal beneath.

Most of these buildings stand alone, separated from each other by long stretches of vegetation and stone. There are no roads connecting them—no clear paths at all except the faint suggestions of repeated foot travel between trunks and over shallow roots.

Technology appears here mostly in the form of what remains: faint metal traces beneath the vegetation, small towers rising from the canopy like lattice fingers, thin cables snaking through the undergrowth, occasionally surfacing as polished lines beneath the dripping leaves before vanishing again into the living mat. There are no moving vehicles, no visible lights, no engines humming in the background.

But there is evidence of industry:

- Metal panels, half-buried in plant matter, that once might have collected solar energy or transmitted some kind of signal.
- Concrete foundations, now cracked and furred with moss, that once supported something heavier than the small buildings now standing upon them.
- The occasional low, humming vibration that appears beneath the water-noise—infrequent and faint, like a distant generator running for hours at a time before stopping again.

The buildings themselves seem abandoned, but not entirely. They show signs of occasional use—doors sealed more recently, tracks pressed into the damp plant mat around them, faint marks where moss has been scraped away around metal panels. There is no sense of a bustling community; only that of a small number of people who visit rarely and briefly, leaving faint traces behind.

The human presence feels like an experiment rather than a settlement. This plateau isn't a place where people dwell permanently; it seems more like a station where someone comes from time to time to check on something—or on something that has been left running here on its own.

---

### Time and change

Time feels slow here.

The erosion that would elsewhere carve the world into deep contours has barely begun. The stone is still young, the water hasn't yet worn its signature into the rock. The streams that appear and disappear are shallow, tentative. They might change course slightly from year to year, but there is no sense of violent movement.

The vegetation changes slowly as well. The architecture of trunks and roots appears to shift over long periods—small trunks growing over older ones, branches merging, forming new hollows beneath. But from day to day, nothing seems to move. The water dripping from leaf to leaf has been dripping for a long time in exactly that pattern; the clouds above have been sitting low over the plateau for months, perhaps years.

The human artifacts seem stranded in this slow time. Rust spreads slowly on the metal surfaces, not in sudden outbreaks but in subtle red-brown stains that creep along the edges over years. The moss thickens in patches where the buildings once stood clean.

There is no sense of sudden decay or collapse. Instead, everything feels like it is slowly settling into place, waiting for the plateau to decide what it will become when it grows up.

---

### The world's rules

This place feels normal in the sense that there are no blatant supernatural laws or physics-defying geometries. But it operates under subtle new rules:

- Water is not a separate substance from the air or ground. It flows continuously through the system—cloud to leaf to root to cloud again—without significant interruption. The plateau doesn't dry up or flood; it simply adjusts its internal distribution of moisture.
- Erosion is not yet powerful. The rocks are resistant to the water that flows over them. The streams themselves are more habit than force—a slow pattern just beginning to assert itself over the stone.
- The living system is self-supporting. The plants themselves moderate the climate by storing water and releasing it slowly. They prevent temperature extremes by acting like a massive buffer layer between the rock and the sky.
- The plateau's isolation is physical but not absolute. It is high above the world below, but the world below doesn't seem to matter much here. The human presence is small and remote enough that it doesn't dominate anything. Instead, it takes advantage of small cracks in the living system—sitting on bare stone patches, laying cables along thin corridors beneath tree trunks, avoiding open conflict with the dominant vegetation.

---

In summary:

The landscape is a high plateau newly made, still deciding its features. Water dominates as atmosphere—condensing everywhere, dripping from leaf to leaf, forming faint streams that barely begin to cut into stone. The vegetation itself forms the ground and the air around it, filling the space with a single rolling architecture of trunks and roots and leaves woven into a dense, living substrate.

The temperature is cool, the light bright but diffuse. The sound is deafening but constant—like living inside a waterfall. The plateau feels open and sparse at the same time: open in the sense of having wide views across the canopy and stone, but sparse in the sense that few animals live here and the human presence is faint and scattered.

Across the plateau are scattered small industrial structures—metal boxes on concrete islands—that hint at some long-running, low-intensity human activity, but do not dominate it. The world feels as though it has just begun: rock still raw, streams still searching, plants still expanding their empire. And through it all runs the slow, patient cycle of water moving through a living, breathing world that is still learning how to shape itself into permanence.
