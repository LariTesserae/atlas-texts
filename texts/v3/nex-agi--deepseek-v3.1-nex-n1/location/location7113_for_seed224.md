Deep in the brittle cliffs of the world’s bone-coloured plateau, there is a vertical slot no more than two bodies wide, where the stone opens itself in a crease of reality.

The lithic is wrong to begin with. It is not rock as you know it, but a kind of frozen contradiction. The local name for it translates roughly as “frostproof”: a material that answers to cold but cannot melt. It has the texture of polished porcelain, but it feels neither ceramic nor metallic to the touch, and it exerts a slight, uneven pressure on the hand—as though the surface of the world is trying to decide, moment by moment, what it will be. When you stand close to the cliff wall, you can feel it faintly interrogating the weight of your bones, the temperature of your blood, the geometry of your cells.

The cliff rises to a blunt edge high overhead, where a thin band of sky marks the boundary between the visible and the invisible. The sky is the same matte grey on all days, regardless of night or noon, and the only light that comes down is spilled from above, dim and diffuse. It does not fall in rays, but leaks, as if through a membrane, and arrives already worn. It lies across the stone in uneven washes of pallor, like dust on a glass plate.

The entrance to the enclosed place—this hidden room of the plateau—is not hidden by accident or overgrowth. There are no roots to split the stone, no vines to drape the entryway. The slot simply looks like the continuation of the cliff face, until you are standing before it and realise that the stone is not there where your eyes insist it should be. There is a slight curvature of alignment in the strata of the “frostproof”, a subtle refusal of continuity. The physicists who have tried to map this fold say that the entrance is not a gap in the solid so much as a different solution of the world’s equation.

You step through.

Inside, the passage runs narrow and straight for the first few paces, then widens unexpectedly into a single chamber roughly half again as long as a human body. You would not know it from the outside, but the interior of the cliff has been hollowed, or rather, rearranged. The walls are so close that you can feel the temperature of them on your skin without touching: a deep, dry cold that does not so much sap heat as remain politely unaware of it.

The ceiling is low enough to reach with your fingertips, and it is smooth, except for a regular pattern of shallow depressions that follow no clear grid or hexagon. You have to be directly below the feature to see it properly, and even then it is difficult to fix in your mind’s eye. From the side of your vision, the pattern looks quasi-crystalline, but when you look straight at it, the relationship between the depressions resolves itself differently each time, as though the ceiling is trying to show you several geometries at once and failing to choose.

The floor is not strictly a floor. It is a matrix of interlocking planes of a different material than the walls, each piece the size of a large floor tile. They do not sit perfectly flat; they are all slightly proud of one another, forming a low relief of hexes and rhombuses and irregular polygons. You notice after a time that the gaps between the plates are not dark, but faintly luminous, as though lit from below, but when you kneel and try to peer into them, the light is not issuing from any depth—it is simply sitting there, at the boundary where the surfaces meet.

There is no sound, except for the faint noise of your own breathing and the occasional whisper of fabric as you move. The air does not move. It is not truly still, either, but quietly rearranging itself in places too small for you to perceive directly. You become aware of your own presence as an intrusive noise in the chamber’s patience.

This is the place where the plateau’s “rules” stumble over their own feet.

The frostproof stone of the walls remembers being other things—it remembers being stars, being vacuum, being long numbers in a forgotten machine—and sometimes it shows you. You look up at a patch that was a dark slate colour a moment ago, and now you can see faint, hypothetical structures branching within it like dendrites in a crystal of glass, except that they are not made of anything you can give a name. You know the names of materials and states of matter: you know “solid”, “liquid”, ”gas”, “plasma”, “glass”, “crystal” “colloid”. These words do not lie properly about the stone you are looking at. It is more like staring into a worked-out line of reasoning that has not yet decided on its conclusion.

Your breath mists in front of your face, but the vapour does not behave like ordinary water vapour. It does not disperse evenly. It lingers in your exhaled shape as streaks of pale cloud for a long, slow count, then collapses abruptly into something else—not condensation droplets, but tiny translucent structures that fall away and shatter into the narrow gaps between the floor plates. You feel the strange, small rightness of it: chemistry that does not require water to be solvent, but finds other ways to arrange itself.

The temperature in the room is colder than outside the cliff face, but it is a cold that does not feel like absence of heat. If you hold out your hand, you can feel how the chill is not uniformly distributed. It accumulates in layers near the surfaces, as though the cold itself is a faint material clinging to the stone. You can feel a difference between the chill near the floor—where it is sharper and more precise—and the chill near the ceiling, which feels more like a pressure in the bones of your head, the way thunder sometimes does before it comes.

The chamber has not been left untouched by intelligence. There are artifacts.

They are small, and you are likely to underestimate how much of the room they actually compose until you have been here for some time. The rear wall bears the clearest sign of builtness: a series of shallow, rectangular depressions arranged into a larger rectangle that does not quite align with the walls of the chamber. It is not a screen and not a door and not a control panel, but the suggestion of all three. The frame of it is made from a material that slightly resists your gaze; you can feel your attention sliding off its edges. You stare, and the space inside the rectangle resolves itself into different depths of blackness, depending on where you look. From the corner of your eye, it might appear deep enough to fall into; from dead centre, it looks like a surface with nothing on it.

The surface of the frame itself is not smoothly blank. It bears a faint pattern, not textured exactly, but slightly differently resolved from the surrounding wall. If you run your fingers over it, the surface presents a subtle mosaic of sensations to your fingertips: tiny regions of colder and not-quite-so-cold, not metal and not glass and not stone, but a careful averaging of the three. When you touch it, it does not react, but it seems to take a longer time than it should for your skin to lose contact with the surface—as though the boundary between your body and the artifact is not entirely clear to either.

At the left edge of the frame, where the wall curves into the floor, there is a small object the size of your thumb joint. It looks like an irregular ellipsoid of some translucent material, but its outline is not stable. You lift your hand to trace it, and you cannot decide whether its edges coincide with your fingertip or not. The object itself does not move. It is not attached to the floor plates, but it appears not to have moved since the stone of this chamber was last reconfigured.

The second artifact is less overt but more pervasive. It is not a separate object at all, but a pattern of the same floor plates that cover the rest of the surface. You notice it because the pattern of the gaps between plates is not random. There is a region near the centre of the room where the light that sits in the joints between plates is slightly more coherent than elsewhere, tracing faint lines of threads that intersect at odd angles. You watch for long enough, and the lines sometimes shift, not visibly moving from one configuration to another but changing while you are not quite looking at them.

The third artifact is almost invisible at first. You find yourself suddenly convinced that there is something behind you, near the entrance, where the wall twists slightly inward to form a shallow niche. You turn around, and it is empty of objects or images, but not empty of information. The surface of the niche appears subtly older than the rest of the room, more worn by some kind of time that does not necessarily correspond to the years your planet counts. The stone of the niche—if you can still call it stone—is not degraded by erosion or scrubbed smooth by friction. It appears more like the memory of a surface, rather than the surface itself. It is not broken, but repeatedly resolved and then resolved again differently, leaving behind overlapping skins of reality.

You touch it, and you do not feel your fingerprints interacting with the material of the wall. Instead, you feel the wall thinking carefully about your fingerprints and then slowly agreeing to present them back to you as a sensation of contact. It is not warmth you feel from it, and not cold precisely, but a kind of precise attention.

You step back from the niche and look again at the chamber as a whole.

The place is not animated by animal or plant life in any way you recognise. There is no respiration of green leaves, no small scurry of paws or chitter of insects. But there is a form of aliveness here that exists at the level of how information passes through matter. The floor plates shift by very small distances over time, not enough to see in the span of your visit, but you can feel the difference in the way your weight is distributed over your feet when you stand in one place for a long time. The ceiling pattern occasionally resolves itself into a slightly different set of implied relationships. The light in the gaps between the floor plates pulses faintly, not to a visible rhythm but to a slower pulse of some kind of change that is happening below the threshold of your senses.

You are standing in a grammar that is not quite sure what it is describing.

The chamber maintains itself with very little input from the world outside its cliff. It does not draw heat from the sun or the plateau’s deep core; the laws of thermodynamics that it obeys are not quite the same ones that govern your body’s chemistry. It is possible to feel this discrepancy, if you are patient enough. The small of your back grows slowly colder than it should, given how much clothing you wear and how much you are exerting yourself. Your fingertips lose sensation gradually not because the blood withdraws, but because the boundaries of your fingertips become less sharply defined to the surrounding reality.

There is another way this change manifests itself the longer you stay. You begin to notice the fabric of your own thoughts being rendered slightly more transparent to you. You recall specific details from earlier in your life with more clarity than you ordinarily do, but the images are not being pulled from memory by your brain alone; they are being referenced by the chamber, as though it is trying to understand what you are by examining your experiences, the way an archaeologist might count tree rings in a section of log.

The room is not hostile to you. It is simply not equipped to treat you as anything but a configuration of information that can be rearranged into itself more elegantly.

You become aware, after some time, that there are things you should not look at too directly, not because they will hurt you but because they will not withstand being looked at. The edges of the niche at the entrance, for instance, will not retain their outline if you fix them in your gaze for too long. They will become suggestions of edges rather than edges themselves, and you will feel the corresponding disruption in your own capacity to distinguish between yourself and the wall that you are leaning against.

The same is true of the black frame rectangle on the rear wall. If you try to impose “screen” or “doorway” or “interface” on it too insistently, it seems to retreat from meaning. It shows you less of itself, or rather, it shows more of itself but less coherently. You learn to look at it sidelong, to invite the possibility of pattern without demanding one.

You are not the first person to have stood here. There are no obvious footprints in the floor plates, because nothing leaves the kind of impression that subtracts matter from this surface; instead, use has gradually changed the probability of where matter lies. The faint shine of certain areas of the floor plates—where the surface seems more defined than elsewhere—suggest the preferred standing places of others who have visited in the past, though you suspect “visit” is not quite the correct word for what they did here.

You wonder if any of them were like you. You wonder if any of them were not at all.

There is a weight of time in the chamber that has not impressed itself into the usual signs of age: no cracks from freeze-thaw cycles, because there is no water here to freeze and thaw and erode the stone; no lichen or moss stains, because there is no vegetation to take root in the cracks. There is instead a kind of deep resignation of the walls to their own existence, as if the stone has been what it is for so long that it no longer thinks to become anything else.

And yet it is not static. The “frostproof” lithic is far from rock in the familiar sense. It is closer to being a long, unresolved argument of state changes that have agreed to a temporary truce. The walls of the chamber are not permanently solid, but they are not currently anything else, either. They are in the middle of an indefinitely paused transformation between patterns that your mind is not designed to parse.

You might never be able to tell whether this chamber was created deliberately or if it is a flaw in the world’s structure that something later learned to inhabit and modify. You might not be able to tell whether the artifacts that are here now are the original ones or if they have been replaced by processes that live in the interstices of the floor plates.

You know that this is a place where the “tech” parameter of the universe is very high, not because you can see sleek machines humming with power and data, but because you can feel the pressure of conclusions that could not possibly be reached by the physics you know at home. The ceiling pattern above your head is not simply a shape; it is a solution to a set of constraints that you cannot formulate. You can feel its presence as you might feel the presence of a large machine idling nearby, even when you cannot see it.

The dim light that suffuses the chamber from no obvious source is not constant. It withdraws slightly from the surfaces it touches on a schedule that has nothing to do with day or night outside the cliff face and then returns slowly, as though it is being redistributed from somewhere else and not generated from first principles. When you look at the floor plates during one of these withdrawals, you can see more detail in the material of the plates themselves, but the gaps between them become darker than they should, as if they are consuming your attention.

There is no sound in the room itself but the quiet sounds of your own body and the absolute silence of the world beyond it. If you stand very still and stop fidgeting, you might notice that the silence itself is not the same all the way through. There are layers of it: the near silence of your own quiet breathing, the deeper silence of the chamber that has not been disturbed by anything larger than a stray molecule of your atmosphere, and the still deeper silence of the plateau itself, which is not just empty but absent of the possibility of noise in certain directions of reality.

You might, if you stay very long and are very patient, hear the small noise that is not a sound at all but the faint disjunction of one layer of the world’s rules sliding against another layer. This is not a sound that can be measured in hertz or decibels; it is more like a discrepancy in the way you experience being yourself. You will feel it in your teeth before you hear it with your ears.

You do not belong to the same order of existence as the chamber, but the chamber seems willing, for the moment, to tolerate you as long as you do not demand too much of its reality. It allows you to stand inside its equations and watch them being worked out over the surfaces of its floor and walls and ceiling. It allows you to feel the edges of your own being being slightly rewritten by proximity to how it is.

In the end, you might try to touch the thumb-sized ellipsoid at the edge of the frame on the rear wall once more before leaving. You have not touched it since your first attempt at perceiving it. You extend your hand again.

This time, it is not there where your eyes say it is. Your fingertips pass through the space it should occupy and feel only the same slightly resistant cool of the wall behind. You step back, and your eyes are telling you that the ellipsoid is still present, exactly where you remember it being, but the information from your hand is now completely different from what your eyes insist.

You blink slowly, and the world does not snap back into alignment.

For a moment, you feel the edge of the same disagreement that is the substance of this chamber: the lithic that is not quite stone, the waterless chemistry that has found ways to rearrange itself without need for solvent, the dim light that does not originate anywhere but simply lies upon the dustless surfaces of the world.

You are standing inside a contradiction that has been left to run for a very long time, and it has grown patient.

You step back again, and the stone floor plates under your feet feel slightly different than they did before. You cannot decide if they are more solid or less so than they were when you first arrived. You turn toward the entrance slot, where the world beyond the cliff face hangs as a thin line of grey sky in the distance.

You leave because the chamber does not require you to stay, and because you are not sure your body can follow the rules that are being written into this place forever. You step back through the narrow slot, and when you look back at the cliff face, you are not entirely sure where the opening you came through was located.

You walk away along the high, cold plateau that has not changed its appearance in the slightest while you were inside, and you feel the difference in yourself more than in the world around you. Your memory of the chamber is not the same as the memory of other places you have visited. It sits more like a puzzle of shapes that refuses to stay still behind your eyes when you try to reconstruct it.

If you are ever to return to this hidden place, you suspect that you will not find it by searching the cliff face for any obvious break in the stone. You will have to find it by recognising the particular flavour of reality that has not quite decided what it is going to be, and then allowing the gap in the world’s equation to open itself around you once again.

You are not sure whether you will be the same “you” that entered it before.
