**The Weeping Engine**

You stand in what was once the heart of an industrial pumping station, now abandoned to decades of slow surrender. The ceiling curves low overhead—perhaps three meters at its highest point—arching like the ribcage of a buried cathedral. The walls are the same corrugated metal that formed the roof, but time has worked its patient chemistry upon them: rust blooms in orange and umber fractals, and somewhere, water finds its way through.

There is water everywhere, but it is never still.

The floor is a shallow basin of perhaps twenty meters across, pocked with maintenance pits and channels that once directed the flow of machinery. Now they direct the flow of something else. A thin film covers the concrete—uniform enough to seem intentional, but shimmering with an oily iridescence. The film moves with a slow, internal circulation, drawn by forces invisible. It weeps from the walls in sheets that never run dry but never gather into streams. Instead, the water beads up and runs sideways along joints and seams, tracing geometric patterns in the metal before dripping down in long, precise threads.

The air tastes of cool iron and moss. Yet there is no moss here.

In the center of the room, a single engine rises—a black iron monolith of caked and layered grease, fracture lines, and exposed gears. Its pistons are frozen in mid-stroke, but a low tremor runs through the structure, humming in the soles of your feet. The metal is tricked with water in intricate networks—not condensation, but tiny engineered channels worn into the surface. Where they meet, droplets form and hang, trembling, before falling one by one into the basins below.

The ceiling is not silent. At intervals along the beams, dark shapes stir—hundreds of them, each the size of a thumbnail, clustering together in a soft, rustling mat. If you listen, you hear their faint, clicking language: the chitin of their bodies clacking together as they shift position. They are not insects, but something else: six-legged, armored, but with translucent, overlapping plates that pulse with faint internal light. They feed on something invisible—perhaps vibrations, or the faint currents of charge that run through the dripping water—and their clustered glow forms a dim constellation in the gloom.

The walls are layered with residue. A thin crust of minerals grows in featherlike patterns across the metal, like frost on a window pane, but these are not crystals. They are too uniform to be random formation—fanning out from rusted bolts like the spokes of a wheel, each pattern a repeating, fractal geometry. When a stray drip of water falls onto them, they flare momentarily with a soft blue-green phosphorescence, a brief, cold fire that lingers behind your eyelids afterward.

The air is dense with the scent of wet stone and oil. Sound, when you move, slaps briefly against the walls and is swallowed. The metal reverberates somewhere in the low frequencies you feel more than hear. The whole chamber seems to be breathing—a long, slow inhale as the water gathers, and a faint shudder when something drops.

The engine does nothing. It produces nothing. It is a machine of immense scale in this enclosed space, but its purpose is long lost. Its shafts and gears are clogged with a hardening resin that runs from the ceiling in amber-colored stalactites. The resin drips onto the engine in slow, precise tears—not from gravity, but from some other pressure, as though the substance itself is seeking contact. Where resin meets iron, it forms dark, glassy nodules that catch what faint light there is and cast it back in fractured blues and grays.

The chambers beneath the floor grates gurgle occasionally. There is water there too—clear, uncolored—but you cannot see how deep. It drains away from some unseen intake and returns to the ceiling through unseen channels, cycled with a patient, hydrological patience. Gravity, you feel, is not the primary force here. The water climbs.

The fauna of the engine room are not shy. They crawl from cracks in great numbers—armored creatures with translucent limbs and pale, veined wings. They navigate the wet surfaces of the engine with ease, leaving faint trails of slick bioluminescence wherever they pass. These are predators, or perhaps scavengers—you cannot tell which—but they move in carefully orchestrated lines, avoiding the dripping resin and circling the glowing nodules like pilgrims around a shrine. They give no notice of you. Their world is sufficient.

On the far wall, beneath a rusted sign that reads “PRIMARY PUMP A – DO NOT OBSTRUCT,” something grows. It is not alive, and yet it spreads—a dense, dark network of filaments that reach up from the floor and curve across the wall in contoured lines. They resemble veins, or fine circuitry. When the water beading above hits them, they glow faintly, conducting a ghost of current through themselves. The light races along the lines in brief cascades, tracing maps and diagrams that flicker into existence and fade away too quickly to read.

The air is thick, saturated with moisture. The ceiling weeps in curtains and streamers, but never the same place twice. The water chooses its own paths, running along seams in the metal and dropping down in long, precise strands that bisect the gloom into shifting planes of light and shadow.

The light itself is an ambient afterthought—some faint glow from the creatures above, or perhaps leaking in from a distant sky somewhere outside. But it is enough. You see, in the slow accretion of time and moisture, how the room has settled into itself. The rust has carved continents into the walls; the water has carved rivers into the engine; the fauna have carved rituals into the air.

This is not a ruin of neglect. It is an equilibrium.

In the silence, between drips, you feel the pulse of the engine—not as power, but as presence. The room is small, but within its boundaries, everything is dense, saturated, layered and occurring. The water is never done. The engine never finishes. The fauna never stop. But nothing needs to happen. It is already complete.

You stand in the center of a slow, wet engine, turning forever in its own cathedral-scale room. The ceiling weeps. The floor drinks. The walls remember. All around you, the small world is breathing—and in its half-lit, industrial heart, you are the only moving thing that does not know its own place.
