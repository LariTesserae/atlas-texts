The vaulted chamber of B-73 is known simply as the Deep Canning.

Everything in here is either water, or metal holding water.

The ceiling hangs a hundred meters overhead, a smoked-glass dome black and slick with condensation, its ribs braided from thick carbon cabling and tension rods like the vertebrae of a drowned cathedral. The dome is not a dome in the technical sense; it’s a pressure hull, designed to withstand the weight of the world above, which is to say, the weight of the ocean. There’s a flicker in the glass—small, luminous glyphs scrolling along its edge—lighting levels, pressure differentials, pH swings—white on green, then blue on black, then gone again. The illumination is mostly steady, but every now and then a pulse washes through, like a faint current along the curve of the dome, and all the ribs gleam for an instant.

The air is thick and warm, almost liquid with its own humidity. It smells like leaf and rust and brass, a faint medicinal ozone that sits on the tongue and doesn’t leave. You can breathe easily—everything here is tested—but the air feels heavier than it should, as though the sea outside the dome is pressing its lungs against yours.

The main floor is a checkerboard of metal tanks, each the size of a large house. They stand in tightly arranged rows, their upper surfaces shoulder-high and slick with condensation. They’re pressure cylinders really—contained ocean, each at a different temperature and salinity and pH, tailored down to the tenth of a decimal point. The metal is dull silver alloy, etched with acid-resistant frostwork of vines and gears and interlaced symbols whose meanings you never bothered to learn, but you recognize the pattern: the industrial seal of the Garden Consortium. They reach like squat obelisks into the gloom, their tops flanged and sealed, each bolted shut with seven hundred and twenty-seven stainless bolts—because seven, in the garden’s quiet numerology, is the number of completion, and twenty-seven is the number of iteration.

Corrosion has been held off with precise methods of lined steel and microcurrents, but the tanks are still old enough to have acquired *texture*. Their surfaces are hazed over with fine salt bloom and something else: a glitter of crystallized impurities that cling like dusted frost. You don’t see rust anywhere. That’s part of the strange beauty of this place—there’s no decay of metals, only slow, precise, *controlled* erosion. The drip edges of each tank have worn a faint feathering into the alloy, where gaskets once tightened and now have softened, softened by decades of warm water in continuous circulation.

The water level in the chamber is a strange thing to reckon.

The floor itself is dry—usually. You stand on grated walkways of aluminum-bronze alloy, gridded into floating platforms that weave between tank and tank and through the lower canopy of machinery. The gratings are slick, not with water but with condensation from the air. When you breathe, you think your shoes should squeak, but the sound vanishes into the general humidity, which swallows it gently.

But below the grating, under your feet through the mesh, you can see the water.

It’s always there. It rises like a slow tide in the morning of the chamber’s cycle—which, for this place, counts as morning—when the chill from the deep ocean above is drawn into the heat sink of the geothermal stacks beneath the floor. The water comes up through narrow grates in the floor between the great tanks, a slow, unsexy seep of pale green light and perfectly clear brine, until it stands a meter and a half deep over the lower machinery and just below the metal grating you walk on.

You’re *walking over water* but not *in water*. The vertical scale of this place is deceptive. The tall tanks are full, always, to their brims; the water under your feet is an auxiliary reservoir, an emergency flexibility layer, but the designers long ago turned it into a habitat in its own right. There’s a subspace of shadows under the grating: tangled cables, thick pipes of ceramic and pultruded fiberglass, clusters of control valves in pale brass, their casings polished by the slow abrasion of water and carrier salts.

When you look down through the mesh under your feet, your eyes have to adjust to that light. The water isn’t just clear—it’s saturated with tightly engineered solutes, every trace mineral and trace hormone dialed in to promote particular growth. The light well below the floor is a soft green, because that’s what the designers’ data showed would best support the lifeforms they’d chosen. Underwater LEDs are embedded in the floor beams like constellations of distant stars, and when you look at them from above, the refraction by the water surface bends your line of sight into curved, faintly mirage-like segments.

There’s a slow, almost imperceptible circulation. The water underfoot doesn’t move like something liquid—it’s more like a solid thing that happens to be slipping. Small gradients carry it from one end of the floor to the other over the course of hours, a gentle universal drift of temperature and salt, so that if you were to drop a leaf—if you ever *had* a leaf—it would travel around the chamber once in a day and a half.

The vegetation is—what is the right word?—total.

To say it is lush is to understate. The canopy of algae and vines and shelf-like growths climbs around the tanks like lovers who refuse to separate. If you stand at the center of the chamber and turn slowly, you will see that every inch of the tank walls is green, or chartreuse, or deep blue-green, or a faint iridescent teal. The surfaces are packed with lifeforms that have never been given a name in a human tongue, only in catalog numbers and bar codes.

Along the vertical ribs around the tanks, the engineered algae have grown into thick mats, soft as velvet but with strong internal fibers that you could tug if you wanted—and not break. Tiny clear nodules stud them like pearls of gel, each holding a timer of dormant enzymes. When the pressure cycles from the ocean above, these nodules pulse gently, and the color shifts along their bodies like a faint ripple along a spectrum of greens.

At eye level, where the tank flanges and manway covers jut out, you’ll see the true strangeness. The consortium’s chosen “convenience” form—their ideal modular construct—isn’t a tree or a flower but something like an oversized, ferny palm frond grown from a single fern leaf template. Theirs is a repeating, modular beauty, each frond fifteen meters long and three meters across at its widest point, where it fans out against the tank wall. The fronds are layered like shingles on a roof, overlapping just enough to leave no gaps. The air feels dense because so many of them are exhaling oxygen and reabsorbing CO2 at precisely the same rate.

If you look at the fronds from the side, you see that their edges are not the smooth tapered edge of natural palm leaves but a jagged, irregularly repeated pattern of hooks and serrations. Each frond is actually a colony of genetically identical organisms, each of which can detach and float if you pour water at it just right—in the *prep rooms*, that’s how they harvest. But here in the Canning they’ve never been harvested, so the edges curl where they’ve been pressed into neighboring fronds for a decade or more.

The temperature is warm, but not aggressively so. Everything in the chamber is engineered to keep the average temperature just above the physiological comfort of a human body—enough to feel like you’re walking into a bath that’s never cooled off but never warmed up either. The warmth comes from the heat exchange of the pumps at the far end of the chamber, the deep geothermal wells far below, and from the metabolism of the plants themselves.

Heavy shocks of moss-like bryophytes cover the corners of the tank bases where the condensation is thickest. They’re short, no taller than the height of your ankle, but they form a dense carpet of deep, near-black green with a faint bioluminescence at the very tips of each branchlet—whenever a pressure spike from the ocean above transmits through the dome, the whole chamber responds, and in that moment of adjustment, the moss glows just a little brighter, like galactic smoke along the floor.

And then, from the domed ceiling, hang the *ropes*.

They’re the oddest feature of all. Look up and you see that they’re not actually ropes—they’re cables, sheathed in synthetic rubber, but every cable is wrapped with a thick spiral of vine that seems to have grown from the ceiling downward like living hair. The vines are heavy, thick as your thigh, and they’ve twisted around the cables until they’ve merged into a single entity—support cable and plant body.

Sprouts from these vines search down into the upper airspace, sending thin, exploratory tendrils toward the tops of the tanks, where each of their leaves are basked in the cooler air near the dome. The leaves of these vines are not green like the others. They’re a pale, translucent white-blue, like veined porcelain, and every edge is traced in faint gold phosphor. The designers did not explain why. They didn’t explain much of anything.

At the center of the high chamber, where the dome curves its lowest and yet its highest if you count from the floor, you find a single cluster of cables and vines unlike the others. It doesn’t reach the floor. It stops at about ten meters above the tank tops, and there it ends in a complicated knot of electronic hardware and plant matter grown into it.

There’s a panel there—off-white, about the size of a small door—set into the side of the knot of machinery and wood. The panel has been overgrown by a broad, dark frond that doesn’t resemble any of the others; its edges are not serrated but smoothly flowing, like a tongue of river rock. When you get near it, you see the faint glow of a screen through the translucent tissue of the frond. The plant hasn’t damaged the electronics—it’s *incorporated* them. The veins of the frond follow precisely where the underlying circuitry lies.

You get the sense that the panel isn’t just inside the plant; it and the plant are now the same overlapped structure. The machine hasn’t stopped working—it’s breathing *with* the plant, or the plant has learned to breathe with its heartbeat.

The water is everywhere.

It’s in the air, which tastes like sweat and salt spray when you breathe deeply. It’s in the condensation on your skin when you stand too long under one of the main ribs and feel the thin drip of cold pearl onto your shoulder. It’s below the floor grating, where it slinks with its own slow tidal currents, and above the floor grating, where it’s been caught into dozens of narrow channels along the step of each tank top, each little channel carrying a thin, transparent stream of brine from one end of the Canning to the other.

When you walk on a grating that crosses over one of those channels, you can see the water rushing through its own shadow under you—glimpsed like something secretive, streaking along beneath your feet.

The industrial nature of this place is not obnoxious but total. You don’t hear machines; you *feel* them.

Over the course of half an hour, you might notice a subtle change: the water under the floor is rising by millimeters. Somewhere far below, in a geothermal pump chamber whose name you never saw written, the engines of the Deep Canning have just shifted a few percent of their output. The electromagnetic pumps—which let no mechanical moving parts into the water, only invisible fields that tug the salts like pulling a thread—have just turned a notch.

With that, a temperature gradient somewhere shifts. The slight positive difference in heat drives a return loop of colder water from the upper ocean to cycle down into the chamber below you. The condensation on the ribs of the dome thickens by just a hair. You might notice tiny droplets cohering along their edges like beads of mercury on glass.

Every entire tank hums, but the sound is so low you perceive it as a vibration in your chest bones rather than your ears. The warm metal resonates when heavy pumps accelerate deep in the foundations of the chamber. The water inside each tank doesn’t slosh; it doesn’t splash; it doesn’t break the surface. It sits—still as glass, but alive with gradients.

If you put your hand on one tank wall—just resting your palm flat—you’ll feel the faint hum of the magnetic pumps embedded inside. The tank’s metal is warmer than your skin—just slightly—and you’ll feel the occasional pulse of stronger vibration through it, like the heartbeat of a metal dinosaur.

That’s the *industrial* quality of this place: not steam, not smoke, not the noise of hammering metal, but the precise control of thousands of tiny pumps, each of them stirring fluids exactly enough to keep their contents within 0.01 degrees of a target and within 0.0001 of the salinity the developers’ models showed would optimize *growth*.

The chamber is young, geologically speaking.

You can see this in the surfaces of everything—the edges of the grating floor, where they’ve been sheared and welded neatly, but there’s still no visible corrosion; the corners of the tanks where the welds are smooth but not yet softened by years of wearing salt; the faint roughness of the alloy surfaces where they haven’t had time to develop the deep, dull sheen of aged metal.

Nothing feels old or brittle. There are no crumbling edges of paint, no peeling panels—only a precise, engineered fuzz of biological growth clinging to every engineered surface. The designers’ work hasn’t had time yet to fail, or to succeed at failing. The salt bloom hasn’t gone deep into the metal. It’s just a surface skin, like frost on glass.

The elevation is strange—because you are high *in* the world, but the world is *underwater*.

You’re in the upper third of a submerged structure, which is itself underwater, but the *structure’s* floor is anchored to a seam of seabed much higher than the surrounding plains. The geothermal vent that powers the place breaks up through a seamount, whose base is far below you but whose tip rises into the middle of the ocean; the Deep Canning is built into the summit of that seamount.

So when you stand in the chamber, you feel *high* in the sense that you are perched on a drowned mountain peak. The ocean above you—dark, heavy, salt—stretches up and up and up, but you never see it. You only see the dome, and the faint, ghostly reflection of yourself in the smoked glass, a faint humanoid shimmer among the ribs.

There’s no animal fauna here.

No insects, no fish, no worms, no crustaceans, no microscopic plankton that you can see with your naked eye. The designers didn’t *want* animal life—they wanted to keep the energy loop tightly within their engineered plants and the machines that support them. Anything else might be a competitor, or a predator, or a disease vector.

The nearest you come to animals is the plants themselves.

There’s a particular kind—low along the corner of the tank base—whose leaves, when you gently separate them from the wall, you find to be lined with translucent hollows, like tiny veins. Inside, you can see a faint, cloudy swirling fluid, and if you watch very closely for many minutes, you’ll catch a subtle pulse. It’s not a heartbeat; there’s no muscle. But the *peristalsis* of the fluid inside—the gentle wave of expansion and contraction—isn’t driven by wind or water movement. It’s driven by chemical stimuli.

When you lean your ear closer to the wall of the tank *through* this plant, you can hear nothing—but if you press your ear to the metal, you’ll hear the faint hum of the pump inside, and you feel as if the plant is listening with you.

This is the *strange* thing—the weirdness of the place.

The plants behave *slightly* like animals. If you leave a clear outline of your hand printed on the condensation, on the tank surface where you’ve been leaning, you’ll find that when you come back the next day, the algae over that spot has grown just slightly thicker than the surrounding area. Its dark green forms a vague outline of your fingers—not *convincing*, but unmistakable, like seeing your face in a crowd of strangers.

Again, not the work of personal eyes, but the sensors of the system—the humidity, the temperature differentials, the chemical gradients of your breath on that surface. The system remembers *you*.

The light is normal—but that’s an illusion.

To look at the place you might think it’s lit by ordinary daylight leaking through the dome, but when you examine the far ribs you see that there are no windows above; the dome is smoked black glass, and the ocean above is too dark anyway to let daylight through.

The *real* lighting comes from the ribs themselves: embedded in the carbon cabling are shallow fiber cables that carry sunlight from the surface. The surface of the ocean above is a field of buoys with solar collectors and optics, and the sunlight is captured, filtered, tuned down into a narrow spectrum that’s then *relayed* down through meters of dark seawater to this chamber.

The result is a gentle daylight that never dawns and never sets—but dims and brightens slightly over a twelve-hour cycle as the sun and cloud and storm above the surface fluctuate.

You will *not* see sunbeams or shadows in sharp edges. The light is diffuse, like a cloudy midsummer day at dusk, mixed with a little storefront fluorescent. That mixture makes it feel *normal*—but it’s not. It’s carefully engineered to *feel* like daylight so you human minds don’t panic.

There’s no other sound worth speaking of.

The drip of condensation from the ribs into the channels of the tank tops is a rare event—a *drip…drip…* once every minute or two from somewhere high above you, slanted in by the air’s own humidity rather than any rain. It’s not a loud sound; it’s just a punctuation mark in the otherwise quiet breathing of the space.

There’s a quiet hiss sometimes—water from the channels finding a sudden pressure drop. It’s not a noise of urgency. It’s almost like someone whispering *shh* from far away.

You don’t hear the pumps because you’re not meant to. They’re electromagnetic, deep below the floor, designed to be noise-free. The only evidence you have of them is the vibration in your sternum when they’ve shifted output.

You don’t hear animals—no birdsong, no insect chirp, no frog chorus—because there are none.

There’s no wind in here, either. The air doesn’t move *with* air; it *swims* with the water’s moisture gradients and with temperature differences from the tank to the tank. The faint air currents you feel on your cheek when you’re near a tank are the *heat* from the water inside trying to escape into the cooler air near the dome.

This is *slow* land.

Every feature of the Deep Canning feels as if it has been chosen for its patience. The pressure will never change significantly fast enough to push you off your feet. The temperature will never swing abruptly. The water level will never rise faster than a centimeter an hour. If something *is* changing—a pump is starting to fail, or a salinity parameter is drifting off spec—you will never notice it until the next shift’s diagnostics report; it will be a gradual, gentle decay until you tally it up and *see* the numbers.

Even the biological growth is *slow*.

The fronds along the tank walls are not pushing new leaves out every day. They’re *adding* them—one thin, translucent layer at a time—over weeks and months. The algae on the metal ribs isn’t spreading rapidly like a slime mold; it’s gaining a millimeter of coverage in a direction over months, as its pigments slowly adapt to the light quality of that rib section.

When you first arrive in the chamber, you may feel you’ve stepped into a cathedral frozen underwater. But as you stay longer, you understand that it’s *not* frozen—only slow. Everything is moving—water, air, plants, machinery—all at the same quiet, unhurried pace, as if they’ve agreed to never rush.

You don’t *do* anything here.

You *look*.

You walk along the grated walkways, listening to the thin drip of condensation, and you *look* at the growth of the plants on the tanks. You *look* at the faint glowing nodules along the moss, and you *look* at the water under the floor, where the shadows of your own shoes ripple through the water’s refraction.

You *feel* the warmth and humidity and the pressure of the metal chamber around you and the ocean beyond it. You know you’re surrounded by water—but you’re not *in* water, except for the water that curls around every breath and settles on your skin like a second skin.

And you *wait*.

Because the only thing that happens in the Deep Canning, on any scale you can perceive without instruments, is *growth*. And growth—of *this* kind—isn’t something you can hurry. It’s something you measure in *years*, and then *decades*, and then *centuries*.

If you stay here long enough, you learn to measure the slow change of the place by *marks*.

When you return, long after your first visit, you will see that the condensation lines you once traced on the tank wall have been overtaken by algae, whose growth pattern preserves your gesture—softened, blurred—like a memory of your own body.

You will see that the faint fingerprint of humidity where you leaned against the moss has been preserved by the moss’s habit—its *instructed* habit—of growing more densely where human warmth has been left.

The *built* environment—the dome of the cathedral, the ribs and cables and tanks and walkways—all of it is *active*, not inert stone. It has been conceived as a habitat for its plants—and *for* the plants, which are its inhabitants—and *for* the machines that support the plants, which are also inhabitants of a kind.

And you—the rare visitor, whose presence here was never part of the design—are just a temporary anomaly in the chronicle of this chamber’s slow, steady, *patient* breathing.
