In this place, the boundary is not clear.

The air is not quite air. It smells like a cold mechanic’s shop after rain: rust, oil, and wet concrete. You breathe and it feels heavy in your lungs, not with heat but with moisture clinging to everything. The sky is a low, unlit ceiling of dripping cloud, not storm but steady dampness, like the inside of a warehouse that has never been fully closed.

The ground under you is not really ground, but the floor of a vast machine that has fallen asleep.

The chamber is huge—even though you get the sense of being hidden, in a pocket within the industrial world—this room is cavernous. It is not a natural cavern; it is a built thing that has been turned over to the outside again. The ceiling drops cables of metal and wire, like vines of steel and copper, some of them still humming faintly with current. Others are broken and hang, ending in blackened tips where sparks have long ago eaten themselves.

Everywhere there is water.

It is in the air, condensing a thin coat of grease on the surfaces that you see but not quite feel. You can feel it on your face. It gathers on the edges of the machinery that stills itself around you: on the arms of cranes that have forgotten to move, on the sides of great engines that sleep like sleeping animals—giant, cold beasts of iron and steel. Each surface is a maze of rust and condensation, beads of dew running in lines down the painted metal and over the black, crusted corrosion.

The ground is not flat; you are not standing high up but low—like the bottom of a basin that has not been drained for a long time. The floor is a grid of metal plates, some of them removed to reveal the darkness below. You can feel the hollowness under your feet: the sense of emptiness between presences, where one misstep could be a fall into a space that has never known light.

The world is not empty, but each object in it is separated by a vast distance. The nearest machine—a blocky engine wrapped with pipes and segmented valves—sits fifty meters away, like a mountain in a small valley. Beyond it, more of them: dim shapes in the mist, all of them still and not yet worn to rubble by erosion. They are young in the way of factories that have been made but not yet broken down. The corners are crisp, the edges still defined. The rust is not the deep, eaten-through rust of age; it is a thin, scabbing rust, like the first gray of morning not yet sun.

Through the voids of this floor, water moves.

You cannot see all of it, but you can hear it. The sound is not the sound of a river or a stream, but of water falling through many levels of metal: clanging, spattering, echoing. The sound is loud and constant, like being inside a machine that is breathing. You hear the drip of condensation from the ceiling, the sudden slap of a larger drop hitting the floor of the next chamber down, and farther below, the muffled roar of water in motion somewhere else.

This is not a quiet place.

Distant doors drag open and then shut with a sound like metal teeth being knocked together. The wind of some other corridor rushes through and you can feel the pressure of it on your skin, not warm but cold enough to make your hair stand on end. Then there is the closer hum of a still-working pump somewhere in the dim, a low vibration that gets into your bones. You feel the thrum as if you are standing inside a machine, not outside of it.

The vegetation that is here is strange and not entirely like plants.

From the gaps between metal floor plates, things grow. They are not green as you are used to green, but more like a darker green-blue-black, the color of oil spilled on water or algae on a very old engine. You see the shapes of them: long stems like cables of organic fiber, snaking from the broken joints of the machine and dipping into the water flowing below the floor. You imagine the water that is there is deep and secret, not in a small way but hidden in the under of this world, where the supports of the factory floor plug into the stone of the earth like pillars into the ocean of another world.

The plants are lush without being trees. They do not reach the ceiling of the machine-chamber, but they are thick enough to be a small forest of angles and thin stems, each one of them covered in tiny, hard leaves like the sculpted teeth of a machine. You see the way the water has beaded on one of the leaves: not as clear dew but as something that has a thin coat of a slick substance on it, clinging to the leaf like the sweat of a machine.

Movement is everywhere in the small things.

The fauna of this place is not obvious at first, because the light is not good. Light is not gone from the world, but dim, like a bad transfusion of color into the air. The ceiling is not sky; it is metal, and where there once were lights, there are now only some of them left, broken floods of yellow that flicker. The rest of the world is made of shadow and the sheen of water on the surfaces.

When you look at the ground, you think the floor is moving, and then you realize that it is. The animals are not small in the way of insects in a meadow, but small in the way of fragments of a larger being broken into pieces. Each creature is a separate presence, but there are many of them.

You see low things that scuttle from one edge of a broken floor plate to another, their shells hard black like polished engine parts. Another crawls from the water bubbling up in a rust hole, and you are not sure if it is more like a metal shell or a shell that has taken metal as its inspiration. Its legs tap on the plate, and the sound reminds you of the sound of a machine starting itself up.

Above you, in the joints of a crane arm that hangs still, there are more creatures of the air: not birds but wings of a thin, membrane-like material, stretched taut like fabric. They are smoke-colored and they move, not in the complicated patterns of flight but in the direct, violent way of something being thrown. The air of the chamber is not empty; it is cut with the motion of these things.

The place is not still in one way, but it is not in any hurry.

This is a cold world. The water is cold enough to make your teeth ache if you think about it. You can feel the cold in the handles of the ladder on the side of the nearest engine machine, where your hand might not even want to rest if not for the sense of having to hold on. The cold is not a winter cold; it is a cold like the inside of a refrigerator that has never been turned off. The heat of the world is not here; the heat has been turned into action somewhere else, in the motion of the water, the hum of some distant generator.

You are in the hidden pocket of the world, not in the open of its factory floors but in the quiet of a machine that has stopped to listen for the next command. The technology of this place’s makers is not around you in obvious form, but it is in the bones of the world. You are not anywhere near the outside of this world; you are in the middle of it, in the small empty of its body.

The world of the outside must be vast, but you are in the strange of it: a small chamber of a machine that has had water leak into it for years and has not yet been fixed. The odd of it is in the way that the world has not come to the end of its making, but not started to unmake itself in a strong way. Erosion has not had time to work its teeth into the metal and the rust.

As you breathe, the sound that you hear is not of your own breath but of the breath of the machine.

This landscape is not made of mountains and trees and sky. It is made of water moving through metal, of cold wet air, and of the dense of sound in the hidden. It is lush with the wrong sort of vegetation, crowded with the wrong sort of animals, and not built in the way you are built to expect but built in the way of a world that machine of production.

You are at the bottom of a low place of a great machine that has water running through its body, and it is alive and not at all in the way you are, but you can feel its life in the motion of the water beneath your feet and the sound of the bark of some unknown thing in the distance, like metal being cut into pieces by metal teeth.
