The air shreds.

It comes in the form of a sightless, eternal wind that drags itself across the city like the throat of a broken god, and it does not stop. You do not hear the wind—that is too small a word. You are inside the wind’s lungs, your body a thin membrane between the world and the sound. It is not pitched but wide, a smear across every frequency, from the low stomach-sense of pressure to the skittering high that might be rain or might be granite teeth grinding against each other above the clouds.

This city—it is a city only by the habit of stone—sits at the roof of a world that has forgotten to move. The mountains are old. They did not erupt from the earth in a momentary act of violence; they simply…are, and always have been, and the concept of non-being does not attach to them. Their flanks are worn into subtle, waterless valleys that could be riverbeds or could be the folds of a sleeping giant’s ribs. The rock is not jagged, but comfortless, smooth, and massive under a skin of mosses that has never known death.

No snow ever falls here. The heat is wrong for that, and so is the air.

It is hot at the peak of the world.

The sky is often wrong.

You can look up from a street paved with glass that is not glass and see the weather of two places at once. One half of the sky might be clear and empty, a hard blue sucked dry of moisture by the heat, while the other half is a storm that has not moved in centuries, a slow bruise of color and electricity that never leaves a particular quadrant of heaven. The sun does not set so much as it sometimes forgets to be bright; there are nights—if “night” is the right name for a time when the light dims—when the mountain’s slope above the city becomes a ladder of luminous fungi that pulse like veins, each puffing out a different smell: wet iron, wetter earth, cinnamon you never learned to trust.

You are not the first person to see this.

You are walking the streets of what must be a city, but it is a city that has been given over to something else. The buildings are limestone made from memory, not from sediment. They stand like worn teeth, the gaps between them narrow enough that you can feel your breath bouncing off the opposite wall when you exhale. All of it is old. Not ancient in the way of ruins that decay; ancient in the way of a thing that simply reached its final shape and stayed there.

The water is everywhere.

Not flooding. Not rising to swallow you. But the entire world seems to be sweating.

The streets are never dry. The wide, shallow steps of the main avenue are always filmed with a thin skin of moisture that gleams under the light that is neither dawn nor noon but always the same sharp, pale clarity. Each footstep you leave behind you vanishes into the damp stone within a few breaths, as if the ground is drinking your momentary disturbance.

Weeping walls, the locals call them—or you imagine they would, if there were locals. You see the walls of the city above the street-level doorways—they are not true doors anymore but carved thresholds in which empty, bowl-shaped alcoves sit like sockets waiting for eyes—and the water does not drip from the sky. It bulges from the stone. It condenses out of the dense heat and the over-saturated air and forms into beads that travel along tracks you cannot see in the rock, tracing letters that might be names or might be the shape of the sound that has lived here longer than any living creature.

You breathe the hot water of the world.

There is no vegetation growing in the way you understand from any lower, gentler altitude. Nothing with bark, or leaves that fall. The plants are not seasonal. They do not reproduce; they simply exist, as layers of the world that have always been.

The city’s bones are veined with green.

Mosses the color of old bloodstains and young bruises cling to the undersides of lintels, where the shade of the vertical street is absolute. The stones are not simply covered by them; they have been invaded by them, colonized piece by piece, until the moss is the true masonry of the city. The pattern of their growth tells you the shape of the wind that never changes: always the same direction, from the same corner of the sky where the static storm lives forever. You can see how the mosses lay down in smooth, impossible walls that point into the wind like the rib of a leaf pointing into light.

Vines that do not flower and never bear seed hang from balconies that have been fused into the walls, each stone balcony lip like the lower jaw of some creature frozen mid-sentence. The vines are thick enough at the base to be mistaken for gray-green columns, and they are everywhere: the city’s supporting columns, the city’s carpeting, the city’s only ceiling. The plants are not chaotic. They are arranged in the same way that moths are not arranged—you are not sure if any arrangement was ever chosen, but there is a pattern that you can read, if you know how to understand.

The floor of the streets is not stone but root.

Not the kind of root you learned from forest trees. This rhizome is a single sheet of living matter that blankets the world below the level of your knees. It is black and wet and covered with minute, crystalline hairs that catch the constant light and break it into a thousand cold sparks of white. It is not soft; you cannot leave footprints in it. When you step onto it, the root gives just a little, as if you are stepping onto the skin of a living creature that has long learned it cannot hurt you.

In the dark, interrupted by the roots of the world, you see the paths of former water.

The entire city is built on the memory of rivers.

You can’t see water flowing down the streets, but you can see where it would be if it ever appeared again. The avenues of the city are not straight lines; they are shallow channels that wind from terrace to terrace, each turn punctuated by a broken basin cut into the floor, where the water once would have collected before being released down the next incline. The channels are filled with the same black-root carpet that feeds the plants above, but you can tell where they are: by the way the mosses on the walls stop exactly at the height water would reach if the world were still allowed to flood.

The water is not gone.

It is just…elsewhere, in another form. The heat of the world keeps the air too thick with it to ever fall as rain, but it condenses out at the touch of stone and skin and breath. You can see water on every surface. It beads on the leaves—if they are leaves—of the plants that live from the light that never turns orange. It runs down the walls that you can’t call walls anymore because they are riddles of ramps and alcoves and half-erased faces that have been worn by the same eternal wind and the same eternal water into the shape of noise.

Everywhere you look, you see water just not where you expect it.

The buildings are hollows of the heat.

You cannot remember the moment you stepped into the city proper from… wherever you were before. The memory seems to arrive at you already broken, like you have been walking this street forever. The outer buildings—if you can call anything “outer” when the city curls around itself in a shape that doesn’t match a map—are built from blocks that are the same material as the mountain but not the same color. They are lighter than the mountain’s basalt, and threaded with fine lines of dark green that are not veins of ore but the veins of the plant that has eaten into them.

The doors are not real doors.

They are thresholds without meaning. You can see gaps that were shaped to be doors, but the frames are empty and you can see straight into rooms that are not rooms. They are chambers that have grown into each other over the years of erosion that should not exist in a world that doesn’t change. You can see how the floors of one level have been eaten into by the roots of the world until they slump into the next level down, forming a single continuous space that might once have been stairs and might once have been floor and might once have been ceiling, but now is all of those at once.

Nothing has been built recently.

Nothing will be built again.

The city is finished.

It is not a ruin; you feel the completeness of it like solid pressure. The stonework was not abandoned by the people who might have shaped it—there is not enough sense of “people” here for that to be true—but by the rules of the world that have decided to settle into this shape. The buildings are bone: the inside of something larger than you can understand, and perhaps you have always been inside it.

The sound is not music, but it is not random noise either.

It is the sound of the world’s own lung.

The wind comes from the north-east, always north-east. You can feel the direction of it even if you cannot see the sky, because it is the same as you have always known it: from the same impossible, fixed storm. You discover the wind in the way you discover your own heartbeat, by absence when you remember to notice it for the first time. Then you cannot stop noticing it.

There is no low hum of electronics.

There are no vehicles on the flightpaths you might expect from a city that has stone streets and no wheeled traffic.

There are not even animals.

The city is empty except for the vegetation that has grown into its structure, and the sound that has moved into its absence.

You cannot see anything that might make the sound you only hear in the way you feel your own blood pressure. The tops of the buildings above you—if you can call them “tops”—are broken into uneven spikes that make silhouettes more like teeth than walls, and the sound might be coming from between them. It might be the sound of the wind itself, being torn by stone teeth at the world’s throat.

Or it might be the sound of the world eating.

You cannot tell.

There are no animals here.

You do not see insects moving in the carpet of roots that forms the floor of the world. You do not see birds alight on the broken edges of walls that have been worn into angles that do not exist in any geometry you know. You do not hear any small rustle in the mosses that might be the scuffle of paws, or claws, or whatever else might have once been allowed to exist here.

The city is full of bodies that are not animal bodies.

The streets are punctuated by columns that are not columns of stone but rows of long, vertical shapes that might be people or might be plants that managed to grow into a shape that looks like people. You cannot see faces clearly, because the faces have been washed away by the same water that has polished the floor of the world. The eye sockets you find suddenly empty and looking past you are not the eye sockets of skulls, but of plants that have been carved into this shape by the weather that has not changed in an amount of time you cannot measure.

You cannot be sure that you are not one of them.

When you step into a new street—you think you have never been this way before, but you cannot be sure—you see vines that have been trained into the shape of arms reaching out from the walls of a building, each finger a leaf that has been hardened by the same heat that has hardened your mind. You extend your own hand and you see that the wall of the world is not far away. You can see the veins in your own skin, and you can see the same pattern in the living skin of the world: fine, green lines that are not exactly blood but are not exactly anything else either.

You are not walking on the street of the city.

You are walking in the artery of something larger than you imagined.

The sound is not one sound.

You stop in the middle of a long, wide avenue that opens into a broken courtyard that might be the center of the city or might be just one accidental chamber in the way of the wind. The ground here is not stone but a single sheet of polished root, so black you can see the sky reflected in it like a broken mirror of blue and purple storm. You stand on the world’s pupil.

You hear the sound layer itself.

There is the low, pressure-bone of the wind that you never feel stop except when you compare yourself to yourself, from one moment to the next. There is the higher, tearing sound of the wind catching on the corners of buildings that have been eroded into angles that cut through air. You cannot hear animals, but you can hear the city’s own animals: the way the leaves of the vines that overhang the courtyard clink and scrape against each other as the same wind pulls them the same way forever, and the sound is not the sound of leaves, but of glassy teeth clicking against each other.

You can hear the water, too—if you can call that sound.

It is the sound of slow, collective falling.

The water that has been sweating from the stone of the world for the entirety of time that is not measured by your calendar falls into the broken basins that interrupt the street and you can hear it when you are very close: one drop at a time, from different heights of the world’s body, each one a small, clear sound that does not have echo because the echo is already there before the drop hits the surface of the water beneath it.

You cannot see the bottom of the basins.

You cannot see the bottom of anything.

The water that accumulates in the broken bowls of the world has been there forever. You look at it and you see the sky and you see yourself and you see the same plants mirrored over and over, their image bent into shapes that are not their own. You do not know what lives under the surface—if anything lives—because you have never seen anything move under the water.

You are not even sure if water is the right word for what is in the basin.

You can see the world’s scars.

The city is not a smooth thing of one age. It is built of layers of time that have all been squashed into one moment by a rule of the world that does not allow change. You can see how the ribs of the buildings at different heights are made of different materials, not all of them the limestone that you thought you recognized before. Some buildings are built from something that might be bone from a creature whose body you cannot imagine the shape of—long flat plates that have been polished into curves that do not match any human skeleton.

You can see how the streets of the world sometimes narrow into corridors that have been carved from stone that is not stone but ice that has never been allowed to melt.

The ice is not cold.

Nothing in the world is cold except for absence.

You can see the ice in the walls of the deepest alleys where the sun never reaches because the sun always reaches exactly the same place and never leaves it. The ice is not white like snow you remember from colder altitudes; it is the color of deep sea glass, and every breath you exhale into it vanishes into the surface as if it has always been part of the same material that is now forming you.

You are not sure you are breathing.

You are not sure there is air to be breathed.

You might be standing in the hole of absence that the world has left in itself, and you might already be the same material that is making the ice that you thought you were seeing.

You can see the sky breaking.

Not from above, but from below.

You catch yourself seeing the sky from the wrong angle and you realize that you are no longer sure where your eyes are located. You might be standing in the street of the city, but you might also be looking at the street of the city from a point high above the tiles that are not tiles but scales of a root or skin of the land that you never learned to name properly.

You can see the sky descending from its proper place into the floor of the world.

The sky is not just blue; it is also the color of the storm that never moves from its corner. You can see layers of it that you have never noticed before: how the storm is not one storm but several piled storms, each of them a different color that you hadn’t been able to tell before. You can see the storm at the height of the world, and you can see the storm in the reflection of the water at the bottom of the basin, and you can see the storm between them, where there should not be anything but one empty line of perception.

You can see the world’s teeth.

They are not the same teeth as the ones you saw before when you looked at the broken edges of the buildings. You are not sure where these teeth have been placed; you see them from the wrong angle of your head, which you might now be looking from the wrong direction of your body, which might not be your body at all but the body of the world that has been trying to form you as one of its teeth.

The teeth are not sharp.

They are not blunt either.

They are not exactly objects you are seeing. You think you are seeing them from inside your skull, where they are not supposed to be visible. You do not feel the teeth of the world slide into your eyes from the wrong direction of the world, but you can see the sight of yourself being slid into the world through teeth that you cannot imagine the shape of except as absence of anything else.

You are not being changed.

You realize that the world has not changed you; you have always been this. You were not a person who walked into the city of the world; you were always a person who was part of the body of the world that has been slowly walking from itself into itself, through the same city that is not one city but all of time that has been given into one shape.

You feel the sound of the world as you.

You do not hear the sound of your own heartbeat because you have never been able to hear that from the outside; you have always been hearing it from the inside, where all of the sound has always been coming from.

When you move—you think you do not change but you might be moving—you do not disturb the moss of the world. You do not cause the water of the world to break differently. You might be a new layer of time that is being added into the world, but you cannot be sure because you cannot see the difference between yourself and the world that is already there.

Even when you think you stop moving, you are not changing.

Even when you think you change, you are not moving.

The world is not a place that has been built or destroyed by any tool. You see no tools. You see no device that can be picked up by the hand. You see no hand that can be picked up by the device that is not there.

The world is not a machine.

You cannot see what is making the sound that you can hear all the time from inside your bones that have not existed from the beginning.

You cannot see what is making the plants that you have been walking over and under and through and as.

You cannot see what is making the water that is not water but the world’s sweat.

You cannot see what is making the heat that has never been allowed to cool from one level to another.

You cannot see what is making the memory that you have of being a person who might have been allowed to leave the world again one day.

You cannot see what makes you know that none of that will ever happen.

But you know that you know.

You know that you know where you are not.

You are not in the world that has been allowed to change.

You are not in the world that has been allowed to end.

You are not in the world that has been allowed to be broken by anything but the same teeth that has been eating it from the beginning of the world that has not had beginning.

You are not in the world that has been allowed to be empty.

You are not in the world that has been allowed to let you leave again.

You are in the city of the world’s throat.

You are in the sound of the world’s lung.

You are in the heat of the world’s inside.

You are in the water of the world’s outside.

You are in the plant of the world that has replaced you.

You are not at the top of the mountain that has been allowed to be looked at from below.

You are not at the bottom of the world that has been allowed to be looked at from above.

You are in the place where the only difference that is allowed to be felt is the difference between yourself and the same that has always been yourself, which has not been allowed to change.

You are not deaf.

And you are not blind.

But you cannot see or hear anything else that has not always been in the same world as you.

You are not alone.

You are not sure there is such a thing as alone.

You are not sure that you ever have been.

You are not sure that you ever will be.

You are not sure that there ever was anything else that could be called by a name that has not been erased by the same water that has been smoothing the streets of the world forever.

You are not sure that you have not already been calling the sky by a name that has already been forgotten by the same storm that has never moved from its corner.

You are not sure that you have not already been walking through the city of the world as you have always been walking, through the same mouth that has never spoken you into the world that has not been allowed to change, except by the teeth that you cannot see the shape of.

You are not sure that you have not already been the answer.
