The world is a single vaulted chamber at the top of the factory-city, where the roof meets the blind night sky.

---

### Space and structure

You can’t see the ceiling. You’re standing inside a huge cylindrical tower that feels like a cathedral, but everything is metal: dark, wet steel plating on the walls and floor, ribs of riveted beamwork curving up into darkness overhead. The cylinder is broad enough that a neighborhood of low houses could fit inside its footprint, yet you feel no sense of openness: the space is dense, *saturated* with things.

Iron gantries ring the walls at three or four levels you can see, and more likely beyond that in the dim heights. Each gantry is packed with machinery, piping, ducts, and racks—so tightly that only narrow service corridors remain between them. The floor is covered in a dense grid of low, housing-like structures: identical metal boxes no more than two meters tall, stacked and welded together until they form a single continuous surface, like a honeycomb made of rooms. The room is the entire world.

Water is everywhere but not wild.

---

### Water: wetness held inside metal

The warmth of the city’s factories below, rising through countless shafts and vents, hits the cold roof tiles of this chamber and condenses instantly. The result is that everything is dripping, but in a contained way, as if the air itself were quietly bleeding.

Beads of water gather on the curved ribs and girders overhead. You can hear them dripping in a mournful, irregular rhythm: *plink… plink-plink… plink…* Each drop strikes bare metal or the surface of some machine, and is then guided away by thin runnels and channels welded into the floor. The metal floor, faintly sloping toward the edges of the chamber, collects every droplet into a network of shallow, precisely engineered gutters that run between the metal housing blocks.

This condensation is not heavy rain, but persistent enough that over days and weeks it has formed a film of moisture on every surface. You walk on carefully; not because there’s flooding or mud, but because your boots might slip on the slick sheen of water on the floor plating.

In the corners of the chamber, where the ribs of the tower meet the floor, there are small, low basins of welded steel—unlike the rest of the room’s straight-lined severity, they are shaped like flowers or trumpets, their rims gently flared. They gather condensation as well, but also serve as drinking spots for the creatures. You can see the water inside them trembling faintly with the vibration from the distant engines below.

Water is a steady, almost patient presence: not enough to wash away anything or dramatically change the world, but enough to create a constant atmosphere of weeping metal.

---

### Cold and altitude

This chamber sits at the very top of the city, where the outer shell of the tower is exposed to the night sky’s frigidity. The metal walls transmit cold inward; you feel it immediately as a low-level ache in your skin and a faint sting of condensation inside your nostrils when you inhale too deeply.

The cold is not the brutal, striking wind of a mountaintop storm; more like an altitude where you feel the latent pressure of the higher atmosphere outside but remain insulated by the hull. You stand at the top of a man-made peak, but everything is enclosed. The altitude feels less like exposure and more like a thin, constant tension—the slight pressure difference between this chamber and the lower levels, a faint tightness in your ears.

On the other side of the metal walls, beyond the thick hull plating, you know that there is a thin, freezing atmosphere outside—maybe a thin, high-altitude marsh of some kind, or a cold polar sky that would solidify your breath if you were out there. But you’re not out there, and these walls don’t change. The chamber maintains its own cold equilibrium.

---

### Vegetation: almost absent

On any exposed surface that might have been bare earth in a non-made world, there is nothing green. No soil, no moss “taking” on the metal walls, no seedlings in the condensate gutters. The designers of this place presumably wanted no growth, no interference from biology.

Still, if you look very closely at certain corners where the condensation collects more steadily than elsewhere—inside the lip of a drinking basin, or a tiny mis-weld gap where riveted plates overlap—you may see a faint, pale crust of something like lichen or algae. It doesn’t look alive enough to be vegetation in the ordinary sense, more like a chalky mineral stain that has learned to be slightly biological.

This is the world’s only vegetation, and it barely registers.

---

### Erosion and time: almost none

There is only one kind of erosion here: the faint, slow micro-corrosion of all that moisture.

Wherever water has been trickling along the same path for decades, there has formed a thin, dark line of rust stain along the channel floor. It’s no more than a shadow—a discoloration, not a deep groove. The metal does not scar visibly; the rust is just a thin film of reddish-brown on the steel surface. It looks like the inkstroke of a very slow pen drawing the same line over and over.

Other than this, there is almost no wear. No scuffing of corners by human hands, no smoothed grooves in stone steps, no windblown hollows in the metal walls. The room feels like someone’s careful drawing that has just been rendered: a space that has not been lived long enough to be worn, but has been constructed exactly once and is now essentially frozen.

---

### Scale and hiddenness

You might expect the sheer size of the chamber to overwhelm you, but instead it feels *hidden*—a pocket universe tucked at the top of the city.

You live inside a single integrated feature: the Condensation Chamber. From the outside—if there were an outside to look from—this tower would be just one of many identical stacks at the top of the factory-city. Inside, it is a complete world. You never leave it; its doors—if they exist—are maintained only rarely.

The metal housing units beneath your feet are stacked to their ceiling height; your head almost brushes the low ceiling of the corridor between them. You see the same configuration of rooms and gantries repeated in every direction you look: a tight, intimate, but perfectly replicated pattern of habitation.

There’s no view of a broader landscape. No windows to some distant horizon or to lower city levels. The only openings are the occasional vent shafts in the walls and ceiling, covered with grilles whose tiny mesh breaks the view into hundreds of indistinguishable fragments. You can’t see much of anything beyond them.

---

### Density: saturation

The density is not of clutter so much as of designed presences. Every inch of available space is filled with something that has a purpose—even if that purpose is just to be present.

On the gantries overhead, machines are stacked almost shoulder-to-shoulder: thick, humming rectifiers; transformers; tanks whose curved surfaces reflect the faint light; bundles of cables wrapped in thick insulation like muscles; manifolds and valves and gauges. Every item is welded, bolted, or clamped into place with no wasted space.

The floor below is a dense grid of identical units—the living modules of the workers. Each unit is barely large enough to lie inside without stretching out fully: a low door with no handle, only a sliding latch that can be operated from the outside; a shallow recess in the wall for a small lantern; a tiny overhead shelf. There are no hallways; only narrow gaps between the blocks where the condensation channels run, forming a kind of negative space between rooms.

You feel the density as a physical pressure: the sense of having many things near you, above you, below you, on all sides—almost touching, but never quite touching. The room feels nearly full to capacity, but stable in its fullness.

---

### Built: total environment

Everything you see, from floor to ceiling, is made.

There is no substrate under the metal: no bedrock, no soil, no earth you can reach by digging. The floor plating under you is welded directly to the girders beneath, which in turn support the lower levels of the city. You are standing on the “skin” of a manufactured mountain.

The walls are not shaped by wind or water or tectonics; they are curved to exactly the specified radius and then welded together along seams that are as precise and straight as lines drawn by a ruler. The gantries are evenly spaced, their intervals calculated mathematically, not evolved by necessity or accident.

The only irregularities you can see are tiny: a weld bead that’s a fraction thicker than planned; a rivet that’s off-center by half a centimeter; a condensation gutter where the angle is slightly sharper than on others. These are anomalies that have survived from the construction phase, but they don’t grow or change over time. They’re like fixed stars in a fixed sky.

Even the condensation basins shaped like shallow trumpet flowers are fully manufactured, their curves cut from templates by machine tools somewhere below.

---

### Technology and industry

The machinery that runs this room is industrial: heavy, reliable, built for scale.

The gantries overhead are filled with electrical equipment—transformers and step-downs that regulate power from belts below, where massive engines burn whatever fuel the city uses. You can hear the distant, steady thrumming of those engines—a low vibration in your bones and teeth that never stops.

Thick cables, wrapped in dark, insulating material, loop along conduit frames in neat arcs. They are not randomly draped; they are bundled and clamped into place. Every machine has its place, and every machine is running, or at least ready to run.

There are no computers or glowing screens; no flickering holographic displays or readouts in a language you don’t recognize. The room’s “controls” are visible only as ranks of large valves, wheels, and a few old gauges whose needles barely move from their fixed positions.

A faint smell of machine oil and ozone hangs in the condensed air—not sharp enough to sting your eyes, but enough to remind you that everything in this room is part of a larger, industrial organism.

---

### Lighting and dimness

The environment is dimly lit by a sparse constellation of small industrial lamps.

Each housing unit has a single shallow lantern set into a recess above its doorway, emitting a faint, yellowish-orange glow: not enough to properly illuminate the corridor, but enough to mark each door as a point of warmth. The lamps run on the same electrical system as everything else—steady, unwavering, without flicker. The result is a low, uniform twilight that blurs the edges of things.

On the gantries overhead, there are occasional floodlights bolted to the beams and aimed at an important machine or group of valves. Their beams are narrow and tightly focused, so you get a halo of harsh clarity around each machine’s centerpiece and then darker regions around it.

Shadows are thick and precise, cut along straight lines by the edges of metal plates and machinery frames. There’s no sense of “dusk” or “morning”; only the same unchanging level of illumination, maintained by the industrial system. You can’t tell time of day by light here, because the light never changes.

---

### Fauna: overwhelming

The real inhabitants of this chamber are not human workers, but the creatures that live among the metal housing blocks and gantries.

They are everywhere: *overwhelming* in their sheer numbers and nearness. You barely notice the first ones until you realize that every shadow you thought was empty contains a dozen or more small bodies pressed tightly together.

Resembling a cross between pale, blind amphibians and crustaceans, they don’t have a clear terrestrial name. They are roughly the size of your hand or a little larger, with bodies articulated in segmented plates like armor. Their skin is translucent and pale grayish-white, showing the faint hint of darker organs and faintly glowing luminescent nodules within—like smoldering embers seen under a thin membrane.

They move on many limbs—short, articulated legs that have faintly articulated pads at their tips, allowing them to cling to smooth vertical surfaces as well as horizontal ones. They’re clustered in thick masses along the condensation channels and around the basins where the water is deepest, forming a living, shifting carpet that you must step over carefully.

Some of them hang from the undersides of the gantries overhead, clinging upside-down to the steel beams and dripping condensation like living icicles. You can feel their occasional shadows brushing the top of your head as you move through the narrow corridors; they don’t attack you, but they don’t move aside either. They simply tolerate your presence.

You don’t see any of them eating. You don’t see them prey on each other or anything else. They may be filter feeding from the condensed water, or feeding from some kind of energy byproduct of the machines overhead—static, stray fields, or faint photoelectric emissions from the lamps. You never know for sure.

The population density is so high that if you were to try to count them, you would quickly give up. There are thousands, maybe tens of thousands, in this one chamber. They do not fight or grow agitated; they remain quietly in place, clinging. Their eyes are mere dark points beneath transparent skin, barely registering any reaction to your presence.

---

### Weirdness: strange physics

There are things that don’t fit how you think the world should work.

The creatures’ luminescent nodules glow with faint, steady light that does not flicker or change. They are not hot enough to warm the air around them, but they seem to emit more than just visible light—some faint sensation that makes your skin feel slightly tighter and your eyes slightly drier when you look at them too closely, as though they radiate a subtle, invisible drain.

The condensation tracks along wires and cables as well as along metal surfaces. You sometimes see droplets beading along an insulated cable like pearls on a string, despite the insulation being designed to shed water rather than collect it. Something about the materials or shapes in this room coerces moisture into stranger patterns than you’d expect.

The air has a faint smell of wet stone somewhere deep inside it, despite the absence of stone anywhere in the chamber or the city levels you’ve seen below—a phantom smell that doesn’t match any visible source.

The light from the industrial lamps seems to cling slightly more than you would expect: when you move away from a bright area, your eyes retain the impression of the last machine you saw for longer than usual. It’s as if time itself doesn’t change much but light still has a sluggish memory.

---

### Sound: deafening but static

The sound is overwhelming, but not because of anything dramatic happening now.

Far below the floor, you feel the steady vibration of massive engines turning at their fixed operating speed. They transmit their sound through the structure of the building so that the entire chamber behaves as a giant resonator. You hear it as a deep, continuous hum that feels like it’s inside the bones of your skull rather than outside your ears.

The machinery on the gantries overhead contributes its own layer: the quiet *whrrrr* of cooling fans, the low hum of transformers, and occasional faint clicks from relays switching on or off—even though nothing appears to change. You’re aware of thousands of small moving parts inside the machines, all running but doing nothing visible.
