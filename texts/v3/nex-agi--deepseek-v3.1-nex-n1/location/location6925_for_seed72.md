This is all underground, and yet not the underdark of myths and caverns. It is the foundation of things in the literal sense—the municipal undercroft of a neighborhood that keeps itself aloft by resting on just such a space below. The ceiling is low by the measure of things above—barely twenty paces of height—but the floor is broken and uneven enough that it feels taller than it is, with the distant vaults vanishing into a constant, pressured heat.

The air is the first thing you feel:

The stone is clammy to the touch, and the thick plaster between the brick courses gathers the slickness at the seams of the world; if you were to touch the wall, it would cool your fingertips for a second, then disappoint them with the absence of true flow.

There is no stream, but there is movement:  

High on the vaults, where the plaster has been eaten by time and neglect, you can see the dark, beaded ribs of the reinforcing bars. On them, there is always condensation. The moisture forms from the warm air of the lower city and the cooler, denser air of the deep tunnels below; it is captured by every surface until the vaults glitter like the inside of a lung. Drops fatten at the lowest point of exposed ribs, then fall to the floor in a pattern of random arithmetic: here, two taps; there, a longer pause; over there, a little cluster like someone laughing while you count.

The floor is uneven and half-defined by this process. The humps of the floor are not hillocks but the result of something more like a tide of time: places where the settling of stone and soil and collapsed fill have been left in the rough. The tears of the ceiling fall into the shallow depressions in the floor and make dark, almost circular pools. Each pool is very small—maybe the width of your footstep—and yet collectively there are enough of them that the space smells like wet stone and wet brick and the faint, musty sweetness of old wood where the joists were not taken out when they built the next level above.

This is a small cathedral, or the size of a small public building: a single open room, maybe eighty meters long, thirty wide, bounded by its load-bearing walls and the thick columns that support the world above.
