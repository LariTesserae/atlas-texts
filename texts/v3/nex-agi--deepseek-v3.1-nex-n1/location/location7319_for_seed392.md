You come first by the lizards.

They are the first living things that you truly notice, because they are the first things that do not look like they belong where you are seeing them. The ground beneath your feet—something you hesitate to call soil—is a pale, cracked, almost ceramic sheet, sloping very gently toward the horizon. It is warm, not quite hot, but as if it had been baked in the sun and is now letting go of that heat slowly, without hurry. The cracks are shallow and orderly, branching like delicate veins, but there are no shards missing; the surface is intact and stubbornly flat.

Along the nearest edge of the horizon, above the crack where the ceramic ground meets something else, you see the lizards: a long line of them, motionless along the rim of the world, as if they have been glued there.

Their bodies are perhaps half your forearm in length, sleek and smooth, not scaled but skin-covered like wax, or rubber. Their colors are wrong: not the browns and greens of earth and leaf and shadow, but the hard, saturated tones of manufactured things—fiery orange, plastic blue, bright yellow with a band of metallic black. They have been arranged, not merely scattered, in a rhythmic pattern: orange, then yellow, then two blues, then orange again.

They are staring.

Not exactly at you, but in the direction of travel you are walking, if walking is what you are doing when every step feels like a rehearsal for the next, identical step. Their eyes are fixed and unmoving; their chests do not rise with breath. When you get closer you see that their toes are not gripping the edge but fused into it, their bellies flush against the pale ceramic like the rim of a bowl painted onto the bowl itself.

A lizard is painted baked.

You are not sure where you learned that phrase, or if you invented it just now, but it feels accurate in a way that ordinary language does not.

Their tongues are out.

Each tongue is a curl of dark material, not flesh exactly, but something like clay or baked enamel, frozen mid-flick in the hazy air. Each tongue curls in exactly the same way—same length, same curve, same deviation from the midline of the jaw. The tongues are stylized, more emblem than anatomy.

Your eyes drift along the line of lizards, and you realize that the length of their tongue, when you squint, is exactly half the width of the nearest crack in the ceramic ground. The pattern is repeating.

Pattern is.

You are standing on something made, not grown.

And yet you smell water.

The air is thick with it. Not damp in the way of a rainy forest or a swamp, but something like the inside of a greenhouse after the plants have been brought in and the misting system has run too long. The sky is a flat, uniform white, or almost white—a pale luminous sheet that gives off light without revealing anything of itself. No sun, no clouds, only a brightness without focus or shadow.

Your shadow, if you have one, barely touches the ground beneath you.

The temperature is not oppressive, but it is steady. It feels like the warm bath of a machine that has been running for exactly the correct amount of time and will not heat up more or cool down. It is not unfriendly. It is simply unrelated.

You breathe in and the humidity is thick enough that you almost feel as if you are drinking the air. It does not condense into droplets on your skin or your clothes; it simply hangs there, a translucent equilibrium. The air is not muggy in the way of a tropical coast after a storm; it is muggy in the way of a carefully controlled environment where someone has decided that humidity should be high and never-ending.

There are dew-points, but almost never clouds.

You hear almost nothing.

That is one of the first true strangenesses you notice, when you finally notice absence at all. You hear no insects, no wind, no distant watery plunk of frogs jumping into a pond, no dripping from leaves. There is no roar of machinery or hiss of steam. There is no voice of bird or beast or engine.

There is just your breathing.

Even that seems quieter than it should.

You move closer to the lizards, because they are the first definite landmark you have seen. They do not react as you approach. You reach out and touch the tongue of the nearest one—the orange one—with your fingertip. It is cool, smooth, and faintly oily, like the glaze on a second-rate vase. It smells faintly of dust and something else, like overheated paint.

The lizard does not retreat.

The lizard has never moved.

You cannot feel whether there is a body under the surface, or if the lizard is simply painted onto the ground. It feels like a single piece, fired as a whole.

A fired ground.

You are walking on ceramic.

It is at this point that you look down again with the word ceramic in your mind and the pattern of cracks in the ground suddenly becomes a language you cannot read but can understand the shape of.

The cracks are not random fractures.

They are evenly spaced.

There are long lines that run in straight, parallel directions across the flat plain of the ground, and these lines are bisected by shorter, perpendicular lines at regular intervals. The spacing is identical along the length of each line. The width of each crack is perfectly uniform, not a millimeter wider or narrower than neighboring ones.

You are standing on a tile.

Not exactly a tile, but on the glaze of something tile-like.

It is hard to judge how large this tile is, because the ground in its low, flat, even slope curves so gently into the horizon that there are no visible edges to measure against. The horizon is itself made of ceramic and other materials, but from where you stand, all you see is an uninterrupted pale sheet broken only by the orderly crack of its lines.

You are on the surface of an object that is larger than any city and yet entirely constructed.

The ground is not sedimentary stone underneath the ceramic glaze, but neither is it concrete. When you kneel and trace the edge of a crack with your fingers, you feel that the ceramic is thin. It overlies something softer—something like compressed clay, or older, fired brickwork—but there is no soil, no natural rock beneath that.

Everything is made.

There is no natural substrate.

Not even a stone.

Your mind flinches at the thought of a world without stone. It has not been prepared for that. You have been prepared for many things—sunlight on water, forests, storms—but not for a place that has never known natural rock.

You are standing on the inside of something.

A shell.

The world is a shell.

As you stand, the humidity lingers on your lips, but there are no droplets forming. The air is thick with water in the same way that the air in a greenhouse after a rain is thick—without the dripping.

You begin to walk again.

Walking is the only thing that you can do that seems to change anything at all.

With each step, you feel the warmth of the ceramic surface through your soles. It is not harsh enough to burn, but firm enough to remind you that your body is not the same material as the ground.

The landscape is not featureless.

There are objects.

They are not placed as you might expect—no trees in rows, no buildings arranged along streets. Instead, they rise out of the ceramic sheet like self-contained units, each one its own small world.

The first object you encounter after the lizards is a low mound of vegetation.

Not vegetation as you know it, but something like the idea of vegetation.

The leaves are thick, glossy, and almost plastic. They are a deep, saturated green that does not occasionally fade or yellow at the edges; every leaf is exactly and identically green. The leaves are arranged in tight spirals, each leaf exactly the same size and shape as the others at its particular height on the mound.

The mound is not large—perhaps knee height at the highest point—and it grows directly from the ceramic surface without any visible root or join. You touch the edge of it and your fingers find no seam between the ceramic and the plant-material. It feels like the plant is simply an extension of the same glaze that coats the ground.

You are in a greenhouse where the plants are grown in the floor.

The leaves, when you gently test one, are flexible but not fragile, like rubber or silicone. They do not tear. They do not have any visible veins or imperfections; their surfaces are smooth as glass. You press your thumb into one leaf, and it leaves a faint depression that slowly disappears, like a memory of pressure, rather than a permanent damage.

The smell is the vaguest hint of chlorophyll, but there is none of the damp, earthy complexity of a forest understory. It smells clean, like washing soap advertised as having a botanical fragrance.

The humidity around the mound is thicker than elsewhere.

If you stay very still and observe very carefully, you might see a faint mist of water droplets form around the edges of the nearest leaf, then vanish again. The air is not moving, but the moisture is behaving as if it were responding to the plant’s presence.

The plant does not sway.

There is no wind.

Everything is stable.

This is a world that has never heard of storms in the way you know them, but it knows about humidity. It has been built to know.

At the crest of the mound, there is a round flower-like object, not exactly a blossom but more like a stylized flower emblem—two overlapping circles of glossy white and a band of yellow enamel in the middle. It does not open and close. It is fixed, like the lizards on the horizon.

You are walking on a glaze that has learned to dream.

The vegetation, when you look further away, is not concentrated in forests or groves, but scattered in sparse, individual islands.

There is a mound like this one every hundred or so steps, but the distances are not random. They fall along particular lines that you can only see when you look far enough. The lines are perfectly straight. They cross the ceramic plain like invisible guidelines drawn by a hand larger than you can imagine.

Between the mounds of vegetation, there are other things.

Bridges.

Not bridges over water or rivers or anything you can see at first glance, but low arched spans of clay-colored material that cross the crack between two ceramic planes. Sometimes the bridges connect one mound to another. Sometimes they connect nothing.

You walk under one of these bridges and your fingers touch the underside. It is smooth and cool, like fired clay but without any roughness. The color of the bridge is exactly midway between the color of the ground and the color of the vegetation—as if someone were slowly mixing the two pigments.

This bridge does not move or shake. There is no water beneath it to reflect the light. There is no traffic to traverse it.

The bridge’s existence is its own answer.

You are in a place where things are built because they can be built, not because they need to be.

Beyond the bridge, you see more lizards.

These are placed differently.

They are arranged in a circle around the next mound of vegetation, each lizard exactly the same distance from the center and the same distance from its neighbor on either side. Their colors are inverted from the horizon line: instead of orange-blue-yellow you see yellow where orange was, blue where yellow was, and orange where blue was.

There are other animals.

Not many, but enough.

Frogs.

They are ceramic frogs, not fired lizards but a separate design. They are squat and rounded, not in realistic imitation of real frogs, but in simplified abstraction—like symbols drawn by someone who had heard of frogs but had never seen one. Their skin is painted glassy smooth, with symmetrical lines of darker green drawn on their backs to mimic patterns that never evolved.

They sit on the edges of certain cracks in the ceramic sheet.

Each frog is placed exactly one tongue-length from the edge of the crack, according to the long lizard tongue whose size you marked earlier. This distance never varies.

The frogs have mouths that open in exactly the same degree, all exactly the same width.

Moths are carved.

Some moths are carved into the surface of the ceramic glaze itself, not standing above it but incised into it, like engravings. The lines of the carving are thin and precise. Each pair of wings is a perfectly symmetrical oval.

The moths are arranged around one light-silhouette embedded in the glaze, not shining from the sky but somehow glowing from within the ceramic: a faint, luminous circle that never wavers or flickers, like the sun is a tiny LED bulb buried under the glaze.

You are in a world where the light has been carefully placed and not allowed to wander.

The fauna—if that is the right word—is abundant, but silent.

Always silent.

You hear nothing.

When you finally encounter water, it is not where you expected to find it.

You expect to see a river, or a pool, or perhaps condensation dripping down the sides of one of the mounds of vegetation.

Instead, you see water showing itself as a subtle sheen on all surfaces.

It is not a pond or stream. It is a carefully maintained humidity that has crossed a threshold into visible form.

On the ceramic glaze the water appears as thin, perfectly circular films whose edges are sharp as if drawn with a compass. Each film is exactly the same radius—approximately the width of two palm prints—and spaced perfectly evenly in a hexagonal grid.

The water forms little circles on the glaze, not quite droplets but more like shallow, perfectly circular puddles that never deepen or spread. They are thin enough that the ceramic pattern underneath can still be seen through them, but they have enough height to reflect light back.

When you kneel down and look at your own face distorted in the surface of one circular film, you see that your reflection is stretched not exactly like you are expecting.

Your eyes are elongated and faceted.

Your mouth is curved in exactly the same stylized shape as a lizard tongue.

You blink and the image does not change.

You are not sure whether you have changed or the water-film is reflecting the version of you that belongs here.

The water does not soak into the ceramic glaze. It remains on top, like a glaze on top of a glaze.

A glazed glaze.

You resist the urge to disturb one film because you sense that it would not replace itself easily. This world is stable. It does not enjoy change.

You eventually stand.

Now you see more things.

Between the circular water films and the mounds of vegetation there are deep cracks—the ones like the cracked glazes of ancient bowls—only these are wider than the others, and visible at longer distances.

You are approaching a larger region of broken-up glaze, where the ceramic plates are not flat anymore but have been tilted slightly upward or downward at the edges, forming shallow basins and low rises.

Some basins are filled with water, not as thin films but as shallow bodies of still water. The water level does not ripple.

Water surface will.

Only you do not see it move.

In each water-filled basin, small ceramic fish are suspended at exactly halfway between the surface and the bottom. They are not swimming but merely frozen at mid-depth, like bubbles in glass.

The fish are identical in size and shape. Each one has three fins on its back and three straight black lines painted on its side. The pattern of the fish in the water—one orange fish, then two yellow, then one orange again—matches exactly the pattern of the lizards on the horizon.

Everything is echoing.

As you walk around the edge of one basin, you notice that the ceramic glaze which forms the rim is decorated with faint, painted reeds—vertical lines of enamel with small feather-like markings at the top. They are painted in exactly the same place as the real reeds would grow if you were in a natural marsh.

There are real reeds too.

Not reeds as you know them, but reeds as someone who had never seen a reed but had been told a very detailed description of one.

They are tall thin things of a glossy, dark green-brown material that is not really wood, not really plastic, and not exactly metal. They grow from the bottom of the basin and reach up to exactly the same height above the water surface—all exactly the same height.

Their edges, where they rise out of the water, are perfectly sharp. There are no irregularities, no broken tips or insect-chewed edges. Each reed stands perfectly straight.

There are gaps between the reeds—wide gaps—where the water surface is perfectly exposed between the stalks.

Sparse.

The water in the basin is warm enough that you can feel it without touching it. When you finally dip your fingers into one edge, where the glaze is rimmed with a painted border of darker blue, the water feels like warm bathwater.

It is still water, not particularly unpleasant or sharp, but it feels intentional.

You are handling the water of a world that could not be bothered to make rain fall.

You find yourself kneeling beside a basin made with a glaze pattern that exactly lines the edge of the water, where the enamel reeds are painted.

Looking at the water, you notice that it does not reflect the sky, because the sky is nothing to reflect.

It does, however, reflect the ceramic fish beneath its surface.

Each fish reflects exactly three other fish above it.

You are in a world of nesting.

It is here you realize that this is not a timeless landscape, but one that has been carefully constructed to be in equilibrium.

Humidity never drops.

Lights never brighten or darken.

There are no signs of erosion.

No edges rounded by wind.

No cracks widened by water.

No ceramic broken by frost heave.
