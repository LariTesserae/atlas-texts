This cavern is not the world’s beginning but its worn-out floor—a long sigh where time and stone and a little water have come to rest. The ceiling is low enough to see, but high enough to feel an open, echoing region overhead rather than a cramped tunnel. You sense not just a room but a system of chambers running out into the cool quiet dark. This is a buried city of the old, where the rock no longer cares to change and everything else seethes with it.

The air is cool, always just a breath colder than you, and the stone is the same temperature: unwelcoming, but honest. It is not moist enough to be dripping, but not dry enough to be dusty. Water is a rumor of dampness along the edges of things—patches on the walls where organic black sheen has learned to live without chlorophyll, the faint silver of a thin film on horizontal stone where rivulets have walked before. The floor is not smooth, but it is tired of stairs: it remembers the sharp ridges of its youth, but now its slopes have been worn into terraces that blur into each other, like a half-forgotten language in which only the most basic words remain.

Your first impression is of light. There should be no light here, but instead the ceiling is a ragged sky of broken mirrors. Pale phosphor stones, embedded like the constellations of some other universe, give out a steady, unfriendly glow. They are not fire; they are old and patient and cold. They brighten the air enough to make shadows harmless but never enough to make darkness vanish. You can see clearly, but everything is at arm’s-length, as if light itself is reluctant to travel too far.

The city wrapped around this underworld sky is old but not primitive in the sense of crude; it is primitive in the sense of limited by the available materials. Stone is king here. There are no metals, except perhaps thin veins of crude ore beaten into ornaments that have been forgotten by evolution. Almost everything is built of stone upon stone, chipped from the walls, stacked, slanted, and settled into this final, tangled form. The buildings are low, because the ceiling does not grant much height. They huddle together like teeth closing, but they are sparse on the ground floor—you see the shape of streets, of wide courtyards half-broken into gravel, and open gaps where the foundations rotted out or were simply removed by the last stirs of energy.

The architecture has been frozen by time. The edges are rounded, not because the builders were lazy but because erosion has been gentle and very long. The corners are soft, the doorways are smoother than they intended to be, the lintels are bowed into slack arches. The stones are all of one color here, different shades of the same rock: faded bone-gray, dirty with the faintest of dark stains where the dampness has remembered to seep down. The walls are uncarved; there are no epics, no names, no records of faces. If this was ever built to be remembered by strangers, it has forgotten that.

Vegetation is absent—not even moss, unless you count the black skin of thin films that cling to the damp patches where ceiling and wall meet, and the faint, lace-like patterns that are not quite lichen but something like the last taste of organic matter. The place smells of stone and stillness and the faint, musty hint that water sometimes licks the walls. The absence of plants is not oppressive; it is simply a fact, like the absence of sound.

The silence is the main sound of the world. You catch your own breathing more than any of the world’s. The sounds of water are a rare, secretive punctuation: a drop breaking from a high crack into a shallow basin, another drop answering a long time later, as if they are counting. The echoes of your steps are not booming but expanding, then vanishing, then swallowed by the stone and the quiet.

Yet against this quiet, the city is alive with inhabitants. Fauna is not abundant in number of species but in the density of movement of the few that have inherited the world. The air is filled with slow, heavy clouds of tiny, pale flying insects like translucent grains of rice, rising and falling in slow columns. They do not buzz or hiss; their wings are thin enough to make no audible noise as they fly. They are white, like the color of the ceiling stones, and seem to glow from within only when they pass close to the phosphor sky. They are always drawn to the brightest points, clustering there until they drift like smoke from one to another.

On the worn floor, there are no hoofprints or claw marks, but the flat stones are not perfectly smooth. They are covered in a thin layer of dust and fine grits from the erosion of the old walls, and this layer is worked by the tracks of countless small legs. The dominant creature is a flattened, many-legged thing that walks like a palm of fingers with long joints. Each footstep makes a tiny indent, but they are so many that they have formed a texture of their own: the stone floor looks like it has been carefully roughed by intention, but actually, it is just the result of long patience and the fine work of this crawling fauna.

At the center of the chamber, where the ceiling dips lowest and the floor is softest, there is a shallow basin, not quite circular, not quite broken. It is not a lake but a long, shallow spill of water that never seems to decide whether to stay or go. The surface is rarely still; it is turbulent at the scale of its own world. The water is not clear: it is the faintest of grays, like thin ash milk. The bottom of the basin is covered by a bed of silt that tears itself to pieces and rebuilds every minute. The mud is not passive—it crawls.

From the mud rise structures of clay that are not built by any hands but by the basin itself. These thin spires are like wobbly fingers of the world, pointing upward at the ceiling phosphors. They do not last long; they are built by the same gentle turbulence that erases them. Fissures open in the mud, then close, pushing the towers of soft silt up from below, letting them stand until they collapse and their material flows into the next creation. They are poor architecture; they lean at every angle possible to lean, yet they belong together, like a city in miniature that is forever being written and rewritten at the same time.

This is the world’s surreal gesture: a self-building city in the mud of an otherwise static and ancient city. The creatures of the basin—the slow mud-dancers—are not dramatic but relentless. They are like flatworms that have learned to walk in curves, propelling themselves with the same rippling that moves the mud they ride on. They are not seeking food; they are food, and they are fully eaten, but not as fast as they reproduce. The whole basin is a circulation of bodies and clay and dim light beneath the steady ceiling of stones.

The human-scale city above its basin is not dead but emptied by the long erosion of time. There are no signs of violent destruction; there are no signs of much destruction at all, except the destruction of disappearance. The doorframes are empty, their hinges of bone or rusted simple ore long gone. The windows are not openings but gaps where stone lintels have bowed into holes that look like closed mouths. The streets are too wide for the size of the buildings, but they are not wide for the sake of ceremony; they are simply leftover from habits that have been forgotten by creatures who went elsewhere to die.

Now, instead of people, there are only the animals. The crawling things are particularly common in the shadow of the walls, where the dampness is just enough to keep the dust bound together in a surface that feels like weak paper. You can see them working in quiet lines, carrying not leaves but scraps of stone dust from the broken floor of one courtyard to another. They are not hauling to build; they are hauling to rearrange. They are like the last stitchers of a world that has already decided its final pattern but still enjoys toying with the fray.

There is a pattern to the madness of movement. The flying insects, the crawling floor herds, the mud-dancers in the basin—they are all part of a single flow of chaotic energy that is not explosive but permanently turned on. It is an eternal turbulence at the edge of exhaustion, like the interior of a sun that has almost given up but has not figured out how to stop. New shapes of mud are born from the basin every minute, but they are always the same kind of wrong; new creatures crawl from burrows inside the walls of the city, but they are always the same kind of strange. The chaos of the world is not in the newness of its surprises but in the density of variations that never amount to different.

You are in the basement of history, under the hard floor of the exhausted world, where everything else has been finally flattened and smoothed into silence. At the surface of this world in the basin, however, things are always being broken and remade. The walls of the basin rise no higher than your knees, and the water spreads no farther than the length of a street, yet the smallness of the scale does not diminish the sense of place: the region stretches out around you, but it is all centered around this heartbeat of clay and tiny moving legs and the silent dance of translucent wings.

If you were to ask what the rules of this world are, they would be simple and not comforting:

Time is cheap but slow. The stone is patient in years; the animals are patient in seconds. The difference of pace between them is the only drama that remains.

Change is not allowed at the big scale but is required at the small. The city will not fall, but the mud will always rebuild itself. The edge of every basin brim will be broken and then fixed again by tomorrow’s little local flood.

Anything is allowed to become more itself but not to become different. The insects are allowed to congregate in new clouds of new numbers, but they are not allowed to grow new limbs or to change color. The mud is allowed to make new kinds of leaning towers, but they will always be made of mud and always fall back.

Water is the memory of the world. It is scarce and intermittent and thin, but wherever it finds a place to collect, it awakens the dust and the animals and the mud back into their brief, churning life of small tumultuous creation.

All the rest is silence, stone, and the light of the cold and broken stars in the ceiling of this final city.
