The world is a cathedral of living water.

From above, it would be a single cloud, a storm-cell the size of a small country anchored to a mountain range that is still being born. But there are no eyes above to see it; there are only eyes within, suspended in the warm, liquid air.

The air itself is almost rain. It flows, not with wind, but in currents – thick, humid streams that slide down slopes and coil around the pillars of the newborn mountains. The peaks are steep, sharp-edged, carved with a knife by water that has not yet had time to smooth them. They rise from nothing to knife-points and ridged flanges of black rock, their surfaces polished by the constant, liquid atmosphere.

Everywhere there is water, not just in the sky but in the air itself. It condenses on the leaves of the trees as thick, clear gel, running down branches in slow, luminous trails. Drops hang from the tips of fern fronds, not falling but stretching and stretching until they form silvery cords between one leaf and the next. The air is thick enough to see these currents, the faint shimmering lines where warm water-laden air slides over cooler.

The vegetation makes a forest that seems to have been built for water alone. Roots do not dig into dirt but coil into the air, seeking heavier moisture, the faintest driest airs, and drinking from them.
