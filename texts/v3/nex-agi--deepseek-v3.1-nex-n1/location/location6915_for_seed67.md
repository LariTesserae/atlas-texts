## The Engine of Winds

The air is thick and warm and smells of oil and fresh rain.

It is impossible to see the ceiling of the world. If there is a sky, it is hidden behind the underside of another engine deck, kilometers overhead, and the caked grease of millennia that has been squeezed into a layer of rust-colored cloud. The temperature is close enough to body heat that the skin does not decide whether this is cold or hot; only sweat knows, clinging silently in the spaces between flesh and clothing.

You are standing on an engine deck of continental scale.

The world is called the Engine of Winds by the few who still know that words have names, though the wind itself is mostly memory and rumor. The air is not so much wind as a slow, heavy tide of pressure, turning from place to place in millennial cycles. The decks are metal, all of it, every surface you can see and countless more you cannot. The metal is old but not old; it is not rusted, not pitted, not worn by friction. It is merely *signed* by time, in the way stone might dust itself with lichen. Tiny imperfections of finish and polish and color are the only memory that anything ever happened here.

The floor of the world is not one level, but tens of thousands of them, stacked like a library of shelves. Each shelf is a machine—a factory deck, an interstitial engine level, a heat-exchange plane, a filter deck, a support lattice woven from beams thicker than cities. The distances between decks are measured in kilometers. The spaces between them are called chambers, and the chambers are named like oceans: Chamber of Steady Pressure, Chamber of Gentle Heat, Chamber of Solvent Dew, and so on.

You are in the lowest of the low decks, where the water is.

### The Terrain of the Low Deck

The metal floor is not flat but sculpted: long shallow ribs of metal, each just taller than a human being, spaced at intervals of ten or twenty meters. They run like the ribs of a drowned machine’s skeleton, vanishing into a distance that does not resolve into horizon but into texture and haze and the next rib of steel.

These are not streets, not plazas, not corridors. They are the structural members of something else entirely—a floor whose purpose is load-bearing and heat exchange and acoustic dissipation and perhaps a dozen other functions that no architect has contemplated for centuries or millennia. The ridges between the ribs are not walked on often; no one’s feet are the sort of feet that step there.

Between the ribs, the floor is not perfectly dry but not perfectly wet either. It is not a pool or a river or a swamp, but a *condensation several*. The moisture gathers from the air, not in rain but as a mist that falls to the floor and hesitates there in the angles of metal and weld seam, in hairline fractures that are too narrow to be called cracks but too wide to be called smooth.

Water is everywhere and nowhere at the same time. It is not rain falling; it is dew being wrung from the atmosphere by the subtle temperature gradients of the machine floor. The metal surface is cooler than the air by just enough that moisture condenses on it in tiny beads, all of them motionless, not dripping, not running. The drops of water are a sea of still mirrors, each a few millimeters wide, reflecting the hazy overhead light of outdated lumens and the glow of corrosion-resistant finishes.

If you lie down on the floor and look at the level of your breath, the world is a flat infinity of circles and slight curves of metal, all of it broken by the sudden verticals of the deck ribs. The ceiling of the next deck above is not visible in the strict sense, but its presence is known in the way of all things unseen: by the pressure of its absence when you expect to see sky. There is no sky, only the underside of another machine deck, and perhaps another and another and another, holding up the atmosphere of the world like a sky of steel.

The ground under your back is colder than the air, by a precise fraction that the machine world has not forgotten in its long equilibrium.

### The Builtness of Everything

Everything is made.

Every rib of the floor is inscribed with the faint signs of manufacture: tooling marks, weld lines, the ghost of a fatigue test from eons before today. There are drains of a sort, but not gutters or storm sewers; these are small pristine ports at the lowest edge of the floor, bored into the metal with the same tolerance as every other part of the machine. They are not “rusty” or “grime-caked” in the old sense of those words; grime and rust and decaying life are no longer things that exist here. The machine cleans itself; not perfectly, but enough to erase the evidence of time.

The supports that rise from the floor and vanish into the ceiling are not columns of stone or pillars of reinforced concrete. They are trusses of complex cross-section, each composed of beams that divide themselves into smaller beams and those into wires, threads, and finally fibers that are too fine to be individually perceived by unassisted sight. At the human scale they are simply smooth grey vertical structures, interrupted at intervals by partially raised rungs of maintenance ladder that lead not into space but back into the machine itself.

It is all built, but it is not built for you.

The scale of the human body is not the scale of the machine. The ribs are shaped to be the right *something* for a machine function that no longer even has a name in the vocabulary of the world; not for you. The ladders are runged at intervals that are too large or too small for your limbs, or not meant for limbs at all. The whole world is industrial in the sense of having been manufactured by minds that understood particles and pressure and heat flow, but not necessarily the length of a step.

The built environment is not hostile to life. It is not indifferent either, in the way people might think of indifference as a kind of cold disregard. It is simply *other*. The contours of the floor, the dispositions of the supports, the angles of the beams—all of them say to the body: *you are an optional inhabitant. This place was not made with you in mind.*

And yet, you are here.

### The Water of the Low Deck

Water is present in the way that is neither life-giving nor death-threatening; it is *environmental*. It is not the salt of ocean or the silt of river; it is the distilled and polished water of condensation on the internal surfaces of a machine.

The rounded floor between the ribs is lined with shallow channels of metal, each perhaps a finger deep and a handspan wide. In these channels, the water of condensation gathers from the air, not falling into the channel but *condensing into it*. The slow, thermodynamic process that is the engine of the world itself continuously rewrites the temperature of the air into a temperature of the metal floor, and that difference is expressed as water leaving the vapor and resting on the surface.

The water is not clear in the crystalline sense. It is not uniform. It carries with it the microscopic burdens of the machine: the oil of forgotten bearings, the fine dust of mechanical wear, the trace minerals of corrosion that has not yet been allowed to become rust. The drops of water are slightly opaque, each a tiny lens of liquid and dissolved machine, the color of asphalt under torchlight.

The channels of water are paths of a sort, but not to anywhere a person would want to go. They are heat paths: the water carries the latent heat of the condensation process from where it is “not” to where it is “more not,” in the internal language of the Engine of Winds. The channels terminate in the drainage ports, which vanish into the machine instead of falling into a lower world.

If you dip your fingers into one of the channels, the water is warm and faintly slick, not oiled but slightly colored with the machine’s own fluids. It does not smell of anything but the faint neutral of treated water. The skin of your hand dries more slowly than in other places, as if the air and its machine are not entirely sure they should be taking that water back.

There are no clouds of mist above the floor. The condensation of water is not happening in the air; it is happening at the boundary of the air and the floor. The metal floor is the surface of a cloud, turned down.

### The Vegetation of Nothing That Looks Like Plants

The instruction you were given said that vegetation is not present here, and that is true if by vegetation you mean any of the familiar forms—tree, root, leaf, flower. The metal of the Engine of Winds does not support the kind of carbon chemistry that makes trees possible.

But there is something.

There is no soil to be seen, only the metal floor of the world. However, in the corners and seams of the machine, where fatigue of thousands of years of vibration has not been perfectly erased, there are *growths*.

They are not biological in the carbon-chain sense, but they are analogous structures: thin veins of filament that spread from a point of error, a place where the machine’s geometry has not been perfectly corrected in the maintenance of equilibrium. They are called the filaments of the error, by anyone who knows to call them anything.

You can only see them if you look very closely at the edges of the deck ribs or where a support meets the floor. There are lines of minute, thread-like strands of something that is not metal and not spiderweb and not plastic. The color of the filaments is not vastly different from the gray of the machine, but it is subtly distinct enough to be noticeable if you are looking for it.

The filaments are not alive by any of the old definitions of “life.” They do not grow from seeds, they do not respire, they are not made of cells. They are self-organizing patterns of a sort of magneto-mechanical code that has been written not by human beings or machine gods but by the long, careful error of the operating environment of the Engine of Winds. The machine itself is, in some rare points of imperfection, generating, from its own operation, a kind of unplanned code that has structure to it.

Its function—if the word “function” applies to something that was not meant to be—is not understood by any of the inhabitants of the world in the way you might understand the function of a tree or a vine. The filaments of the error are not climbing the supports of the machine to get closer to a light; they are not gathering anything that they “need.” They are simply the pattern in which local error has been stabilized by the machine’s corrective algorithms.

There are no leaves, no roots, no flowers.

There is only pattern.

### The Temperature and the Dynamic of the World That Does Not Change

The temperature is not hot; not furnace, not desert of sun. It is warm enough to be almost always uncomfortable if you are dressed for the cooler air of mountains or seaside cities. The humidity wraps the body in a subtle layer of sweat and not-quite-breathing.

The temperature of the air is not the same everywhere, but gradients are not strong enough to be felt as currents of wind. The temperature is not the same from the floor to the height of your knees, and not the same from there to the height of your head, and not the same from there to the height of the beams of the next deck above and not the same beyond that into the machine.

The Engine of Winds is in a state of deep, long-term equilibrium. The machine is not static—the supports of the world are not actually frozen. They are, at the level of molecules and atoms, vibrating under the load of the thousands of decks above them and the heat of the atmosphere of the world. But the change in time of the whole of the Engine is not carried on the scale of human lifetimes or centuries of them.

The temperature field is a solution to a equation of heat and flow and pressure and mechanical load. The water of the condensation is not rain falling from the sky of another machine deck but the result of that equation being continuously solved, by the machine of the world itself, at the scale of the continent.

The world of the Engine of Winds is not dead in the sense of being empty and cold and unchanging. It is not a museum of machines. It is very much *working*, but it is working in time of its own kind, a time whose gradient is so slow that to the human perception of time and change and difference and event and story and progress and decay and rebirth and new and old and young and ancient and *movement* in the usual sense of the word—

—to that perception, the world of the Engine of Winds is not moving at all.

The low deck of the world, where the water condensates from the air into the subtle channels in the machine floor, is not aging in the dramatic way of mountains and valleys and rivers and forests and cities and wars and civilizations and the rising and falling of seas. It is not eroding in the sense of being worn away and smoothed into a new shape of itself.

The erosion it is experiencing is the erosion of the error correction of the machine.

The equilibrium of the Engine of Winds is not the same as being perfectly unchanged. It is being almost perfectly corrected by the machine of itself. The few places where fatigue of time and wear and mechanical load have not been erased by the corrective algorithms of the world are the *young* features of the landscape of the Engine: the seams and the disturbances and the filaments of the error that are the only places of any “building” or “breaking” that the machine of the world is not in perfect control of.

The low deck of the Engine of Winds is not “old” in the sense of being ruined by time and decayed into something else. It is not “new” in the sense of being freshly built or still under construction. It is somewhere in between: not absolutely pristine but so close to it as to be indistinguishable from that state to the senses of the inhabitants of the world.
