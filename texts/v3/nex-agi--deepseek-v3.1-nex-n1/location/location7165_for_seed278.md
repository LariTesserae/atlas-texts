You are on a high basalt plateau that is not exactly a planet and not exactly a machine, but something that has become both over time. The air is thin and permanently cold—not biting, but a steady, low-grade chill that has been here for ages longer than you. The sky is a ceiling of low, matte cloud, stretched taut without a seam, glowing faintly with the diffuse reflection of industrial processes occurring far away and long ago. It is never truly day or night here; instead the space is underlit, as though by a city hidden beneath the world.

The ground underfoot is solid and yet not entirely rock: a hardened composite of old lava flows and something older and more deliberate than stone. It flows in broad, shallow waves toward an unseen horizon, where the plateau simply stops and the sky continues without it. There are no sharp mountains, no jagged peaks. Instead, erosion has been patient—ceaseless, almost—wearing the plateau into long hummocks and shallow ridges. You feel how each surface has long since settled into its own shape, as if the wind were a polishing tool rather than a destructive force.

Water, here, is rare and carefully choreographed. You cannot smell rain, but you can feel the faintness of moisture in the air—condensation as much as weather. Low, thin mists gather in the depressions between the basalt swells, where the temperature drops slightly and the air’s dampness finds a surface that is colder than it is. These mist pools are thin, scarcely ankle deep, but they are the world’s only reservoirs. They glisten in the dim light with a greasy sheen, as if the water itself is faintly oiled by some distant, leaking machinery.

The silence of the place is thick, but not absolute. It is less a soundlessness than a sound held very far away. You might hear, at the edge of perception, the muted hum of something vast idling below the surface, or the distant creak of long metal tendons cooling under the world’s skin. Closer at hand, the quiet is broken by occasional tiny pings of contracting metal, by the faint hiss of condensation dripping into hidden drains, and by the soft, almost inaudible click of thousands upon thousands of small feet.

Because the fauna here is overwhelming.

This is not the teeming jungle you once imagined when you heard that word. There is no lush roar, no chorus of screams and songs. The fauna of this plateau are overwhelming by sheer number and persistence, not by noise or size. They are small, segmented, arthropodal-like things that have never been named. You step carefully and already notice that you are never truly placing your foot on bare ground. You lift your foot from a patch of hard, dark surface and, where crusty dust has been brushed away, you see movement beneath: thin, jointed legs; countless tiny bodies; a slow, churning carpet of life.

They are not uniform. Some are armored, dark and metallic, reflecting faint light from the cloud-ceiling. Their shells are not smooth but etched with a fine grain, as if eroded by the whisper of wind. Others are softer, translucent, revealing the slow pulse of internal lights that mirror the faint glows in the sky. They creep along the edges of the basalt ridges, where moisture gathers, and line themselves up along the lip of each shallow depression, where they dip their heads and drink from the mist-pools with the mechanical precision of machines on a schedule.

Their movements are slow—so slow that you doubt at first whether they are alive at all. They are not frozen, but they are caught in the long rhythms of the plateau. They step, pause, step, pause. Each step is measured, as though they are counting, or testing. If you stare at one patch, you will see it gradually change over the course of minutes—edges of tiny bodies covering an inch of ground as you yourself might crawl across an entire valley of your world in the same time.
