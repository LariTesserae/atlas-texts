The sunk is a chamber of warm water and nothing else, at least not visibly. No plant flesh, no fish scales, no clean shoreline of sand or stone to mark where the world ends and the wet begins. No skyline, either, because there is no sky.

On the parameter map of the world, this place is a narrow, almost-hidden abscess: a high, warm darkness in the rock, where water is very near and the air carries itself like a body about to sweat. Technically it is up high, in the sense that if you could follow the stone of the ceiling all the way out you would eventually climb to the sunlit world. But height is abstracted here by darkness and the water’s monotonous murmur. The walls of the sink are not cliffs of scale but curves of intimacy, close enough that you can feel their breath.

The ceiling of the sink is not smooth. It is not finished yet. This is the beginning of a future cave, or the end of one, depending on whose calendar you read. Geology is still thinking about what form to take. The stone is a raw, mottled grey-beige sedimentary something, somewhere between soft and brittle, and the water is figuring out how to persuade it to move. Most of the world is already sculpted; this is an exception. Time doesn’t change it because you, in your brief span, are too small to perceive the pace of its decision-making.

But water already has opinions.

Where the ceiling dips lowest, droplets of it condense in the warm, still air and hang as long as they can bear themselves before letting go. They’re not like rain; there is no rhythm to their falling. They’re like single thoughts of a larger mind that hasn’t decided to be fully present yet. Each droplet falls into a pool of water that makes the slight sound of a tongue being clicked inside the mouth of something vast and not very interested in speaking. This is the only sound, if you are alone and not moving.

The pool of water is not broad. The sink is not a lake. It’s maybe three body-lengths across if you are average of limb and unlucky of setting. Maybe less. The edges of the water are not an edge at all in the bright world’s sense of the word: there’s no line of sand; instead, there is a thick, curving lip of stone that flows into and beneath the surface, as if the sink is trying very hard not to be defined, to be water instead of wall and wall instead of water.

Where you stand, if you can be said to stand at all, is on this uneven apron of stone that runs around the water like a misplaced collar. In most places that stone would be called “floor,” but here floor implies something more generous than this. This is the floor that is being auditioned for whatever comes next.

The stone is surprisingly warm to the touch. Not warm like something sun-baked—because sun has never been here—but warm like blood, like the internal organs of the world beneath you. This is not one of those places where cold settles down from the ceiling and crawls into your bones; it is one of those places where you stand and think, if you’re not being careful, that the rock is alive underfoot.

It is not exactly grooved yet by time. There are no river channels, no obvious patterns of water’s anger or joy. Erosion is still trying to decide whether it wants to be sculpture here. There are hints of scallops in the wall where water has tastefully sampled the stone’s soft spots, but they’re not yet deep enough to be called anything definitive. Tiny ledges and textured patches of stone where something might someday drip more seriously, if you happen to be around ten thousand years from now.

The water in the pool itself is not a clear medium in the usual sense of clarity. It is clear of living things, but not of absence. It’s a heavy, dark liquid that doesn’t reflect so much as absorb. If you bring light down from the world above—a slab of bioluminescent flesh or very carefully protected oil lamps—you will discover that the water acts like an ink that has somehow decided to be water at the last moment. It doesn’t mind you, but it doesn’t offer you your image back.

There is no smell in the way of a world full of trees and rot and algae and sun. There is a smell of wetness, and the hint of something metallic that isn’t metal, but only for those who have been here long enough to stop breathing the surface.

In the water, things are not happening quickly. There is a density of water in the sense of water being near itself; every molecule of the pool is close to other water molecules, and in that sense the space is dense. But nothing else crowds that density. Nothing is vying for room in the water column. There are no fish, no columns of translucent eggs, no algal mats, not even any obvious skeletons of previous attempts to colonise the place. This is not barren water; it is pre-biotic water, or water that has decided to keep itself to itself.

Moisture is not shy here. The air is not quite foggy, but it is about to be. You breathe and you feel the water attaching to your inside. Your lungs, if you are the kind that has lungs, become a little more of the world’s architecture than they strictly should be. You exhale and you notice that the air you push out seems to be already half of the same substance as the surrounding air.

Up against the walls the condensation is more visible than above the center of the pool. Here, water is not so much falling as migrating. It is not in a hurry to be the river that will slide in many years from the ceiling of the world above to the floor of the world below. It is in the near-touch of surfaces, where the stone and the air meet each other and agree to make each other wet.

There is almost no vegetation in the usual sense. There are no ferns, no flood-resistant reeds, no algal mats. But you might find, if you look with something more sensitive than real eyes, that there is something like a thin film of memory of plant being spread on a few stones where the water slips most regularly. It is not colour, so much as a hint of texture: where the stone appears to be slightly ashamed of its own bareness, and has decided to pretend to be something else.

Or maybe it is not that tricky at all. Maybe you are looking at a very thin, very sparse crust of something that will be lichen-like if it gets around to it in time. This is not a forest or a meadow; it is a single hesitant intention of greenness that has not yet decided to become fully visible.

The temperature here is constant. Not hot exactly, but warm enough that you understand your own body is not the one setting the temperature of the place. If you’re very cold when you first arrive from the surface, you’ll blame this for how you forget to shiver after a while. If you’re from somewhere else, where the temperature shifts with the time of day, you might find it strange that there is no time in here. There is only a temperature, and temperature is not something that changes. It is something that exists.

No lights are made here, except visitors’. If you are a creature who needs to see by luminance reflected from objects, you will not see much in this sink. You will be aware of closeness of walls and water, but mostly by proximity of presence and by sound. You know the wall is near because you can feel the air push against you as you move toward it, the same way you learn where someone is standing in a cramped room when you can’t see their eyes.

The soundscape of the sink is not impressive. It is not music. The water sounds from the ceiling are not orchestrated. They don’t match any pattern you can learn from paying attention to them for short time. You might be counting and you find that you are waiting a longer time than you expected between one drop and the next. Then maybe one comes before you’re ready. This is not a pattern; it is a language of unremarkable punctuation.

Underneath the water you might hear, if you lower the better part of yourself into the pool and hold still for long enough, a deeper kind of murmur—not a sound, but a sensation. This is not water being excited. It is the water listening to itself in the places of the stone where you cannot be. You cannot decide if it is moving toward or away from something.

Maybe you are not fully alone here. There is not a fauna in the sense of herd animals, or even insects, if this is such a world. But every temperature of world and density of place and dampness of air tends to drag out a few things that are not impossible. If you are here and you are paying attention, the fauna might be you.

Or maybe there is something else that has come here and not yet been persuaded by the world’s gentle pressure to be elsewhere. You might set down your tools in the darkness and turn to fetch them and not be entirely sure the shape you find is exactly the same shape you left. This might be a trick of the mind, or it might be that there are animals in a very thin sense of the word—thin like that film on the stone. Not enough of them to be seen as a species, but enough to be counted as one or two individuals who have the same idea you do.

They are not afraid of you, because fear belongs to worlds where change is possible.

Built things are not here in the sense of huge constructed surfaces of metal and glass and cement and seal. You are not looking at a settlement. This place is not a place where anyone has decided to be permanently yet. But people or their predecessors have been around because someone—something—has scattered a few concepts of structure in the corners and along the edge of the water.

There is a notched piece of stone that you might, at a distance, in low light, call a step. It has not yet been shaped by hands of concern to be perfectly flat; but it has been more of a flat than the stone around it. It is not enough to be a floor; it is enough to be a suggestion of floor, if anyone else ever comes along and agrees to be interested in that suggestion.

There is a hollow of a kind of wall that might be the beginning of a shelf for tooling or storage. In the corner of the sink where no water falls from the ceiling, but where water likes to condense on stone instead, there is an uneven cavity of space that is not quite natural but not quite finished. You might find yourself resting your hand on it and not thinking it is strange at all.

Possibly you see a tool, or something like one. It might be a rough blade of—not metal, because tech is not that high here—but of stone that has been chosen and not shaped by accident. It might have been left by someone who came here for water and then decided not to stay, or who was staying and then decided that staying here was not what they wanted to do after all. If you bring your own light source you can see it; otherwise you will find it by touch: when the tool’s edge cuts your skin and you feel the wet of yourself in the dark, you will be sure of it.

If you try to use the tool on the walls of the sink—you might not be able to help yourself—you will notice that they are not yet ready to offer you much resistance. They are not ready to be worked by someone else either. They give in ways that are not entirely satisfying. You carve, and the stone yields not as if you’re sculpting but as if you’re being watched by something that is not interested in helping you make a better shape of things.

The world here is not weird in the sense of the impossible; you will not find floating chunks of continent or singing rings of stone that are not stone. But it is not entirely usual either. There is something unusual about the combination of heat in the rock and water in the air and stillness of time and scarcity of life and lack of surface noise. It is weird enough to make you think that if the world were ever going to try out a new idea of environment, it might be doing that here, in a very quiet and experimental way.

You might notice that the direction of water’s movement in the air is not what you think of as down from a rigour of law. There is one corner where you can feel air moving more than it should, moving almost like a breeze of faint, wet breath, and if you are not careful you might think that edge of the sink is trying to be something else—maybe a door of air, maybe not. You don’t ask it out loud.

You might also find that if you stay here long enough, the warm of the stone and the water begin to feel like they are trying to learn your body rather than comfort you. This might not be true. It might be that you are not used to a place that doesn’t pay attention to you, but that doesn’t mind you being here.

Because you do not matter in the sense of change here. Nothing is changing on a scale you can perceive. Time is not happening in the bright sense of seasons and suns arching. If you close your eyes and you open them again, you will not be able to tell the difference. This is not because of your sleep; this is because of the sink’s schedule.

In the spectrum of quiet, this is not total silence. There is enough sound to remind you that you are not in the void of somewhere else. There is not enough sound to make you feel like you are in the company of anything that has an intention toward you. This is not the loud world of sea and forest and wind and the movement of crowds of animals in complex systems of survival and predation. This is the world of one or two sounds trying not to disturb each other.

You might sit at the edge of the water and let your hand fall into it. It will be warmer than you expect if you’re from a surface place where water is only as warm as it has been allowed to be by sun or absences of sun. This water has been warming by itself from the inside, for reasons of rock and of not being very interested in moving at the moment.

You will not see the bottom of the pool. You might not be able to reach it with the length of your arm if you are of the size that normally finds such things in spaces of this size. This might not be because the pool is deep, but because you’re not sure, in the dark, where your body ends and where water and stone and air intersect.

In the balance of environment and being, this place is weighted in favour of environment. You do not feel like a centre here. You are a detail in a corner, perhaps one that has already been figured out. Or maybe not being figured out at all, because there is nothing to figure out.

At the edge of perception, you might begin to notice that the density of water around you feels not uniform but layered in some way that doesn’t strictly respect physics. You might think that the water near your hand is not exactly the same water as the water near your face if you could taste them. You might not be able to explain that difference in any language you have, except that you would be uneasy if you were the type of creature who disturbs water.

Most of the time you don’t disturb water. You are just being nearby.

The built suggestion of things is not demanding enough of you to require any work of maintenance in return. They’re not asking you to fix them or finish them. They are not reminders of someone else’s ownership of this space, because no one has established that here yet. This is not a place of being owned by anyone else, except possibly by water itself, which is not taking notes of you.

If you look at it from a particular conceptual angle, the sink is not very hidden at all. It is in the world. It is not below the floor of something else; it is that the world’s skin has folded itself here and has not yet decided whether to make a scabb or a window of the fold. It is pocket-scale in that you could contain your idea of it in your idea of a room, but it is larger than any of your pockets. If you are very small or you are very big, that might change your opinion of how pocket this is.

You might find that you don’t want to leave immediately not because of the excitement of the place but because of the way it doesn’t care whether you stay or go. This is unusual enough to make you sit a little longer.

And then, when you finally do decide to move—when you set the tool back where you found it or where you are not quite sure you found it, and when you rise from the edge of the water and you feel the air of your movement being absorbed by the air of the sink—you might notice that what you were leaving behind was not so much a place as a possibility of one.

Behind you, as you climb away from it and back into whatever you call the world proper, the sound of the water drops does not change. Whether there are more of them or fewer of them in the next moment of time is not a question you can answer from a distance. You might not have been able to answer it when you were sitting next to them.

You leave behind a warm, dark, wet, near-featureless, near-lifeless, slightly-built but not-quite-built, not-patterned but not random, not changing but not exactly frozen, intimate, not-even-certain-it-is-a-cave-yet, pre-formation of space in the interior of rock: the sink.

And you carry forward with you the certainty of water having an opinion about the shape of the world, even if you were not allowed to understand what that opinion actually is.
