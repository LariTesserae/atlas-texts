The sky is low and wet-white and the world is a breathing table of moss.

You are standing on the highest roof of the world, and the roof is alive. Under you, the moss is not a carpet but a body – thick, warm, taut with stored water. It hums when you walk. Not with sound exactly, but with a vibration in your bones, as though every step you take is being answered by something under the floor of the world.

Elevation here is not about height but about being at the brim of everything. The clouds are not above you; they are beside you, sliding in slow, wet herds over the edge of the plateau where the world falls away. You can see no horizon, only a white wall of mist where the sky stitches itself to the earth. The air feels like you could wring it for rain.

Light is a gray-gold, diffused by a cloud ceiling that acts like the ceiling of a greenhouse: the sun is filtered back to a uniform glow, no shadows, only gradients of green. There is a constant brightness without sharpness, as if the whole world is under a dome of frosted glass.

The terrain is not chaotic but it is restless. You are on a wide, flat highland that seems to have been poured rather than carved. The moss is not uniform. It rises in gentle swells, like the backs of sleeping giants, each mound darker than the last, some of them several stories tall, though from here on the highest roof you look over them rather than into them. In between these swells are narrow channels, like the seams of a quilt made of water and moss. Those are not rivers yet, but they could be at any moment.
