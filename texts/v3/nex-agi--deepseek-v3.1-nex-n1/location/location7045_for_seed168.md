High on the flanks of a mountain range that doesn’t know yet whether it wants to be mountains, the air is warm and still, and the world is not quite finished.

This is not a desert of the usual kind. There is no rain of water, no dust of wind-blown sand, no salt pan cracked and brittle. There is no water at all. The sky is a clear, depthless blue, and not a cloud stirs in it. The sun burns bright and steady, but the heat never seems to rise beyond the pleasant threshold of a long afternoon; it feels perpetually three hours before sunset. The light is normal, undimmed, but the world beneath it is not.

You stand on a wide plateau of raw stone, still half-exposed, like the skinless muscle of a world. The rock is pale, almost bone-white in some stretches, banded with subtle tones of iron and copper where minerals have begun to bleed out but not yet run. The plateau is large enough that the distant edge of it is blurred by the air, but small enough that you can still make out the shapes of individual ridges and the hollows between them. It feels like the region around a small town, but there is no town here—only this single table of stone, raised high above dim, lower slopes that fade away into haze.

The stone is rough and unfinished. It has not been smoothed by any river, no glacier has planed it down, no wind has scoured it into curves. There are no gullies or channels carved by water, no riverbed scars, no deltas of any fluid. The edges of the plateau are sharp, almost like the edges of a crude model cut out of a sheet of something else. The cliffs are steep but only partly formed, their faces broken into uneven facets like the first rough cuts of a sculptor’s block. Some rock faces are still glassy, where once molten material cooled abruptly and never learned to erode.

In the great silence, you can hear almost nothing. There are no insects, no birds, no animals of any kind. No rustle of leaves, no stirring of undergrowth, no calls, no distant hum of grazing herds. The world is not dead—it is more like it has not yet decided on what kind of living to be. The silence is the sound of not-yet existence.

And yet, against all expectation, there is vegetation—more lush than you would have thought possible in a place without water.

It grows directly from the stone.

Not as trees, not as you know them, but as low, thick carpets of fibrous matter that spread in wide, slow patterns across the plateau’s surface. Their color is not quite green and not quite black—somewhere between deep mold and tarnished copper, with a faint metallic sheen when the light hits at the right angle. They are not plants in the familiar sense; there are no leaves, no stems in the way you know them. Instead, they are composed of thin, overlapping layers of something like cellulose or chitin mixed with mineral compounds, laid down in bands like shelf fungi, but densely interwoven into a single mass.

Each growth begins as a faint, crystalline pattern on the stone surface—a spiral of almost invisible lattice lines, like frost etched onto glass. Then the substance thickens, fibers pushing out from the pattern, growing thicker and softer, until a whole section of rock is covered in a soft, springy mat. You can walk on it; your boots sink a few centimeters into its warmth, and the faintest of squeaks or compressions can be heard if you listen closely, a sound so quiet you almost doubt it’s there.

The growths do not devour the rock entirely. They only colonize certain surfaces—preferring flat expanses or the gentlest of slopes, where their bands can spread out radially like slow, frozen ripples. Some mats are small, no bigger than a tabletop; others are enormous, covering hectares of the plateau. They generate their own processes from patches and threads of mineral compounds running through them like metallic veins, and from the steady light of the sun above.

In the midst of this lush but barren world, the air feels unusually empty. There is not much of it, by human sense—though enough to breathe, there is a subtle pressure drop, a hint that you are higher than you think you are, and that the atmosphere here is thin. The sense of near-void is not the absolute emptiness of vacuum; it is more like the feeling of standing at a great height in a world that has not yet fully gathered its atmosphere. The density of matter around you is low: wide gaps between the growths, open spaces where nothing happens at all. No bugs, no small animals scurry away from your footsteps.

The temperature is warm, but not heavy. It does not feel like a jungle’s oppressive heat, or a desert’s dry baking. It feels like the heat of a sealed room where someone has been welding long enough to warm the air but not enough to raise the temperature. The sunlight feels like it has been regulated, somehow; not glassy, not artificial, exactly, but eerily consistent.

From this vantage point on the plateau, you can look out over a region that is surprisingly open. The mountains are not high, jagged peaks; they are more like raised sections of a world being built from uneven sections of crust. Some stretches are simply missing, replaced by smooth, lower plains that look like they have been filled in with a darker, finer-grained material than the surrounding stone. Other areas are bulged upward like the first crumpled wrinkles of future mountain chains. You can see dozens of such features in the blue distance: rounded rises of raw stone, some completely bare, others cupped like shallow bowls where thick vegetation has already begun to couch.

But there are no buildings anywhere.

No human structures, no alien architecture, no walls or foundations. No roads, no bridges, no fences. No signs of any tool-using presence at all. To the unaided eye, this world is untouched by any kind of builder. You could walk across the plateau and not find a single straight line or symmetrical corner that has not been produced by accidental fracture.

And yet, if you look more closely at the vegetation itself, you begin to suspect something else.

On the undersides of the thicker mats of growth, where you can lift them away from the stone—they are not hard to pry loose; they come up like heavy sheets of rubber or sponge—small formations are tucked into tiny recesses in the rock surface. These are not roots of the usual sort; they are thin threads of metallic color, branching out like the dendrites of neurons, weaving into microscopic fractures in the stone. They don’t draw up water; there is none. They draw up something else.

A reductive trace.

Some of the thicker threads are thicker than your index finger, and if you trace their path upward from the stone into the dark mat of growth above, you can see the faint ripple of motion along them, slow but real. Not like blood flowing, but like something being pulled across in tiny pulses. You can feel a faint warmth through them, if you hold them in your hand for a time.

Over the seasons of a world, you might imagine that this process would be slow and silent. But the world itself is not moving through time in the ordinary way. The vegetation does not grow in the way you are used to thinking of growth—no seasonal cycles, no bloom and wither. Instead, each patch of growth seems to be working out a particular pattern of minerals and light and non-aqueous chemistry, and that pattern is being fixed, like the hardening of a hundred layers of slow varnish.

If you follow your gaze along the edge of the plateau where it meets the sky, you notice that the sky itself does not change. No clouds drift. The light does not shift toward morning or evening. Even if you were to stay here for hours, your shadow would not lengthen much. The world seems to be in a strange kind of frozen balance, a time-invariant equilibrium where nothing happens quickly but everything is inching toward some eventual configuration that has not yet arrived.

And yet there is industry.

You cannot see it directly from the ground. But if you were to shape a way to look from the air—you might imagine an aircraft, or a satellite, or simply your own elevated understanding—you would see that the vegetation on the plateau is not distributed randomly at all. The patterns of the mats are too precise, too regular to be entirely accidental. They follow the curves of underlying mineral bands, orbiting around areas of high metal concentration in spirals that are almost like production lines.

On the ground, you notice something else. When the wind does not blow at all, when there are no animals to stir the air, when there are no falling leaves or shifting dust, you begin to see that many of the larger growths have been shaped into rings of precise thickness. The rings are not like any machinery you know—no gears, no cogs, no engines in the mechanical sense. But if you were to trace the path of a particular metallic thread through the mat of plant matter, you might find that it travels through certain regions that are darker than others, where reactions are taking place that are chemical, but only distantly connected to the water-chemistry of life on Earth.

These regions are like factories, but they are not factories built of steel and concrete. They are factories of matter itself.

The dark threads rise from the stone below and into these thicker zones of vegetation, where the temperature is slightly higher than the surrounding rock—measurable only if you had a thermometer, but your skin can feel the gentle difference. The mats are warm not because the world is hot, but because they are generating and maintaining their own internal processes of transformation and reduction.

These are industrial engines in the sense of being engines of transformation. They take something from the raw stone, and bit by bit, they convert it into something else. The new material is pushed out along the outer edges of the mats, slowly laid down in thin layers, like the slow extrusion of a new kind of substrate from the rim of a growing machine. You cannot see the motion; it is too slow to watch with the eye alone. But if you were to return after years—you somehow imagine that you could not—you would see that the edges of the mats have all advanced by exactly the same distance, in exactly the same pattern of rings and spirals.

This is not mechanization in the sense of steam engines or factories with smoke stacks and conveyor belts. It is more fundamental than that. It is the world itself being turned into a machine—without builders, without tools, without any conscious mind making it happen.

The signs of this industrial process are subtle. You begin to see that the stone surfaces between the growths are not entirely random either. In some places, carved lines have been etched into the rock—no more than a few millimeters deep, but perfectly straight or gently curved, like pencil marks drawn by a ruler. They angle between patches of vegetation, intersect at precise nodes, and terminate at regions where the metallic threads of the growths have dipped into shallow fractures in the rock surface. You can imagine some kind of fluid or process of crystallization being guided along these lines, but there is no fluid you can see.

The soundscape of this place reinforces the sense of industry at rest, and not fully formed yet.

The faintest of hums is sometimes noticeable if you put your ear close to the thicker mats of growth. It is not a steady sound; it comes in irregular pulses, like slow heartbeat, but only once every minute or two each. It is not loud enough to be comfortable, but too quiet to be alarming; you can only barely hear it when the wind is not stirring at all—and here, there is no wind to stir.

Occasionally, when you are very close to one of the dark regions within the mat—where the temperature is slightly higher than normal—you might hear a tiny click, like the cooling of a hot stone that someone has dropped into water—except that there is no water here. The sound is sharp and brief but isolated, like a single word recorded in a vast empty room.

Other than that, the world is silent.

The industrial quality of this place is not the result of any obvious technological civilization. It feels more like the world itself has been designed to be industrial in its very nature, from the ground up. The rocks are not just rocks, they are raw materials for the slow-growing factories of the vegetation. The air itself seems to be part of the system—a thin but perfectly balanced atmosphere that can maintain the temperature within a narrow range, without storms or even weather in the usual sense.

The light of the sun is not necessarily artificial, but something is not quite right about it. The vegetation does not seem to care about the angle of incidence; whether the sun is high or low—and you somehow imagine it would be high all the time—the growths are evenly distributed in their patterns, regardless of slope orientation. On the plateau, there is no concentrated shading of one side of the valley by the mountains, because there are no valleys, in the usual sense of water-carved gullies and channel beds. There are only raised sections and lower ones, and the vegetation covers the flatter conditions of both.

The world is not only emerging but pre-formed. Its mountains are un-smoothed, its rivers not yet run. There is no fauna of any kind at this point, no animals to disturb the geometry of the growing mats of vegetation. No predator tracks in the soft, fibrous growth; no grazer teeth marks along the edges of the mats. You kneel and see that some of the older mats are covered in a faint layer of dust, but when you brush it away, you notice that the dust is not organic, in the usual sense of rot and decay and shedding of bodily parts. It is more like fine fragments of the mineral threads that have been shed from the upper layers of the growth as they hardened into something else.

If you look at the world from this vantage point and ask yourself what you are looking at, you might come to a conclusion that is unsettling but somehow not terrifying:

This is not a landscape that has been ruined by industry or constructed by it; it is a landscape that is being prepared to be industrial by its very nature, from the ground up.

Perhaps you imagine that this plateau is part of a larger region being prepared to be a factory-world, where the vegetation itself is the first machine. The growths are not yet self-contained and fully operational, but their pattern is already beginning to resemble the layout of a factory floor: rings of production lines, nodes of transformation, thick hubs of intense reaction connected to thinner feeder lines. The stone below them is not being consumed by erosion but by being processed into something else.

Perhaps you imagine that the world is not done yet.

Perhaps you imagine that, in the future, when everything else arrives here, this plateau will be covered in machines much more familiar than these slow-growing factories of vegetation. But for now, there are only these silent mats of dark fibrous life, turning warm rock under warm sunlight into something that is not rock yet, not water, not air, but some other form of matter whose name has not been invented yet.

And in the unvarying light of this high, open, empty, and not-quite-real world, you walk alone on the soft surface of the future factory floor, your footsteps making no sound that matters to the slow machines already growing in the stone beneath you.
