The air of the Maw-City of Kavsara is a thick spice of dust and bone and hot, dry metal.

Here is one of the hollow gods of the world.

---

On the high plateaus of the continent you would find nothing of this—only wind-honed rock and the thin, salt-crust lakes of sallow water that the Kavsara call “false tears.” No rain for years, sometimes decades. The world’s living water is hidden far below, in wells and forgotten aquifers and the deep, reluctant veins of the planet itself.

But Kavsara does not drink rain.

Kavsara drinks the sky.

---

The city is a single, monumental machine carved into the throat of a collapsed mountain: a wide, circular sinkhole three kilometers across, its walls stepped down in terraces and galleries and stacked warrens of stone and baked clay. The top rim is a broken ring of jagged peaks, like black teeth biting the heavens. From the air, if anything could fly here, the Maw would appear as a dark eye staring blindly upward.

No river enters the bowl. No streams trickle over its lip. The stone is dry to the touch, polished to a dull, metallic shine by generations of palms and shoulders and cloth. The lowest streets are warm underfoot at all times; from narrow cracks in the bedrock floor, faint heat slips up, like breath from the planet’s sleeping lungs.

---

### Architecture: The City Within a Single Thing

At first glance you’d think the city is huge, but an hour of walking reveals it is not. You cross markets, climb stairways, pass through arches, and realize you are still only halfway down one curve of the bowl. The sense of vastness comes from depth and repetition and exacting precision, not from breadth.

The architecture is primitive but not crude. No metal frames support the walls; no engines smooth the stone. The city is carved and piled and shaped by hand tools:

- Chisels of bronze and bone,  
- Saws of wire and sand,  
- Grinders of polished riverstone,  
- Pulleys of rope and dried sinew.

Generation after generation of Kavsarans have been cutting at the same single hole in the world, their homes, their factories, and their shrines all part of the same bloody architecture.

All the stone is the same color—a deep basalt black, slightly veined with green where rare minerals show—and it is everywhere. You don’t see streets of cobble or flagstone; you see streets where the bedrock has been quarried directly into the predesigned depth of the floor, so the floor *is* the road. The retaining walls of the terraces are not added blocks; they are the living mountain cut into shelves and left to support itself.

Roots wring out of the walls in living fringes, where the city’s hidden ecosystem of climbing plants insinuates itself into the cracks. The foliage is dense but not wild: there are no forests in the Maw, no trees to speak of—only a thick tapestry of vines and low shrubs with waxy leaves and small, hard fruit that smell like rust and camphor when crushed.

This vegetation is not accidental water-drinkers. It is part of the machine.

---

### Sound: The Deafening Voice of the Maw

You hear Kavsara before you see it.

From any distance, the island of sound rises around the horizon like a storm. You think the wind must be howling in a cave of impossible size—but then you realize the wind itself is silent.

The noise comes from the air.

---

At the center of the bowl, suspended in a grid of great bronze rings and bone-white cables, is the Heart-Bell—

—a hollow sphere of hammered copper and bronze three times the height of a person, hung like a throatless larynx in the open air of the Maw. It is not struck by clappers or hammers; it does not need to be struck.

The Bell is open top and bottom. Its skin is pierced by thousands of narrow, parallel holes like a colossal flute or a deep-bore pipe organ.

The wind of the world is forced through the Maw’s geometry and funneled into the Heart-Bell, and when the wind blows—as it almost always does—the air shrieks through the perforations, producing a single note sustained for hours or days at a time:

A deep, mortal drone that never wins.

It is not loud in the way of thunder or explosions. It is *absolute*—a sound that becomes the absence of silence. You stop hearing it after a while, and then you stop hearing much of anything else.

The Bell’s tone is, for the Kavsara, the voice of their god and the prayer of their world. It articulates the weather in the plains of the sky: the baritone for high winds over the plateau, the thin, edge-of-hearing keen for days of calm, the subtle microtonal shifts that the city’s diviners read to forecast storms or months of drought.

---

Surrounding the Bell is a ring of smaller structures: the Pipes of Bone and Stone.

These are vertical shafts, open to the air and carved with internal resonant chambers like the lungs of a statue. They are arranged in concentric circles of decreasing height and diameter—the smallest ones closest to the center. Each pipe produces a different pitch, depending on its length and the width of its internal windings.

The result is a polyphony that rises and falls around the Heart-Bell’s unwavering drone:

- High, metallic whispers like falling knives,  
- Deep, groaning notes that feel more in your chest than your ears,  
- Furious trills when wind eddies whirl through the shorter apertures,  
- The sigh of air rushing through small holes carved into walkway railings.

---

Further away from the center, at the rim of the Maw, the noise of the city’s own mechanisms adds to the soundscape:

- Bronze wind chimes hanging from every lintel and stairway—they are not decorative ornaments. They are alarm bells set to oscillate in specific resonance patterns when the city wind changes; their sound *means* something to the priests who listen to them.
- Carved horns that project from the city walls into the basin, like listening ears aimed backward into the city itself. They capture and amplify stray voices, market-calls, and ritual songs, sending them into the bowl, where they blend into the wind’s chorus.
- The purposeful scraping of tools against stone; the city is always being repaired or rebuilt, sections of wall remade into new, larger horns or chambers.
- The rhythmic slapping of bare feet on polished stone stairways, as messengers and singers and work-children are sent to adjust wind-catchments or redirect sound flows.

---

### The Wind of the City: The City’s Blood That Isn’t Water

The wind of Kavsara is not water, but it behaves like water.

The Maw is shaped like a huge bowl—but its wind-exits, dug into the circumference walls, are not symmetrical. They taper and narrow and twist upward and outward through the living rock, forming invisible channels for the air to follow.

When the sky winds blow over the plateau, they hit the encircling peaks, curl over the edge of the Maw, and plunge down the inner walls in a spiral of pressure. The geometry of the city’s galleries and terraces guides the wind not like streets but like riverbanks and dams and locks.

---

Everywhere, there are wind-traps.

Some are simple stone channels, open-mouthed and carved like flared hands that reach out to catch the downward air and redirect it horizontally into galleries. Others are thin screens of perforated bronze or tightly woven sinew-and-bone mesh that squeal and hum when the wind passes through.

In the long, deep arcades along the lower walls, you can feel sections of floor warmer than others, with air rushing up through grills as if from a furnace. The city’s heat is the earth’s heat; it rises and pulls the cooler air from the rim above, creating its own body-scale circulation that burbles through corridors and rooms.

There are no doors in the way you’d know them—no hinges, no locking panels. Only thresholds of different sound and pressure and warmth.

You step from one zone of air to another and feel the wind change velocity and direction, your body becoming the probe of the city’s weather.

---

### The Vegetation: The City’s Lungs and Mirrors

The lushness of Kavsara is not fertile in the sense of fat earth and deep roots.

The vegetation is sparse but omnipresent: thin, hardy leaves in every crack and on every terrace where wind dust and occasional heat-driven moisture condense. The city’s greenery is composed of drought-resistant species:

- **Wind-gourd vines** with coiled leaves, like tightened springs, storing water in matte black bulbs that swing when the wind pulls them.  
- **Drum-rushes**, whose hollow stems vibrate in the constant wind and produce a faint percussive background—like rain that never hits the ground.  
- **Glass-shrubs**, whose leaves are translucent, brittle as thin sheets of ice, and form small lenses that focus sunlight onto panels of obsidian polished into mirrors.  
- **Root-moss**, whose fine filaments insinuate themselves into the smallest pores of the basalt, extracting trace minerals and moisture and leaving a faint greenish patina on everything.

---

There are also trees, but they are deliberately stunted and shaped. The Kavsaran call them *ora-sah*—“wind-sons.” They grow from the narrowest ledges, their roots exposed and trained to spiral down rock walls into artificial basins where runoff dust gathers.

The ora-sah trees bloom with small, hard flowers that smell like iron and ash when crushed; their seedpods are shaped like aerodynamic gliders, and they release their seeds only when the wind reaches a certain velocity and direction, so that they are launched from the bowl’s rim into the distant plateau.

---

The vegetation is not allowed to grow uncontrolled. The city’s sound must be precise; the wind must pass through the instruments, not through a chaotic mess of growths. Therefore, pruning and shaping are civic duties.

The rooflines of the terraces are carved into shallow troughs that collect any condensation that forms during the night and direct it to the planted ledges. The work of hauling water—even such small amounts of it—is done by hand. Children carry clay jars up the steps and pour water not into cups but into the mouths of semi-dry root networks.

Everything is watered not because of the rain’s bounty but because of a tiny, persistent reclamation of every breath of humidity the city manages to trap.

---

### The Hydration of the City: Drinking Without Water

The Kavsara do not gather water in rivers or lakes. They gather it through air and through heat.

---

On the highest inward-facing terraces, there are **dew-screens**—thin membranes of waxed linen and silk stretched over frames of bone and metal wire. When the night wind drops in temperature, the relative humidity rises slightly, and a thin film of moisture collects on the screens.

The screens are textured by tight, microscopic fibers of cloth that draw the moisture into droplets. These droplets slide under gravity into small channels carved into the stone behind the frame, following a downward gradient of gravity and groove.

---

At lower levels, the **wind-pools** function differently.

They are not pools of liquid but basins of faint heat and humidity, surrounded by walls and roofs that force the wind through narrow chokes and sudden expansions. The air temperature in the basins increases slightly but the geometry of the space creates a pressure difference that encourages condensation out of the heated air.

When the wind shifts at night and the outside air grows colder, the warmer walls of the basins condense fog onto their surfaces. The walls are veined with shallow carved channels that catch the condensation and run it into narrow cisterns carved from the bedrock itself.

---

Drinking water is stored in deep, narrow shafts drilled into the rock floor of the lower terraces. Called **silos of silence**, they are sealed with thick bronze disks and engraved with resonant patterns that discourage the passage of sound through the water, as if any noise would disturb its purity.

---

The Kavsara also drink:

- **Wind-paste**: a thick, translucent gel made from shreds of wind-gourd, mixed with dust from the air and ground limestone, then preserved in cool niches near the heat-cracks of the bedrock floor. It is eaten with small wooden spoons or smeared on bread baked from wind-grass seeds.  
- **Drum-rush syrup**: harvested from the juices of Drum-rush stems, collected drop by drop into sealed jars and stored underground for years.

---

Everywhere the water, rare as it is, becomes part of a ritual economy.

You offer water to the carved wind-gods at the corner of every terrace—you bring a single cup and you pour *past* the stone, into the air, where the wind scatters it into invisible droplets and takes it away. You are not feeding the stone god; you are feeding the wind.

---

### The Fauna: Rare, But Everywhere in the City’s Prayer

Animals are rare in the classic sense; there are no herds of beasts roaming nearby plains, because nearby plains are empty scrublands and salt fields where nothing grows in large, continuous stands.

But animals are not absent from the city.

---

The **shriek-wings** are the most common fauna you notice: small birds with thin, translucent wings and long, needle-shaped beaks. They are not songs of bird but songs of *bone*—their hollow-rod bones are pierced by air holes, and when they fly in the strong winds of the Maw, their wings produce high, thin whistles.

The shriek-wings nest in the walls of the Heart-Bell and its surrounding rings, where the wind-speed is highest. They feed on insects and dust that collects in the city’s niches, and they are tolerated by the Kavsara because they also eat insects that might otherwise hurt the wind-gourds and drum-rushes.

---

There are **hand-worms** too—fleshy, segmented invertebrates that live in the deep heat-cracks of the bedrock. They have no eyes but many sensitive fingers along their body, and they move slowly through the narrow stone tunnels where it is too warm and dry for almost anything else.

Every year, during the festival of the Deep Wind, the city’s priests lure them out with sound. By drumming on specific sections of floor, they compel the hand-worms to gather and surface, where a thin layer of condensation has formed in the darkness of the night. The worms are allowed to drink first, then the people after.

They are rare. You might see ten on your whole journey through the city—but they are sacred, because it is believed they guide the moisture of the deep earth upward by the simple fact of their bodies forcing heat and moisture into the cracks where humans can’t reach.

---

There are also people.

### The People of the Sound-City

The Kavsara are tall and thin-limbed, their skin dark and patchwork from where it has been burned by unseen wind and sun and then reshaped by scarification patterns that mimic wind-lines.

They wear layered garments of thin, tightly-woven linen, dyed in patterns of black and pale green that reflect the city’s color palette of stone and rare leaf. They walk barefoot or in thin sandals, their soles hardened to the polished stone floor.

---

Their society is built around sound and memory.

Because nothing here is written in the way you would recognize, there is no great paper archive or library of texts. Instead there is **the archive of the ear**.

Consistory rooms of enormous circular stone chambers, each with a different geometry of amplifying and diffracting walls, are scattered throughout the city. Each hall corresponds to a particular domain of knowledge—laws of the city, wind measurements, cadastral records of who owns what section of terrace wall, myths of the deep heat.

---

When you enter the Hall of Judgments, for example, you are brought into a circular space with a high ceiling bored into a complex pattern of holes and channels. The wind enters through one side and produces a different dominant sound for each day of the year.

You stand before judges and avowed speakers, and they speak your case into the hall. The walls of the chamber are designed to reflect sound waves in such a way that certain phrases and frequencies are reinforced, others suppressed.

You hear your own voice amplified or distorted, depending on what parts of your statement the city judges to be “true” or “false.” The priests are trained to read the distortions of the echo, and they speak their verdicts back into the wind so that the city itself participates in the judgment.

---

There are no schools like children’s rooms of wooden desks. Instead, there are **walking classrooms**.

Groups of children and apprentices are paired with mentors who lead them up and down the city’s many paths, listening:

- *Here, where the wind hits the western wall at three different angles, you can hear the three gods of the sky arguing over the direction of the season.*  
- *This gallery of bronze mirrors heats the air near this wall; you hear the faint crackling of the glass-shrubs warming up.*  
- *This terrace of carved wind-screens—notice the sound of the wind is different in the morning than in the evening.*  

---

They are taught to distinguish the different timbres of wind:

- The **death-sound** of a wind that comes too dry and from the wrong angle, threatening to desiccate the city.  
- The **mother-sound** of wind saturated just enough to bring moisture to the dew screens.  
- The **tool-sound** of wind that is strong enough to be used in turning certain windmills but not so strong that it threatens the Heart-Bell.

---

Their religion is not anthropomorphic but respiratory.

The Kavsara believe that the planet itself breathes through the Maw of Kavsara and a handful of other similar sites scattered across the world. They do not think of their god as a person but as a huge breath that cycles perpetually from the deep earth to the high sky and back again.

The city’s job is to maintain the throat of the god—clear its wind channels of debris and overgrowth, keep its sound pure and steady, and not claim water from the air that belongs elsewhere.

---

### The Daily Life of a Machine as a Single Living Being

The city’s day cycle is driven by sound.

At dawn, when the wind overhead shifts from the cold of night to the warmer air of morning, the Heart-Bell changes its tone slightly—a deep drop in pitch or a subtle harmonic overtone that the city’s attuned listeners interpret as a signal.

---

The **Chorus of Opening** occurs:

Priests in the lower galleries pull on certain ropes and chains that redirect a series of wind-catchments into new paths. The shifting wind entering through these paths produces a series of short, sharp notes that echo around the city bowl like a fanfare.

---

The **Clearance of Tongues** begins.

The city’s wind-channels are not allowed to clog with dust and leaves; they are cleaned every day. The cleaning of the wind-paths is not done by flooding but by scraping and brushing and carefully sounding the note of each channel before and after cleaning.

Each channel of wind has its own characteristic sound when empty, and if any one of them sounds dull or muffled, the workers know that there is debris or blockages somewhere inside the wall’s hidden heart. They use sound to locate and clear the problem.

---

The **Feeding of Black Leaves** is the third great cycle of the day.

Black glass-shrubs are tended to in the morning hours when the sun is not yet direct—but their heat-mirrors are already positioned by careful workers with simple bronze tools and polished stone reflectors. The sunlight refracted through the glass leaves is focused onto plates of basalt which heat slowly.

When the heat becomes sufficient, the city’s cooks use these surfaces to bake thin wafers of bread made from wind-grass seeds.

---

The **Silence Hour** occurs midday.

When the sun is highest and the heat is strongest, the wind of the world often slows for a short time. The intensity of the Heart-Bell’s drone weakens, and a deep, quiet interval fills the basin of the city.

In this time, people lie down. They lie on their floors with their ears near cracks or hollows in the stone walls and listen to those distant places of underground sound: the faint murmur of heat-cracks deep below the city floor, the rustle of unseen roots, the occasional whisper of air passing through empty channels in the spine of the mountain itself.

---

The **Second Chorus** occurs when the wind returns with evening.

The wind-pool basins are checked; their surfaces are inspected for any condensation that might have formed overnight. The city’s smallest children are given the task of collecting dew droplets on the topside screens with soft brushes made of feather and bone, then squeeing them into irrigation jars.
