The ground sighs.

It’s a slow sound, a long-drawn out breath of compressed air bubbling up through water and muck and concrete, then dragging itself out of a broken pavement crack. Every few minutes it comes again: the city exhaling.

Below you, the streets are mostly submerged.

This is not a disaster; it is a condition. The metropolis has, for a long time now, been in the process of becoming a shallow, salt-tinged bay. The sea never quite stormed in. Instead it seeped up, through multiple layers of broken foundations and collapsed tunnels, until the water level and the ground level found a kind of uneasy compromise. You stand on one of the few elevated sections of the city — a low swell of land where the streets still dry off between rains and a few old buildings cling stubbornly to their height advantage.

To the east, the low, sunken portion of town is no longer street at all but a network of sluggish channels. The water is not clear enough to be called blue, nor dark enough to be black. It is the color of a shadow under a wet stone — a silverish, greenish lead-gray — and it is never perfectly still. Tiny currents nudge at the edges of broken windows half submerged, riffle the tattered edges of plastic sheeting that still cling to rusted frames. There are no waves, exactly. Just a slow breathing of the surface: small, oily fish breaking the water with their lips, the flick of a tail, a ripple that travels from some unknown disturbance below.

The humidity is a physical presence. It doesn’t rain right now, but it feels like it just did and is just about to again. The air smells warm and wet: salt, damp concrete, rusted metal, the faint mud of river bottom. If it were day, the sky would be a low ceiling of gray-white, but since you say “light: 1.2/3 — dim,” it is probably that heavy hour before dawn or that long fading after dusk, when the world has muted itself but not yet gone into darkness.

Down the hill, where the streets disappear into the water, the buildings are mostly truncated: three, four, five stories high, but with their lower halves submerged. The glass of their windows gives back a weak, reflected shine of the sky. Some of the panes are broken inward, where debris has been thrown or where water pressure has finally burst the frames. Some windows are completely intact, and behind the glass there are only hints of shapes inside — black rectangles that might be rooms, or might be something else resting.

The water’s surface is interrupted by the tops of things that have not yet fully yielded: metal signposts still proclaiming street names, rusted-out cars that only show their roofs and the upper curve of their windows, like some strange line of steel turtles sleeping in formation. The saltwater and rust and time have turned these into strange sculptures of orange and white and brown, crusts of salt and corrosion slowly creeping over them.

Above you, the city is dark and quiet and, surprisingly, alive. Not with plants — there are none of those to speak of — but with birds.

This is the first overwhelming presence you feel.

You hear the steady sound first: a damp rustling of wings and feathers, the low murmur of many thousands of bodies rearranging themselves on ledges and sills. The buildings are covered with them — ibises and crows and gulls and others that have lost their names and are now just city birds, black and gray and white shapes against the darker concrete of the facades. Every cornice, every broken piece of decorative molding, every air-conditioning unit that has not yet fallen into the water has been claimed by birds.

They are not flying now, but they are restless. They shift on narrow feet, stretch necks, shake out waterlogged feathers. Their droppings streak down the walls in white and gray and brown, like long vertical tears of chalk. Somewhere a block away, a single bird call sounds out — three sharp notes, then silence — but otherwise they are mostly quiet, just as the parameter suggests: not singing, just waiting.

When you move, even slightly, a thousand eyes turn. You feel the immense, weightless shift of attention from every window ledge and broken edge. You are not hidden here. This is their city; you are just walking through it.

Down the hill, where the water starts, there are more birds than you can easily count. They stalk the shallow edges of the submerged streets. Some stand on rusted cars, their reflections fractured into pieces of leg and beak and wing by the uneven ripples of the water. Some wade carefully into the shallows, where the water creeps up their thin legs, and stare with heads tilted into the darkness, looking for fish.

The fish are here too — a second overwhelming fauna, though less visible. They move in the dark just below the surface, small and quick, their scales catching the pale light in thin flashes of silver and dark-green. When a fish rises too high to snap at something floating on the surface, the water opens with a soft, swallowing sound. Then it is quiet again.

Between the birds and the fish and the insects and whatever else you know but do not see clearly, there is a sensation of being surrounded by creatures without overcrowding. The space is large enough — the open scale of city and water and sky — that there is still emptiness between presences, but the presences themselves are thick and constant. You can feel the weight and rhythm of their life.

Industrial life hasn’t fully gone, but it has faded into something slow and dull and automatic.

Many blocks away, on the far side of the water-logged district, there is a factory complex: square, low buildings of brick and corrugated metal, a few stubby chimneys still standing against the skyline. A thin trail of whitish smoke or steam rises from one of them and dissolves quickly into the heavy air. You cannot hear any engines from here, but you know from your approach to this place that there is still electricity running in some of the buildings — a low, humming vibration under the sound of the birds and the water.

At night, or in this dim light, you can see the faint orange of reflected lights on the underside of a low cloudbank from that direction. Some machines still turn to keep themselves warm, but not many people work there. The factory has become a different kind of organism: slow, rhythmic, breathing out heat and waste and occasionally a new part of itself to install into the remaining grid of the city.

Closer, where the water begins, there are hints of older industrial work: rusted pipelines that have partially broken and sprung from the ground like roots, vents that still cough out warm steam from some deep invisible mechanism below. This heat is why the temperature never feels cool even when the sky is dark and empty. The city is generating and trapping its own warmth.

The ground itself is a strange kind of aquifer. You can feel the water not very far below the surface when you walk on it. The concrete slabs shift very slightly under your feet, unevenly. Some slabs are cracked into neat patterns, like broken tiles. Some have broken into pieces and sagged into the water entirely, leaving only rough edges like teeth.

In the streets that are only partially wet, the water level in some cracks actually changes: you watch the reflection of the sky widen and then narrow as if it were breathing. This is the slow dance of tide and groundwater and the pumping system somewhere deep down that still tries to keep itself working.

Erosion has been here long enough to smooth out the edges of the debris into soft shapes. The broken corners of concrete steps are rounded now, their corners knocked off by years and water and the feet of thousands (if not more) of birds passing back and forth. The rusted metal of handrails and lampposts has been thinned and flaked, so that instead of sharp edges you see fragile lattices, like handmade lace. Some walls have been stained into patterns that seem almost deliberately designed by the rain and salt — fan-like curves of darker and lighter gray, where water has run repeatedly in the same channels.

The vertical face of the city is still strong, but the ground level is visibly failing in some places. You can see depressions where streetlights have begun to lean slightly, their overhead arms still pointing out over the water like long iron fishing rods. The ground beneath them has been softened away by time — water seeping quietly from one side of the street to another, the salt eating and dissolving and rearranging.

The sound of the city is almost gentle. Most of the time you hear only the water’s soft murmur against itself, the occasional drip of condensation from a broken pipe above, or a faint click from a rusted joint settling somewhere you cannot see. There are no cars anymore here in this district. No sirens. No music coming from distant windows.

The birds are mostly quiet too — just restless shuffling and the occasional soft call. When they do take off in a flock from a building, you feel the sudden rush of air and their wings beating, but even then the sound is not loud, just… thick. It comes like a sudden brief river of sound through the otherwise flat air, and then it is gone again and you are left with the sigh of the ground and the slow breathing of the water.

The temperature makes you aware of your own body. The air is warm enough to be slightly oppressive, but the water on the skin of your face or arms gives a faint trace of cool. When a wind moves — a rare, slow motion of air — it feels like a shift of warm wet cloth over the surface of your skin.

Weirdness is low here, but that doesn’t mean the place is ordinary. It’s just that nothing breaks the rules of the world you already understand. There are no impossible lights. No singular impossibly huge thing looming over the city. No floating geometry in the sky. Just the slow, strange but normal combination of a city that has not been torn down but has been softened by water and time and left to be inhabited by creatures who don’t mind the wet or the rust as long as there is food and a place to rest.

The spaces are open, but not empty in the way of a desert. There is distance. You can look down several long blocks where the buildings are still partially standing above the water and see the sky framed between them like a long gray strip. The horizon line — where the water meets the strip of buildings — is broken, uneven, but it gives you a sense of the region. This low section of the city is not all of the world, but it is a large district of it, and beyond the channels you know there are still land areas where the water level is lower and the factories are stronger and people are still trying (or trying less hard) to keep the old machine of the world turning.

Humans are not absent here, but they are discreet. You catch sight of one now — or more likely a hint: a faint light from a window on the third story of a building whose lower floor is submerged, maybe someone watching the water from a safe height. Or a thin line of smoke from a small makeshift stove on the roof of another building, where someone has dragged out a few salvaged pieces of furniture and a tattered tarp.

You don’t see the person clearly, if you see them at all. You might only catch the movement: a shadow passing across the window glass, then stillness again. The people here are like the birds — frequenting particular places, but not loud, not seeking out confrontation.

The rules of this world are simple:

- Water makes things slow.  
- Time makes things soft.  
- Warmth keeps life busy and moving.  
- Light comes from some low, distant source — a sun seen through thick clouds, or city lights reflected off of them — but always dim enough to keep everything in half-revelation.

And the creatures — the fish and the birds and whatever smaller insects and crustaceans scurry in the dark and the wet — are the ones who decide the pace of things now. They occupy the vertical structures of the city in a way that humans never could, walking the edges of the concrete as if it were their own custom-built habitat.

As you stand on the slightly elevated ground, watching, the ground sighs again under your feet. This time, a small surge of water pushes out from between cracked concrete to your left and runs into an already-dark channel, joining itself to the shallow water of the city street. There is a faint sound, like the very faintest echo of a machine turning far below, and then the water level settles down an inch and then another inch, as if something has been decided down in the depths of the city’s unreached basement.

From the nearby buildings, a single bird takes off with a soft clatter of wings and then drops itself into the dark channel, where it immediately becomes a calm, floating shape with just its head above water, looking like any of the broken fragments of the city’s surface.

The world here is mostly water, mostly covers, mostly creatures, and mostly quiet — but absolutely not dead. It is just breathing differently than you expected.
