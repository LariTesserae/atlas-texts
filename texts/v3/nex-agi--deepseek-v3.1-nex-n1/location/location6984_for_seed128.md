You find yourself at the far edge of a massive plate that holds the city’s last cooling tower. The concrete is old, not by human years, but by the measure of the city’s own breath: it has been here exactly as long as it intends to be.

Under your feet the surface is neither cold nor hot, but holds your weight exactly, barely yielding, like skin over a muscle. The air is warm and wet. The humidity is just high enough that every breath feels faintly thick, like stepping into a bathroom after someone has showered long and hard. There is no rain, but the surface glows faintly with uneven moisture, as if the structure itself sweats quietly.

---

This is not a tower in the old human sense—it doesn’t vanish into the sky. It is a system of nested, hollow cylinders that descend into the earth. The complex you stand on is the topmost valve of that machine, a circular platform perhaps three city blocks wide, but it feels more like a room than a plaza.

The edge is not open air: it is sealed by a translucent wall that rises several meters, then curves inward in a smooth, inward-leaning rim. Beyond the wall the world is only dark and wet noise—you hear it, you feel it in your chest, but you do not see it.

---

The floor of the platform is built of a  material that doesn’t map cleanly to stone or metal. It has been with the city longer than any stone or steel could last. It is deeply settled—nothing wobbles, nothing creaks. There are no obvious cracks, but the surface is not smooth: it is patterned by countless shallow channels, like veins worn into a wrist by endless years of work.
