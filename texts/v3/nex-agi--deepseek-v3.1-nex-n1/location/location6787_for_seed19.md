The plateau of Cael-Himir is not a place of change or striving; it is the final sentence of a world that has exhausted all its verbs.

From a distance the mountains look like the teeth of a vast jawbone clamped into the sky. The stone of the peaks is not gray, but bone-white and ivory-black, threaded with faint iridescence where the minerals have been compressed by Time into something that still counts as crystalline. The air at this elevation is thin, sharp, and chill, but not in the way one associates with cold storms or snow; the cold here is the absence of heat, rather than the presence of anything else. It tastes like dust and ozone and the faint metallic flavor of a low-grade, permanent aurora.

Height is the first thing that imposes itself on you. You are so high that the horizon below you is not a line of land but a curve of cloudless sky—endless blue, shading without ceremony into the black of deep space. You are floating above the world on a raft of rock that was once a mountain but now is more a plateau, a great altar slab of stone that Time has pushed and scraped and polished until it is almost smooth.

---

## Terrain and stone

The ground itself is dense. This is not the loose scree of young mountains or the soft dust of low deserts. The rock of Cael-Himir is compacted to a point where it rings faintly when struck with metal or bone—a sound like striking porcelain. The surface of the plateau is not flat but crowded with subtle forms: low ridges, shallow basins, faint furrows. The rock is laced with geometric veins of minerals, arranged in polygons and hexagons that have no apparent geological explanation—no cracking from frost, no drying from water—only the pressure and geometry of a fundamentally different sort of solid matter.

The stone is layered with strata after strata of ancient events compressed into thin lines of color: bands of bone-white, ash-gray, and faint glass-green; threads of copper-red that are not oxidized but still metallic; deeper seams of something dark like obsidian, but with the faint smokiness of quartz instead. There are occasional disc-shaped inclusions of a darker stone that are not quite circular and not quite random—they read like places where something else briefly existed and then was compressed out of existence.

Erosion has been at work for so long that there is nothing else for it to do. The plateau has long since passed through youthful jaggedness, mature valleys and rivers, and even old, rounded hills. What remains is a final form of landscape—a landscape that has settled into a configuration in which there is almost nowhere for energy to go. No significant slope gradients, no sharp cliffs left to fail, no loose sediments for wind to move. The stone has been smoothed until the last few irregularities interact like stones in a tightly-packed jar: they cannot move much without all others moving.

This is erosion as pure inertia: what has fallen has fallen; what has eroded has eroded; what remains is locked in place.

## Atmosphere and light

The light is normal—brutally so.

There is a sun in this sky, but it is not warm. Its light is pale, clear, and very precise. It falls on the plateau with a constant angle of incidence and intensity that has not changed in longer than any species still remembers. The sun’s disk is a small, precise circle of white, not yellow or gold or red. It seems to sit at perpetual, motionless noon, without rising or setting to any meaningful degree. The stars that are visible during the day—this high above any atmosphere that might scatter them away—are small, stationary, and unwavering. They do not twinkle. They do not move. They look like dimples punched into the sky.

The sky is less blue than it is a deep, translucent color—closer to the bottom of an ocean than the top of an atmosphere. The air is thin but not transparent; it has weight. It looks like you could scoop it up with a hand if it were thicker.

The plateau of Cael-Himir is not a place of rainbow or haze. Its light is clean and sobering. It reveals the stone of the plateau and the impossible entities that occupy it without mercy or ambiguity.

## Sound and silence

There is almost no sound.

The wind at this altitude is strong enough to be constant, but not strong enough to be noisy. It is a thin, continuous presence of motion against your skin rather than a roar in your ears. It rustles nothing—there are no trees, no leaves, no grass to whisper—and so it passes over the rock without producing the songs of a young world.

The stone itself does not resonate. The rock underfoot is too dense, too strongly bonded, to allow vibration to travel far. Footsteps fall on it as dull, compact thuds—if they are loud at all, which is rare, because the fauna here are careful not to disturb the silence.

Sound absorbs into the air like ink into paper. The great plateau is a silent stage for a vast performance of presence and absence.

## Waterlessness

The plateau of Cael-Himir does not have water. There is no ice, no snow, no dew, no rain, no hidden lakes, no puddles that have not yet evaporated.

There is not even the dryness of a desert—because deserts, at least, remember water. They store it in hidden pockets, or see it as a distant rumor of rain, or recall their rivers of a long-ago age. But Cael-Himir has forgotten water. If water ever existed here, it was as a brief error in the chemistry of the world—a mistake long since rectified.

The stone of the plateau is not porous in the way of rock that can hold water. It has been fused and compressed and reworked until its pores have been obliterated. The chemistry of this place is not water-based. There is no corrosion of iron by rust, no erosion by salt, no frost wedging—no movement of ions through a watery medium.

What replaces water here is not another fluid but a kind of solid geometry—compression and static charge and angular pressure. The minerals of the plateau’s stone are not arranged into crystals that have their structure because of hydrothermal solutions. They are arranged because of angles and interfaces and the necessity of packing matter into the least space available. The stone’s faces are flat where their crystal planes met; their edges are sharp where they could not afford to be round.

The absence of water is not an absence of something—it is a presence of something else. The air between your fingers and the stone feels subtly more rigid than it should, as if the world itself is tighter, tighter and tighter.

---

## Vegetation—what little of it

Vegetation is sparse, but it is there.

The plants of Cael-Himir do not use green energy or leaves. What might be called “plants” are low, hard, and mineral. The most common of them look like mazes of black glass rising a few fingers’ height from the stone. They are not crystals but colonies—something like the lichens of other worlds, but utterly mineral. The outer surfaces are thin shells of transparent silicate, enclosing a faintly darker smoke of fine matter inside.

They are attached to the rock by threads of fibrous mineral that are almost indistinguishable from the stone itself. They do not grow in the familiar sense; they propagate by placing new patterns of deposition around themselves, like a mathematical formula being played out in stone. If you break a glass-maze, you may find that its cross-section reveals a branching pattern that is not random but fractal—and yet each piece of the pattern is identical, not truly self-similar, but simply repeating. The texture of their “growth” is like that of a code running.

Because there is no water, these “plants” do not draw liquid from the ground. They obtain their matter and energy from subtle pressure differences between strata of rock and from static charges that accumulate across the plateau’s surface. Their metabolic processes are not organic chemistry; they are something like a solid-state chemistry that has never been cooled by water or disturbed by the motions of fluid.

They are not arranged in forests or meadows. There is no canopy of photosynthesis or spreading shade. The glass-mazes are patchy—sometimes you walk for minutes without seeing one, and then you come across a small “colony” where a dozen of them are crowded within a meter’s radius. In these small colonies, the structures of the glass-mazes almost touch—almost, but not quite. There is always a thin line of stone between them.

The vegetation is sparse, but it is not a weak appendage to the landscape. It is a structural part of it.

## Built things—the faint marks of who knows what

The built environment of Cael-Himir is not gone so much as it is dissolved.

The plateau is too old to preserve any standing architecture from the time of its builders, if builders there ever were. What remains are scattered and faint artifacts—partial footprints of engineering that have long since been eroded almost smooth.

The most common of these are faint disc-shaped depressions in the rock surface, sometimes only a step or two across, sometimes wider than a human height. The edges of these depressions are not sharp but gently graded, as if the stone has been melted by something more like pressure than heat. The inside surfaces of these discs are smoother than the outside plateau, and sometimes—very faintly—show residues of geometric patterns inlaid into the stone. These patterns are not carved or scratched but appear to have been printed into the crystal structure of the rock itself—threads of color so fine they cannot be seen with eyesight alone but reveal themselves under a microscope in the form of subtle color shifts.

The “discs” are not strictly arranged in any recognizable pattern, but they are not random either. They occupy something like a lattice, if the lattice is defined by a very long time interval that has allowed the stone to distort and drift. The relationship between discs is minuscule but perceptible: if you were to measure their positions accurately enough, you would find that they once sat on a grid whose spacing matched some obscure unit of measurement now known to no one living.

There are also pillars—or what remains of pillars. They are not towering columns of heroic architecture. They are low nubs of stone that have survived the endless erosion of the plateau because they were made of something harder than the surrounding rock. They are smooth to the touch, but show uneven colors of banding where they absorbed and then compressed material different from the local stone. They do not look like they ever supported roofs or statues; they are more like anchors of some kind—or beacons.

Some artifacts show faint inscriptions of a language no longer comprehensible. The inscriptions are not written in ink or pigment but in displaced lattice defects within the crystal structures of the stone—a script that exists as a subtle misalignment of atoms, rather than as ink or paint. The letters of this script are not linear but radial—rays of slight misalignment that emerge from centers and then branch outward, forming patterns like tree rings and ice fractals combined.

There are no tools left lying around. If there ever were tools or metal or wood, they have been completely eroded—or have been absorbed into the stone by whatever pressure has been at work here.

There are no buildings. No ruins. No fragments of walls or roofs. No intricate machinery of metal gears and glass. The built environment of Cael-Himir is not at the level of metal and fabric, but at the level of geology and crystal geometry. The plateau itself is the last artifact.

## Technology and primitiveness

The technological level of those who inhabited Cael-Himir—if they were inhabited, rather than constructed—is not easy to discern from what remains. But the traces suggest that whatever tools they used were not devices of metal and fire and industrial manufacture.

This world feels like the remains of a pre-metal or barely-metal civilization. Its artifacts do not involve rusted iron or tin bronze. The stone that surrounds them shows no signs of fire. There are no slag heaps, no ash layers, no soot stains on old ceilings, no charred layers of timber.

The technology that seems to have been at work here was more like a careful manipulation of crystal structure and pressure and chemical bonding. It is a technology that operates at the level of tiles and crystal interaction and geometric alignment. The disc-shaped depressions, for example, might have been platforms or interfaces for some kind of solid-state computing or communication—plates whose crystal lattice could be reprogrammed by pressure and temperature.

The tools required for such manipulation would have been simple—and “primitive” in the sense of not requiring complex machinery. But they would have been highly precise. The kind of tools that existed might have been:

- Stone wedges or chisels for precisely fracturing rock along natural planes.
- Small plates or tiles that could be placed on surfaces to transmit pressure or static charge.
- Vessels of nondescript stone—almost toys, by other worlds’ standards—but used to concentrate or redistribute faint charge differences between rocks.
- Crystal lenses that could not form images but could be aligned to transmit subtle patterns of light or heat.

The “tech” of Cael-Himir is primitive not because it is crude or weak, but because it operates in a very minimal way—at the lowest possible level of material and energy. It is like a world that mastered the alphabet of physics before inventing the printing press.

## Fauna—the pressing, motionless multitude

And yet despite all of this—despite the absence of water, the sparsity of vegetation, the thinning of light—Cael-Himir is overwhelming with fauna.

The fauna of the plateau are not animals in the sense of warm-blooded, furry, or scaly things. They are closer to architecture—or architecture is closer to them. The plateau of Cael-Himir has been colonized by a variety of entities that sit at the boundary between organism and artifact, and there are a great many of them.

The most common of these creatures are not visible to casual eyesight. They are thin films of something that lies between stone and flesh—sheets of translucent matter that coat certain surfaces of the plateau’s rock. They look like faint stains, or like oil films on water, but with more angularity to their patterns. They are not colonies of cells or organs of one body but something like continuous skins—one immense creature that has flattened itself to cover the plateau’s stone.

This film is not motionless in the sense of being dead or frozen. It is very slightly alive, but does not move in ways that can be seen by quick observation. It is active in the same way that a crystal is “active” when it grows—by incrementally positioning new matter upon its surface. The “growth” of the film across the stone is not measured by years or decades, but by geological ages. If you watched the same patch of the film for a human lifetime, you would not see any definite change in its configuration or pattern.

The film is not uniform. It is thicker in some places than others—forming “patches” or “spots” where the color is slightly deeper or lighter. These patches are not organs or eyes—they are more like nodes of some kind of network. If you are skilled enough (or mad enough) to look at them with special instruments, you may see faint threads of a solid matter connecting the patches, forming a web that is not entirely physical but woven into the crystal lattice of the rock beneath.

There are many other forms of fauna on the plateau. The stone of Cael-Himir hosts others that are more like pillars than films—vertical cylinders of stone-colored matter rising from the plateau’s surface. They are not “built” pillars; they are fungus-like or barnacle-like structures that have arisen from the mineral rock of the plateau. They stand slightly taller than a human—sometimes twice, or three times—but not towering high. They are not smooth: their surfaces are covered in intricate microstructures of ridges and pockets and indents.

The pillars grow in clusters, but the clusters are not random forests; they are arranged in configurations that mimic physical lattices—hexagonal or triangular or square—but with subtle distortions that have accumulated over time. These pillars do not sway or move; they are anchored to the rock with deep, fibrous roots that are not so much roots as they are extensions of the stone’s own crystal structure.

There are some “fauna” that are not films or pillars but more like embedded stones. They are spheres of a darker, smoother matter—smooth like polished ceramic—that are embedded partially or fully into the rock of the plateau. They are not “eggs” or “stones” but something like solid-state organisms—entities whose structure and behavior and replication are all buried in crystalline matter.

These spheres lie in clusters, but not randomly. The clusters form patterns—some rings, some lines, some spiral—that are not immediately obvious but can be seen if you look at the aggregate distribution of their numbers. They are distributed over the plateau in such a way that if you were to map their positions accurately enough and peer at the pattern from high enough, you might see a faint skeleton of curves—a faint outline of some larger entity that used to occupy or still occupies the plateau.

The fauna of Cael-Himir are overwhelming in density but not in activity. They are present everywhere—but they are present as the landscape itself. They are the plateau. They are the stone. They are the air.

There are no “behaviors” in the sense of animals eating, hunting, or fleeing. The fauna of the plateau are not engaged in ecological struggle with one another. They are not engaged in anything that can be recognized as “life” in the sense of self-driven motion and metabolism.

## Reality breakdown—world and not-world

The fauna of Cael-Himir are not discrete individuals—and yet they are not clearly unified either.

This is where reality starts to break down.

The boundaries between “creature” and “landscape” are not sharp. There is no clean skin separating a film-like organism from the stone it covers; there is only a smooth gradient of matter from one to the other. The same is true of the pillars and the spheres—and of the “glass-maze” vegetation.

There are individuals—up to a point. You can name a single pillar, or a single sphere, or a single patch of film. But you cannot be certain where one “individual” ends and another “individual” begins. The boundaries are not crisp edges; they are probability gradients.

The matter of the plateau’s fauna and flora and geology is not uniformly made of “normal matter.” There are patches where adjacent matter is not the same from one atom to the next, but instead from one “state” to the next—states that are superpositions of position and structure and existence. The stone of such locations is not strictly solid, but solid in a blended way: a superposition of two configurations of the crystal lattice at the same time.

This is most visible where you encounter “shadows of places.”

Here and there on the plateau of Cael-Himir, you may find spaces where the ground is not quite ground and the air is not quite air. There are no visible distortions—but if you walk into such a space, your body is not entirely sure where it is. Your limbs, when you look at them, appear faintly doubled—or rather, there are faint versions of them that are in slightly different positions, but which you are not actually occupying. You feel as if you are both here and one step to the left, one step backward, one step ahead.

The “shadows of places” are not stable. They are not fixed points where space and time have been torn open. They are more like places where the world has hesitated between two configurations of geology and life—and somehow, both of those configurations are still present.

In these “shadows,” you may see faint versions of the plateau that are not exactly the same as the one you are standing on. There might be a version of the plateau where there is more water—rivers of faint, improbable moisture—or a version where there is more vegetation—forests of glass-like trees. There might be a version where the pillars are taller, or more numerous, or arranged differently.

These other versions of the plateau are not “alternatives” or “parallel universes” in the sense of being strictly separate from your own. They are more like overlapping frames of reality—frames of the same film, being played simultaneously and projecting onto each other.

When you stand in a “shadow of a place,” you are not traveling to other worlds. You are standing where reality did not finish choosing.

## Rules and behavior—what the world permits

Because of all of this—the erosion gone to completion, the absence of water, the overwhelming but static fauna—the plateau of Cael-Himir is close to a timeless equilibrium.

One of the rules of this world is that movement is expensive: change is costly. The stone of the plateau is so dense and compressed and bonded to itself and its fauna that the energy required to move any significant part of it is enormous—far beyond what would be reasonable for any kind of “normal” animal.

This means that the fauna of Cael-Himir are not dramatic or energetic beings. They are entities whose existence and behavior and reproduction—if the word even applies—have been stretched over geological time scales. Their metabolic cycles are not seconds or days or years; they are thousands of years or more.

A “pillar organism” might take a million years to “eat” the minerals it needs to sustain its structure and repair its defects. Its “digestion” is the process of aligning the crystal lattice of the rock it is made from, adjusting its microstructure to reduce internal stress and entropy. A “film organism” on the surface of the stone might “reproduce” by expanding its territory across the rock—but that expansion is not measured by meters or kilometers per year; it is measured by millimeters or centimeters per million years.

The plateau of Cael-Himir is not the “end of time” in the sense of being dead and barren and worn. It is the end of time in the sense of being so close to a final thermal and structural equilibrium that there is almost nowhere else for it to go. The entropy gradients that usually drive weather, geology, and biology—wind and rain, erosion and growth—have been flattened almost to nonexistence.

This is not a place of chaos or decay. It is a place of stability and subtlety—but not cessation.

Another rule of Cael-Himir’s world—a consequence of its reality breakdown—is that observation itself is not neutral. The “shadows of places” where you see faint versions of other plateau configurations are not stable. They are not stable because they depend on some kind of interaction between the matter of the world and the matter of the observer.

When you enter into one of these “shadows,” you are not an outside observer looking at the world through a transparent window. You are a part of the world, and you interact with its unresolved superposition of states. Your presence may—infinitesimally but measurably—nudge the plateau of Cael-Himir slightly toward one configuration or another. The superposition of “states” of the plateau is not resolved by the choice of a god; it is resolved by the interaction of matter with matter.

The more “solid” observers—you, if you are a solid observer—exert a stronger pull on reality than the less solid. This means that if you were to stay very long in one of the “shadow” places, you might find, over geological time scales, that the superposition of realities around you slowly “crystallizes” into one configuration rather than another.

Of course, no one observer lives long enough to see this happen. The world of Cael-Himir does not change on human time scales.

A third rule of this world—not a law of physics but of consequence—is that there is no safe water. None. If you attempt to bring water—from other worlds, or from other times—onto Cael-Himir’s plateau, it will be violently unwelcome. The matter of the plateau is not “water-friendly” or “water-compatible.” The crystal lattice of the plateau’s rock and fauna and flora are not configured to handle water molecules moving between their layers.

If you tried to place water here—from your skin, or your breath, or your containers—it would be rapidly absorbed into the crystal lattice of the stone and of the fauna film.
