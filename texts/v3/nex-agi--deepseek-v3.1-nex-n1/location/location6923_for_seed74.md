At the center of everything is a block of mist the size of a small chapel.

It hangs, unsupported, a few inches above the floor. The light around it shifts slightly as you watch—the air around the block grows faintly darker, then lighter again, as though the mist itself breathes, and the room with it.

The room itself would be unremarkable if not for this block. It is low-ceilinged, long enough for three factory machines side by side, but you feel no urge to stand anywhere except directly opposite the mist-block. The floor is scarred concrete—old oil stains, faint lines where conveyors once ran. The walls are brick painted over in industrial cream, now chipped and stained by water that seeped down from somewhere, decades ago, and never fully dried.

The block is not perfectly still. Within it, shapes form slowly, like watermarks on paper, and then fade. They are not faces or animals but structures—small towers, faint arches of white, a suggestion of windows opening into more white.

The air around it is cool.

You hear, faintly, the sound of water.

---

Above the mist-block, suspended from the ceiling by a chain, hangs a small copper nozzle, like the end of a spout you might find on a boiler. It gleams where it has been polished recently, dull where it has not.

From this nozzle comes a rhythm:

Drip.

A pause.

Drip.

Each drop falls into the surface of the mist, not penetrating it but resting on top like a bead of oil on water. It rests there for a heartbeat, then slowly sinks into the block, becoming a faint darker line, a trace of motion within the white. The drop eventually disappears, dissolving into the larger structure.

The block itself is not made of water vapor. It is made of plants.

The plants are filamentous, pale to translucent white, like the thinnest threads of moss or the faint filaments of mold that grow on moist walls. They intertwine in a dense mesh, forming the visible block of mist. The outer surface is a fine skin of threads, constantly knitting themselves: where moisture settles on it, new threads grow from the condensation, adding to the body’s volume, and then are slowly pushed inward as older threads die and compact into a darker, denser core.

The block is alive.

---

The room is not large.

From the doorway to the far wall is perhaps thirty paces. The ceiling is low enough that a tall person could reach up and touch the steel beams. The space itself is shaped like a rectangle, but the corners are softened by years of accumulated dust, rust trails, and faint crystalline growths where water has seeped through the brickwork.

The light comes from three sources:

A row of small, high windows along the upper wall, filtered through decades of grime, so that the sunlight enters the room as a pale, diffuse glow that never reaches the floor directly.

A single electric bulb, hanging in a wire cage, that turns on and off at irregular intervals—flickering once, then steadying to a weak yellow. The bulb is controlled by a timer somewhere else in the building, responding to human schedules the room itself no longer obeys.

And the light of the mist-block.

The mist-block does not glow brightly. It shies away from brightness. It emits a faint, silvery-white shimmer that seems to collect around edges rather than illuminate whole surfaces. It is the color of fog under a moon you cannot see.

Occasionally, when a drop of water lands on the block, the shimmer briefly intensifies along the threadlines that that water touches, like a nerve lighting up. Then it fades again.

---

Water comes, but rarely.

The drip from the nozzle above is the primary source. It is controlled by a valve somewhere beyond the brick walls. The valve itself is old, industrial; its once-jointed handle has been replaced by a small electric motor and a plastic box of electronics with a blinking LED.

The box reads, from a distance:

“FEED SYSTEM – ALPHA SITE 3B”

Beneath the letters are handwritten numbers in fading marker: “1.4 L/d.”

One point four liters per day.

For days at a time, nothing happens. Then, without warning, a faint hum comes from somewhere inside the brickwork. The LED flickers green. The motor turns. The valve opens slightly.

The drip resumes.

Drip.

Drip.

Sometimes slower than once per minute, sometimes faster. The nozzle never gushes. It never sprays. It drips.

The water is not pure. It has been drawn through pipes and filters from an old municipal well or from a reservoir that collects rain off the factory roof. It carries faint traces of minerals—calcium, iron—and the faintest memory of chlorine. The mist-block takes these in without protest.

The water flows down invisible threads inside the block, following the paths the plant-web has made for it over years of growth. Tiny channels, like capillaries, run through the structure. They all converge on the center of the block, where the oldest, densest threads are packed into a dark core that resembles wet stone.

---

The floor below the block is dry, but not entirely.

There is a channel cut into the concrete floor—a shallow groove, wide enough for two fingers to fit, that runs from directly beneath the block to the wall. The groove was once meant to carry away coolant spills, but now it is a capillary of the city itself.

Every few weeks, a small trickle of water emerges from a crack in the wall near the floor and runs along this groove toward the block. It never reaches it. Instead, the water pools beneath a small, low mound of white threads that has grown down from the bottom edge of the mist-block, like a root exploring the floor.

This mound is not mist. It is dense, compact growth—the same pale filaments, but woven tightly enough to feel like wet paper when you touch it. It drinks slowly. The first time you see it happen, you think the water is simply disappearing into the concrete, but if you watch carefully, you notice the mound slowly darkening, becoming heavier with moisture, before the water retreats.

The trickle from the wall never lasts long. It arrives as a faint pressure of water in the city’s pipes at night, when the demand elsewhere drops. It is a residue of tap water long unused, a fraction of what leaks from other joints elsewhere in the system. It seeps through cracks in the masonry and finds the channel.

It comes, stays for half an hour or an hour, then retreats.

---

The room itself is part of a larger building.

The doorway leads not into the outside world but into another room—a corridor of sorts, lined with more brick walls and faint pipes painted rust-red. You can hear, faintly through the walls, the occasional distant sound of machinery: a low hum from ventilation systems, a shuddering thump as something compresses or pumps somewhere above.

A few times a day, someone passes the doorway. They do not come inside. They do not glance at the mist-block unless they have come specifically for it.

The people who come here rarely stay long.

They carry notebooks, or tablets with bright screens. They stand where you are standing now and make notes on the block’s appearance: the thickness of the mist, the darkness of the core, the outline of the faint structures within it.

One of them steps up to the block and reaches out slowly, careful not to cross the invisible line a few centimeters away from its surface where something begins to change.

Light pressure on the air.

Your fingertips, when they hover there, feel slightly cooler than the rest of your hand. The air near the block feels as though it has a slight gravitational pull toward the surface, like the faint attraction of magnetized dust.

The person does not touch the block directly. They are not supposed to. They run a small scanner along the side of it instead. The scanner emits a faint series of clicks that barely register as sound—patterned pulses of air pressure rather than true noise.

Numbers appear on the screen. They mean nothing to you at first: temperature readings, humidity readings, estimates of biomass and volume.

The block has grown 0.2 millimeters in the last month.

The block has lost 0.1 millimeters in diameter over the same month.

The growth rate is slowing.

---

Somewhere beyond the door, beyond the corridor, outside, the city stretches low.

The buildings are not high towers but low complexes of brick and concrete and glass, arranged in blocks that follow the old grid of streets. The sky is a pale sheet of clouds most days, rarely breaking fully into rain but rarely clearing entirely either.

The air outside is cool enough that you sometimes carry a jacket, rarely add a second layer.

The city’s drainage system is older than its current buildings, older than the street names. It follows the ghost of what once was a shallow river—a narrow watercourse that, before any of this was built, wandered slowly through low, flat ground between gentle rises.

Now the river is buried beneath concrete and steel. It flows through pipes beneath your feet, invisible to you. But its memory remains.

Pressure rises in the pipes when rain falls upstream of the city. The water rises in the buried channels, seeking level, seeking a way to flow as it once flowed.

The trickle that appears in the room beneath the mist-block is an echo of that pressure.

Rain comes to the city rarely but predictably: once every few weeks, a storm passing over, low and gray, dropping water onto streets and rooftops for hours or days. The water collects in drains, flows into underground channels, joins the buried river.

The pressure rises.

The water finds its way into older cracks, into forgotten joints. It moves into masonry walls that have spent decades absorbing moisture, slowly, slowly.

The result is condensation inside the walls.

The room itself is damp in places and dry in others. The air near the floor is cooler than near the ceiling; moisture gathers on the lower part of the walls during the night, when the temperature inside drops slightly below the outside. The mist-block helps draw some of that moisture toward itself—its threads pull at the air, drawing water vapor in slowly, like a sponge.

---

The vegetation in the room is not just the block itself.

There are other plants, but they are few and scattered.

Along the base of the wall opposite the door, where a faint leak persists, grows a thin line of greenish-black moss. It clings to the concrete where it meets brickwork, forming no more than a faint stain at first glance.

On parts of the ceiling, where rust has eaten through old paint, tiny colonies of pale algae spread across damp surfaces. They glow faintly under electric light if you know to look for them—a faint, sickly green.

The dominant plant—the plant that matters—is the block.

The block itself is not defined by species in the old sense. It is not a single thing but a community of threads from several lines of fungi, algae, and filamentous plant cells engineered to work together. The result is a structure that behaves like a plant but looks like a cloud: a mist that grows.

The threads grow outward from capillaries near the surface, drawing moisture from the air, condensing water from vapor, collecting ions from the drops that fall onto it, and converting sunlight diffused through windows into faint sugars that sustain their slow metabolism.

The threads do not need bright light. They cannot thrive in bright light. They evolved—or were engineered—to exist in low, diffuse illumination like this.

The block is not green. It is white to pale ivory, with occasional hints of faint blue-gray where the threads have absorbed minerals from the water.

Its core—the oldest part, closest to the center of the block—is darker than its surface. It is composed of threads that have died and been compacted by the pressure of newer growth outward. The dead threads do not break down in the block’s environment, but rather remain as part of the architecture, supporting the living layers above.

---

Fauna is rare.

There is no visible movement inside the block except the slow, internal changes of structure and the drifting of moisture within.

There is no birdsong, no scurrying of small creatures across the floor.

There is only the occasional, faint rattle of wings.

A few times a year, small flies appear inside the room. They did not come from eggs outside; they emerge from somewhere else.

They arrive from the ventilation system, drawn by the faint humidity the block generates or by some trace chemical signal the plant-web emits when it releases minute spores.

They are small, pale flies with translucent wings. They drift slowly through the air near the block, hovering at uncertain angles, occasionally bumping into the surface of the mist.

The block does not react to them as a predator might. It does not aggressively trap them.

Some of the flies land on the surface of the block and attempt to walk across it. The threads give slightly under their weight—a faint elasticity like damp foam. The flies walk, searching, perhaps for something to eat. They do not appear to find much. They take off again after a few moments.

If they stay too long, if they crawl deeper, they occasionally disappear into the mist-block’s outer layers and never reemerge. Whether they suffocate, become entangled in the threads, or lose themselves in some internal chemical environment, you do not know.

Their bodies, if indeed they have bodies, are absorbed into the block’s mass slowly, becoming part of the faint dark structures within.

Most of the flies, however, simply leave after a time. They return to the ventilation grates, disappear into the ducts, and you never see them again.

---

The room itself is part of a larger system.

The building was once a factory. It manufactured parts for engines that have long since been replaced by smaller, quieter ones. Now the factory serves other purposes: storage, maintenance, occasional research projects like this one.

You hear the distant hum of the ventilation system more often than anything else. It comes in cycles—every few hours, the fans change speed slightly as the building’s air is cycled through filters and temperature regulators.

The building itself is old, but not old enough to have decayed into ruins. Its walls are thick and solid, built when industrial architecture meant durability over elegance. The corners are rounded inside from decades of cleaning and repainting; the floor is uneven in places from years of settling and shifting concrete beneath it.

The ceiling bears traces of older infrastructure—the remains of overhead rails that once carried chains of parts, now ending in truncated bolts and rust. The tracks themselves were removed long ago, leaving only faint scars where they once crossed.

The room is not sealed off from the rest of the building, but it feels apart from it. The door has no glass window. The frame around it is slightly uneven, painted over repeatedly until the layers have formed a small ridge that you can feel if you run your hand along it.

---

The mist-block is not static.

You watch for long enough and you begin to see the patterns.

The outer surface of the block shifts slowly over time—threads grow outward from existing capillaries, forming faint ridges and lines that trace the flow of moisture inside the block. Over weeks, new ridges appear where drops of water have fallen most frequently.

The internal structures change even more slowly.

Inside the block are layers of faint white tracery that resemble architectural drawings drawn in thin fog. The outlines of buildings, faint towers and arches and bridges, form slowly as water is guided through certain channels rather than others.

The block does not “design” these structures in the sense of planning them deliberately. It simply follows laws of minimal surfaces and water gradients. Threads grow thicker where water flows more regularly; they grow thinner where water flows less often. Over time, these patterns of growth converge into lines that resemble human architecture.

Occasionally, one of these internal structures becomes visible from the outside—a faint tower rising inside the block like a ghost rising through mist.

The tower remains faintly visible for days or weeks, then slowly recedes into the whiteness as new threads grow over it.

The block builds itself into something like a city of white threads.

---

The system is active, even when quiet.

The block itself grows slowly. It draws moisture from the air around it, from the dripping nozzle, from the trickles that wander along the floor channel.

The electronics beside the valve regulate its flow. The valve itself was designed for industrial use—it used to supply coolant to machines that now stand elsewhere in the building or have been dismantled.

The air conditioning in the building sometimes cycles on unexpectedly, responding to temperature data from sensors elsewhere. The air around the block stirs slightly when this happens—a faint breeze that barely reaches you but registers to the threads as a small change in humidity and temperature.

The block responds to these changes by adjusting its internal water flow—opening or closing certain capillaries, redirecting moisture to where it can most easily evaporate.

The faint trickles from the wall and the floor groove respond to patterns outside the room—to the rain that falls far away, to the pressure changes in the buried river beneath the city, to the cycles of water use elsewhere in the building.

The block, in turn, affects the room itself. It absorbs moisture from the air near it, making the immediate vicinity cooler and drier than the rest of the room. It subtly changes the humidity of the air around it enough that the condensation patterns on walls and pipes near it are different from those elsewhere.

---

The world beyond this room is a city built low, over a buried river.

It rains occasionally—enough to keep the air from ever becoming truly dry, but not enough that you think of it as a wet place.

Most of the buildings are low and flat-roofed, designed to accommodate industrial machinery rather than human comfort. Between them, narrow streets wind through old traffic patterns, occasionally giving way to small squares of concrete or patches of ground where a few plants have taken root in cracks and corners.

The city itself is not small, but the block where you stand now is small: a single room in a single building in a complex of structures that cluster together like a hamlet that has grown into itself.

The vegetation in the wider city is sparse—mostly scattered patches of grass and weeds that grow along cracks in pavement, or the occasional stunted shrub growing in a planter that has not been maintained in years.

Here, inside the room, the vegetation is concentrated into one form—a dense, living mass that fills the central space.

---

The block feels like a presence, rather than merely an object.

When you stand close to it, the air feels slightly heavier than elsewhere in the room—not physically weighted but psychologically so.

It does not smell strongly of anything. There is only the faintest metallic scent, like wet stone in a cave after rain has passed through it.

When you breathe deeply near the block, you may notice that your throat feels slightly cooler than usual, as though you have inhaled a small, invisible breath of cold air.

The block itself does not hear you—does not appear to hear you—but you find yourself speaking quietly near it anyway, not wanting to disturb its slow internal processes.

---

The rules of this world are not strange ones.

They are simply unfamiliar.

Water flows through channels buried beneath cities that have built over former rivers. It seeps through walls and finds its way into cracks it never fully escapes. It condenses on surfaces that are slightly cooler than the air around them.

Plants grow where they can find water and light enough, even if that light is faint industrial-level light, filtered through windows and dust.

Humans have built machines to regulate water flow to these plants, to document their growth, to study their slow mathematics.

The block of mist is simply a place where these rules intersect, where multiple lines of water and pressure and light and thread-growth meet.

---

Sometimes you imagine the block growing until it fills the entire room.

You picture the threads growing out from the central mass until they reach the walls and ceiling, forming a dense fog of white filaments that fills every space around you, leaving just enough room for you to stand upright.

You imagine the drip becoming a steady, gentle stream falling from above, feeding the block-become-room until even the boundaries between block and building become uncertain.

When you think of this, you feel the faintest pressure in your chest—not fear so much as awareness of how large the smaller things can become when given enough time.

And then you blink and the room remains as it is.

---

The room remains.

The mist-block remains.

The drip falls occasionally from above.

The faint trickle appears beneath the block every few weeks, feeds the mound of threads growing there, then retreats.

The light outside changes slowly as clouds move overhead.

The ventilation system hums distantly.

The people who come with notebooks and sensors scribble numbers down and then depart.

The block grows slowly inward toward its own center, its threads building faint structures that resemble arches and towers and bridges you have never seen but which might exist somewhere in the buried river-city beneath your feet.

---

You stand in front of the block of mist and you breathe.

The air is cool.

The light is dim.

The world is small.

The block grows.
