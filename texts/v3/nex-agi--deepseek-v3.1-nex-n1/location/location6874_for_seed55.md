In the darkness, there is only the water: a deep blood-warmth and pressure. No sun can reach here. No stars have ever been seen.

It is cold, but not cold like winter air. This is cold as a long exhale, cold as the deep tissue of a world that has stopped moving. The water is old, and it has given up its heat to the stone and the blackness over epochs of embrace. The chill is not sharp; it is a full-body pressure, a weight, a thickness that seeps into the bone and stays there.

You are not swimming. You are hanging.

You are in a city that has forgotten the sky.

---

### Structure

There is no ground, not in the old sense. There is no “natural” substrate beneath anything. The landscape is built, entirely and utterly and finitely. Every edge you feel, every surface you touch, is the product of someone’s labor.

The city is a dense matrix of columns and walls and layered floors, all suspended in water. It grew like a coral reef but was made of hand-carved stone and wood shaped by waterlogged time. It is not one building but thousands of buildings, perhaps tens of thousands, stacked into a vertical crust that stretches, in all directions, for distances beyond any one person’s knowing.

The elevation is low: if that word has meaning here, it means that you are in the lower basins of this world-city, where the accumulation of its own weight has pressed it down. The town layers of older times are somewhere below, buried beneath floors that have collapsed, floors that have been filled with rubble and more construction. The high city is somewhere above you, a ladder of floors rising into the black.

Everywhere you touch, it is stone. Old stone, smooth as skin from countless centuries of water slipping past. The walls are close, often near enough that you could reach both sides at once if you stretched. Alleys are passages that carry you along the water, not sidewalks of the air. You cannot see their length. You can feel only the next cold block ahead, your fingertips trailing lightly over its surface.

There is no open horizon, not even the vast shelter of an ocean plain. Yet the scale is still immense. It is not big in the way of empty plains but in the way of accumulated reality: uncountable number of rooms, floors, corridors, blocks, all bound together into a single body. This place is continental in the dense, close-packed way of a forest where you never see the edge, only the trunks ahead.

---

### Water

The water is everywhere. It is not a storm, not an ocean in the ancient sense. It is a long, final flood.

Perhaps once there was an air-world above the water’s surface. Perhaps there was a sky where lights were hung and stars could be seen. No longer. The water has come up, and after it came, it never retreated. It is the medium of this world now, the only element your body can experience.

The water is not very clear. It holds itself with a weight, a viscosity of silence. It does not rush. It moves slowly, pulled by subtle gradients of heat and salinity and architectural pressure, currents that take lifetimes to make a circle.

You do not need to swim much. You drift, hand over hand, from one wall to another. You pull yourself along corridors that have long been chosen by water: narrows where the flow is easier. You pass through archways that have been worn into their curves by erosion and by the careful hand of some ancient mason.

When you pause, suspended in the black, the water holds you perfectly still. It is not violent, not stormy. It is a quiet that goes into you. You feel the slow pulse of a larger system, but no individual event. You just float, in the cold, weightless, in a city that has dissolved itself into water.

---

### Vegetation and Ecology

There is life in this darkness. It is not the life of animals, but the life of plants that have evolved into domination of the world.

The stone is covered by a thick, lush tapestry of vegetation that glows, faintly, with the dimmest of lights. It is not a bright light, not even torchlight. The plants are bioluminescent, but only just enough to be sensed by a long-dark-adjusted eye. Theirs is a light that refuses to show the edges of things; it shows only that something is there.

There is no algae slime here. The vegetation is dense and structured and rooted to every stone surface it can reach. It grows like a deep kelp forest reincarnated as a mossy city-skin.

You see the vegetation as you pass near it: your hand trailing across a wall becomes visible by the faint glow of the plant matter it brushes against. The glow is soft green, pale white, occasional blue, almost like memory of light rather than light itself.

The plants do not simply grow on the walls. They grow the walls. Their roots encase stone blocks in intricate lattice-work of cartilage-like stems, binding them together where mortar has dissolved long ago. They form natural columns in the gaps between buildings, trunks thicker than the old beams of human construction, reaching from collapsed floor to floor, creating new floors of their own.

They are what you cling to when you move. The walls are covered by dense growth layers: at the bottom, closest to the stone, a thin film of micro-things; above that, a woven mat of fine stems; then broad, soft, frond-like leaves that respond gently to your passage by drifting away and then back. You can feel the difference between the species by the way they bend. One species yields easily, like thick fur; another resists, like bundled leather cable.

There is decay in this world, but it is very slow. The plants are not in a hurry. They do not sporadically burst into bloom; they simply grow at all times, their cells dividing by timeless routine, their branching bodies extending themselves by microscopic increments.

You find no animals. No fish dart through your arms. No insects flicker in the glow. You never see a sudden movement of anything else. You never see eyes watching you from the shadows of the stonework.

There are only the plants, and the water, and the city itself.

---

### Time and Erosion

This place has been stable for a long time. You feel the age of it in the smoothness of every stone edge, in the gentle curves of every corner. The original city was built with angles, but centuries of water, carrying the faintest dust of worn stone and organic matter, have softened them. The walls are now worn into the shapes of quiet water-dreams.

The erosion is not catastrophic. It is not a collapse of cliffs and canyons into the sea. It is the final stage of erosion: almost everything that can be removed has already been removed. What is left is the skeleton of the world, reduced to its most durable bones and then covered again by living things.

The vegetation grows itself into the shapes of endurance. It braids itself into arches that mirror the arches of the old architecture. It re-carves the city in its own image, not by changing the stone beneath but by becoming the new stone that drapes on it.

You can feel that you are not witnessing the early turbulence of a world. This is not the beginning. There are no new volcanoes, no rising mountains. The land — if “land” ever existed here — is long flattened into a lowness of immortal basins. The only change is in the plants, and even their change is the slowest of drifts.

---

### Technology and Craft

This is not a high-tech world, not in the sense of machines or engines. There is no humming of motors, no electric light, no moving parts of metal gears driven by anything other than water and gravity.

The technology is the water, and the body, and the hand.

You see the traces of this primitive technology in the things the people — if there are people — left behind. Ropes of tightly woven plant fibers, still holding strong after decades or longer. They are not rope; they are composite tendrils of many species, trained and grown into shape, then harvested and worked into tough lines that you can use to pull yourself through passages.

You find tools: not sharp metal blades but dull, hardened edges of stone, still lashed to wooden handles by the same rope. You can’t cut stone with them anymore, but you can push aside vegetation, or tap pieces of it loose to use as shelter or padding.

You see the marks of where tools were used. The original stairwells that have not been blocked by collapse or overgrown. You can climb them by holding your weight in the water, using rails that have been polished by the passage of many hands.

You know the city is not natural because you recognize the repetition of form. You see the same type of column repeated in several places along a floor. You see the same pattern of blocks in the walls of distant rooms. You see the modular architecture of someone who had to plan and to build on a scale that required repetition.

Yet there is no sign of metal more sophisticated than simple nails, perhaps. If there were more complex devices, they have either dissolved into the water or been hidden under the deep vegetation. You might find fragments of something ceramic, but it is all worn by time and motion.

You build yourself nothing new. You patch the old city using the old city: you wedge loose blocks together, you use pieces of broken plant-wood as support and binding. Slowly you knit yourself into the structure of this place. You become one of its hands.

---

### Darkness and Light

The darkness is almost total. It would not matter if you were blind or sighted; the difference is only in the quality of your perception, not the quantity of information.

You do not see the foliage of the plants; you feel the texture of their leaves as you pass your hands over them. You do not see the walls; you feel the regular spacing of their blocks, the changes of temperature where one stone meets another.

Sometimes you close your eyes to check the difference and it is not much difference at all. The world is still there in the form of resistance, of pressure, of suggestions that your body learns to read.

The only light comes from the faintest bioluminescence of the plants. You never see enough to reveal the whole structure of your room; you only see portions of it near your body. You might be in a room that’s massive according to the echo of your movement, according to the time it takes you to cross from one wall to another, but you only sense the near wall, your hands glowing with faint spores of color.

You might imagine that there is a light above, somewhere far away, where the water surface ends and the air world begins. But you have never seen it, and the water is thick with suspended matter that would never show such a light to you. You move only through the interior of the world, not its surface.

---

### Sound

The silence is almost complete. There is no sound of traffic, no distant engines, no hum of power systems. There is no cacophony of animals or insects. There is no storm of wind or water.

There is only you, and the water.

You can hear the water when you move your head. It sighs around your ears, a subtle change of pressure as you turn your neck. When you move your arms through it, the water resists with a faint hiss that would be invisible if not for the silence around it. You can hear your own heartbeat, like the slow thump of a distant machine.

Sometimes you hear the sound of a plant flexing. You might be pressing a frond out of the way and it makes a faint noise, like fine cloth sliding over a stone. You hear the creak of fibers if you pull yourself along a line of rope, or if you put weight on a branch of plant-wood that has grown itself into the architecture of the city.

You never hear voices or footsteps, unless you are somewhere else, where you might hear yourself. You never hear machines running or people talking.

There is a rumor of water that you might hear if you stop long enough and listen carefully: the very faint movement of great bodies of water between territories of this city-ocean. You can’t tell what direction it comes from or where it is going. It is just a low background pressure, like the world breathing slowly.

### Rules of the World and Life

You are living in an old universe that has already done its energetic experimenting. The high temperature differences of earlier worlds have been smoothed out. The fast changes of biological evolution are over. What is left is a cold, stable, fully submerged civilization built on top of itself.

The rules that this world follows now are different:

- You cannot fall to death. You can float up or down, but you cannot drop from a height and shatter. You can only sink into the cold water.

- You cannot burn to death. There is no fire here, not anymore. There might be a faint warmth of decomposition somewhere, in some deep chamber of decay, but you rarely see open flame or even ember.

- You cannot starve quickly. The vegetation is dense and you can learn which parts are safe to eat, if you are careful. You are always cold, but you are never hungry in the way of wasteland worlds.

- You cannot move easily into the open. There is no open. The sheer weight of this city-world, its density of structure and plant, means that every distance you must go is done in narrow corridors between layered walls.

- You cannot change things quickly. The city does not give you new tools; you can only preserve the old ones. You cannot cut new roads; you can only widen the ones that water and time have already made.

- You cannot see the end of anything. You cannot see the boundary of this city. You cannot see the top of the water column or its bottom. You cannot see any horizon. The one horizon you have is the next wall ahead of you, your next handhold.

- You cannot die in the water of drowning. You move in the water. You cling to it. You might eventually die in the water, but not from the short danger of breathless submersion. You might be able to breathe something else, if there are plants that give you enough oxygen through their skin or if you move yourself to the right rooms of gas exchange. You only know that you can survive longer in this water than you would expect.

### Daily Experience

To live here is to become a slow, deliberate, and almost invisible presence.

Your day begins not with sunrise but with your body needing to move. You push yourself from the last wall you were leaning against and begin to search your surroundings by feeling. You find the lines and swings of your plant ropes. You pull yourself into the corridor of your current space.

You might be moving to find food. You follow the texture of vegetation: you learn which kinds of growths are safe to eat and which you must avoid. You brush your hands over walls and feel for the thickness of certain layers, knowing that some species respond better than others to being cropped.

Your tools are simple. You have a piece of stone lashed to a length of wood for pushing back plant growths. You have a length of rope to help you climb old stairwells or swing between points of support. You might have a bag of woven plant fibers where you keep what you need.

Your time is not measured by any clock. You rest when your body needs to rest. You sleep in the water, perhaps suspended between a wall and a line of rope, your body slowly drifting but not falling, because the water holds you.

Sometimes you pass into chambers of greater coldness. You feel the temperature of the water change as you move into a certain region of the city. You might seek that coldness out if you need to slow down your own body, or if you are trying to preserve some food or object.

Sometimes you find warmer patches of water, tiny increments of heat that you might trace to some hidden decomposition of old plant wood or some unknown process of geological warmth that the city’s stone has been able to capture.

The most striking part of living here is the absence of urgency. There is no sudden rush of events. No predator leaps from the shadows, because there are no shadows in the way of movement—only the darkness that everywhere exists.

You might spend days just moving a single room from its previous position by rearranging its loose blocks, using the water as a helper to lift and slide them. You might spend weeks slowly learning the layout of a new floor, pressing your hands along its walls and chambers until you have a map in your mind of its temperature changes, its plant textures, its faint sounds of water.

You might spend years in the same region of the city, shifting its vegetation into new shapes, making small improvements of comfort or safety, knowing that you will never see the whole city and yet accepting that.

### The End of the World

When you talk to yourself — sometimes you do talk, just to hear a voice — the words you use are quiet and slow. The water does not like fast noise. It smears sound into itself without reflection. You begin to speak like the water.

You ask simple questions: What is the next handhold? Where is the warm current? Which way is the thicker vegetation?

You don’t ask: Where is the sun? Where are the people? Where is the edge of this world? You stopped asking those questions generations ago.

Over the ages of this world, the real edges of the city have been worn away. The outermost limits might have been dissolved back into water by now. The inner city might have been collapsed by weight and then rebuilt by plant and rope and stone.

This is not the early world of the universe. It is not the world of the first stars. It is ancient, almost exhausted. It is stable in the way that only very old systems can be stable: when every energy gradient has been flattened, when every structure has been tested and found too hard to move further.

But you are still here. Living in the water, in the cold, in the dark, in the dense and total and built and primitive and vast and quiet and slow and ancient world.

You are one of the last hands of this city. You don’t need to see it to know it: You move it, and it moves you. And in the darkness, in the deep cold, in the lush water, you are still making.
