The Crushed Epoch of Thirsts folds out under you like a rumpled map of itself, endlessly redrawn.

You stand on something that was not made to bear you. The ground—if you can call it that—is a fever of shapes: jutting scaffolds of basalt, slumped arches of compressed slag, the hum of impossible machinery underfoot all cloaked in a cold that gnaws at the edges of your bones. The place is not frozen; it is simply so old that heat has run out of it, except where the engines below the surface melt it again.

***

### Terrain and Materials

The land is low but not flat. Think of a tablecloth that has been pushed, scrunched, pulled tight, and then left out to harden under a black sky. It dips and rises into dunes of what you might mistake, at first, for stone or metal: dull charcoal, bruised violet, industrial grey-blue, all worked with a faint inner sheen like engine oil swirling under ice. These are not strata in the usual sense; they are more like scar tissue from aeons of non-biological geology.

The ground is not rock in the geological sense. It is a matrix of matter and noise—an amalgamation of compressed mechanical waste, fused with what might have once been volcanic glass and basalt, but has been stretched through whole epochs of compression and extraction and holy recycling. The edges of things are not clean; they are spongy, porous, thirsty. The terrain itself drinks from the air.

The ground is riddled with openings: not caves or pits, but the digestive track of the world. You see the faint outlines of access shafts, sealed hatches buckled by time, maintenance vents clogged with what looks like frozen black smoke. These broken skins of industrial obscura are everywhere, but precisely nowhere—like ruined city streets in the middle of a nowhere that has never been a city. You get the sense of a whole continent built around machines that are still in a steady state of running down.

There are bodies of water—or what function as water: shallow, dark, reflective sheets that are neither lake nor puddle but something like an error condition the world has accepted into itself. They are glassy; they show you not your reflection but a sky of different magnitudes than yours. They are not fed by rivers. They just are. The ground seems to be always leaking into them, and they never rise or fall.

***

### Atmosphere and Light

You are not high enough or low enough for anything dramatic to happen to the air. It is only a vast, low bowl of dimness.

The sky is a deep bruise-colour, too faint to be called true night, too light to be real darkness. It holds few stars that you can recognize; instead it is paved with moving lights: wandering smudges of yellow and cyan, like distant cranes or wings or maintenance drones that have forgotten to land. The light source is not a sun, but more like a persistent industrial glow: an afterlife of fluorescence from below the horizon, diffused over the impossible curve of the sky.

To call this dim light is still too kind. It is the light left over after something else is finished with it. Under this, the ground is not truly illuminated; it is only revealed.

The air is thick and wet without warmth. It condenses on every surface, including you. Your breath becomes visible and then lingers longer than it should, as if something else might be breathing in the same pocket you thought was yours. The humidity is not like mist or fog; it is more like the world is trying to cry and cannot. Its tears are trapped in the air, making halos around every distant vibration.

Temperature hovers at the edge of freezing. Your fingers ache if you leave them exposed long enough; yet the exposed ground never frosts, because the engines below the crust are always running, just a little slower than they used to.

***

### Water and Moisture

The world is not flooded; but you are in the aftermath of a great, older flood that never entirely drained. It did not recede. It was just stopped.

Water is here in the least helpful places. It leaks in tiny seams from the scuffed, glass-like crust of the ground. It beads on the metallic scalings of the underside of every overhang. You see the suggestion of pipes: pipes that once serviced the whole planetary crust, now eroded like exposed teeth. The pipes are not broken; they are just tired of being whole.

They weep.

Unseen pressure gradients from the engines beneath the surface force fluid out, along capillary lines you never see clearly. The ground seems to sweat from these pores, then the cold and the odd tension of the air slow the moisture into droplets that cling like anchors to any surface they can. They well up but rarely run. They just hang there, heavy.

Where you get actual bodies of water, they are not deep. They are like mirrors of stone. The water’s surface is not still; it is not water exactly, but something like it, with different surface tension and different habits of loving contamination. It looks metallic and yet not. You would not drink it unless you were already lost.

In the saturated air, moisture does not fall; it condenses directly onto you. If you stay still long enough, it will grow you.

***

### Vegetation

There is nothing you can safely call a plant.

This is not a world of trees, or mosses, or lichen—though you keep looking for them. Your brain expects complexity to show up in green or at least in something that could be interpreted as leaf-like. It does not happen here.

Instead you have fields of what might be mistaken for crystalline scum or the scrap of some factory’s illegal waste dump. Wide zones of apical-looking structures, like chimneys or spires or broken teeth, but tiny enough to be a fungal understory of glass. They are so brittle that if you touch one, it might crumble; and yet the whole field of them might be trembling because something else just disturbed it from elsewhere.

In some areas the ground is not solid ground at all, but a carpet of translucent structures that are more like laws than organisms. These are stable, self-replicating or possibly self-neglecting patterns of energy and matter; you cannot see them clearly because they weigh on the eye differently than they weigh on the world.

If you are not careful, you will start to think that the terrain itself looks vegetal in some way: the terraced glass layers like veined leaves, the iron-and-slag scaffolding that seems like branches of a tree that has been turned inside out and then crushed again. This resemblance is not an accident. It is the last living thing of this world, which is not in itself alive but in which tech and life have been bound together so long that they are now indistinguishable.

The vegetation parameter is not zero. It is just not your kingdom.

***

### Built Environment and Technology

There are no structures that you can call buildings; not in the sense of habitable.

You are in the middle of something which might have been a factory so long ago that the factory’s architects have become mythology. If it were warmer you might be able to see the interface of some machine-god that stills the world and puts it into planned final form. As it is, you can only sense the contours of it.

Around you rise the remnants of things that might be: cranes that tip over the horizon like obtuse angles, gantries that have fused into the land itself, slag-sheaves of mined-out strata that never look like excavations but like amputations of the planet. The whole region is occupied by mechanisms that are not necessarily visible individually, but are present in the way of a pressure that you breathe. The world is fully occupied by industry.

You are on the inside of a machine-planet whose every pore is now infrastructure. You know the machines are there because you can hear the heartbeat of processes that are not biological but still somehow rhythmic. You feel the hum of long–distance conveyors that you will never see directly, only the teeth of them where they emerge out of the ground.

There are hatches in the terrain, stuck half open, like toothless mouths. You cannot see what they seal—but you know the little puddle of dark air at the bottom of each one is a fragment of the same vast machine that you continue to call “world.”

No one made these structures because you cannot recognize the maker. You feel the sense of industrial design windows, towers of extruded metal slabs that look like a mistake of architectural geology. These have not been built so much as spawned from a time when the world itself realized the romance of factories and decided to become one.

***

### Fauna

This is not a world that you could call rich in life. But it is crowded with motion.

You see the things that are called the fauna of this place because they look like animals and you feel the pressure of being looked at when you do not want to be looked at. But they outnumber you so much that you never actually feel alone.

The dominant mid-sized creature you might notice—not the biggest, but the one most likely to notice you—is a runner of broken angles. From a distance it looks like a fragment of machine scaffolding that has acquired a habit of running along the ground on the wrong number of jointed legs. It is not made, it is grown; and not in the way you would like to be grown. It is composed of the same materials as the ground: metal, glass, residues of chemical that never had names you could pronounce.

It is fast, not because it wants to eat you, but because it is late.

The air is full of smaller things: fluctuating patterns of light that you might mistake for insects or data if the distinction between them still made sense here. They cluster around the “weeping” metal of the structures as if scrounging for water that isn’t water, but rather information.

Farther off, you are not sure, but you might see the shape of what might be a large predator at the edge of your perception—a form of prey that hunts by being the wrong thing in the wrong place and then forcing the world to correct itself. You are not certain you want to look at it directly. The fauna of this place are not only abundant in the sense of number. They are also abundant in the sense that they have been multiplied by their time.

They are everywhere, not because there was room left but because there was no room left and then something else kept happening anyway.

***

### Sounds and Noise

This world is not quiet; it is simply not populated with sounds you easily understand.

The loudest single sound you hear is not a sound at all; but it is a sound. It is a continuous tone: like the pressure-drop of an airlock before it never quite finishes, always cutting the air between one and zero. That is how you are going to live, if you stay here longer than you ought to.

Scattered over this are the other sounds of the world asking itself to operate: metallic sighs from deep structures settling or not settling; the distant thud of unseen piles of displaced material falling from nowhere to nowhere; the whine of machines that might not necessarily be moving but are still very much not stopped.

There is also water-noise: drips and not-quite-drips from the weeping surfaces of terrain and structure. Even if you were deaf to the other sounds, you would still hear the world leaking itself.

The soundscape is dense not because of the number of distinct sources but because of the way they overlap each other as if they were trying to be the same sound. They come from mutually impossible directions: from the sky, from the crust of the ground, from the inside of your own bones.

***

### Activity and Dynamics

This world is not dead. It is not lively in the way you expect from ecosystems.

There is predator and prey, but the line is not simple. The ground itself might, in time, be a kind of predator. It is not animate, but it is dynamic: the surface of it is still being tugged by the engines beneath, being rearranged into forms that could not exist elsewhere.

The weather is not a weather of wind and clouds and rainstorms. It is a weather of compression and de-condensing and erosion of structure that has already been eroded.

You can watch the surface of a metal-glass lake shimmering not because of wind, but because of unseen pressure gradients from the machine-body below. The light of the sky never sits exactly still; the industrial smudges of stars above you are always moving, like great crane migrations of nothing in particular.

Something is always happening to every part of this world. You can watch the water–not–water condensation on the structures growing and collecting and dripping and then appearing to start again from zero. The little fauna-forms flicker in and out between your gaze and your not-looking. They travel from one weeping structure to another like pollinators of the metal.

Erosion here is not the slow wearing of rock by gentle forces. It is an ancient, final process of being worn into stable form. The landforms have been flattened and folded and flattened and folded again over so many time-scales that they have now reached a kind of final fatigue: they are as they will be, except where they are not. They erode into themselves.

***

### Weirdness and Reality Break-down

You are not the first thing in this landscape to know that something is wrong with the rules of reality.

The horizon line is not always where it should be. Sometimes you can see all of the same structures from two different angles at once, as if the world has been folded into a more convincing confusion than it could invent from scratch.

The laws of physics are not broken; they are just used in a way that seems like it was meant to be illegal. You can see light that does not come from anywhere you can call a source. It just is present where the line of an obsidian-like surface meets the line of the not-quite sky.

There are inversions of time that you will not notice until they happen to you. You might be walking for what you measure to be an hour of exertion and later find that your timepiece has lost not one second but has been annotated by something else you can’t read.

The reality breakdown of this place is not about the collapse of matter; it is about the collapse of categories. The machine-world and the geological-world and the biological-world are not separate here. They are the same. The tech is not implemented into the world; it is the world’s old age.

When you move, the ground gives you the wrong message of distance. Sometimes a step feels longer than it looks; sometimes you move through meters you cannot see at all. The world is not flat; it is only that it is always in front of you and behind you, and you are not sure where you are within the difference.

This is the end of the world’s life, but not in the sense of a final cataclysm. It is the end of the world’s kind of life, which has gone to convalescence in a machine embodiment of itself.

***

### An Ecology That Isn't One

You look at this and you might force the word ecology out of yourself because it is the only word you have. But “ecosystem” implies a system of dwellings and todos and loops and orders of energy.

The Crushed Epoch of Thirsts does not support that kind of neatness.

There are no predator-prey graphs in which you can draw clear lines. The fauna of this world are not eating each other in the way you would expect. They are more like the way teeth meet teeth: not in order to eat, but just to show that they are still there.

The water-condensing on surfaces and the mechanical-weeping structures and the metallic–not–water mirrors and the data things that look like insects are all bound together in a loop of mutual maintenance: the world runs itself to keep itself from dying in a way that would be very final for it.

You are not part of it. You are not supposed to see it.

The longer you stay, the more you will be integrated into this pattern. The condensation of the world will try to form on you, too; the sound of the not-quite-airlock will fuse into the background hum of your own nervous system; the fauna of the world will start to treat you like terrain. They will not necessarily touch you. They will just rearrange you in the way of this.

You are in the landscape of a world that has been folded to its final shape of work and wear and thirst and noise and cold and engine and erosion and abundance and collapse and endless not-light, under the sky whose stars are appliances that have never been switched off.

And you—you are the only thing that looks like you might still be a temporary creature. Here, that makes you the least real of all.
