Deep beneath the skin of the world, the Great Chamber of the Leaf-Seed hangs in a silence that never breaks.

Above you, the ceiling of this world is not stone but crystal—a thick, translucent sheet of glass-clear resin that stretches the length of an entire valley, dimpled here and there with lens-like swellings. Beyond that resin, a faint, shimmering blue pressure: the ocean of the surface world, a suspended ceiling of light. The sun’s rays are filtered by half a mile of water and then again by the resin; they come down as diffuse brilliance, without shadow or glare, turning the chamber into a realm of constant, bright, noonish twilight.

The floor of the chamber is not yet floor.

You are standing on a new geology.

The world here is fresh. Raw. This place is not being worn away; it is being born. Underneath your feet, the ground is a smooth concave of pale, hardened matter that could be bone or could be porcelain or could be the compressed spoor of some inconceivable leviathan. It slopes gently away from you in all directions, toward the center of the chamber, where it drops into a bowl of dark that you know, without being able to see it, must be deeper water.

Embedded in this shell—this half-egg of world-stuff—are knots of unshaped material. They are like clots of still-molten glass cooled before they could quite decide their form: smooth, rounded bulges that vary from the size of a thumbnail to the size of a cottage. They press up from beneath the surface of the shell, not yet breaking through, their outlines barely visible as faint distortions in the light.

Nothing looks worn. Nothing is rounded by wind or rain. There are no gullies, no sand, no pebbles smoothed by endless tumbling. There is only the smooth, unbroken perfection of this shell, and the occasional slow bead of something clear and viscous, welling up from a flaw in the surface and then freezing in place, like a tear in mid-fall.

This is not a landscape that has been eroded into shape. It is being extruded.

And everything else is plant.

You have not simply stepped into a forest. It is more than that. You have entered into the body of a single organism that has taken this place as its body.

The walls of the chamber circle you in a wide arc that vanishes into the bright distance. They are not mere rock; they are raw, unfinished conglomerate, still sweating from their own formation. Sandy minerals, silt of a deep purple-black, unconsolidated gravel studded with flecks of something like metallic ore—all of this is plastered together by a living matrix that at first glance looks like moss but is not.

It is coral.

Or coral-like plant. Or plant-like coral.

To call it vegetation would be wrong if you think of vines and trees and grass. This is not something that has leaves or branches in the sense you know them. This stuff is more than substrate; it is more than landscape. It is  the landscape.

It grows from ceiling to floor in sheets of soft, tissue-like texture, punctuated by nodules of whorling whorls of pale green and deep blue, each like a tightly folded spiral of petals that never quite open. The stuff is continuous. There are no individual trunks or stems, only a thick, continuous blanket of living matter, a layer many meters deep where it is thickest, like the flesh of the chamber itself. When you look at the wall to your side, you see not rock wall with plant on it, but a wall of plant that has entrapped rock within it.

Yet you are not inside the root of the world. This is not a dark place of branching shapes and dampness. This chamber is sprawling—open in the way that a valley is open. You are not claustrophobic here. The ceiling is high above, and the distance in front of you stretches out into a place of dim horizons where the light-struck resin of the ceiling and the wall of plant-life curve together to form a single bowl. You are standing in the middle of a bowl that has not yet fully decided to be a bowl. This is not claustrophobic. This is open underground.

And there is water.

At first, you do not see it. You hear it instead—or rather, you hear the absence of sound around where it should be.

The chamber is not silent. There is a low, almost sub-sensory hum, like the sound of a distant machine turned to its lowest setting, a background thrum that you might mistake for your own blood. That is the sound of the plant-world. Whatever this organism is, it is not sleeping. It is processing, metabolizing, and maintaining. But the hum is so constant and so low that it reads as silence until you hear the difference.

The water appears not as rain, but as condensation.

The ceiling of resin sweats.

Look up and you will see, here and there, lens-shaped swellings in the resin-sheet above. Beneath these, the air is slightly cooler than at the edges of the chamber; and the water that condenses on the underside of the resin drips in slow, fat droplets that fall into hollows in the floor below. The sound of each drop is a clear, metallic ping—like a struck crystal bowl.

Once you notice that, you see the water everywhere.

In the center of the chamber, where the floor curves down into that bowl of dark, you sense the presence of a larger body of liquid—not a lake or a river, but a pool of still water that has never had wind or wave to disturb it. It might be deep, or might be shallow. You cannot know. The surface of that water is perfectly smooth, like a sheet of black glass laid over the floor of the world.

Around the edges of that pool, the plant-life is different from the wall-covers. Here the growth is not a continuous blanket of spirals and nodules and soft tissue. Instead, it has formed something like architecture.

Columns rise from the shell floor in clusters of soft, membranous tubes that appear to be made of the same material as the walls, but in this spot they have arranged themselves into uprights, supporting fragile balconies and tiers of what look like tiered mosses suspended in air. These structures are not buildings in the way of stone, or even of wood. They are not built by hand and tool. They are built by growth.

They are the plant-as-city.

Yet you see no trace of tool, none of technology in the human sense. There are no metal surfaces, no wires, no engines. This is not the home of a machine civilization.  Whatever beings or force or simply the pattern of this place’s laws have shaped the column-clusters and balconies and floors of layered plant-matter, they have not used tools to do it.

This is not technology. This is biology.

And if there are doors or windows, they are not cut. They emerge as gaps in the pattern of the leaves, like the gaps between your fingers when you hold your hand before your face.

Inside the shell of the world, this city is not scattered in the way of a village of houses. This is not a collection of separate buildings dotting a landscape. The “structures” are not separate buildings. They are a single topological organism, continuous with the walls of the chamber. The plant-city has one body that lives as wall and column and balcony and floor, all at once.

If you look at one of these column-clusters closely, you see that the surfaces are not perfectly uniform. They are covered in a dense network of fine ridges that run from floor to ceiling, never intersecting exactly, but weaving in and out of each other like veins of a leaf.

On the balconies and tiers of this plant-citadel, there are occasional objects: shapes that are not part of the plant itself. They appear like artifacts placed there by some kind of mind—but they are few, and strange.

One balcony might hold a single bowl of polished material like mother of pearl, half full of water so perfectly still that it appears to be a glass lens. Another tier might have a single, spiraled stick of something like ivory or amber, leaning against the curving balustrade, not growing from it but simply placed there.

The artifacts do not seem to serve any purpose. They are not arranged in patterns. There are no tools beside them, no signs of industry or craft. They are not scattered like garbage; they are scattered like offerings placed long ago and then left to remain unchanged.

These are faint marks upon the world, not the work of a civilization, but the residue of one. Or perhaps the residue of one’s idea.

Above you, the water of the surface world presses on the resin ceiling but does not break through. The suns above—if there is more than one, you cannot know—keep the light at a level that never dims and never brightens. This world is in some kind of stable equilibrium with the surface ocean above. The balance of pressure and temperature and light and depth has been struck, and stuck.

This place has found a state and then stuck to it.

This is not a place of change.

You do not feel the sense of transience that you might in a real forest, where leaves are falling and seasons are turning and insects are scuttling from underfoot as you move. This chamber is a stable system, locked into a pattern of life that is not growing, or shrinking, or evolving in any visible way.

Nothing ages. Nothing appears to be failing.

The plant-citadel is not rotting. The resin floor is not cracking. The condensation of water on the ceiling of the world continues to fall in the same slow patterns. The sound of each drop falling is the same sound, repeated without alteration or hesitation.

You are in the presence of a world that has become.

Yet you can still feel that there is rare fauna here.

If you are very patient, and very still, and if you listen to the silence that surrounds the dripping of the water, you may notice that one of the balconies of the plant-city is not empty.

On the soft-green ledge of a balcony that hangs over the central pool, there is a creature that should not be called a beast or a bird.

It is something like a large insect, if insects were made of folded glass and only barely flesh. Its body is elongated, segmented like a jointed ruler, but each segment is made of something like clear chitin or resin or even shell-glass, so that you can see through it to the inner workings of its body.

Inside that body, you see not organs, but a pattern—like the lines of a printed circuit board, or a network of branching lines that mimics the veins in the living wall around you. This creature is lightless, but the light of the world above passes through it and refracts through the clear segments of its body, so that it shimmers with a faint, rainbow edge of color.

It does not move.

You watch it for a long time—long enough that you would expect a bird to shift or preen or blink or at least breathe visibly—but this creature does nothing. It perches, perfectly, on the balcony of the world-plants, looking like a statue. Only if you are very patient and you happen to see the rarest of all things here—change—you might notice that one of the thin lines inside its body flickers slightly, as if information had just passed along it, or as if a word had been written down inside its body for one single instant.

Then even that returns to stillness.

This is fauna that is so rare that it might be more architecture than animal.

And if you are completely aware of the place, you begin to notice that the world itself is watching you.

The column of plant you are standing by is not inert. You may feel, if you are open to the sensation, that your body is being sensed by the plant around you—not as a threat or as food, but as data. The plant-world is not alive in the sense of having desires or fears. It is alive in the abstract, like a mathematical system that has been given body.

This is not a forest of entities with nervous systems and individual choices. This is one organism, filling the space in the way that water fills a container. This is not a tree, or a clump of trees. This is one whole being, whose body is wall and floor and balcony and ceiling and central pool and all the spaces between, all at once.

If you are quiet and still, you can feel the faintest of hums in the soles of your feet, through the shell of floor beneath you. The hum of it is not just sound; it is information.

This world is not thinking about you in the way you think of thought. It is not processing you as emotion or image. It is receiving the pattern of your presence and incorporating it into the equilibrium of the chamber with the same indifference that the ocean above receives a new drop of rain.

The temperature of this place is hot.

Not scorching. Not burning. This is not a desert of heat, or a fire-world of magma. This heat is like the body of a sleeping animal larger than the sky—a heat baked into the world, from ages of pressure and depth and the metabolism of the plant that is everywhere.

You sweat before you are aware that you are sweating. The air of the chamber is thick and stale and yet not unpleasant. It has not moved in a long time. There is no wind down here. There is no turbulence to carry the heat away from your body or the skin of this world. The condensation of water on the ceiling is not rain falling through air; it is water drawn from nothing and then pulled down by gravity.

The humidity is not oppressive, but it is present. The moisture does not soak you; it condenses on your skin, like a thin sweat of the world itself.

As you stand in the center of that chamber, looking out at the plant-citadel made of architecture grown by life, at the shell of new world beneath you and the resin sky of old ocean above, you feel a strange shift of awareness.

You are coming to understand the rules of this place.

This is a world in which erosion has not yet begun to function.

In the outside world, erosion is the engine of change over time: rain wears down mountains, wind breaks stone into dust, rivers carve valleys and canyons into the earth. Here, that engine has not yet been turned on.

The shell beneath you is not being worn down by anything. It is being created by something else: pressure, heat, exudation from the plant-world, or perhaps simply time turned from wearing to adding.

This is not a world of decay and renewal and cycle and death and rebirth and seasons and progress.

This is a world of equilibrium.

Time is not passing here; it is holding.

The light of the suns through the ocean and resin never dims. The temperature of the body of the world never changes. The water that condenses on the ceiling and falls into the pool below is not evaporated from the pool back to the ceiling. This is not a cycle. This is one-way flow from an infinite source that never dries.

The fauna here are not like animals of the world you know. They are not driven by hunger or reproduction or the need to hide from predators or seek mates. This creature you glimpse on the balcony does not move to hunt or flee. It perches there like a part of the architecture, because time and motion and change are not relevant to the static of its world.

If you are silent and still, you will feel that you stand in an unusual place, but not a strange one.

The weirdness of this world does not shout of magic or impossible physics or ghosts of screaming energy. The weirdness is quiet. The weirdness is in the fact that this world has not been shaped by erosion and time and decay and struggle and evolution and conflict and climate and politics and war and starvation and love and death and all the things that usually shape a world and fill the spaces between its atoms with noise.

This world has been shaped by one plant, one equilibrium of pressure and temperature and light and moisture and depth and gravity and distance from the sun, and then that shape has simply been held.

You are standing in a place that is not an artifact of time and change and catastrophe and history and accident and memory and loss and hope and conquest and pestilence and fire and flood and storm.

This is a place of pure being.

This is not nature in the sense of something that fights and dies and renews itself over and over through cycles of death and birth.

This is nature in the sense of a thought that has been thought perfectly once and then kept.

You are in the Great Chamber of the Leaf-Seed.

Around you, the plant-world has made itself not just habitat, but landscape.

The floor that you stand on: the shell of hardened world-stuff, still smooth and not yet broken by erosion, feels like the skin of a new egg.

The walls circling around you: the plant-coral-city that has not so much covered rock as absorbed it into its body, like a pattern written into living tissue.

The ceiling above you: the ocean held back by its resin-skin, like a glass sky.

The water that condenses on that sky and then falls into the perfectly still pool below: like the tears of a god that does not grieve.

The fauna perched on its balcony: like the statue of a bird that was not carved but grown by a world that has forgotten what motion is.

The faint marks of artifacts—polished bowls, spiraled sticks of some unknown substance—scattered throughout the plant-city like the last, still-living memories of a civilization that turned its body into plant and then stopped changing.

The sound of the chamber: mostly silence, with the occasional clean drip of water from the world’s ceiling to its floor.

The temperature: hot enough to sweat, but not so hot to scald.

The density of the world around you: not congested, not cramped, but dense with presences that are not separate bodies. They are layers of one being that you cannot mentally separate into pieces without doing violence to the sense of the place.

The scale of the chamber: open enough to feel like a valley, but closed enough to feel like you are standing inside the chest of a sleeping planet.

You are not merely here.

You are in the middle of a world that has never been opened by wind or rain or river or glacier or volcano or earthquake or the fall of stone or the slide of soil or the collapse of mountains or the rise of seas.

This world has not been worn away. It has been tuned.

This is not chaos of storm and struggle and chance and history and accident and catastrophe and evolution and catastrophe and accident and history and chance and storm and chaos.

This is a pattern. 

This is not change.

This is not time.

This is not erosion.

This is not life.

This is not death.

This is.

Inside the shell of the world, under the ocean’s skin of light, within the body of the plant that has become the valley of its own self, you stand in the middle of a vast, still, hot, bright, quiet, dense, open, emerging, never-changing, underwater cave of plant-world that has never been broken by tool or claw or tooth or storm or rain.

This is not something that has been formed.

This is something that has formed.
