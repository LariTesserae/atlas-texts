There is a ring of weeping stones leaning on all sides of this small courtyard garden, and everything else listens to them.

Each stone stands a little taller than a woman, its surface polished and drilled to the precise angle of a cathedral vault’s baseless arch. From the inner edge of each arch a thin sleeve of water hangs, unbroken and glassy. It does not ripple. It does not splash. It does not fall. It simply hangs there like a curtain made of a single, suspended sheet of rain, and from the glassy water-surface the faintest of hums comes, constant and low.

The courtyard is not large. Six steps can take you from either wall to the stone ring, and a dozen more circle the stones themselves. You are not so much in a garden as behind a vast, high, teeming greenhouse of the city above—a maintenance or overflow or sacred chamber at the root of things. The air is warm and wet and tastes faintly of green metal and fermenting flower petals, and the condensation on the walls is not random but ordered in hexagonal patterns, like a honeycomb of glass, each cell trembling with a single still drop of water. The humidity is held at such a precise point that the water is not content in either air or stone but stops at that surface, and waits.

Nothing natural grows here. Or nothing grows here that is not shaped by purpose.

Moss and algae and fern are woven into the walls in a living tapestry of greens and deep indigoes, knit into a lace of plastic and copper and filaments of wet ceramic. The moss grows where the architecture permits it, and prunes itself by the tint of light that the warm walls give out. The moss grows to the border of fiberglass threads and stops there, as if a boundary of heat has been drawn across it, and the moss simply accepts that.

The floor is a single, slightly curved surface of polished synthetic stone, a color of deep grey that is almost blue, and in its center the ring of suspended stones stands like frozen fountains turned inward. Between the stones and the wall runs a shallow trough of clean, slow water in which long, meandering stems of green metal are rooted, each ending in a complex of mechanical leaves that turn and follow you not with nervous quickness but with the patient turning of a sun. The stems are smooth and cool and gleam with wetness, and when they rub against each other there is no dry rustle but the soft hiss of water flowing over glass.

The ceiling above is a low dome of some translucent composite that is not quite glass and not quite plastic. It is not entirely transparent; you can see the shapes of things above like a dark, blurred shadow of city—a long glass corridor of yellow, broad, lucid rays of sunlight that filter down but never seem to reach the courtyard floor. They rain into the dome until they liquefy into a murky amber and seep into the walls instead, leaving the scene below in a cool, luminous dimness that comes from nowhere particular and yet everywhere at once, like light in a cathedral under low clouds.

The air is still but not stale. There is a faint, continuous sense of motion in it, like a held breath that is never permitted out. The water hanging from the sides of the stones does not move but breathes: the meniscus in the fine pores of the stone’s shell rises and falls in a sign of a pressure difference you cannot see or hear but can feel as a slow, slow heartbeat in the air. When the meniscus rises very high, the threads of water hanging inside the stone ring ripple slightly, and then resume their stillness.

The weeping stones are not making this water. They are taking it from the air, and from the pipes of a city that you never see but feel in the walls. The stones are organs of condensation and distillation and direction. The moisture condensing on the dome’s walls and running along threads of hydrophilic glass and into the walls’ ceramic honeycomb is being collected, cleaned by the plant’s tissues and the machine’s membranes, and then delivered into the stone rings where it accretes to the suspended water-curtain. The stones are bells of water and metal and nerve and purpose, and they sing quietly over the courtyard garden with a low, continuous note of tone.

The garden is not large but it is layered with work. Around the weeping stones, a narrow ring of sculpted resins and polymer resins are packed into tight, tightly coiled forms of what we might once call roots but which are neither root nor engine but both. The roots are laid out in a precise pattern that is partly hydraulic and partly electrical, and they are all covered in a thin film of water that keeps them from drying but not from operating. There are condensation chambers, and tiny heat engines that are not much more than glass thorns full of liquid that expands and contracts with minuscule precision, and a network of thin wires that goes into the walls and disappears into the greater city and its systems of light and heat and air.

When you step quietly and listen to the garden, you hear that it is mostly quiet but not quite silent. There is a low, steady hum from the stones themselves, and a faint clicking of something like metal valves, and the soft movement of small mechanical leaves turning and adjusting themselves to the movement of your body and the changing humidity of your breath. Everywhere there is the sound of water not dripping but being, and that being is not random but rhythmic.

There is no animal life here by the old definition, but the garden hums with a kind of domesticated life. The metal leaves like a wind of slow, slow gears; the condensation chambers swelling and collapsing with pressure; the water-curtains trembling in their frames; the moss on the walls breathing out oxygen in a thin, invisible film that is sent away through ducts you cannot see but can feel in the faintest currents of air that move from ceiling to floor to walls and back again. The place is tiny but it is dense with operations and circuits and the slow, steady activity of an engineered metabolism.
