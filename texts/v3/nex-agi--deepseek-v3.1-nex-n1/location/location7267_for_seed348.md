It is called the Station of the Leaking Sky.

Walk in from the outside at the appointed hour, and the first thing you notice is the sound of God being wrong.

A long metal roof, three stories overhead and pierced by a thousand small holes, rattles under a rain that never seems to fall anywhere else. The drops are thin and precise, each drilled from the cloudstuff by a perfectly positioned puncture in the plating. They fall into channels, troughs, and funnels that have been bolted and welded and riveted across the cathedral-scale interior of the station: a nervous system of gutters and pipes and flanged collectors, all of them singing.

If you stand still and close your eyes, you can hear the place resolve itself: the high, clear note of a single bead falling into a copper basin; the rougher chatter of a galvanized spillway; the deeper, throatier hum of main conduits carrying the water down into the machinery below the floor. The sound of it is polyphonic and immense, but it is completely stable. There are no crescendos, no sudden storms, no silences. The rain always falls at exactly the same rate, the same volume, the same insistence.

The city outside the station is low, built mostly of brick and blackened stone, and perpetually cool—not the crisp freshness of autumn air, but the cool of a back room that never quite warms up. The street air tastes of chimney smoke and damp mortar and the faint, metallic tang of distant factories. Large, heavy-wheeled vehicles clank by on rails set in the cobbles, their engines producing a low, continuous thunder that rolls under everything else. The skyline is broken by the squat drums of silos, the thin steeples of industrial exhaust, the occasional bulbous dome of a gasworks.

And yet, if you ask anyone where the city gets its water, they will tell you to go to the station.

The entrance is a pair of rusted iron doors, each six meters high and hinged with a mechanism of gears and counterweights that must have been beautiful when it was new. Now it is merely efficient, and also noisy—when you pull the latch, the counterweights drop with a deep, booming clang that sets the whole frame ringing, and the doors swing open as if they are already tired of you.

Step inside.

You are under the roof of the world’s strangest forest.

The ceiling is a forest of hanging metal, inverted from how you’d expect: instead of trees rising from the ground, you see “roots” hanging from above, each one a different arrangement of tubing and joints and collectors. They are grouped by spacing and size and pattern of branching, like species of engine. Some are thin webs of copper wire and glass, others are thick, trunklike conduits of riveted steel. From each of them, water drips, or runs, or is coaxed into carefully shaped falling curtains.

The ground beneath the visitors’ platform is a maze of shallow basins and low, curving walls of concrete and ceramic tile, all of it stained by mineral and moss and by the tracks of walking, swimming, crawling things. The water is never deep enough to be a lake, but too wide and connected to be called puddles. It is more like a thousand connected eyes of water, each reflecting the roof and the day and the small, moving figures of the inhabitants.

You are first, and always, aware of the inhabitants.

This is not a place of plants, but of animals. The word “vegetation” here, if you insist on using it, refers to organisms that have adapted to live without chlorophyll—fungal, bacterial, mechanical, or something else entirely—but the main life of the station is not that. It is fur, and shell, and claw and feather and carapace, all of which have been drawn by one of the world’s strangest forces to orbit the Leaking Sky.

Moss rats are everywhere first, because they are everywhere and because they are always first. Small, quick, with fur that is not brown but a kind of engineered blue-green, like algae and metal filings, they are at the fringes of every pool, running along the edges of the tiles, always in motion. They are not afraid of the water; they are afraid of stopping, because the water is always dripping and they always need to know where. Their ears, if you look closely, are not round—but shaped like tiny, cupped funnels, and when they run, the water that hits them from above is collected and channeled along the folds of their skin into their mouths.

They are small but overwhelming. There are thousands. They give the sense of a single, nervous fluid, more nervous than water, always at the edge of things.

Then, in the deeper basins beneath where the roof is particularly perforated, you see the spinners.

They are the size of your forearm, translucent and shelled, like elongated crabs but not crabs. They have legs, but they also have wheels, of bone and keratin and fine, interlocking plates of something that gleams like steel but smells like toenails. They are built, it seems, for rolling in guided paths along curved edges of the concrete basins. The noise of them is another layer in the station’s roar: the click and clickety of legs on tile, a sudden skrr of wheels on polished stone, a low, continuous rattle as dozen of them move around each of the “rain-roots” in the ceiling.

They are the ones, after a fashion, who built the water.

At least, that’s what the maintenance engineers say, in the tones of people who have been forced to accept a mystery they would not have chosen. The maintenance engineers are the main human inhabitants of the station. They are not many, but it is still their place, if only by default of being the only things who talk.

There are perhaps three dozen of them, and they share the station with a number of animals that can be described in tens of thousands.

The engineers wear layered oilskins and rubber caps; their boots are steel-toed and always wet to the ankles. They are constantly moving along the catwalk that circles the interior of the station, like monks on a balcony above their liturgy. From time to time one of them will climb down from the catwalk, using a ladder or a narrow spiral staircase, and step into the water with careful, slow movements of their boots, as if not wanting to disturb the things that live in it.

Pools of glassfish part around their legs. The fish are finger-length, and their scales are transparent, exposing the iridescent organs beneath. In each transparent flank, a small, constantly spinning turbine gleams—not metaphorically, not visually, but physically and audibly, like a minuscule engine. Each glassfish has a minute turbine of salt and protein and something else, and as they move in schools, you hear not the splash of water but a low, continuous ringing of many tiny engines.

They are everywhere the water is deep enough to cover their fins. Their combined hums make the surface of the water vibrate, and the air above them warps with the heat of their engines.

On the drier parts of the floor—where the tiles are raised by the thickness of a single brick—curl the cable-snakes. Long, limbless, and metallic, they are sheathed in overlapping plates of some alloy that shimmers with heat and condensation. They are warm to the touch, but not hot enough to burn. They are anchors to something else; the ends of their bodies vanish into the walls or the floor, or into the junctions of the main water pipes. If you are very quiet, and if you press the side of your head to the metal floor, you can feel the vibration as they pull, and pull, and pull.

The engineers say that the cable-snakes are the “roots of the engine below,” but they never say what engine. “You’d know,” one of them, a short woman with a permanently furrowed brow, tells you. “Once you’ve gone down there.”

Her eyes are guarded and oddly bright, the same color as the algae-blue on the moss rats’ backs.

Above the water, in the air and amid the hanging pipes of the roof, are the wings.

They are not, technically, birds. They are faunal—organisms, not artifacts—but they are not structured like any known bird or bat or insect. Each of them is more like a folded banner of tissue and tissue and metal and membrane, like a sail of bone and hair and copper wire. They are silent in flight, but not in presence: when the air moves they click and rustle; when they gather together in the dozens, they become a hanging clatter of a thousand wings folded and unfolding and folded again.

They are filter feeders of sound and vapor and temperature, and they are one of the reasons, the engineers say, that the station never warms up or cools down. The wings crowd the beams of light from the high windows—big, dirty, panes of glass and wire and film, which are thrown open in the morning and closed by a system of pulleys and weights at night. The light is not bright; but it is sufficient, muted and gray-blue like the light at the bottom of a deep pool.

And yet, despite the wings’ preference for cool, the air is not cold. It is just the same as always.

The faunal density of the station is not at the “peacefully humming with life” end of the scale. It is at the “you are swimming in soup” end of the scale, except that instead of soup, you are in a space crowded with animals that are not trying to eat you, but are definitely in the way.

You are never alone. If you are not stepping over a moss rat, you are stepping around one of the cable-snakes that has partially unrolled across the tiles. If you look up at the wrong moment, a wing will be closer to your face than you expected it to be, staring at you with compound eyes that are partially covered by a kind of metallic lens, like polished goggles.

They are polite, in a territorial sense. They do not injure you. They do not attack you. They simply assume that you are not important enough to be honored by careful avoidance.

The floor of the station is not covered by soil or grass. There is no plant life in the sense of green, growing leaves reaching toward the sun. The “vegetation” here is a very thin, almost invisible crust of something else—maybe fungal, maybe bacterial, maybe something else—that grows in fine lines where the moss rats and the glassfish and the wings haven’t yet disturbed it. It is dark, almost the color of the tile itself, and when it is thick enough you notice it as a shadow of color, not as living texture. It glows, when the lights are out, but not in a way that is helpful to visitors.

The engineers call it “skyline,” because if you look at it from above, it seems to draw a map of the city’s water system in phosphorescent lines.

The “built-ness” of the station is everywhere you look. The city outside the doors, the town-or-settlement that the station is meant to serve, has not invaded here; but it has sent envoys: iron columns, steel beams, concrete supports, and scaffolded platforms of old and new construction, all of them braced and cross-braced for the relentless drip of water from above. The rust on the iron is not rot; the rust is a layer of accommodation, patina of cooperation with water and time and the wings’ feet.

There are long tables of metal and wood, bolted to the walls and the floor, where tools of both engineering and biology lie side by side: calibrated glass valves used for regulating the flow of water into and out of certain basins; porcelain plates covered with different kinds of “skyline”; small, focused arrays of lenses that focus the station’s dim light onto a particular pool of glassfish, or a particular wing perched on a particular bar of the roof.

A set of gauges shows water pressure, humidity, “something else” pressure, and “temperature.” The temperature gauge always reads the same number. The engineers have stopped checking it.

The industrial nature of the place is not loud in the sense of a factory engine, but in the sense of a thousand mechanical sounds layered together. The pipes of the roof, each of which is attached to a different part of the city’s hidden system of cisterns and reservoirs and cooling channels, are wired to a series of small, self-powered turbines that are not themselves engines but “catchers of energy,” as the engineers say, “for the engines below.”

These turbines are cylindrical, mounted on ball joints, and when they spin—which they always do, because the water always falls—they produce a low sound that is not a hum but a kind of continuous swallow of the air around the blades.

And somewhere, deeper than the floor, you can feel the engines that the turbines are feeding. The engineers are shy of explaining them, but they are not shy of pointing out the result of their existence: every city district, every factory, every house with the right valve and the right contract and the right thin hose of pipe and metal and algae, has access to water that falls from the sky only inside the Station of the Leaking Sky.

The city outside, for the most part, does not see rain. The clouds that are drawn over the region are not the same kind of cloud that water falls from, in the sense that you might understand. Those are sticky, and electrostatic, and low, and filled with tiny, faintly phosphorescent creatures that the wings of the station catch and “sort,” as the engineers say, “by the sound of their wings.”

So the rain of the city is not really weather. It is a transaction of biology and mechanics and something else, which happens to manifest itself as a leaky roof.

The strange thing about the station is not the fauna, or the roof, or even the sound of it all, which is like a cathedral built of noise and water and engines and breathing and the turning of tiny turbines inside glassfish. The strange thing is how everything has always been this way.

There is no change in the station, except that which someone has intended to stop.

The moss rats are not replaced by “new” moss rats; they are the same moss rats, continuously replaced at the edges, by an unknown mechanism of regeneration and shared heredity, but not in a way that produces cohorts of “young” and “old.” The oldest of the engineers swear that they have seen the same individual moss rat in the same corner of the station, with the same patch of missing fur on its left ear, for the entire time they have been here.
