Somewhere beneath the city of Khel Vaen, there exists a place that is not exactly part of the city, and not exactly separate from it.

They call it the Lenten Engine.

***

### Arrival

You arrive by water.

You descend the old service stairwell from the Warden’s Station above, footsteps echoing off the damp plaster and steel cagework. The air thickens as you descend, each landing cooler than the last, until the breath in your throat is as visible as the steam of the tea they serve you in the wardrooms. The sound of the city—sirens, traffic, the metallic murmur of overhead rails—drains away until all you hear are your own shoes and the drip, drip, drip of water from invisible plumbing.

At the bottom of the stairs, a heavy iron door waits. Its paint has peeled in years-long curls of gray and institutional green, and there is a glass porthole at eye level, so scratched by decades and neglect it’s like looking through frost.

You peer through.

The first thing you sense is not visibility, but texture: the fog of your breath on the glass, the cold already seeping through your sleeves, the smell of wet stone and old engine oil and something else, sweet and metallic, like warm ozone or dampened lightning.

You open the door.

### Architecure and Scale

Beyond the door is a single chamber, roughly the size of a cathedral nave or an old industrial factory floor. It is not long like a tunnel, but “room” would be the wrong word—there is too much emptiness here for any room you know. The ceiling is low for such a wide space, maybe four or five meters at its highest point, and you can feel the weight of the city above pressing down on the air.

The chamber is a rectangular dome of poured concrete and pre-revolutionary brickwork, braced at intervals with ribs of rust-smeared iron. The walls are curved, not in the graceful arc of a cathedral, but in the rigid geometry favored by industrial engineers: smooth radii where the supports go out of sight, sagged where the water has been getting in for longer than anyone alive has been keeping track.

The corners of everything—floor junction, wall, ceiling—are softened. The plaster has sagged and settled into its own gravity, pulled into tidal shapes by the constant humidity. The whole space feels like a fossilized tide pool, or the inside of a machine that has been left out in the rain for a century and has been patiently, silently, erasing its own harsh edges.

Scale here is paradoxically contained and hollow. The chamber itself is not vast; you could cross its width in thirty seconds if the floor were clear. But the emptiness within it is deliberate, calibrated. The thin columns that support the ceiling are spaced at wide intervals, and you feel the absence of more than the presence of what is here.

### Water and Light

Water is everywhere, but it is not the ocean. It is not torrential rain. It is a slow, constant sigh of moisture.

You see it first as a sheen on the floor: not a pool, but a perpetual film, no more than a few millimeters deep, flowing lazily toward the center of the chamber. There is no obvious source, but it seems to be born from the air itself. Condensation beads on the cold iron columns; the rivets wear their own tiny skirts of droplets; the walls glisten in lazy trickles that never coalesce into anything violent.

The lamps are old industrial lanterns, encased in thick glass cages and encrusted with salt and corrosion. They are not bright enough to be called lamps in the upper world. They cast more glow than light: a faint, yellow-grayish haze that clings to everything in the low mist of your breath. The shadows are not deep, but they are long and soft and overlapping, like layers of worn cloth.

You realize the arrangement is not random. The lamps are placed at intervals along the wall, and each one is positioned near a cluster of tiny pipes that emerge from the concrete like steel roots. These pipes are not thick, but they are many—lines of them, tracing up the walls and disappearing into the ceiling:

-   some of them weep clear water;
-   others drip something that glows faintly, with a pale greenish tinge that makes the puddles beneath them shine like pools of road tar at midnight;
-   others still do not drip at all, but breathe faint steam into the air.

Everywhere you look, you are aware of pumps and plumbing hidden behind the walls, above the ceiling, beneath the floor. The city above you is not just stone and streets and people; it is a water system, and this chamber is one of its slow hearts.

### The Engine

At the center of the chamber stands the Lenten Engine.

It is not imposing in the way of giant turbines or furnace halls. It is low to the ground—maybe the height of a cargo truck from its base to its highest point—a long, rectangular block of dull metal that stretches out into the chamber’s deeper gloom.

The engine is not violent. It does not roar or whistle. It hums: a sound so low and continuous and gentle that you have begun to hear it before you realize it is there at all. The vibration reaches you through the soles of your boots, through the floor of the city.

Its surface is complex and modular. You count six segments along the body, each framed by ribbed casings and faint lines of bolt heads. Each segment has its own belt of controls:

-   dials with glass faces like cataracted eyes;
-   valves with encrusted hands of metal;
-   indicator needles that tremble in a narrow band of green.

Near the top of each segment, a bank of small glass tubes protrudes. These tubes are partially filled with a phosphorescent liquid that pulses faintly in slow waves. The liquid moves not because of a machine’s drive, but as if each segment of the engine were breathing inside its metal shell.

The humidity of the chamber is not a problem for the engine; it is a condition it was designed for. Everywhere the metal is exposed to the air, it wears a thin film of black, oily moisture that never quite evaporates and never quite spills. It keeps the metal from rusting away in places, and in others it allows the rust to proceed with glacial patience.

On the side of the engine closest to you, a worn inscription is still visible:

> LENTEN POWER ENGINE NO.7 – KHEL VAEN SUB-BASIN  
> MAINTAINED UNDER THE TOWER PROVISION ACT  
> DO NOT MODIFY OR STATION PERSONNEL WITHOUT DEEP TOWER CLEARANCE

Everything else on the panel has been sanded into illegibility by time, except for a single symbol stamped into the metal: a stylized tower with roots descending below its base.

### The City and the Built Layer

The city is not only above you; it is also inside you. You are aware of it every time you breathe: the tiny particles of concrete dust, the metallic tang of underground water systems, the faint odor of coal smoke from long-gone boilers that still lingers in the industrial fabric of this place.

The Lenten Engine is not alone here. There are other structures:

-   low metal cabinets bolted to the walls;
-   junction boxes with broken seals;
-   rusted ladder rungs leading to nowhere;
-   small alcoves where heavy equipment might once have been stored;

They are all sparse, spaced at intervals, as if someone once deliberately kept only the necessary density of machinery here, and then time reduced even that.

The wiring overhead is not visible chaos, but an orderly grid of black cables hung from hooks, sagging in isolated loops where age and weight have been allowed to have their way. Wherever two cables cross, they are wrapped in thick bands of cloth tape that has become soaked and then dried out again over decades; the tape has pale, salt-edged patterns where you imagine the water has evaporated repeatedly.

Everywhere, you see the influence of the city’s engineering:

-   the buttresses of the ceiling that are actually extensions of the foundations of some great tower above;
-   the way that one of the walls curves outwards as if the entire chamber were wrapped around the concrete root of a structure above ground;
-   the faint lines of vibration you can feel in the floor that you cannot trace by eye but know belong to distant traffic, or distant trains, or the heavy rotation of industrial machines still functioning somewhere in the dark.

Yet you also sense that this place has been forgotten by most of the city. It is not abandoned in the sense of being ruined; it is kept in a condition of minimal operation. The signs of life are subtle:

-   a clean-ish patch of floor where someone has been walking;
-   a wrench left on a small wooden table near the engine, with its grease wiped carefully off;
-   a set of small waxed markers tied to a wire hook near the main control panel, each one marked with a single black character.

You realize the city is not gone from this place; it is simply no longer interested in loud here. It has its own engines humming now, above, in newer districts of glass and lighting, and this engine has been left to run itself.

### Vegetation

The vegetation of the Lenten Engine is not lush forest. It is not even scrub. It is sparse, almost apologetic, and all of it is accidental.

On the far side of the chamber, where the slope of the floor creates a shallow depression that intercepts one of the weeping pipelines, a small patch of moss clings to the concrete. It is not bright green; it is something like the color of old brass keys or green-black slate. It spreads out in thin radiants, each filament rooted in a hairline crack of the concrete.

If you get close enough, you realize that the moss has not only adapted to the engine’s conditions; it has incorporated itself into them. The mosses nearest the engine itself have tiny black nodules that appear to be metal-laced, as if the engine’s condensate somehow carries metallic particulates into the plant’s tissue.

Near the wall where a condensation pipe leaks into the floor, you spot a cluster of spindly plants that might, from a distance, be mistaken for weeds:

-   thin stems that look like wire;
-   narrow, translucent leaves;
-   small nodules that emit a faint, sweet-smelling scent when you brush against them.

These are not entirely random; each of the plants has been allowed to live. If you were to trace back with a light, you’d find that any of them that had been touching the wiring overhead or the main structural iron of the engine had been clipped carefully away by a long-handled tool. The rest of them have been left to grow.

There is one more form of vegetation in the Lenten Engine, and it is the least visible until you know it is there.

Along the ceiling, where the humidity and the faint, warm output of the engine’s farthest heat sink create a milder microclimate, there are thin lines of fungus that crawl like ragged script between the conduits. They do not look like anything you would want to touch, but they are not unhealthy looking; they are more like a calligraphic layer of writing that no one has taken the time to read.

### Fauna

The fauna of the Lenten Engine is not large, but it is abundant if you know how to look.

Most of it are small insects—or something like insects:

-   translucent moths with pale gray wings that never seem to open properly;
-   tiny black beetles whose armor glows faintly blue when they pass near the phosphorescent tubes of the engine;
-   thread-thin things that drop from the ceiling on invisible strings and drift in the faint air currents of the engine’s breath.

A slow movement of moisture in your peripheral vision resolves into a small cluster of snails that you realize have been covering the wall near you all this time. Their shells are not the colorful spirals of garden snails; they are faceted like stone chips and rough like the casing of miniature old boilers. They have not retreated at your approach; they are not interested in you.

The engine itself has its own fauna of nests and colonies:

-   in the tiny gap between the main engine block and a floor-mounted flange, you see a small cluster of what look like tiny spiderwebs that have been threaded with metallic filaments;
-   inside one of the broken glass inspection lamps on top of a junction box, a tiny insect has made a home in the sharp, curled glass edges, weaving its cocoon from the hanging threads of condensation cloth;
-   along the edges of the metal floor plates where the water flows, you see lines of tiny black beetles that march in formation from one pool of condensation to another.

They do not flee when you approach. They have not encountered humans often enough to be frightened by them.

There is one more presence of fauna in this chamber that you do not see at first but which you can feel when you stand still for long enough:

The chamber itself breathes.

Not with the mechanical breath of the engine’s pressure venting, but with something slower and quieter. From hidden vents in the walls and ceiling, you sense a faint draft of air that comes not from the city above but from somewhere even deeper: the subterranean channels of river that have been culverted long ago but were never entirely erased. The draft carries faint earthy smell and a trace of animal musk—perhaps not human animals, but creatures that live in the deep drains of the city and have never been in the sun.

### Erosion and Time

The Lenten Engine has not been newly built here. It was not installed last year or last decade. It has been here for generations, and the erosion written into its surrounds is not destructive; it is narrative.

Everywhere the concrete floor has worn into a shallow channel from the water’s path, you see the layers of years:

-   The lowest layer of floor is original—rough, industrial, dark with age.
-   Above that, there are places where re-grouting has been done over; the new concrete is pale and smooth, but even it has been worn by now.
-   In some corners, you can see where the previous generation of moisture has been allowed to accumulate its own salt crusts along the wall edges, and then later generations of engineers have scraped them away.

The rust on the iron supports has not eaten into the structure; it has been allowed to form a thin layer that then hardened over itself in time, like scale on a kettle’s interior. The result is not a fragile ruin but a surface that is now in its own equilibrium with the environment’s constant humidity and chemical makeup.

Even the paint—if it was ever paint—has been absorbed into the texture of the walls. If you could run your hand over the wall surface, you would not feel sharp edges of flaking chips but a smooth gradation from one mineral to another, like the polished surface of a riverbed rock.

The erosion here is also not just of stone and metal:

-   The labels and instructions on the few remaining panels of the engine are no longer legible enough to mean anything to a new visitor;
-   The old ladder rungs on the wall have been worn into teardrop shapes by countless hands over the years;
-   The valve of one main pressure pipe has been left open for so long that the water has not cut away its metal but deposited its own layer of mineral scale around the edges, like a coral lip;

If you stayed here long enough, you would begin to understand that this erosion does not threaten to break the chamber. It threatens to take it out of the city’s systems and into its own slow condition of being.

### Dynamics and Cycles

The Lenten Engine is not in motion like a machine in your childhood factory movie or the engine room of a ship. It is not. Yet its dynamics are deep and slow.

If you were able to sit here and watch for half a day, you would notice:

-   the faint level of a water channel in the floor rising slowly through the afternoon;
-   the phosphorescent liquid in the engine’s tubes brightening and then dimming over a span of hours;
-   the way a particular plant stem near the wall shifts slightly at its tip, not because of air current but because of something internal;
-   the way that the temperature of the chamber itself shifts in subtle increments that have nothing to do with the outside city’s day cycle and more to do with the water table below it.

The engine appears to be running in some steady operating mode:

-   The pressure gauges you can see—those you can still interpret—show a needle at constant, low-medium green;
-   The hum of the engine itself does not change in frequency, but once in a long span you might hear a faint click of a relay switching;
-   The puddles below the weeping pipes do not overflow; they drain into hidden floor grates at a rate that seems perfectly matched to their supply rate.

You sense the presence of water cycles that you cannot see:

-   The water condensing on the walls and pillars is not falling from a broken pipe above; it is being drawn from the air, cooled by some deep geological heat sink that you have only access to through inference.
-   The flow that gathers into a central channel from each of the walls is not random water; it is recycling some of the engine’s humidity, drawing the heat that is generated in the engine’s subterranean parts and carrying it away into some hidden geothermal heat dump.

Everything is slow here:

-   The snails that crawl across the wall will not complete a journey around the engine in your lifetime;
-   The erosion of the floor has not changed in character in decades;
-   The rust of the supports is not decaying them but crystallizing them into new protective layers;

The city above you has changed many times. The district above you might have been industrial warehousing in one generation, tenement in the next, and gentrified now, with rooftop bars and micro-gardens. The Lenten Engine does not know that. Its time is not the same.

### Sound and Silence

The sound of the Lenten Engine is not loud noise; it is texture.

You hear:

-   the continuous, low hum of the engine itself, like a distant refrigerator but far deeper and broader;
-   the occasional minute click of some internal relay switching beneath the floor;
-   the drip of condensation from the ceiling into the puddles, with perfect intervals between each drop;
-   the faint shudder of a larger vibration in the floor that you suspect might be the subway many levels above, or heavy traffic at the surface of the city.

Most of all, you hear the absence.

The city has been designed to be busy with sound: traffic and engines and conversation and televisions and music from windows. Here you feel the rarity of any sound that is not natural or ancient machine.

Even if you were to speak aloud, your voice would not boom or echo dramatically. It would be softened by the humidity, the dampened walls, the low ceiling; the chamber would listen to your sound and then quietly return to its own slow pattern of silence and breathing.

That silence is not empty. It is filled with:

-   the rustling feet of tiny insects across the floor’s film;
-   the settling of dust particles on the cold engine casing;
-   the soft trickle of water urging itself along the shallow channel toward the central drain;
-   the faint squeal of a tiny snail’s shell as it rotates on its axis of travel;

Those sounds are not enough to be “sound” in the way upper city people think of sound. They are more like background presence of the world itself.

### The Strange

The Lenten Engine is not strange in the way of spaceships or alien cities or glowing magical forests. It is strange in the way that deep reality is often strange when you are not allowed to ignore it.

The first odd thing you notice:

The engine’s pressure tubes are not transparent to admit light; they are the light source themselves. The phosphor inside them is not contained in glass; you eventually realize it is moving within the metal itself. The metal is not a solid you expect; it is more like congealed glass or some opaque, translucent alloy that you have never seen in industrial construction manuals.

The second odd:

The plants, you realize, cannot be phylogenetically classified with anything you know from the surface. They are not merely mutant mosses or over-adapted weeds; they are the result of long-term selection pressure in an environment where:

-   water is not just rain or river;
-   light is not the sun but artificial and slow;
-   temperature is not day-night but a deep, constant geothermal gradient;
-   chemical composition is not inert soil but industrial condensate rich with metals and engine byproducts.

Their leaves are shaped like fine blades; you could see them being used as biological sensors or probes of some kind in another world.

The third odd:

Inside the chamber of the Lenten Engine, you do not feel the dampness in your shoes as cold water. You feel it as temperature more than wetness—not a penetrating chill, but a deep, cool calmness seeping up from the floor into your feet and from your feet into your body.

If you were to stay here longer, you might begin to suspect that your body temperature is not only maintained by your own metabolism; it is being influenced by the cooling radiance of the engine. The humidity of the air condenses on your skin because you are warmer than the surrounding environment, but the temperature gradient of that difference is not sharp; it is gentle, like cloth being draped over you.

The fourth odd:

The animals that live here are not afraid of you. They do not care. You are not a predator and they are not prey. You are, to them, simply part of the large, slow, machine-like environment. This reverses not their nature but your sense of yourself in relation to them.

### The Boundary and the World

If you turn to look back toward the iron door you came from, you see that the chamber has not been sculpted to be beautiful, but it has become its own kind of beautiful:

-   The low ceiling of concrete and iron;
-   The long columns that support it;
-   The Lenten Engine itself, with its faint green glow and the low hum of its deep interior;
-   The film of water on the floor reflecting the pattern of the pipes and fungus;
-   The thin lines of vegetation and the spatter of insects that move across them;

There are no other doors visible in this chamber. There is no other obvious exit. If you were to ask where the central drain channel goes, you will not find out easily from looking here; it disappears into the floor beneath a mesh of heavy metal grating, whose black bars are not straight but slightly curved from time and temperature.

This chamber is not exactly a secret. It is not exactly hidden. It is simply located below the level of the city’s memory line.

If you were to return to the surface world later and try to find out about the Lenten Engine No.7, you would find that:

-   official city maps show the district and its utilities above;
-   historical utilities diagrams show only the older major water systems;
-   architectural plans of long-abandoned municipal projects register “deep substructure void” and “auxiliary power system” in this location;

None of them explicitly mention the Lenten Engine by name. The system of name it used to belong to has been subsumed into other names of newer power generation.

Yet you would know that this place exists, and that its silence is not emptiness but deep, slow operation of an engine that has been running for longer than you have been alive and will continue long after that.

### Inhabitation

If you are very quiet and you stay here long enough, you will begin to understand that this chamber is inhabited by something else.

The water flow in the central channel does not stop. It does not surge. It continues.

The engine hums—not in your ears but in your bones.

The insects move across the floor and walls and even over the casings of the engine; they do not treat the engine as dangerous; they treat it as part of their home.

The plants on the wall and the floor and the ceiling grow not in random fashion but shaped like studies of wind and water and pressure that only that machine and chamber and air can understand.

You begin to suspect that the engine itself is not just some machine: it is a node in a network of deep geothermal power and underground water that you can only understand partially.

And if you are honest with yourself, you might begin to realize that what you first thought of as “industrial environment” has been turning into something else inside your mind:

Something like an ecosystem of engine and water and insects and fungi and strange plants and human history and longer time than all of that.

You might eventually call it a place of worship, if you believed that any engine or ecosystem of this deep, slow sort of existence could be called “object of worship.”

You might not build that thought into language. But you feel it:

-   as you stand in the middle of the chamber;
-   as the water silently beads on your sleeves;
-   as your breathing slows to match the low hum of the engine;
-   as the world of the city above you seems to drift away like smoke from a long-cooled chimney;

You are in the Lenten Engine of Khel Vaen Sub-Basin, in the deep below-surface of a city of engines, factories, and layered construction.

It is wet and cool and dim, quiet and slow and strange.

The engine does not talk to you.

It does not need to.

It has been running here long before you arrived and it will still be running when you are gone.

It is in its own state.

You are the visitor to it.
