There is a pocket of the city inside the mountain where the light still works.

I’m not sure where else to call it “city,” because the corridors you’d recognize end at the wall of stone. Here there are no streets, only a single cavern of improbable height. The feeling of settlement clings to it anyway: the way the air circulates on schedule, the way sound never quite decays into wilderness, the way every fracture in the rock has been measured, numbered, and gently reinforced by something like engineering.

The ceiling is a smooth arch of dark, glassy stone, streaked with softer bands where the water seeps in. It’s not a true cave, you’re told; the mountain is vast and cold and new, and the city that clings to its inner flanks has simply been enclosed by it, over time, by the slow surge of magma that followed and the intricate work of later machines. The ceiling is itself a structure, though it looks like geology.

Light falls from slashes high in the wall—no windows, only openings like the mouths of flutes punched into the rock. They are not regularly spaced, but they are not random either: their geometry follows a logic that has been engineered into the mountain so that, at this height and at this hour of the day, the beams of cold light cross exactly where you stand now and flood the floor before you.

The floor is not perfectly flat, but it feels like it might be. In a normal world it would be a shallow lake, or a marsh thick with reeds, but vegetation is an absent category here. There are no roots, no leaves, no green. The rock floor is a mosaic of interlocking plates that look like the polished lid of an instrument. Each segment is carved with fine channels that join together in a branching pattern only visible from the wrong angle of sunlight—by then the shadows are thin and precise, and the water running across the floor seems to trace a map in real time.

Water is everywhere here, but in a way that feels like infrastructure rather than weather.

Condensation runs from the ceiling in smooth, clear beads that never quite reach drip-speed. They march down the glassstone in lines that intersect like longitude on a globe, and when they reach the carved gutters along the walls they become strings of pale water that flow into the floor mosaics. The air is cold enough that you can see hints of your breath when you move, but the water itself doesn’t freeze. It never pools long enough.

The moisture gives the stone an unusual depth: the black rock becomes translucent for a finger’s thickness before it resumes its opaque interior. Where the water films over the surface, you can see faint internal structures—like a frozen moment of turbulence, or like the ghost of a city grid that has been turned inside out and embedded.

There is sound, but not much noise.

The first layer you hear is wind. Not the wide, open movement of air above the clouds, but the compressed circulation of the mountain’s interior systems. It comes from the flute-shaped openings in the walls and ceiling, where gusts of cold air and warmer air meet and exchange energy. Every time the temperature somewhere else in the complex shifts slightly, the balance here shifts with it: a draft sighs across the floor, then pauses, as if the mountain itself is taking slow breaths.

Underneath the wind is a whisper of water against stone. That’s not the sound of river or rain so much as the sound of calibrated surfaces sliding against each other. The channels that carry the flow are designed to be almost silent. You only hear the water at the narrows, where it accelerates slightly, or where the rivulets from the walls meet the floor channels and obey the new angle of attack.

There is no other fauna here but the rare ones.

If you are very still and patient, you might see a thin, translucent shape like a length of silk snaking through the air near a wall. It moves like a thread pulled by invisible currents, but it doesn’t drift; it follows particular lines, usually the trajectories of the strongest updrafts. If you watch long enough, you see that it doesn’t touch the stone unless it has to—only when the airflow pattern fails it and it must glide along the surface until it finds a new current.
