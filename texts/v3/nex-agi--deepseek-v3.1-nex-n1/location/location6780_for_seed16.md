On the hottest nights of the year—when there’s no pretending to seasons anymore—the city of Khe’len goes down on its knees to drink.

By day it hides.  

By night it opens its throat.

---

## The Place of the City

The city sits in a deep bowl where the old stone of the plateau has been worn down by centuries of heat into a shallow, hollowed depression. The land itself is a closed basin: no river flows in or out, no water but the faintest spray ever leaves the rock walls that ring it like a cupped hand.

This is not a desert of emptiness. It has no dust storms, no shimmering dunes. The bedrock beneath the city is a pale, porous limestone threaded through with darker bands of slick silicate—glasslike veins that writhe and knot where they ooze out of fissures, like black tongues tasting the air.

You can smell the stone when you climb down into it. The air smells of chalk and heat, of baked dust thirty years old and the faint lingering tang of something metallic. Not metallic like iron ore, but like the aftertaste of lightning.

---

## The City Without Water

Khe’len is built on water’s absence.

The city’s bone-white limestone terraces and stepped courtyards are carved into the sides of the bowl, each house buried like a fossil into the rock. There are no gutters, no sewers. No one drains away anything, because nothing comes in.

A few public wells pierce the deepest levels of the city, but they are mostly symbolic: their shafts are dry, their mouths stopped up with stone plugs carved with reliefs of waterbirds and waves that remain purely theoretical. No one has ever seen a bird here.

The roofs are flat, shallow basins rimmed with low parapets. The walls are honeycombed with narrow, angled channels cut into the stone. No rain falls into those channels—not in years, not in decades. But every night Khe’len plans for water.

If you walk through the city at midday, when the heat sits like a weight on your shoulders and the air is thick and still, you’ll see only a few people moving between shadowed doorways. The city seems empty, almost abandoned. That’s the illusion of low density: the whole place feels like a handful of scattered beads dropped into a bowl.

---

## The Vegetation in the Bowl of Darkness

The bowl’s interior holds almost no people—at least not visibly. But it holds life.

From the top edge of the basin rim, where the wind carries faint hints of pollen from far away, the hollow looks at first like a black pit ringed with the faint line of the city’s terraces. Then you notice the color: the lower slopes and the basin floor are not black but a deep, bruised indigo.

The present grows there.

It grows at night.

---

### The Present-Garden

The present (the word is untranslatable, but the city-mothers call it that) grows in thick, low mats that blanket the basin floor and climb the walls in irregular patches like submerged stains. The “plants” are not plants in any earthly sense: they are rooted, but they root in stone, not soil.

Each present-form grows in layers:

- On the bottom are the **founders**, thin, translucent threads like glass veins that spread under the bedrock surface, dissolving avenues of calcium into the air and leaving behind hollows where the stone once held solid.
- On top of them are the **sleepers**, dormant nodules of hard, glossy tissue that look like lacquer dropped into puddles and allowed to set.
- The vertical **speakers** rise from these in irregular, translucent columns like dark fingers made of stained glass. They never grow tall enough to be called trees, but they thicken over decades into short pillars you can lean against.

At night, when the temperature at the bottom of the bowl is still punishing, the present comes awake. The speakers open along their lengths like long lips peeling back from dark teeth. Each fold is lined with tiny, translucent sacs, like dew stolen from somewhere else and stored in glass.

---

### The Sound of Lush

The sound of Khe’len is not the sound of water.

It is the sound of glass breathing.

When the present wakes, the air inside the bowl fills with a low, continuous susurrus—hundreds of thousands of glass sacs swelling and contracting, releasing and reabsorbing the faintest moisture into their cells. The sound isn’t loud at any given instant, but it accumulates: layer upon layer cancels out silence until you stand in a constant hum like breathing just beneath the threshold of hearing.

The upper terraces, where the city falls asleep to the hum of a glass jungle beneath them, surround the basin with a low, continuous moaning. The “speakers” are named for what the city-mothers thought they did first: not speak but carry sound.

---

## The Night of Thirst

The temperature rises by night rather than by day in Khe’len’s bowl: the vertical walls trap heat, and the stone radiates what it has soaked in during the day. The climate here is diurnal, but its cycle runs backward:

- Day is cool, quiet, empty-seeming.
- Night is hot, loud, busy.

In the first hours of darkness, when anyone outside would still mistake night for day by the heat alone, the city-mothers gather precise tools of stone and bone, polished wood and carefully shaped glass knives. They wear their white masks and their black robes, and they move in single-file down stone staircases cut into the basin wall.

---

### The Ritual That Is Not a Prayer

At the bottom, where the present-garden is thickest and the speakers rise like columns around them, they perform their version of meteorological engineering.

They do not cut water from stone. They cut water from air.

They press their glass knives to carefully selected points on the speakers’ surfaces—limitations carved into the stone maps by generations of city-mothers before them. Where the knives touch, the hard glass tissue cracks along faint lines already written into it by cycles of heat and weathering.

From each crack comes a faint exhalation of steam.

Not water: not in any way you can touch. It dissipates into the darkness almost before you can see it. But the pressure drop in the speaker’s air sacs forces the dense air around the column to rush inward—and in that sudden rush, something changes.

Above the bowl of the city, where the night sky is black and close and the stars are sharp as broken glass, cooler air lingers. The present-garden draws this cooler air down in thin streams like invisible fingers of wind reaching into the basin.

As the deeper air no longer sits still, it begins to circulate.

Microscopic droplets of water that have traveled for thousands of kilometers in invisible suspension finally condense on the cool surfaces of the speakers, forming a thin, slick film of moisture that the city has never named rain.

---

### The Hard Work of Thirsting

The city-mothers work in teams. Each team knows the precise cut they must make to one particular column of the present-garden: not enough to kill it, not enough to cause it to collapse inward in a sudden hiss, but just enough to open a small airway into the hollow of its glass sacs.

The work is primitive. It relies on pressure and heat and geometry rather than on pumps or engines or machines. It relies most on knowledge of erosion.

The present-garden has been growing here for centuries, slowly changing the basin’s shape. Each year it hollows the stone a little more deeply; each year it raises the columns slightly higher; each year it changes the pattern of air that flows in and out of the bowl. The city-mothers have kept careful track of this slow reshaping of stone.

They have carved their maps into stone tablets, each tablet showing the basin’s shape at a particular moment in time—where erosion has hollowed deeper, where the present has thickened, where air has begun to move differently. They overlay these maps like layers of sediment laid down over older layers of sediment.

They know, by instinct and calculation, exactly where to press their glass knives tonight.

The work is loud.

---

### The Voice of Khe’len

When dozens of teams work at once, the sounds of the present-garden rise into a chorus.

Speakers crackle like glass parchment being folded and unfolded. The air inside them moves in thin, whistling tones like breath through constricted throats. The air currents themselves stir around you like invisible rivers flowing just beneath the surface of hearing.

The city-mothers call out to each other across the basin: short, sharp phrases that have been reduced to only essential information over generations of practice.

“Too deep,” one woman will shout, and another will respond immediately with “Less,” or “Withdraw,” or “Hold.”

City-mothers on the uppermost terraces lean out over their low walls and listen to the hum below. They interpret the song of the present-garden: the way tones shift, the way pressure builds and falls. They send signals down to their colleagues with hand gestures carved in stone below their balconies—no need for light, no need for paper.

This is Khe’len’s true voice: not the whispering of the present itself but the layered wavelengths of call and response between women who read the landscape like a book that has been writing itself for centuries.

---

## Built Environment in the Cradle of Heat

The city itself is built only where the rock walls are thick enough to hold hollows without collapsing inward. The stone houses are carved back into the cliff faces rather than outward; they sit like lenses embedded in the rock itself.

Each house has a narrow entrance facing inward toward the basin, so that at night the residents can look down into the darkness and listen to the present-garden breathing.

Each house has several small chambers carved deeper into the stone where the inhabitants sleep during the day when the heat outside would make them ill. These chambers are cool, not because they are insulated from the heat but because the rock itself conducts it away: slowly, over the course of hours, into the deeper parts of the plateau.

---

### The Civic Space of Khe’len

The bowl itself is the city’s main public space. There are no streets or plazas in it; no market stands, no children playing, no animals moving between houses. There is no dirt for animals to graze on, no undergrowth for them to hide in.

There are only stone paths leading from house entrances down to the basin rim where the city-mothers perform their work each night.

At the top of the basin, where the rim’s uneven edge meets the rest of the plateau in a gentle slope, there is a single ring of stone-roofed shelters facing outward. These are the guesthouses, where travelers from distant places (if any ever arrive) are supposed to stay, but in practice many of them remain empty year-round.

Because the population density is low, the “city” feels more like a scattered collection of small households grouped around a central, hollow space rather than like a dense urban settlement. The nearest city-mother might live a hundred paces away, but the distance between them feels like a hundred miles because of the emptiness between.

---

## The Rules of the World

The world of Khe’len’s bowl follows a few simple rules that have been learned through observation rather than experiment:

1. **Nothing Flows Inward.** Water does not flow into the basin from anywhere. No rain comes. No streams flow. No ground water moves beneath them.

2. **Nothing Flows Outward.** Water does not flow away from Khe’len’s bowl. No rivers flow out of the basin, no groundwater drains out beneath it. It sits, sealed, like a closed system.

3. **Everything Moves as Air.** The only movement that enters and leaves the bowl is air movement—heat escaping to the sky at night, cooler air drifting down to replace it.

4. **The Present Takes Up Air’s Moisture.** The present-garden draws out of the air the tiny specks of moisture that have drifted across continents and stored them in invisible clouds until tonight.

5. **The Present Slowly Changes Everything.** Over decades and centuries, the present-garden hollows the stone deeper, changes the pattern of erosion on the basin walls, alters which way air flows. It reshapes its own environment.

6. **The City Must Adjust Its Cuts.** The city-mothers must constantly adjust where they cut their speakers, how deeply they cut them, and how often, so that the present-garden does not hollow the stone so deeply that the city collapses inward but also does not starve itself.

---

## The Living of Khe’len

The inhabitants of Khe’len have learned to live in the absence of water.

They drink no liquid water, because there is none to drink. They do not eat water in any obvious sense. They absorb moisture through their skin from the faint films that sometimes form on surfaces in the city-mothers’ most carefully tailored cuts.

They have learned to live in thin layers of condensing and evaporating moisture that drift between the present’s columns and the stone at their feet like ghosts too shy to manifest themselves fully.

They have learned to measure their movements by the heat on their skin rather than by the light they see with their eyes.

They have learned to read the sounds of the present-garden as carefully as they would read written words, if they had decided to invent writing on paper instead of on stone.

---

## The Weirdness of the Place

The weirdness of Khe’len lies not in the obvious—the glass garden, the absence of water—but in the subtle, slow bending of the world’s rules around it.

Within the small bowl of the city, the world has been slightly inverted:

- life grows lush without water;
- heat grows stronger at night;
- cities grow louder without commerce;
- and erosion becomes not an enemy of the world but its partner.

The city-mothers understand themselves as guardians of a subtle balance between air, stone, and glass rather than as guardians of water. They think of the present-garden as their co-worker rather than as their food or their tool. They think of erosion as a slow, careful collaborator reshaping the landscape beneath their feet.

---

## The Scale of Khe’len

The landscape itself is small. You could walk across the basin in less time than it takes a city-mother to cut three speakers. You could circle the rim in half that time again.

It feels vast because there is so much room between presences: between houses, between speakers, between people moving in the darkness below you.

Everything you can see at once matters deeply. The present-garden matters because its sprawl across the basin floor is the only visible sign of life outside the city itself. The city’s terraces matter because each terrace houses only a handful of people but sits between them and the heat that would otherwise kill them.

There is no room for waste motion here. No room for redundant gestures. No room for things that serve no purpose.

Each night the city-mothers perform their precise cuts in the glass garden, each woman knows exactly where she stands in relation to every other woman, every other speaker, every piece of erosion that has shaped the basin until tonight.

---

## The Most Important Thing About Khe’len’s Landscape

Khe’len is not about water.

It is about what happens in the almost complete absence of water.

The landscape is shaped by water’s ghost rather than by water itself. The glass vegetation exists in silent conversation with invisible moisture carried by air currents too thin to see. The stone’s erosion has been shaped by centuries of slow, subtle changes that are invisible to any eye except the eye of time.

The city itself is built where water would be if it existed, but it does not exist.

The most important thing about this landscape is that even though there is no water—no rivers, no lakes, no puddles, no rain—it feels full of the presence of water’s promise.

Standing at the rim of Khe’len’s bowl at night, listening to the glass breathing beneath you and feeling the heat rising upward as if the world were trying to exhale itself out into the sky, you understand that this landscape is not about water.

It is about the city that learned how to drink thirst.
