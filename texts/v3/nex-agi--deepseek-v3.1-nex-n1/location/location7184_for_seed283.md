The wetlands are a low, hot darkness full of the bodies you cannot see.

First and most of all is the heat. It sits on the place like a second mud, the air too thick to breathe as easily as you are used to; it carries the scent of wet plants and countless animals layered in a way that makes “stench” and “perfume” the same word. The sky is a low roof of storm cloud, but at night the clouds are only an absence deeper than the dark below them, black on black, and the unseen rivers of warm air flow over you from the direction of the sea you can smell but cannot see. The temperature is constant and even, the kind of air that never leaves your skin; clothes stick to your body within minutes, and every breath tastes faintly of salt and rot.

The land is almost perfectly flat, a slow basin that the rains have been filling for millennia: water not as a lake, but as a logic. Everything is either underwater or would be if the rain had been heavier last week. For a few days of every month the rain really comes, and then the whole world seems to be rising, silver sheets of water stretching from thigh to hip to chest high; then for weeks the water retreats, leaving behind the true skin of this place—a cracked, glistening floor of mud and plant and bone and shell.

You are standing at the edge of a drowned riverine forest, where the water is shallow enough that the trees have been able to settle, but deep enough that they have never been able to forget they are living half in water. The trees are not giants but they are dense, a packed stand of smooth trunks rising straight out of the mirrors of the water. Their roots are hidden under the surface where you can feel them but not see them, a web of knuckles and arches that slip and then catch beneath your boots. The bark is slick with moss and algae; the mosses have been here so long that the trees seem to have been grown from felt rather than wood.

Between the trunks and under the water grow the grasses and sedges and low, floating-leafed plants whose names you do not know but whose presence you feel as a relentless pressure against the skin of your legs. The water is never much more than waist deep, but the plants are so dense that wading through them is not like wading at all—it is like being slowly swallowed by a motionless, green-tasting animal. You can’t see the sky reflected below because the water is never clean glass; it is always skin of pollen, leaf fragments, and insects.

The animals of this place have been here for a long time, and the landscape has been shaped by them as much as by water or wind. You are not aware of them all at once, but you quickly become aware of how much of what you thought was background noise is actually body, motion, breath.

Frogs crowd the world. You cannot hear them as a chorus because there are no pauses between one sound and the next, only a texture of sound about the pitch of distant machinery, always running, never varying. You hear them as a pressure rather than a melody. The water is thick with their eggs in the right season, and the undersurface of the water carries a second layer of life in the form of tadpole clouds, black moving dusts of them drifting between the stems of the plants.

The fish are not shy but cannot be properly seen, either. The confusion of leaves and murk and hanging roots hides the true abundance of them, but you feel the water against your legs as if it is being continuously divided and rearranged by unseen bodies. The fish are always moving through the world you have just entered, as if you have been dropped into their bloodstream.

Above the water the insects swarm the heat with the weight of tiny rain. You almost never see one clearly. They are present as a blurring of the air around your head, a constant taste of wings and feelers and legs against your lips, the backs of your hands, the corners of your eyes. The swamp’s darkness is heavy enough that they are not the visible glitter you might expect; instead they are a texture in the hot air, a sensation of quantity rather than detail.

Birds are everywhere and not always heard as distinct voices. The canopy of the trees is not high but it is dense, and the birds are so thick in it that the difference between leaf and feather and flock can be hard to understand. You hear them as a low, continuous simmer of soft calls and rustles, with the occasional sharp outcry that disappears almost before your brain has registered it as sound. The birds’ lives are bound to the water and the forest and the insects and the fish; the system of each day depends on them and they have no time for you.

Small reptiles slip through the upper margins—the places where the water meets the trunks of the trees, the branches of the undergrowth, the edges of the mudflats that are briefly exposed after the water goes down. The water snakes and the little lizards are not rare but are hidden by the shade of the leaves and the constant motion of birds and insects above them. They are only the flick of a pattern that you almost never see clearly, but the pattern is always there.

Larger mammals are present in the same way. The water deer are slender things that have been living here so long that they have grown to love the darkness of the forest, and in the dim, humid heat you are more likely to smell their faintly sour hide than to see their shapes. They are drawn to the water edges, where for a few days each month the water level drops low enough that they can browse on the grasses that grow up through the surface of the mud. The rest of the time they are wandering ghosts moving through the shallows between the trees.

Somewhere out there between the bay and the forest edge are the crocodiles. The crocodiles, of course, are not monsters of story but patient animals that have already been here for thousands of years and expect to remain here long after you are gone. They are not frequent but are present, always, in the same way that death is always present in a place that is so perfectly alive. The crocodiles are the invisible pressure along the banks of the deeper channels where the water swirls with more purpose than you feel comfortable admitting.

For humans, this place has never been easy to build on. The ground is too soft to hold foundations; the trees are too strong to be simply removed. The few clearings that exist are almost always temporary, where a tree has finally fallen in the mud and the floating plants have immediately rushed in to reclaim the light. The few signs of human industry are not communities of dwellings but the distant roar of a ship you might hear from beyond the far trees—the engines of a river barge or ocean freighter making its way along the coastal edge of this world, bringing containers of metal goods and machinery parts to the shoreline factories that are somewhere else entirely.

Because you are not where the factories are. You are in the quiet that the industry produces: the place where the water filters the waste of that distant, electrified, engine-driven world, where the heavy rains slowly wash the air clean of the smoke that the rest of humanity has been making. The machines of the world beyond the forest edge have been at work for a long time now; the factories and the cities and the roads and the cars are all far away, but they are still present here as a faint taste of iron and oil carried on the occasional breeze that you rarely feel.

In the darkness of the swamp, night and day are not so different from each other. The clouds that cover the sky during the day are replaced by a cloudless night sky that is still invisible to you under the tree canopy and the humidity. The sun does not break through the trees in blades but as a faint greenishness, the way that light underwater seems to come from everywhere and nowhere at once. At night the moon is almost always absent or clouded over, and the darkness becomes thick enough that you can imagine the air itself has been replaced by something warm and breathing.

The sounds of this place are not loud but are continuous. The frogs and the insects and the birds and the slow stirring of the water around the roots of the trees combine into a steady texture that has no obvious beginning or end. You do not notice the sound until there is a break in it—when, for a few seconds after a fish surge or a bird scuffle, the texture of the world briefly falters and you hear the absence as clearly as you have ever heard a sound.

There are few rules in this world except the ones that have always been true here: the water is the boss, the light is slow, and the animals are the majority of the matter of the place. Humans are not necessary here but they are, occasionally, tolerated. You can walk through the swamp for hours if you are patient and very careful. You move slowly, not only because of the mud underfoot and the hidden roots that you can feel with your boots but because of the pressure of the many bodies in the air around you, all of them watching you with the same evenness that the weather itself must show.

The smells of this place are not strange but they are intense. The smell of wet wood and leaf and root and mud and insect and bird and mammal and fish and frog and algae and moss and flower and decay and new growth and old decay and newer decay again. The heat of the air makes the smells of everything stronger than you are used to, and after you have been here for a time the smell of the outside world of roads and dry dust and engine smoke seems like the memory of a place that could not possibly have been real.

The rains are not dramatic storms but slow events, the clouds that have been waiting above the world finally letting loose their water over the course of many days, the water falling straight into the leaves and the mud and the waiting mouths of a thousand thousand animals. The water rises and then, after weeks, it falls again, leaving the world of the swamp both exactly the same and completely new.
