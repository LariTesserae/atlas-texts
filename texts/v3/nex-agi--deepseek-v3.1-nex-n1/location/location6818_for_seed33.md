At the edge of everything, the city of gongs rises from the ice of a broken sky.

You arrive first as sound.  
Not loud sound: a faint, metallic shivering, like a struck tuning fork left to resonate in a glass bell. It comes at intervals, irregular yet measured, as if the landscape itself breathes in bronze and crystal. The air is thin enough to feel like absence, and the cold sits politely on your skin, not biting yet, but patient.

You climb a long ripple of black stone that shelves outward in steps too wide for any human leg. Each step is glass-smooth, not polished by machine but worn by something else that passed here over millennia—maybe ion wind, maybe the memory of weather. The stone is veined with faint luminescence: threads of phosphorus green and cyan that trace angular patterns like frozen circuitry. No heat accompanies the glow; this light is cold, borrowed from elsewhere.

Step by step, the world resolves.

You are high. Extremely high. The stairs rise across a radius of sky that has no good horizon. The dome of what you would call “night” above you is not perfectly dark but smeared with auroras that never seem to fall: curtains of violet and indigo that hang like torn silk, unmoving yet shimmering on the edge of motionless time. There is no sun, but something else must illuminate the air—because you see detail, though everything is faint, as if seen through a frosted window.

Below you, the stairs fall away into a cloud deck of frozen fog, a solid plain of gray mist so compressed it looks like stone. From this height, the cloud deck seems like a floor beneath the city, and you cannot tell how far it descends beneath that—if there is ground at all, or only the endless architecture of sky and crystal and metal.

The city itself is built along the flanks and peaks of a mountain range made of something that is not quite rock and not quite ice. It is a monochrome spectrum of grays and whites, with sudden flashes of color when a distant light turns on for no reason you can discern. The buildings are towers—some slender as needles, others squat as polished boulders—all carved from single pieces of material that looks like quarried glass or metallic ceramic. No windows in any normal sense; instead, surfaces curve inward or outward into lenses and hollows, catching faint auroral light and refracting it in strange geometries.

Around you, the world feels empty but not empty.

Empty in the physical sense: there are no streets thronged with bodies, no market clamor, no smoke, no machinery. No soft life of plants or thick pelts of vegetation. The stone, the ice, the glass—all are bare of vines or moss or soil. The air is dry as a vacuum chamber. There is no water here, not in any form you can recognize: no rain, no rivers, no clouds driven by steam. No humidity to catch in your throat or frost your exhalations.

But there is something else.

In the near distance, a tower of translucent gray gives off a low, metallic thrum. You watch the sound: you do not hear it so much as see it, as if the air around it has a slightly different texture every time the tone shifts. The surface of the tower vibrates faintly in response, crystalline patterns appearing and dissolving in its depths like glyphs forming and reforming. No technology as you know it—no wires, no engines, no visible tools—just the slow, resonant response of matter to pressure.

This is a city without craftspeople that you can see, but it clearly was constructed. Or, perhaps, constructed itself.

The temperature is cool, not numbing cold, but sufficient that your breath does not mist much; the air is too arid to hold visible vapor. No heat rises from vents or chimneys—no industrial burn of fossil fuels, no radiant warmth from living bodies. The warmth, if it exists at all, is subtle and indirect: the faint glow of the phosphorescent veins along the steps, the occasional blink of a deeper light within the towers, like a pulse behind closed eyelids.

The mountain range beneath and around the city creates a sense of vast scale. Your immediate vantage is only one terrace of a system that climbs and falls away on both sides into silent distances. The city stretches along ridges and peaks, visible for kilometers—or perhaps tens of kilometers—before the haze eats away detail and the towers become a suggestion, a line of needles against the sky. You can see how weather, if that is the word, has shaped this place: the edges of the towers are slightly rounded where they face the direction of wind (or whatever wind-equivalent flows here), and deposits of microscopic frost gather in the lee of overhangs, forming crystalline ridges that look like frozen waves.

Yet there is no sense of ruins or abandonment. This is not a city “after people”—it is a city in its own state, fully inhabited by forms of process that do not need bodies as you understand them.

The silence of the place is deceptive. Quiet, yes—but not absolute absence of sound. There are distant metallic tones, spaced by intervals so long you can forget to count them. Sometimes there is a soft hum from the stone under your boots, as if something beneath you is shifting in slow motion. Occasionally a faint whistle like air escaping through a crack too thin to see—but no air rushes past your face; the sound originates without wind.

Fauna exists here, but it does not seem like animals you would recognize.

You see movement first as a flaw in the air, a shimmering lens that drifts horizontally along a terrace one level below you. It has no limbs or wings or eyes that you can see, but it follows a path that seems too deliberate to be random convection. It passes near a tower, and as it does, the tower’s faint glyphs brighten for a heartbeat—then dim again as the thing passes. It continues on its way, veering slowly around an invisible obstacle you cannot perceive, and finally disappears through a wall of glass as if the wall were a suggestion it was allowed to ignore.

You cannot call it creature or machine; it is something else entirely.

A little later, another motion appears higher on the slopes: a pattern of thin lines, like scratches in glass, that glide across the surface of a pale tower. They rearrange themselves into geometric shapes—triangles nested within circles—then break apart and scatter, leaving the surface unmarked. No tool marks them, no hand draws them; the surface simply remembers different lines for a while and then forgets them.

This is the fauna of the place: rare, subtle behaviors of matter and physics that have learned to repeat themselves.

The vegetation—if you can stretch the word—is equally unfamiliar.

Here and there along the steps, you notice small, crystalline growths no more than knee-high. They are like translucent ferns made of frozen salt, or branching coral of glass. No leaves, no stems, no green. No cells that you can see without magnification. They emerge from fine cracks in the stone steps, where the faint veins of light meet the surface. They pulse faintly with that same cold light, cycling at irregular intervals—sometimes faster, sometimes slowing for long stretches.

When you approach too close, these growths retract slightly, not all at once but like a tide receding: one branch dims, then shrinks as if melting into the stone, leaving only a faint scar. Then, when you step away, it slowly extrudes again, like breath forming frost on a window. You watch one cycle: retraction, then emergence, then retraction again, over what you guess might be minutes or hours. Time here feels strangely elastic.

The rules of this world have not been broken so much as bent beyond recognition.

The stones do not suffer erosion as you understand it—no water rain, no biological acids, no freeze-thaw cycle of ice in the ordinary sense. Instead, erosion here is a matter of subtle resonance: certain towers or sections of rock hum faintly when the auroral concentrations above them shift, and the surface responds by losing or gaining microscopic layers of material. You see a tower where the lower third is darker and smoother than the upper two thirds, as if decades of invisible wind have carved it away without leaving any obvious dust or debris.

But there are no dunes, no scree fields, no landslides. No storm clouds massing or thunder rolling. This is weather without pressure fronts or humidity gradients; weather as a conversation between rocks and sky and silent light.

The sky itself is a constant reality-break.

You look up, and the dome of night is not stably curved above you. Some sections are darker than others—not in patches like clouds, but in regions that seem to have different depth or texture. One region of the sky looks brittle, as if a hard enough blow might shatter it and reveal whatever lies beyond. Another region looks slightly thicker, like smoked glass, and moves faintly against the backdrop, like a film slipping over itself.

Occasionally, a crack of light appears—not lightning but a thin incision of brilliance, like a knife cutting across the sky fabric from one horizon to the other. It leaves a temporary scar of white light that slowly fades, leaving behind a faint afterimage for your eyes and, perhaps, for the stone below, which can sometimes be seen adjusting its internal glow patterns in response.

There are no stars here. Or perhaps there are, but they are not allowed to show.

The city, despite its scale and clarity and the apparent complexity of its structures, feels hollow.

Empty spaces between the towers stretch vast: no clutter of smaller buildings, no market squares or parks or alleyways. No traffic of any kind—no vehicles, no animals moving in herds, no crowds moving in patterns dictated by hunger or fear or curiosity. The density of presence, if that’s the right word, is extremely low—just enough entities to convince you that you are not alone in the universe, but not enough to feel crowded.

Yet you cannot escape the sense of urbanity: the repetitive arrangement of towers, the systematic angling of surfaces to catch or avoid certain directions of distant light, the way that certain structures rise along civic lines—like main avenues or district boundaries—even though there are no streets. You can see zones where the towers’ spacing and alignment shift subtly, marking neighborhoods or perhaps functions you cannot guess.

You might call this a city of patterns.

These patterns are not static. They flicker. You blink, and the tower two hundred meters away seems to have shifted its outline by a few centimeters. When you look fixedly at it, nothing moves; but when you glance away and back again, you suspect that the angles have changed. You try to fixate on a single tower and watch for change; but the longer you watch, the more your sense of certainty erodes. The tower itself seems to hold slightly different possibilities at once, and the act of observation forces one possibility into stability—for now.

You start to suspect that the city remembers its past differently than you might.

Perhaps there was no moment when construction workers laid stones in lines or masons carved glass from mountainsides. Perhaps the city has always existed here, in this form, more out of idea than out of material. Perhaps the towers are not the structures of this world but its natives—each tower a living concept of “place” and “height” and “direction” that has learned to repeat itself enough times to become visible.

You climb higher.

The steps continue, rising in broad sweeps that seem indifferent to human leg length or fatigue. You do not tire as much as you expect you should; the air is thin but not aggressive in its thinness, and your body adjusts to the cool, quiet pace of the place. You pass levels where the towers crowd closer to the stairs, forming corridors of dark glass. You pass through narrows where the stone above closes over your head in a low vault, and the faint phosphorescent veins in the ceiling wax brighter, as if responding to your presence.

Once, when you enter a particularly narrow section of stair, you feel a pressure in your ears that has nothing to do with altitude or air. The stone beneath your feet resonates slightly, and for a moment you feel the stairway as a vast instrument—string or plate of metal or crystal, stretched across kilometers of sky. The sound you imagine, if sound were a thing that could be felt in your bones rather than heard in your ears, is like a deep gong being struck once very far away.

The gong-city’s name.

The name does not come to you in words but as a sensation of vibration deep in your chest cavity. You understand that here, names are not things spoken but things felt, and the city’s name is a low chord struck repeatedly across unimaginable spans of time.

When you emerge from the narrows onto a broader terrace again, the vista opens wider than before.

From here, you can see more of the cloud deck below and the way the city rises above and into it like an archipelago of peaks in a motionless sea of frozen mist. The towers themselves are not uniformly spaced: you see regions where the density is higher—not exactly crowded but simply more present—and regions where a single tower dominates a wide area of nothingness, like a lighthouse on a vast plain of glass.

In the far distance, one region of the sky is darker than the rest, a concave hollow of shadow that seems to draw the eye more than its surroundings. You stare at it for a long moment, and your mind tries to resolve it into a storm cloud or a mountain peak obscured by haze. But the shape refuses to settle. It is not solid enough to be a mountain, not diffuse enough to be a cloud, not motionless enough to be part of the sky dome.

It is a shape of broken sky extending upward—or inward—or both.

Something like a faint ribbon of light trails from it toward the city below, like a filament of silk connecting the darkness to the glass towers. You blink and the ribbon is gone. Or, perhaps, never was there at all. The architecture of reality here allows for such discrepancies.

You look at your hands. They seem unchanged, but you feel a slight sense of wrongness when you try to reconcile your presence here with the place around you. You are not cut out for this kind of emptiness—no density of bodies, no familiar smells or sounds of water, no carpet of living things underfoot. Yet the city seems to make room for you, adjusting subtly around your presence like a fluid that accepts an intrusion as long as it is temporary.

You sit down on one of the steps, letting your legs dangle over the edge that overlooks the cloud deck. The stone beneath your palms is cool and smooth, with the faint vibration of distant gong-strikes still trembling through it. You have the sense that if you stayed here long enough—months or years or centuries—you might find yourself aligning to these patterns of sound and light and absence.

You might become one of the rare phenomena that occasionally drift across perceptions like flaws in the air, or patterns etched on glass.

You realize that this city does not need to be built any more than it already is built. It is already fully established, settled into its state of being. Weather happens without needing storms; erosion happens without needing rain; time happens without needing clocks. The few processes that move here—auroras, resonant tones, faintly glowing glyphs on tower surfaces—are enough to keep the world active without overwhelming it.

You stand up again and continue climbing.

The steps become fewer and farther apart, and the air feels even drier than before, though there is no mountain wind to carry away your breath. The sky above you seems closer now, the auroras brighter and thicker, like paint layered over pale glass. You feel as if you could reach up and touch the brittle regions of the dome, if you only had a way to extend your reach.

You never reach the top; there may not be one.

But from somewhere near the limit of your endurance, you glance back the way you came, and you see the city as alien pattern and built geometry, rising from a frozen sea of clouds beneath a sky that refuses to behave like sky. You feel the faint vibration of the gong city beneath your feet and hear—or think you hear—a distant response from somewhere far above and beyond.

You realize that you have been walking for a long time without noticing any thirst or hunger or pain beyond a mild ache of effort. Such biological needs feel faint here, as if forgotten by the world, or perhaps replaced by something else entirely.

You sit down again on the edge of the world and watch the occasional faint lights drift inside the distant towers. You listen to the quiet, metallic hum of the city breathing around you.

You do not know if you are in a place where time ends or begins or merely loops around itself like a ribbon of light connecting darkness to glass.

All you know is that here, at the edge of everything, the gong city rises from the ice of a broken sky and hums its low, unchanging name into the void. And for now, that is enough to make this place real.
