High above the tree line of any world that has trees, the plateau of Keth-Var stretches itself beneath the sky like a scar of bone and iron. The air is thin and clear—sharpened to a cutting edge by the altitude—but the light is astonishingly bright: a white, merciless brilliance that ricochets off every polished surface and sends long, stark shadows into an impossibly dense crowd of stone. This place is not empty. It is not open space. “Open” is only what you feel when you look out over the rim and realize the plateau below you is in fact a region—not a room—but every step within it leads you into tighter corridors of rock.

The first and last thing is rock. The plateau is not built of anything; no wall here has ever been laid by tool or intention. Yet the world has been its own mason. The rock is a dark, almost metallic basalt, shot through with veins of something paler—like quartz or fractured glass. The cliffs are layered into shelves and steps, as if the mountain had been a stack of plates someone left leaning askew an eternity ago. Everywhere the stone has been chewed by time and water and something else unnameable; not into smooth, rounded forms, but into intricate geometries—shattered facets, zig-zag edges, fins that run like knife blades through the open air.

The plateau is high, older than the valleys below, and the light feels closer to the sun. The sky is a pale blue-white and not particularly deep; it feels as if there is little between you and the vacuum beyond the atmosphere. Clouds are rare and thin when they appear, more like scratches of vapor than objects of their own. No haze, no softening glow: everything is sharp and clear-edged to the point of unreality.

Beneath and between this forest of stone is water: not the great presence of an ocean or a river, but water everywhere. The plateau is drenched without being drowned. The rocks are perpetually wet, not dripping with obvious drama but gleaming with a thin sheen of condensation. The air is cool enough to encourage this—always on the brink of sinking into a chill that never quite arrives. The moisture feels spectral: more atmospheric than terrestrial. It seems to emerge from the air itself as much as from any pool or stream.

There are no proper pools. No large lakes, no familiar banks, no slosh of water at your ankles. Instead, water carves its presence into the stone in ways that are almost architectural. The rock faces are lined with shallow channels—runes of water movement—each polished by a slow, never-ending film of liquid. In some places the channels run straight down vertical cliff faces; in others they trace spirals or faint zigzag patterns like inscriptions on an old currency. The water is clear enough to be invisible but for the way it catches light: it turns the dark stone black and reflective, making the world feel doubled, mirrored, unreal.

You hear the water before you see any of it. The sound is not a single note but a chorus of smallnesses added up—a faint, continuous ssss of film over rock surfaces, the occasional tiny click of a bead of water striking stone below. The air is full of a faint mist, not enough to blur the skyline but enough to make the light skewer everything in haloes and faint rainbows where the cliff faces twist into shadow angles.

The plateau is not quiet. The world is screaming.

There is no plant here. No leaves, no moss, no fungi clinging in the cracks of stone. No half-dead tree hanging on the edge of a cliff. No tundra of low shrubs. No lichen in brittle patches of gray and orange, nothing to soften the hard edges or the sensory violence of all this rock and water and light. It is as if this world decided to stop at one level of complexity below flora—a mineral landscape, pure and unsoftened, where the only softness is the thin film of water that gives the stone a false suggestion of gentleness.

The terrain of Keth-Var is not violent in the way of a shifting faultline or a collapsing cliffside. This place has found its equilibrium and clung to it for eons longer than any mountain range below. It is “established” in the way bone is established: finished forming long ago, set now in its final shape. The stone is deeply weathered, but the weathering has not broken down into soft soil or scree slopes; instead it has carved into itself, like a sculptor carving more detail rather than less into the same block over time.

The plateau feels very nearly packed. There is no continuous open floor between you and the skyline. Instead, the rock rises up in pillars, fins, and narrow walls so that you cannot walk more than a few steps in any direction without something solid occupying your path. The “open” quality of the region exists only conceptually: when you look from the vantage point of one high wall, you can see that the plateau continues beyond more than you can walk into in any reasonable time. But that space is not empty space; it is filled with more rock.

The walls of Keth-Var are not chaotic; they create a vague lattice, a structure of corridors and chambers and narrow courtyards. None of this is built. No mortar, no toolmarks, no laid blocks, no lintels or raised thresholds. No ruins of some prior civilization. No obelisk stuck into the stone floor. No abandoned machinery of a long-dead race. Nothing of technology at any level—no wires, no faint hum of power, no subtle glow of runes. Nothing of biology either, except the faintest of options: suggestions of patterns in the water film that might be moving, might be more than mere fluid flow, but nothing that declares itself as life.

You are not alone in the world, but almost. The fauna of this plateau is not just absent—not an empty hollow where animals should be—but rather reduced to a rarity that feels intentional, as if the landscape itself could only tolerate a few small exceptions to its stone rule. If you stand still long enough among the echoing corridors, you might notice something thin and translucent moving across a wet stone wall: not quite insect, not quite lizard, not quite anything from a familiar biology book. It is more like a tendril of solidified water given an awkward locomotion of ripples and faint contractions of shape. It has no eyes to look at you, and if you look away it might be that when you look back, it has vanished or never been there at all.

Sometimes there is a sound that is not the water—a faint chime of something like glass colliding with itself, drifting from somewhere else through the stone lattice. You cannot find the source. No bird-like creature launches from the cliffs to the sky. No rodent scurries among the cracks of the floor. There is only condensation, circulation, and this faint, ghostly suggestion of motion that might or might not be counted as “fauna.”

The temperature is not harsh, but the constant thinness of the air and the faint moisture of the world give you a sense of being just slightly outside any comfortable range of climates. The air feels cool without ever becoming cold; your breath never steams, but your skin might feel perennially damp. No breeze stirs the world into any obvious change; no gusts set up any swaying or bending of invisible things. There are no seasons to speak of, except perhaps faint variations in the intensity of the light that sweep over the latent geometry of the plateau, but these are slow enough to be indistinguishable from any day you stand and witness.

The most unusual aspect of Keth-Var, beyond the absence of plants and the presence of so much water without any obvious source, is the way sound and sight refuse to separate into distinct objects. The stone is loud not because it is noisy itself but because everything in the place vibrates with the sound of water over unyielding surface. You cannot escape this white noise of the world; the way it never changes or fades makes it spiral into a kind of silence at the edge of hearing. The world itself pulses faintly with the gentle rise and fall of the film’s volume over textured surfaces, but never breaks into a new rhythm or ceasing. No storms arrive to cry out and then retreat. No geysers erupt to shatter the calm. No thud of rockfall punctuates the millennia; this world has achieved its final form and has no intention to submit further to change.

The overall sense, as you stand in a narrow gap between two walls of polished basalt that rise into a sky so white-blue it hurts the eyes, is that of being in a cathedral designed by no one, for nothing. No congregation has ever gathered here except the condensation and the light. No altar has ever been built except where the stone floor dips into a shallow basin and the wet film spirals into a faint pattern that might be the signature of some long-departed creator, or only of time itself.

There is no outward sign of time. No growth rings, no erosion layers exposed to count in neat strata of years, no crumbling of mortar into new dust. No rustling leaves to mark autumn and absence. No snow to cover and then retreat. The light is bright enough to show you everything clearly and yet will reveal no new thing tomorrow that you did not see today.

This is not a world of change. It is a world of long-standing presence, saturated in its own form of being. There is no distance between things: no space between wall and wall, no gap between stone and water film, no separation between the sound and the silence that is not just silence at the bottom of hearing. All is packed like the cells of some crystalline structure, except here the structure is not a lattice of atoms but one of rock and light and time.

As you move through the narrow corridors of Keth-Var, you become the only variable that the place has any interest in acknowledging at all. Your footprints, if the ground were any softer, would be erased almost instantly by the endless film of water. No monuments of your own making will last longer than the next time the water decides to trace its pattern over the stone floor.

And yet the pattern never changes to erase anything. The pattern is itself unchanged from day to day, year to year, epoch to epoch. This landscape has found its balance in the persistent, endless quiet roar of water over stone, high up where the air is thin, the light is harsh, and the world of living things has been reduced to a faint, translucent anomaly whose existence you may never quite confirm to your own satisfaction.
