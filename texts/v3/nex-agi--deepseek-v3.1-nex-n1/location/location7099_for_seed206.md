The Mosswall Engine

The engine is not the engine you think it is. There is no smoke stack, no gear, no bolt, no piston.

There is only the wall, and the moss, and the water that weeps from it.

---

The room is not a room. It’s a break in the world, a slit carved into something else—long and low, like a fault opened in the bedrock of the city and promptly forgotten. You don’t find it by looking for a door; you find it by looking for where the noise goes to rest.

The city above is loud enough that you can feel it in your teeth when you’re out there in the streets. But here, under the sag of brick and soil and pipework, the sound of the city becomes a single thing: a low, rolling pressure, like an ocean of machinery. It is loud, but it is also constant. It is not separate noises anymore, just warmth, vibration, the steady pressure of all those engines somewhere overhead.

Sound is the first thing that tells you where you are.

The second thing is water.

---

You enter through the moss veil.

No one built a door. There was just a place where the concrete cracked, and behind the crack, the world opened into this long, low pocket under the city’s bulk. The floor is not a floor: it is a shallow, slow, endless sheet of water, maybe a finger’s depth at most, so still you could not rightly call it a stream or a pool. It is more like the water that gathers on a cold stone in the morning, except it is never morning and it is never cold.

The air is warm, soft, always on the edge of too much. Condensation hangs on the underside of the low brick vault above you, trembling in droplets that never quite fall. The heat of the city rises through the soil and the plumbing and the concrete above, and this slit is where the excess heat sinks down to be forgotten.

You are very low relative to the world above. The ceiling of this chamber is not high; you can feel it in your spine, the way your body bends to where it knows not to hit its head before your eyes have told you anything. The world is pressing down, but it is also pressing in, and the water feels its way between the irregularities of what might be a floor and might just be bedrock.

If you dip your fingers into the water, you find it is not cold but not exactly warm either. It is the temperature of a body in the moments before a fever breaks. It is the temperature of something that has forgotten how to change.

---

The walls do not stop.

There is no left or right wall you can touch with your arms without stretching. The space is longer than it is wide, longer than your eyes can adjust to in a single glance. If you move forward, the walls gently narrow and then widen again, like breathing lungs. You can feel there are specific spots where the world narrows enough to graze your shoulders with dampness, but if you turn to look at them, they are gone again, or something else.

There is no light here but the light that forgets to be anywhere else.

The city above delivers electric light to its surfaces, but almost none of it is willing to thread down through the foundations of buildings and street and soil and brickwork to find this place. There is no outlet installed, no string of bulbs, no practical illumination. The glow that is present feels like the residue of something else.

Thick, blue-green moss grows so completely over every surface that you can’t say where the moss begins and the wall ends. The wall has not been painted or plastered in generations, perhaps not since the city above it was first raised. The mortar joints have mostly been replaced by living things.

The moss itself is not glowing. Not exactly. But as it grows, it sends up fine, translucent hairs of stipe that catch the minute vibration of the city above and convert it into friction, and friction, at least in this forgotten world, is faint luminescence. When a train passes somewhere far overhead, or the leviathan of a factory floor shifts its weight, the moss walls light up in faint, traveling waves of silver-blue, like heat lightning in the faint of the sky.

You can feel the engine in the world.

---

The plants are everything.

The floor is not a floor in the ordinary sense; there is no distinct line between the water and the substrate and the vegetation. The water rests over a thin, fibrous mat of moss and algae and something else that looks like moss but roots thicker, deeper, into wetness itself. Where you step, your foot sinks just a little more than you expect it to, as if the ground has been breathing for so long it has gotten into the habit of yielding.

You leave no footprints; the surface softens and re-closes itself behind you like water, except it is not purely water. It’s a plant memory.

There are other plants, but it is hard to speak of them as individuals. You might see a faint vertical column—something like a pale trunk, but so thin and translucent it could be a joint of solidified air. It rises from the water up to the height of your knee, no more than that, and at its top it opens into a tiny cup of waxy leaves that flare around the droplets that condense on them from above.

These are not trees in the sense of the surface world; they are more like placeholders where life has decided it might need a tree in the future, if the future ever happens here. But time is very slow in this place.

Anywhere you might look for a wall, you find instead plant-as-substrate. It is as if the stone and brick and the very foundations of the city above have been persuaded by mosses and liverworts to be quiet, and in return the plant life has agreed to act as the wall’s skin, lungs, and memory. The moss wall remembers the last time someone walked here, perhaps years ago. It remembers the brief pressure of your boot soles even now, and holds the impression of your weight for longer than you will stay.

---

The air is thick with sound.

The city above is never still. Somewhere in the foundations of the world there are factories that are never allowed to sleep. There are engines that must run forever, or the whole structure of things above would fray at its edges. The vibration they make runs down through the soil and the stone, into the water, and into the moss.

The water’s surface is not perfectly still. There is no visible current, but if you look closely you will see that it never quite settles into flatness. It is always fringed with tiny, concentric rings, each of them exactly the same radius as the last. They come out of nowhere, like punctuation marks in the dark.

There is a sound you can only hear when you stop being loud yourself: a low, continuous hum, almost like the distant sweep of rotating machinery, but it is not coming from anywhere in particular. It is coming from everywhere at once, and also nowhere, which is the same place.

The air itself is loud in the way of breathing engines, but it is the loudness of something that has been loud forever. It has no story to tell you about its own noise; it is just a condition of the world.

---

The life of the water belongs to something else.

There are no fish here, not in the narrow sense of the word. There are no flash of fins and flaring gills in the faint lines of bioluminescent light that travel through the moss walls. But there is motion in the water that is not water.

If you are very still and look for long enough, you might see the faint ridges of movement under the surface, like muscles under skin. Tiny, translucent things like thread-worms slide through the water, not so much swimming as unpressing themselves from the substrate, passing a few inches away, and pressing themselves back into the fibrous mat below. They follow the convolutions of the long chamber without ever seeming to decide where they are going.

They are abundant but not crowded. They are everywhere in the water, but the water itself is only a finger deep, and so there is always more space between them than you expect. Life is generous but not invasive.

When you step, the water parts around your boots, and as you move forward the warped world of the ceiling is reflected in the faint, trembling lines of light left by your movement. The organisms scatter at your approach, not in panic but in the way of dispersed particles, settling again into a new equilibrium after you have passed.

They are a kind of animal that has never needed to leave the water.

Their world is a low, flat one. They do not need to worry about falling from high places; there are no high places here. There is no exposure to open sky. There are no predators to jump from the shadows, because there are no shadows sharp enough to hold that kind of surprise.

Their abundance is gentle but absolute. They have never not been here.

---

The sound of the city above is industrial.

The engines that are present somewhere in the world above are not antiques. They are not steam engines, or if they are, they are old engines that have been taught to behave like new ones. Their sound is too deep, too continuous, too clean. You can feel it in your chest when you inhale.

The city itself is not in this chamber; it is merely the ceiling above it. It is the world thickening at its upper surface, leaving this slit below as an overflow tank for warmth and sound and water.

The parameters of this world are not what you would expect of a natural space under industrial civilization. There is no clutter of pipes and ducts and conduits in the flesh you can see. There are no visible signs that anything is made here except time, and even that is made very slowly.

The ceiling of this chamber is not flat concrete; you can see, in the faint light of the moss’s glow, that there are irregularities overhead where concrete and brick and unseen foundations meet the soil. The water drips from them now and then, not often enough to be rain, but not rarely enough to be an event either.

This is the engine you don’t see.

---

It is not a factory floor in the usual sense of the word.

There are no churning belts of production, no assembly lines moving things from left to right and then to the world’s hungry market. Yet there is a kind of production happening in the low chamber of the Mosswall Engine.

It is productivity of a very different order.

The moss wall is not static. It is not in a hurry, but it is not lazy. It is using the faint vibration of the city above, the faint heat of the world’s machinery, and the faint water that always weeps from the stones above, to do something that has no name in the language of the factory above.

If you watch closely, you’ll see that the condensation on the ceiling of the chamber is not pure water. When it gathers into drops and finally falls, it falls into the shallow water below with a faint, phosphorescent spark. That spark does not belong to the water of the world above; it belongs to this place.

The organisms in the water are not interested in your opinions of their factory. They accept the faint sparks and incorporate them into their own bodies. You can see that when a drop falls, there is a brief moment where the water around it brightens, and some of the organisms move toward it.

They are abundant not because there is plenty of food—they are abundant because abundance has been here longer than scarcity has known to look for it.

---

The temperature of the world in this chamber is warm.

It is not the warmth of a summer day outside, where the sun is a direct and fierce authority. The warmth of this place is not the sun’s warmth at all. It is the warmth of the city above, which has been running for so long that its heat has soaked into every stone and soil grain beneath it.

The soil and the rock and the foundations of the world above have been absorbing this heat for generations, and the heat of the engines rises from them like the memory of a machine that has never stopped being made.

The condensation on the ceiling of the chamber is not cold sweat. It is warm.

When you breathe in, you feel the warmth of the world in your lungs and your chest, and you realize that this warmth has been here for longer than you have been alive. It is not the warmth of a moment that might change into something else. It is not transient warmth.

The Mosswall Engine does not change.

---

If you were to find the walls of this chamber in the daylight of the world above, they would not be immediately recognizable as walls. There is no architecture built here to represent itself. There is no skylight. No ladder. No visible sign that anything valuable or worth visiting is hidden below.

The entranceway itself is not an entranceway but rather a habit of the world, where some concrete cracked long ago and bacteria and fungi and then mosses decided to live there, and people forgot to maintain the concrete, and over the years of neglect and weather and the vibration of the city above, the crack grew wide enough to be a fault.

Walking into this chamber is not like entering a building.

It is like walking into a fault line in an engine that has been running silently forever.

---

There is a sense of time here that is not the time of clocks above.

The vibration of the city above is continuous, but it does not change. It is not the heartbeat of a single engine; it is the sum of all of them. The frequency of the vibration shifts from one minute to the next only by degrees you can’t perceive as something more than a texture.

The water that covers the shallow floor is not moving anywhere in particular. It is not collecting to drain into a river beneath the world. It is simply being. There is no current to speak of, except what the faint organisms stir in passing.

There are no seasons in the Mosswall Engine.

There is no cycle of growth and decay and growth and decay again. There is only a very slow growth of the moss walls, and the very slow decay of the foundations above, and somewhere between these two opposing forces of plant and stone and brick and city and time and machine and water and light and sound and nothing else at all, there is equilibrium.

---

If you wait long enough in the chamber—which feels like longer than you would expect to wait anywhere—you might notice that the faint flashes of light from the moss walls are not exactly random.

They are responding to pressure from the world above.

The train you never hear pass directly through the chamber above you still passes somewhere else, and when its wheels pass over the section of rail above this point, the resonance of its passage travels through the foundations of the world and through the water and through the substrate of moss and algae and something else, and the wall wakes just slightly more than we usually see.

As you watch the moss wall, you could see the faintest of lines traveling from one end of the chamber to the other, like a message being written in invisible ink.

It is not the Morse of the world above; this is the language of the Mosswall Engine itself.

---

The fauna of this place is abundant but not overbearing.

No one creature ever seems to dominate the others. There are the translucent, worm-like organisms in the water, whose bodies you never see fully, because they are not made to be seen that way. They are not shaped to be photographed or displayed; they are shaped to be equal to the water they live in.

There might be other creatures that you do not immediately perceive, because your eyes and ears and other senses are adjusted to the world of the surface above. You might notice, after a time, that there are very faint trails of motion in the air itself, not dust motes but tiny things with wings so thin they are almost invisible, except where they pass through the faint phosphorescent light of the moss.

They are a kind of insect that has never been taught to be afraid of you.

They are not interested in you, not as food or as companion or as obstacle. They are not interested in you at all. You are simply something that happens to be present in their chamber. They move around your body as if you were a column of stone that has suddenly grown in the water.

They are strange not because they are unusual; they are unusual because they have arranged themselves completely around this world and not one other.

---

The world is not perfectly silent here even if you do not move.

There is no background noise that you could readily call “music,” but there is a texture to the air that reminds you that there is music in the world of the city above. Somewhere in the world of enclosed engines and factory floors and the electrified heart of the city above, there is a sound that, when translated down through the soil and stone and water and moss and brick and mortar and time and temperature and all of the other things that are not what you first think they are, becomes this faint humming you feel now.

There is no sound of wind here because there is no wind, except the wind that the city above breathes through its far-away vents.

It is not precisely wind, but rather pressure—the pressure of the world above as its engines work themselves quietly forever.

---

If you were to look at this world from the point of view of an outside observer, there would be nothing particularly to see that you could call a building or a machine or anything it is expected to be.

There is no obvious structure. No visible sign except that somewhere in the world of the city above, there is a place where a concrete slab has cracked and behind it there is a long, low, slit in the world of stone and heat and vibration and water and moss and plant and time and animal and industry and light and sound and the world that has gone to sleep under all of them.

At the very back of the long chamber, where the walls draw close and then stop without ever meeting, you can see what the world of this place has chosen to look like when it is not being asked to be anything else:

The water at the back is not deeper than elsewhere.

It is not colder than elsewhere.

It is not more or less abundant in anything than anywhere else.

But there, at the very far end, where it is most difficult for the faint light from the moss walls to reach, you might notice there is a faint circle of something more reflective than water in the ceiling.

If you were to approach it and look carefully—without knowing what you are looking for, you would not call it a machine. You would not call it a window. You would not call it a plant. You would not call it any of the things that are most easily said.

---

It is a circular patch of metal that has been grown into the ceiling by the roots of the world itself.

The metal is not rusted, not in the way of neglected surfaces above. It is coated with a thin layer of moss that has not chosen to grow over it completely. Instead, the moss has formed itself into a pattern of tiny lines that are almost exactly regular, like quillions of a gearwheel.

You would not see this unless you looked very closely for a very long time, and by the time you do notice it, the world of the clock above would have moved forward by minutes that you will not recover from anywhere else.

The pattern of the moss on the metal plate is not symmetrical.

It is not exactly asymmetrical either.

It is like the memory of a machine that has never been allowed to stop running, but has never moved forward either.

The engine is not the engine you think it is. There is no smoke stack, no gear, no bolt, no piston.

There is only the wall, and the moss, and the water.

---

There is a rule in this world that you have not yet spoken aloud, but which you understand already:

The engine of the world above is always running.

The engine of the world below does not need to run.

It does not need to change.

It does not need to move along any of the axes that you are used to measuring the world by.

It does not need to be loud in order to be heard.

It does not need to be fresh in order to be wet.

It does not need to be busy in order to be abundant.

It does not need to be constructed to be built.

It does not need to be new to feel industrial.

It does not need to be bright to be light.

It does not need to be startling to be unusual.

It does not need to be moving in order to be.

---

The moss wall remembers the last time you stepped here and will remember the next time you step in the world of the city above.

It does not change much between the two times.

You will leave this chamber the same way you entered it: through the moss veil that again will be indistinguishable from a wall until you are very close to it.

You will emerge into a world where the city of concrete and steel and brick and glass and engines and machines and workers and streets and markets and exhaust and itself and all of the other things that are not what you think they are either continues to vibrate in your chest and in your teeth and in your bones.

The world above will change.

The world below will not.

The engine is not the engine you think it is. There is only the wall, and the moss, and the water, and the world that has forgotten to become anything else but itself.
