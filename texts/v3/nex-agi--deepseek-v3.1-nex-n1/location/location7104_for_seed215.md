You are standing on a high wetland shelf beneath the spine of a new world.

### Horizon and Sky

The sky is a clean, cold silver, almost white, but not from cloud or human debris—there is no cloud here at all. Instead, the sky itself seems to hold a very thin, luminous fog of its own, as if the air at this altitude has been polished to a faint glow. The sun is distant, pearly-white, and low even when it is technically noon; it glides in a shallow arc just above low, broken peaks of raw rock that are still being born from the planet’s crust.

Light is normal, but everywhere it feels slightly diluted. The ground is pale under it: not quite snow, not quite stone.

To the north, the world drops abruptly in a single, impossible line of dark cliff, where the continent’s edge is still being cut from nothingness. Southwards, the shelf tilts slightly down, into an immense, shallow depression of standing water veined with low earth-bridges. This is the heart of the wet: an endless, high marshland spreading farther than weather can remember.

### Weather and Time

The world is still making itself here.

Time is not a long chronicle of events but a short, sharp breath of becoming. The mountains are raw: not ancient eroded stumps but fin-like ridges of naked stone thrusting up from the plains. The rock is blue-gray, almost unweathered, with few soil layers or plant colonies to hide its cuts. Snow settles in pale seams into their cracks and then slinks downward again as ice veils.

The air is just cold enough to be uncomfortable, but not biting. The temperature is at that plateau where cold is not a violent presence but a constant texture—like thin glass pressed to your skin. Your breath ghosts faintly in the air: not thick clouds, but hesitant silver ribbons.

Weather in this place is slow to begin and quick to end. 

When storms come, they arrive from the west—where the world’s great edge crumples towards the sky—as thin black curtains that are not quite rain, not quite snow. They pass over the high marshlands in a few hours, leaving behind a slick sheen on rock and a shallow glitter of water on the ground. Then the sky clears again to its silver. The ground stays damp for days after, because at this altitude and in this built, hollowed ground, the water does not drain easily; it stays in basements and the lower levels of the city as if afraid to leave.

### The Ground: Under Construction and Porous

The landscape itself is under construction.

Beneath the thin crust of soil and paving and city, the planet’s crust is still forming. This part of the world was not peeled from some older continent; it is being made in place. The rocks are ridged with fresh fault lines, and the ground on the lower slopes of the mountains juts with sharp teeth of stone half-hidden under films of moss and stitched root.

This is not a loose, eroded land of collapsed hills and heaped sediment. It is a land of structure: still stiff from its birth. The rock is smoother than one might expect from freshly uplifted terrain, because the atmosphere itself is slightly abrasive—water vapor condensing into minute crystals of ice that swipe across stone surfaces and then sublimate away without leaving much debris.

Belowground, the geology is memorized rather than shaped by brute force: the planetary substrate is designed to register stress and impact as a kind of architectural data. The stone under the city secretly knows where it will be weakest in five thousand years’ time and lays itself a little thicker there.

### Water: Everywhere and Nowhere

The place is high and wet at the same time, which is a rare combination in old worlds. In this one, it is deliberate.

The atmosphere is thick with humidity but cold, so moisture often condenses directly onto surfaces as a gleaming skin. You see it on the railings of bridges and walkways—a fine film of water that never quite thickens into droplets or freezes. Instead, it flows like slow mercury into the channels of woven stonework floors and vanishes from sight.
