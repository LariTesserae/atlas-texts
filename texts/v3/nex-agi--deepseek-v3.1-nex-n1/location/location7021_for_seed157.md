Below the canopy the world is not black, but almost black. A fine silt of shadow fills every space, leaving only the faintest glows where water and stone and something like lichen make slow exchanges of light.

The air is cool, always cool—cool at the throat when you breathe, cool against your lips, cool enough that you can feel the heat of your own skin where you touch your arms. The cave itself hangs like a hollow tooth in the side of the mountain, a dry pocket of the high forest where water would rather weep than fall: runoff trickles down walls in a thousand thin, invisible routes, finds ledges and mosses and then the stone floor, where it gathers in a near-still pool at the back of the chamber. The air is not mist, but dampness felt more than seen—a condensation that leaves the cave walls perpetually faint with luminescences.

This is a small place, cathedral only in the sense of proportion: a high, uneven ceiling of ancient stone, perhaps five or six times a man’s height, and the floor no longer than a longhouse, but the room is not empty. It is full. The floor is uneven, not with boulders but with a dense terrain of forms. The thick pool takes the inward half of the chamber, its surface oil-smooth and only occasionally broken by the slow drop of a bead from the ceiling. Around the edges of the water, the rock rises in low, hollowed terraces—steps worn by centuries of slow water or slower scraping—so that bare stone pokes out just enough to make a treacherous path around the room.

Everything is porous stone, the kind of rock that seems to be dissolving while it hardens—a matrix of tiny holes and wormlike trails where the harder parts of the stone have been left behind like a skeleton. The walls of the cave are not jagged but rounded, all the corners of the world softened down, as if the mountain itself is exhaled slowly over time. The erosion here is not dramatic; there are no deep cracks, no brittle collapse. Instead there is a long, exhausted equilibrium: the mountain has been shaped like this for long enough that every surface has found a way to be comfortable with water and weight and time.

Vegetation persists in the absence of the sun. The lower walls of the cave wear a dense skin of something that is more moss than algae, except that it never entirely greens. Instead it grows in low, felted cushions, a dark grey-green that is almost indistinguishable from the stone until the light catches it faintly from within. The mosses layer themselves like overlapping scales, each colony a circular pattern creeping outward until its margin meets the margin of another, and then there is only pressure. There is no soil between them; they root into the stone itself, into the minute cracks of the erosion, and draw the water that runs down the walls in thin films.

There is no tree here, no bloom, no color. But the lushness is real. It is a lushness of density, not of leaf. The mosses crowd every available surface, from knee height down to the water’s edge, leaving the upper half of the walls bare only because the drips from above would wash them away faster than they can grow. In the lower, dampest regions of the wall, there is also a thin layer of something that looks like a black film of greening glass, a transparent skin whose biology is not immediately obvious. It feels like wet gelatin under the fingertips. If you were to stand there long enough, you would feel the minute tides of its moisture content shifting, like the lightest of breaths.

The water itself—the pool at the heart of the room—is not transparent, but thick. A faint cloud of microscopic shelled things hangs in it, invisible to the eye but present as a veil of diminished clarity: if you dipped a hand in, it would vanish sooner than you expect. The surface of the pool is not flat; it is textured with a low relief of slowly shifting forms. A dark skin of something like oil lies on the water in patches, but it is not oil; it is a living membrane full of almost invisible vesicles that respond to the slightest disturbance of humidity with a minute roll of their surface.

There is no metal here, no machine, no sign of tool. There is only the strict, narrow rule-space of moisture: the water must be still enough to hold skin, but must move enough to feed the walls; the air must be cool enough to condense water on the stone, but not so cold as to freeze the pool or the mosses; the pool must sit shallowly over stone that can handle a slow dissolution of itself over ages without fundamentally changing shape. The cave itself is a single, self-correcting system, a ball of conditions that reconciles all contradictions toward neutrality.

Life is not scarce in this neutral place. It is overwhelming.

Barely visible in the gloom, the ceiling of the cave hangs thick with something that is not exactly stone but is not entirely anything else either. A strange semi-mineral growth of angular forms, like rows of miniature teeth or uneven geometric honeycomb, hangs from the highest points of the roof. Each unit is about the length of a finger, but there are so many of them, and they are so packed together, that the ceiling seems like a lower, more textured surface than the rock itself suggests. The “teeth” are pale grey or off-white, but not in the sharp way of new bone; they have a dull, weathered finish, like bone that has been left out in the weather for centuries.

Each element of this ceiling growth is hollow. A minuscule hole opens at its lower tip, so that a very fine, almost invisible thread of water emerges from it on the rare times when the film above is thick enough. The hollows house minute colonies of things that are not exactly crustaceans and not exactly insects, but something like both and neither. They are not built to see; there is nothing for them to see. Instead, they feel the slow, weightless gradients of mineral content in the water that seeps through the roof of the cave above.

This mineral layer is the place of the unusual. The living forms in the stone teeth are not static individuals but shifting patterns. A slow, collective migration of them turns through time, not in the sense of space as we know it—for there is no clear space to move through—but in the sense of configuration. Each generation of these things is not a separate being but a continuation, like the pattern shift of a mosaic when the light hits it differently.
