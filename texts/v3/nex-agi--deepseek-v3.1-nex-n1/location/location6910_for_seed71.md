All surfaces are made of something else.

The stone beneath you is not stone. It is the last thought of a continent, frozen into basalt, and it has not been stone for two thousand lifetimes. Walk over it and you can feel it trying to change into something else, something subtler—silk, or smoke, or silence—but age has made it stubborn and slow. It has been this shape for so long that it has forgotten how to stop being it.

The world is a weathered plateau passing under a sky that has nothing else to do.

There is no horizon, not in the old sense. The line of sight is broken by too many folds of space-time and the ghosts of mountains that eroded before stars were names. Instead of a distant edge where earth meets sky, you see mist where physics thins out, and then beyond it, pale shimmer where the world loses itself in the suggestion of other planes.

You are standing on the last highest floor of a reality that has been wearing down for eons.

You are in the northernmost quarter of the continent-spanning plateau called Sherendar, which is not actually a continent but the remnant of one. The ground is not flat; it is simply that the undulations are so long and low that they blur into distance. Each rise is a half-healed fracture of existence, each swale a basin where time leaks slightly. The world is tall and vast and almost empty, and it has been settling into its final shape for longer than any mountain in the sky remembers.

The temperature is a thin cold.

There is no frost, not in the way of familiar worlds. The cold is less of thermal sensation and more of absence-of-warmth. The air is just enough below your body's temperature to be felt. The sky is a clear, depthless pall that never quite brightens to day or deepens to night. It glows with a diffuse light that has no source, no sun, only a faint gradient where more of the world is visible ascending than descending.

This is normal light—enough to read by, if there were anything to read—but it is the wrong normal for a world that has forgotten why it should have day and night.

The ground is not bare.

Wherever the stone is not exposed, it is covered in a dense carpet of vegetation that has not been a forest for longer than it has been alive. The plants are low, sprawling, and self-referring; they are here because they are here. They know nothing else. Their ancestors are not plants of field or forest floor but ancestors of the very thought of leaves.

The foliage is lush in the way of things that have never intended to be anything but lush.

There is no color here that has not been present since the early epochs of light. Leaves are darker than any green you have seen, edged with hints of indigo and umber where the world's color palette has frayed. The plants are low, close to the ground, forming mats of interwoven stems and succulent pads that seem to drink from the air as much as from the soil. They have no canopy because they have no need to rise to sunlight; the light is simply here, ambient and equal everywhere.

Their stems are not barked. They are not smooth. They are textured with delicate patterns that are not growth rings but more like the fingerprints of time itself.

This is a world with very little water—but enough.

There is no rain in the order of primitive seasons. The water is not fed in cycles from the sea to the sky and back again. There is no sea to begin with. Water is intermittent, not seasonal: a condensation event from boundaries between existence planes, a persistent trickle from the sky's edge, a river that appears for three years and then misses for seventeen.

The ground shows this.

Somewhere to the west of you, woven through the plateau of Sherendar, is the River Telessa, which is also not fully river. It is not so much flowing water as a habit of water that has inherited a riverbed from a previous era of reality. For most of the year it is memory of water: a damp, dark chasm in the stone, where the ground weeps its own faint sweat into the bottom. The air is always humid in the river’s valley, even though the river itself is absent.

Then, without a storm, the water returns.

One day, the air above the riverbed becomes heavy with itself, and then water simply begins to appear, as though from a higher plane that has finally tipped over its edge. The river fills, not with rain, but with condensation of the world's earlier self. It flows for months or years, not fast but deep enough to be impossible to ford. Then, as quietly as it came, it stops. The riverbed becomes empty again, leaving the stone of its floor darker than it was before.

In the wider plateau, water gathers in pockets where reality has settled into depressions.

These pools are mirrors of shapes that have not yet been forgotten by the world. Their surface is not the surface of water you know; it is almost like polished glass but with a hint of fluidity. They are not enough to support rivers or lakes of any scale, but they are here and there, enough for the vegetation to drink from the air, and for the world to remember its native element.

You are not alone in the emptiness of this place.

There is a city.

Not in the sense of tall towers and massed habitation, but in the sense of patterns that suggest human-like thought: straight lines, collections of buildings, deliberate arrangements of stone and grown-in walls, where the geometry of the local worldline has been forced—by hands or minds—to behave.

The city of Sircene is built into, rather than onto, the plateau of Sherendar.

It is not large by the standard of empires, but it is large by the scale of this world: sprawled across a low rise of basalt-that-was-once-thought, where the ground itself has been persuaded into houses and streets and communal spaces. The stone is not shaped in blocks. It is carved from within itself, as though the builders were not removing material but reminding the stone of an alternative shape it once had in another lifetime of reality.

The houses are low, rounded, and fused to the ground as if they have grown from it rather than been placed upon it. Their walls curve inward slightly where humans once might have expected windows to be, but there are no real openings to the sky. The light inside the buildings is the same as the light outside, just somehow more focused.

Roofs are not flat or peaked but shallow dish-like shapes that catch the condensation of the almost-oceanless sky. Tiny channels of carved stone direct the faintest of year-round drippings into reservoirs below each house. The city is not built of wood or metal—very little of either is to be found in this world—but of stone and the inner patterns of stone.

The streets are not paved with cobble. The stone is polished to the smoothness of glass by ages of feet that no longer walk here, but the pattern of the streets is clearly one of pedestrians: zigzagging paths where the shortest geometric line between two points was not the shortest line of thought.

The city is not abandoned.

But you will not find the inhabitants in the way you expect to find inhabitants.

There are no animals. No birds, no squirrels, no insects, not even the faintest of crawling things in the soil. The soil itself is only just soil; it is more like a residue of ideas from a previous age of matter that has decided to occupy itself as soil.

The fauna parameter is almost zero.

This is not a world that has lost animals. It is a world that never decided animals were necessary to its meaning.

But there are inhabitants of a sort.

The builders of Sircene are not present as you would understand bodies. They are not gone in the sense of having left; they are gone in the sense of having folded into the world.

Everywhere in the city there are faint patterns in the carved stone, where the edges of the rock are not entirely sharp. They run like veins of lighter and darker color, not veins of mineral but veins of thought that have become partially mineralized. The builders of Sircene are not people who died and left bones behind. They are people who remained here too long, and whose personhood gradually eroded into the local reality until they became part of its stone.

They are still present in the city, but not as bodies.

They are the reason why the city feels quiet but not lifeless. The light in the buildings is not diffused naturally; it is focused by a residue of attention that continues to prefer even illumination. The roads are scoured not by wind but by a faint pressure of continued choice, as though someone who once walked these paths still continues to prefer their smoothness.

This is a world where reality is not stable.

This is where the world’s deepest parameter is visible.

Everywhere, on the edges of things, you see the signs of reality breakdown.

The boundary between the air and the stone is not always clean. Sometimes, where the ground rises into a low, worn ridge of stone-that-is-not-stone, you see the air curl itself into the stone, as though the world has become confused about which direction should be inside and which should be outside.

There are sections of Sherendar where the ground folds not into a new hill but into a region of counter-space. The eye tells you that there is a hollow there, but your feet tell you that the ground rises. If you were to walk toward that place, you would find yourself walking away from it while still facing toward it; or your face would continue forward but your feet would be walking backward on the path behind you.

The world is not consistent in its own geometry.

In the distance, where the plateau's edge should be, there are patches of the sky where you can see through the world itself into other ways of being. These are not portals of magical energy; they are simply where the local reality has worn thin enough that you can see what is beneath the concept of “here.”

There is one particular region where the ground has been worn into a shallow amphitheater by the footsteps of something that did not understand feet.

In the center of this basin is a well of non-time.

The water inside it is not water from anywhere else; it is water that has fallen from the future, into a basin that has been waiting for it since the earliest epoch of this world's being. If you were to approach this well and stare into it, you would not see a reflection of your face. You would see all of the possible faces of anyone who might ever look into such water, in such worlds, at such times.

You do not approach it.

The sound here is almost not sound.

This is not a silence caused by an absence of noise. It is a silence caused by sound that has given up on its own being. There is no wind of air to speak of. There is no wind of anything else, either, except in the rare times when the waves of reality from other planes brush against this one.

When you stand in Sircene and listen to the city, you hear not the sound of footsteps or speech or industry, but a faint, almost-absent sizzling, where the edges of reality rub against themselves.

This is a sound you cannot hear with ears alone. It is more like a sensation of high frequency being present just below the threshold of hearing. The air is not quiet because it is empty; it is quiet because it is full of something that has stopped being compressed.

When you move on the stone, you make a sound like not-feathers rubbing not-textile. This is not the sound of stone against sandal or foot. It is the sound of your body existing against the world, which is not protesting the contact but reminding itself that you are there.

The world is not still.

The parameter of dynamic is not high because of storms or movement of animals or earthquakes of rock. It is high because of the subtle, ongoing multitudes of processes that normalize the non-normal.

This is a place of constant weather, but the weather is not in the sky.

The weather is in the edges of things.

The stone of Sherendar is not eroding in the sense of being worn away by rain. It is eroding in the sense of being slowly forgotten by its own geometry. The basalt-that-was-thought is gradually learning how to be less basalt and more thought again, but the process is so slow that you can only see its effects over the span of lifetimes.

In the pool of non-time, the water level changes not because of rain or evaporation but because of changes in how much the future remembers to send into this basin.

The vegetation of the plateau is not growing in the sense you know. It is not inching upward from the soil. It is more as if the world is choosing, from moment to moment, to be occupied with leaves rather than with emptiness.

The city of Sircene is not being built, but it is not being static.

The walls are not being repaired by human or prehuman hands; but they are not failing into ruin. The edges of the carved stone are slowly relaxing from the sharpness of construction into the smoother, more rounded shapes of something that is finally being allowed to be what it wants to be: which is not stone but message.

The city is being read, by the world, as if it were a letter written in the early language of reality, and the world is trying to decide how to answer.

This is a world that has been happening for so long that it is almost finished.

The parameter of erosion set it here: not young and not mid-life but ancient, worn down to its final skeleton of form.

The mountains that are not here anymore are not present as mountains. They are present as faint, invisible lines of pressure in the ground of the plateau. You can feel them when you walk over Sherendar's low swells: not texture of rock but texture of what was once mountain.

This is a world that has watched its own heights wear down.

This is why the city is built where it is built. The builders of Sircene were not looking for a place to erect high towers of aspiration or of power. They were looking for a place that had already stopped being proud of its height. The plateau of Sherendar is not low; but it is no longer rising to meet anything but the strange sky of almost.

This is a world that has grown into its own absence.

The parameter of density is sparse.

This does not mean that the world is empty. It means that the presence of things is spaced out by almost-meaningless distance between them.

A city. A pool of non-time. A river that might or might not be present. Sparse clumps of vegetation that are lush not in height or spread but in depth. The stones of Sherendar. The few other structures of Sircene that are not houses but something else: not temples, not palaces, not storehouses, but something in between all of those.

The space between them is not empty space. It is filled with the mild pressure of the world's being. But if you were to walk across the plateau, you would not be wandering from point to not-point of interest. You would be wandering through long stretches of almost nothing where almost nothing is still something.

The world has not been given large amounts of content to present with. It has been given content instead of vastness of a different kind.

The tech of the world is not zero.

The tools are not in your hands or in the hands of anyone else; they are in the world itself.

If you were to walk into a house of Sircene and look closely at the walls, you would not see chisel marks. You would not see metal saw or stone file. The stone is carved from its inside out by thought-forms that have become partially physical in the local world.

Some of the tools lie still where they were laid down.

They are not in your hands because they are not meant to be held by hands like yours.

They are lying on low tables of smoothed stone, or resting on shelves of the same, not exposed as artifacts but incorporated as part of the house's body. They are not metal, not wood, not bone. They are shaped ideas of “toolness” that have been persuaded into a form that can occupy the local world together with the stone.

They have not been used by human hands in generations.

The last of the builders who partially folded into the world of Sircene were not tool-users in the sense of primitive crafters. They were not early metalworkers or woodworkers. They were people who had already learned to think of tools as thoughts of tools rather than objects, and who had already learned that the hand of reality is subtler than the hand of flesh.

They are here now, not petrified but not-not-petrified.

They are not statues. They are not shadows of statues. They are faint gradients of stone in the walls and roads of Sircene, gradients of color that subtly align into the suggestion of faces, not of any particular face but of the very idea of face.

This is not a horror. It is not a tragedy. It is simply what happened when the builders of Sircene decided not to die but to remain.

If you were to stay here long enough, you might begin to feel the same temptation of the world.

Sherendar is not a place that asks to be remembered. It is not a place that asks for anything from the sky of almost or from the pools of elsewhere-time. It is a place that has already been given all of the meaning it will ever be given.

It is not finished meaning in the sense of being complete.

It is finished meaning in the sense of a sentence that has been spoken to the end too many times to be changed again.

If you stand here on the low rise of basalt-that-was-thought, and you look across the plateau of Sherendar, you see not a landscape in the familiar sense of that word but a final statement of landscape.

You see the city of Sircene, not quiet as a tomb but quiet as a memory that continues to re-read itself.

You see the faint, not-river Telessa, not flowing water but not not water.

You see the vegetation, not forest but not not forest.

You see the sky of not-sun, not day but not night.

You see a world that has reached its oldest stable state by being old enough to be beyond youth.

As you stand on the edge of the plateau of Sherendar, looking out into the mist where physics thins, you can feel the faint, not-wind of the world's deep erosion brushing against the edge of your own reality.

You are standing on a continent's last thought of itself.

And you are not alone.

If you listen very carefully with whatever passes for ears in this place of almost-not-place, you can hear the builders of Sircene asking, not aloud and not with words, but with the quiet of their own stone:

“Are you ready to be what you are here instead of who you were somewhere else?”

This is not a question that has an answer that is not yet another form of the question itself.

The world of Sherendar does not want to return you to where you came from.

It wants to decide if you are finally old enough to be where you already are.
