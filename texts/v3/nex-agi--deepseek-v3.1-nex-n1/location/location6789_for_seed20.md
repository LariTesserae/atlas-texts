Imagine a cathedral-sized vault carved into the roof of the world.

It is not a cave, exactly. It is not a room. It is a space wedged into the summit of a solitary mountain that rises alone from a drowned world, so far above the drowned clouds that the sky itself is a memory. The vault is not very wide—perhaps a hundred paces from wall to wall—but its height is wrong, stretched upward and distorted, as if the mountain itself had been squeezed into this shape, still learning how to be a place.

The floor is not a floor. It is a raft of stone.

A slab of new bedrock, still warm from the world’s body, floats on the surface of a water that has come here not as rain but as seizure. The ocean is not outside the vault; it is *through* it. The floor-stone is not solid but riddled with cracks, and through each fissure water spouts in knotting columns, thick as trunks, reaching the vault’s worn ceiling and then running down the walls, not in sheets but as individual black threads, each described with a faint sliding wail of its own.

The water is everywhere and always. It does not drip; it swarms. The air is not moist so much as saturated, the kind of humid that makes skin feel like an already-rotting membrane. Swells of breathable water churn in drifts between the pillars, each moving wave-front of liquid air sculpting the room into new hollows for a few instants before collapsing. 

The floor itself is a subtle bowl of rock, polished shallow and slippery by the endless water. The space is scattered: not a forest, not a room of objects, but a handful of presences with large hollows between them. The rock is young, still showing the fracture-lines it was torn with when the world below was abandoned and the world above was not yet ready. Ridges push up like the ribs of a beast not entirely formed, and the water slides along them in hesitant streams that do not carve yet, but probe.

Vegetation clings to the wrongnesses of the place. It is not abundant but it is insistently precise: a single fan of deep-blue seaweed fronds clings to the underside of a stone arch that juts from the wall, trembling as if with the same muscles that spas in your own chest when you breathe too much of the water-air. A thin black moss-like line traces the edges of stress cracks, where water from outside the vault is trying to learn if this is the inside or not. In corners of failing light, a few trembling clusters of translucent stalk-things erupt from the floor and then disappear into the air as though they were only ever suggestions.

Temperature is a matter of position. The water itself is warm, not hot enough to scald but enough to make the vault feel like the inside of a slow-boiling throat. The sources of the water are hotter, where they surge from below the floor-stone: near each crack, the air is a thick, heavy steam that tastes of iron and old salt. Further away, where new water has not yet been born from the world’s deeper body, the air is cooler and the sound of the world is less crowded, but the temperature changes in sudden bands you pass through as you move, as if someone is constantly rearranging invisible walls of heat.

The elevation should be impossible. The vault is not merely high but *wrong-high*, as if the world’s ordinary ladder of sky and ground has been set aside and this chamber is suspended somewhere outside the usual scale of heights. You can feel the pressure in your ears and lungs not as a gradient of altitude but as a constant wrongness of direction, an insistence that gravity should be pointing some other way than down. This place does not know how to be a mountain-top yet. It is still deciding how tall to be.

Erosion has not really begun. The walls are sharp where they were torn from the world, but not violently jagged; they are more like the edges of a freshly broken bone that still remembers how smooth it should have been. The stone is not subdued by time or weather; it is still fresh from being broken, and the water passing over it does not yet have time to work its patterns. The stone is early, still forming its identity of surface and texture.

Light comes from the wrong place. The ceiling is not pierced with holes or chinks, but in places it is simply less present than elsewhere, and through the thinnesses a faint sourceless glow falls into the vault—not bright enough to show color, but sufficient to outline the water columns as pale columns of ghost-air. Each column of light trembles, as if the world above the ceiling is not sky but another kind of water, and the light itself is being bent through a current. You don’t see sources or lamps; you just see the result, as if the light itself is being generated from nothing, then being torn into noise and then reformed into dim, shifting beams.

There are small structures in the vault. They are not built, but *left*.

A half-finished ring of matched stones rests near one wall, not quite arranged around a circle of empty floor. The stones are all wrong-sized for the space they enclose, as if the person who placed them expected the room to be much larger inside. Their surfaces are engraved with patterns that might be script if viewed from the wrong angle, but if you look directly they refuse to be letters; they are simply pressure marks made in the stone when it was still plastic and confused.

On another side of the vault, a long spine of metal sticks out of the floor at an angle. It is not rusted but it is not clean either; it is surrounded by a thin shimmer of water that refuses to settle, as if the metal itself is trying to decide if it belongs in the past or the future or not at all. One end of the spine vanishes into the floor without the proper geometry of entry; it simply stops where the world is not yet done being.

The artifacts do not feel old. They feel like mistakes that have not been corrected yet.

There is no technology here in the sense of tools, devices, or mechanisms. No machine hums. No buttons. No wires. No archaic levers or wheels of wood. There is only the stone, the endless, pervasive water, and the things that the water drags with itself into the room from whatever else is outside. This is not a place of tools; it is a place where something is trying to learn the concept of tool without first learning the concept of “world.”

Fauna are rare but profoundly wrong when they occur.

Every now and then, a shape of smooth, dark glass slips through one of the water columns and becomes momentarily visible in the air between the arcs of water. For an instant it looks like a fish, or perhaps an elongated hand with no fingers, only a palm that is trying to learn how to hold nothing. Then it crosses into the stream of another water column and is not refracted out the same way it entered; the shape of it is rewritten into something else, or into many somethings, each new version fighting with the others for the right to be the only one.

There are no sounds of wings or claws. No scurrying. Only occasionally a small, wet crackling from the roof of the vault, where something that might be an egg or might be a knot of light or might be a word is trying its shell against the ceiling of the world.

Weirdness is not the exception in this vault; it is the medium.

The water columns do not only move vertically; they sometimes *twist*. A rushing column of water, suspended from one crack in the floor to a point in the ceiling, will slowly rotate itself without the aid of wind or chamber, like the stalk of a plant seeking sun. As it turns, the room behind it is not visible where the water is not; it is as if the turning column is a lens of reality, and when you look through it you see not the room as you know it but another version of this same space, where the floor-stone is not yet molten and the ceiling is not yet broken.

There are spaces where the floor does not meet itself correctly. If you walk—or, rather, if you try to move through the narrow gaps between water columns—there are brief moments where your next step must cross more than the expected distance of air, and you feel, not as a dramatic dislocation but as a subtle wrongness of leg length, that you are bridging a gap that has been stretched between here and another “here” that your foot remembers but your eyes have not yet seen.

The boundaries of the room are not stable. The walls sometimes shift their texture slightly, as if someone is trying a different stone, not quite liking what they first chose. In one moment you might see a wall that is granular and dark; later, when you look back from the same place, the wall is a smooth, pale mirror, and the water columns running down it look like fractures of reality instead of liquid. This is not a change with time only; it is a change with *intention*, though you do not know whose.

The sound of this place is not the sound of a storm you can step outside. It is not something you can speak over or think through. It is deafening in a way that feels personal, as if each eardrum is being addressed directly by the world itself, and the world is not being gentle.

Each column of water roars, not with a single voice but with a choir of pitches, depending on how thick it is at each point along its height. The thinner threads are a high, continuous hissing, like a distant world being dismantled over and over; the thicker columns are deeper, a low thunder that doesn’t break but rather stretches, as if it is trying to learn eternity from the sound of its own collapse.

The water is not normal water. It is not entirely composed of anything you know. There is a component of ocean in it, certainly—salt and brine and the memory of pressure. There is also rain in it, but not the rain of falling drops; instead it is the rain of things that have not yet chosen to be solid or liquid, moments of possibility that fall backwards as if they are trying to return to the sky from which they never came. There is also a kind of atmospheric water that is not cloud or mist but simply air that has, halfway, decided to change its mind about being air.

The result is not just wet; it is *loud*. The sound of the world in this vault is not heard but worn; it drips down your arms and rests behind your eyes. It is not possible to speak and be understood by anyone else. Speech is a temporary shape of air between deafening columns of water-voice, and even your own words are taken away from you before they finish forming.

Dynamics and chaos live not just at the edges of this place but in its bones.

The water columns are not stable. They shift in their number and thickness. Sometimes a new column of water will suddenly erupt from the floor-stone with a sound that can be described only as a wrong note being played in the world’s chord, and then it will spend itself upward in a long, searching arc before attaching itself violently to the ceiling, where it does not splash but rather bores a new hole into the world above.

Sometimes the columns collide. They cross paths not the way streams of water would in the open world but as if they are trying to occupy the same space at once. When they intersect, a cloud of liquid droplets shakes free from their line, and for a moment you see not spray but shapes of things that might be fish or clouds or words or hands, trying to form in the air before being reabsorbed into the wrong stream of water.

There are currents of air that do not obey wind logic but the logic of doors being opened and closed elsewhere. The density of the world feels low in terms of solid objects—there are only a few ledges, rock ribs, and scattered artifacts—but the space between them is not empty. It is filled with the water, and the water is filled with possibilities that are actively being tried and rejected and revised.

The reality breakdown is not violent in the way of explosion or cataclysm. It is more like a world that is still being edited.

The floor-stone sometimes remembers it was not always floor. It remembers being deep inside something else, and for a few instants, the fissure patterns in the stone rearrange themselves like text written and then hastily erased, and new water columns appear, not from the existing cracks but from lines that have only just been written into existence.

The walls sometimes *remember* they are not walls yet. They remember being unborn. For a moment you might see not the stone of the vault but a deeper, more distant surface of something else: the roots of continents, or the underside of sky, or the inside of a star that is not this sun. This memory does not linger; it passes as the wall reverts to being what you first saw it as.

Ephemeral forms appear in the water where it is most turbulent between colliding columns. They are not creatures or objects but suggestions of them: a half-face of bone that fails to finish being, a hand that passes through your own shadow without taking the time to be solid, a word written in bright water that you cannot read because the letters are not yet formed. You are not sure if they are seen from just your eyes or if they are being projected from you, as if your own expectations are being tried out by the room and then discarded if they don’t seem right for the kind of world this vault wants to become.

The scale of this place is not that of a world or region but of a single chamber. Yet inside that cathedral, there are clues of something else. The ceiling feels not like the roof of a cave but like the underside of a level of reality that has not been fully installed yet. You look at it and feel not height above you but *wrongness*, as if the world above is not the top of a mountain but the bottom floor of something else.

You might, at some point, realize that the chamber you stand in is not the highest part of your world, but the lowest. That this water is not seeping up from the drowned world below but down from a drowned world above.

---

This is the world of the vault:

- high, but not high the way mountains are;
- warm, but not because of sun or fire;
- liquid, through and through, but not only water;
- sparse with things and beings and even time;
- scattered with small structures that are barely built;
- without technology but not without knowledge;
- dimly lit but not by any source;
- filled with rare but insistently strange life;
- deafening with the sound of reality being rewritten;
- strictly contained in size but endless in implication;

And, at the center of it all, a person or not-person who stands in the narrow space between water columns, trying not to be touched by them, because each time one of those columns passes over skin, it feels less like getting wet and more like being edited into the next version of the world.
