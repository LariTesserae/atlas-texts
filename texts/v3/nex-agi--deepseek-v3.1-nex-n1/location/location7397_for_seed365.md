You stand on the edge of a sunken forest bowl, high in the mountain range that still hasn't chosen its name.

The air is thin and sharp with cold, but not bitter enough: damp enough that your skin always has a faint film of moisture from mist, not sweat. The sun never comes here directly; the sky is just a lighter gray smudge melting into clouds. The world is a low bowl hollowed into the plateau: a few hundred paces across, concave as though someone took one spoonful from the stone’s skin and left the rest untouched.

Around you, the slopes are almost black with moss—not the soft green of lowlands but a deep, bruised emerald that eats light. It grows in thick mats like fur laid over angled stone, and everywhere else teeth push out from the moss: spines of fresh rock, broken and crystalline at the edges. They rise like ribs from a buried giant, ribs pointing into the bowl, as if the mountain has arched its spine and this is the stretched hollow beneath.

Below you, the bowl falls into a bowl of forest. The trees are dense but not uniform; it’s a layered forest that feels ancient but isn’t—just impossibly lush, compressed into a space that wants to fit them into a tighter, cleaner poem than nature can remember.

The trunks are lean, pale-barked, and climb straight up before they fan into wide, shallow carpets of leaves. Those leaves are not green but something like green-black and ink-blue mixed together, and they reflect almost no light. Instead they absorb it, turning the sky into a kind of darkness they chew quietly and then release as heat.

You are standing at the edge of a city, but you wouldn’t know it unless you knew what to look for.

The city is in the trees.

Between the trunks, at heights uneven, like clouds caught in different seasons of growth, there are platforms of woven living wood and fibrous root. You see them first by the gaps in the canopy—slices of empty air, not because there is nothing there but because there is something so carefully shaped to human use that it forces the forest into a pattern.

The paths of the city are not on the ground but elevated, moving between trunk and trunk, suspended at heights where light still filters down but the ground is already a shadowed ocean. You can see a few faint figures moving along these lines, slow and careful, like dust carried in thin currents of air.

Below, on the forest floor itself, almost nothing happens that you can see—but that doesn’t mean it isn’t active.

If you listen carefully—which you don’t, because it’s dangerous to listen carefully here—you hear the faint, almost inaudible scrape of tiny legs moving across leaf litter. The forest is alive not just with the usual birds and insects but with something else, something that has bent the ordinary rules of life into new shapes. The fauna here is overwhelming: not loud, not obviously violent, but present everywhere.

Everywhere you look you see movement.

At the base of the trunks, where the moss thins and bare stone peeks out like the bone between a wound’s lips, there are soft-shelled creatures that look like stones until they blink too many eyes at once. Their shells are cool and damp to the touch, and they breathe not through mouths but through pores that open and close like tiny gills.

Above you, hanging between the lowest branches, are what look at first like hanging baskets of wet moss—but the moss isn’t moss; it’s the body of something else. Each time the air shifts slightly, you see faint tendrils retracting from the air around them, as if the air itself were solid enough for them to climb.

You know the creatures of this place have strange senses.

There are flying things that look like glass leaves, drifting weightlessly through the air on invisible wings. They navigate by sound, but sound is not the same here as elsewhere—slower, thicker, more viscous. You can feel them when they pass near you, not just hear a faint hum like you might expect from something else’s world.

The air in this place is thick with silence. It is not that nothing makes sound; it’s that sound falls quickly to the ground and ends there. The mist is heavy enough that sound travels through it differently; your voice, if you speak, comes out as if you are saying things into a pillow.

The dampness and the cool keep moisture from ever rising fully into rain clouds, but the stone doesn’t care—it reaches out into thin strings of crystals that form from condensation dripping downward instead of upward. You see them hanging off the overhangs around the bowl’s lip like tusks made of salt or sugar, depending on the light.

The ground itself is uneven, not trodden to smoothness but left to rise and fall into smaller hollows within the hollow. Each dip holds a shallow pool of standing water, reflecting the sky and the strange leaves above it like a shattered mirror. You see the water’s surface dimpled by tiny dots—lifeforms so small they only reveal themselves as patterns, not as individuals.

The fauna of the bowl are not concerned with you so long as you don’t move too quickly or speak too loudly.

They are active in ways that are subtle but intense:

- A slow-moving, segmented shape that you might mistake as the trunk of a fallen tree glides across the forest floor beneath one platform. It has no head but has dozens of tiny limbs working in unison along the underside of its body, like cogs made of cartilage instead of metal.  
- A few of the glass-leaf creatures settle near one of the hanging “moss baskets,” and the air around them fills with a faint vibration like the ringing of a very distant bell. You can feel it rather than hear it clearly, as if your bones are listening on your behalf.

- On the platform nearest you, someone—human, or close enough—steps carefully on a walkway made of living root. You can see the faint movement of the fibers responding under their weight as if the tree itself is trying to remember how to support them.

This city is not built of stone and metal but of woven fiber and the living mechanisms of plants.

The tools used here are simple: hand-shaped chisels of bone and hard stone, sharpened fibers from the trunks themselves, containers of cured shell-like substance from the creatures of the damp. You can see a few tools hanging from the platforms by thin ropes of sinew or fiber—nothing that looks like advanced machinery, but everything carefully formed to last.

The architecture itself feels strange in how it fits into the forest rather than reshaping it.

There are no walls cut into tree trunks—only platforms wrapped around them like softly stitched collars. The gaps between trees are bridged with flexible fibers that are not entirely dead but not entirely alive; they are half-grown, half-woven. You can see where someone has carefully trained the growth of certain branches into a pattern that supports a walkway without the need for nails or bolts.

The light here is dim but not oppressive—it’s the kind of dimness that comes from being underneath layers of canopy rather than from lack of sun. Even though the sky is brighter above you, the leaves absorb more than they reflect; the forest is like a sponge that quietly soaks up light and then slowly leaks it out later as heat.

When you blink, you sometimes notice small differences you didn’t see before:

- A faint bioluminescent glow at the base of certain trunks, like a faint line of paint drawn at knee height.
- A pattern of holes carved into one branch that looks random but recurs exactly on several others around it—something like writing but made of space rather than ink.

You realize that what you see at one glance is not static.

The fauna movement isn’t chaotic; it follows patterns long enough that your eyes eventually adjust to them, but every time you think you understand something, another detail comes forward.

A creature that looks like a bundle of thin sticks gathered around a central knot suddenly unfolds itself and reveals wings that were not there before. It floats silently up to a higher branch and refolds into something that looks again like a bundle of sticks.

You feel the dense moisture of the air each time you breathe, and you notice how the simple act of exhaling has tiny, floating bits of life moving through your cloud like dust stirred by a wind that doesn’t exist.

The vegetation is lush to the point of being overwhelming.

Moss hangs off not just stone but older bark as well—like beards of green-black beard that drip slowly and silently into the pools below. In certain parts of the bowl, where water collects more deeply, you see thick mats of plants that resemble ferns but with leaves shaped more like lenses or dishes—curved to catch whichever faint specks of light they can.

The ground beneath you is uneven at the scale of your steps rather than miles. It rises and falls like shallow waves frozen in stone, each ripple bearing a thin layer of moss.

You take a step.

And immediately stop yourself because the ground feels strange underfoot—too firm in some places and almost hollow under others, as if beneath the thin skin of rock and moss there is something large breathing, or chewing slowly, or moving slowly toward you.

You back up carefully.

The stone edge you are standing on is sharp enough that you can see where fragments of it have broken off and rolled down into the bowl, forming small ridges that look like white veins against the darker stone below. The plateau itself is still young—the mountains around you are still grinding themselves into shape—and you can see patches where the rock looks like it was torn away rather than eroded. It looks like a wound before healing begins.

Around you there are almost no clear signs of human presence except for the subtle platforms wrapped around the trunks below you.

You see a faint glow that isn’t bioluminescent but comes from a small, carefully contained fire—its light shielded by layers of translucent shell or something similar—so that only a faint, warm glow leaks out around the edges. You notice someone sitting near it, wrapped in layers that look too thick for the climate but are clearly practical for whatever they need to protect themselves from.

You don’t call out to them.

That’s the rule of this place: if you’re too loud, something older than you in the forest will notice you faster than you can notice it.

The silence is not silent because there is no sound; it’s silent because sound is absorbed here into something else. You can feel that faint hum of something else’s movement deep beneath your feet—a deep, slow pulse like a heartbeat but not neat enough or regular enough to be your heartbeat.

You cannot see the full extent of the city, but you know it isn’t large.

From what you can see from where you stand, the habitation platforms form only a thin ring around a central core of untouched forest. The center itself is darker and thicker than the edges, like the heart of something that doesn’t want to be disturbed.

You can see, faintly, that there is something in the very center of the bowl—not exactly a tree, not exactly a building, but something vertical and thick and deeply entangled with its surroundings.

You can’t tell if its shape is natural or if it has been carefully trained over many years to grow that way, but it feels deliberate in the way that it aligns exactly with the deepest point of the hollow, like a spine pushed down into the center of the bowl’s curve.

Around you, the world feels both sparse and dense at the same time.

There are few physical structures scattered across the bowl—but each of them is layered with spaces of its own. The platforms themselves are thin but not flat; each supports several layers up and down rather than outward.

You see:

- Small platforms below that one might only be accessible from the trunk itself rather than from neighboring paths.  
- A thicker platform above you that seems to be woven from branches that rise from more than one trunk, like a web of threads that someone has carefully trained into overlapping rings.

Everything feels balanced between emptiness and fullness. There is enough space between structures to give you the feeling that you could breathe, if the air weren’t already thick with moisture and breathing life.

You hear—or half feel, rather than hear—a faint sound, like a distant bell being struck underwater. It resonates through the stone beneath you more than through air, and after a moment you realize it isn’t a sound produced by metal but by something organic hitting something else at exactly the right frequency to sing through stone.

You are not sure where it comes from exactly, but you feel a faint response from the center of the bowl—something like vibration traveling through roots you cannot see.

You cannot move farther into the bowl without being noticed more clearly.

You are already drawing attention, though you don’t know it yet.

The fauna around you are not threatening you yet—just watching, or sensing in whatever ways they sense instead of watching—because your body doesn’t match their patterns of movement quite enough to be invisible.

You notice, faintly, that one of the glass-leaf creatures has drifted closer to you than you thought comfortable. It hangs in the air near your shoulder, not touching you, but you can feel its presence like a faint weight on the edge of your awareness.

It doesn’t do anything else.

The silence around you stays thick.

You stay still.

You think about the rules of this place as you’ve pieced them together:

- Movement is respected but not rushed. Anything too sudden is treated like prey.
- Sound is not loud—but that doesn’t mean it is harmless. The forest absorbs sound differently, and something that listens to sound in ways you cannot imagine uses that absorbed sound as its own medium.
- You do not touch the hanging moss-baskets unless you want to invite something else’s digestion into your body slowly.
- The water pools are not safe to drink unless you know exactly which ones are safe, and which ones have been shaped by the city’s inhabitants to be safe. You vaguely suspect they have taught some of the water creatures to filter what they consume carefully.

- The city’s structure itself is alive and not entirely under human control; you have seen faint fibers moving slowly of their own accord in response to pressure or temperature or humidity or something you cannot perceive clearly.

You look back toward the bowl’s center and notice something else:

The sparse vertical structure you saw earlier, at the heart of the bowl, is not alone. There are thinner, vertical structures around it now—ones you swear weren’t there before—or perhaps they were always there but you didn’t see them until you saw that central spine and then your brain reorganized the world around them.

You see:

- Thin poles like petrified trees—or perhaps living trees grown to be straight—that support additional platforms in the very center above the deepest point of the hollow.
- The faint glow of more shielded fires up there—not bright enough to call “light” but enough to suggest that someone lives in that core too.
- The faint outlines of movement that you cannot fully identify—humanoid or not?

You realize that you are standing at the edge of something small but deeply active.

The scale of the place feels intimate enough that you could walk across it in less than an hour if you didn’t mind disturbing the balance and drawing attention to yourself—but you don’t do that.

You hold still instead.

The world around you continues in its own quiet ways:

- The slow-moving segmented creature finishes crossing beneath one platform and disappears into thicker foliage where the ground rises slightly, like a wave pulled into the shadow of another wave.
- The hanging moss-baskets sway gently, not because of wind but because something inside them is breathing.

You feel yourself extremely aware of your own heartbeat in the silence of this place, as if it has suddenly become louder than everything else around you.

And you know that if you stay longer, you’ll start seeing even more of the strange patterns in this landscape—the subtle shaping of branches by hands you don’t see, the careful arrangement of certain stones into lines that seem like maps but aren’t maps in your language, the way the creatures move around you in patterns that aren’t random but speak to something you don’t yet understand.

You also understand that this place isn’t safe for you—not because it wants to hurt you specifically, but because you don’t belong in its equilibrium yet.

So you step carefully back from the edge, leaving behind the small bowl hollowed into the mountain plateau, where damp, cool air holds a lush but silent forest of strange creatures and a quietly woven city of fibers and shadows, suspended in layers above the stone that still remembers being torn from the earth itself.
