The sky is low iron cloud, seamless and cold. What you see first, under it, is the fog.

---

**Ground & Foundation**

It’s a vast plateau, still being made.

You are standing on what is almost raw bedrock—not polished by ages of rivers but flayed and hurrying into being: a fractured sheet of dark stone, still warm from its own birth, still steaming faintly where the planet’s heat leaks up through cracks like suture lines across the world’s skin. The rock is mostly black basalt welded into crude plates, but there are patches where it’s been tugged and torn apart and you see the raw, still-hot core beneath—a glassy, cellular slag that glows faintly orange in the cracks.

The plateau is not flat in the way you expect of highlands. It buckles, rises in sudden low ridges, falls into shallow depressions that catch the cold air like cupped hands. None of these are old yet: no rounded valleys, no river-gouged gullies. The edges are crisp, sharp, almost brittle. The stone has barely learned to erode. It still breaks more than it wears away.

In places, water has seeped up through fractures and turned parts of the plateau into a vast shallow basin of damp stone and cold puddles—not yet soil enough to call it mud, but something more like a dark, glistening sandpaper. The moisture sits on top of the rock like sweat on skin, beading in thin films that shiver but never quite run away.

---

**Water**

There is water everywhere and nowhere.

The air is so saturated it feels as if someone has just turned off a rain that never properly fell. The borders of things are blurred with condensation: edges of rock smudge into faint halos. The plateau itself is a damp sponge laid over a hotter world beneath. Water weeps from fractures in thin, translucent curtains that tremble in the imperceptible breeze. When you touch the stone, your fingers come away slick, not with ice but with cold water.

Where the slope dips, shallow pools gather in scallops and hollows carved more by thermal stress than by time—small mirrors of sky that tremble at the slightest disturbance. They are not deep enough to call ponds or lakes yet; more like water left standing on a tabletop after a storm, but frozen in time at the edge of freezing.

Everywhere there is the faint sound of dripping: not the loud percussion of waterfalls but the soft punctuation of single droplets falling from overhanging lips of stone into puddles below. Each drop makes a tiny metal ring that echoes thinly across the emptiness.

---

**Vegetation**

What grows here grows in spite of the place.

Vegetation is sparse but not absent. In the cracks where there is shelter enough to gather a thin dust of grit and moisture, stiff mats of low growth cling close to the stone. They are not grasses but something more primitive—needle-leaved, lead-gray rosettes that look like overlapping scales of rock armor. They follow the moisture like contours drawn in faint green ink.

In sheltered hollows, where a few seasons of trapped water have softened the stone, you find thicker cushions of moss: dark, almost black, with tiny glistening spore stalks like pinheads of frost. They thrive in the cold, wet air, drawing nutrients from the rock and the sky itself.

Where the rock is warmer and water oozes steadily from a seam, there are thin, translucent filaments like pale algae, growing in thin films across the stone—a shimmer of life that looks almost like mineral veins until you realize it pulses very faintly with slow growth.

---

**Temperature & Air**

The cold is not killing but constant.

The air sits just above freezing, so that every wet surface is slick rather than rimed, but your breath still hangs in faint silver clouds. The cold feels heavy, as if it has weight, pressing the moisture down into low places and against your skin.

The wind is not strong but it is persistent—a thin, cold draught sliding along the plateau surface as if it has been doing this for centuries and has learned all the low paths. It carries the faint, metallic smell of newly exposed rock and the distant tang of something like brine or factory smoke, though you cannot place where.

---

**Sound & Silence**

The world feels mostly quiet, but quiet is not empty.

There is the constant drip of water, the occasional whisper of wind against stone edges, the faint gurgle of a hidden thread of water moving beneath a slab.

There is also a distant mechanical murmur that rides just beneath the threshold of hearing, like the memory of engines or the steady hum of a machine somewhere over the horizon. Infrequent, low, metallic clangs roll faintly across the plateau from time to time—not loud enough to echo like thunder, but more like someone hammering on a distant anvil, muffled by thick fog and distance.

---

**Fauna & Motion**

For all its emptiness, life is thick here.

You notice first the birds—or creatures like birds—that slide through the dim air, low over the plateau surface. They are not winged but propelled by some other means: slender bodies with faint red pulses down their lengths like veins of light, moving in smooth curves without visible flapping. They cling to the rock edges where moisture collects, drinking from the film of water like dark swallows at a pool, then vanish again into the fog.

They hunt the smaller things that rise from the damp stone: tiny, segmented crustaceans no larger than your thumbnail, their translucent shells glowing faintly with internal machinery. They scuttle across the wet stone in slow waves, smearing faint trails in the condensation.

In deeper pools where the water has collected for longer, there are larger forms—flat-bodied swimmers that are mostly a thin, translucent sheet stretched over a framework of pale bone and metal. They drift like condensed shadows beneath the surface of the water, occasionally breaking the skin with a slow ripple that sends concentric rings expanding across the pool.

---

**Technology & Industry**

The plateau feels untouched by human hands, but not untouched by industry.

There are no buildings here, no ruins of human structures, no rusted frames or broken walls. But the world itself has been engineered: the distribution of moisture follows a faint regularity of channels and seepages that suggests the rock was drawn into shape by machines before you ever saw it.

In places, you find exposed conduits—not like cables, but like veins of metal fused into the stone, running in straight lines beneath a thin crust of basalt that has cracked open like an eggshell. They pulse faintly with light or vibration, almost imperceptible unless you stand still and watch. They keep the rock from fully freezing, keep the water weeping just enough to sustain life.

The distant clangs you hear are not from construction on this plateau but from somewhere over the edge—perhaps deep in the chasms below, where larger machines are reshaping strata, drawing raw material up from the planet’s interior to be laid down here as this new land.

---

**Dynamics of Change**

This whole plateau is still learning how to be land.

The rock continues to settle, shifting in small increments with audible clicks like teeth settling in a jaw. Sometimes a slab will slide by centimeters, releasing a faint steam of moisture into the air and sending a ripple of water across shallow basins.

There are faint cycles in motion: the temperature of the rock rises fractionally, then falls again over hours that you only feel by watching the condensation patterns change. The pools of water grow and shrink with the rhythm of heat leaking up from below and cold leaking down from the sky.

The creatures that inhabit this raw place follow their own slow, attentive cycles. They move across the stone in patterns you cannot immediately read—gathering at certain seepages when they are warm and retreating when they cool, clustering around one fracture for days then suddenly abandoning it entirely.

---

**Overall Sense**

So you stand on newly born ground, high up where the air is thin and cold, under a sky that feels like low metal ceiling. The world is almost empty of human things—no roads, no buildings, no signs of any civilization but the faint skeleton of engineered rock beneath your feet.

Yet it feels like a factory floor turned inside out: machinery moved beneath the surface, processes hidden, leaving you only with the result: a high plateau being formed in real time, wet with cold condensation, populated by creatures feeding quietly off the stone’s own sweat and the heat leaking from below.

It is open and vast and seemingly empty, but when you sit still for long enough, you realize that every surface is alive with motion—small, precise, and purposeful. The quiet is not the absence of activity but the presence of many processes happening just beneath sight or at the edge of hearing. This is a world still being printed from the planet’s core, still resolving its own shape, still deciding how to weather.
