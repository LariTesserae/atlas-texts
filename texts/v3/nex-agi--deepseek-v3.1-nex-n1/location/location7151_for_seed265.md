At first glance it might pass for a swamp at night.

But you won't be looking for long.

---

The ground is not flat earth and root, but a low, uneven scatter of metal plates, laid edge to edge like a crude mosaic floor, the spaces between them sealed with a dark rubbery caulk that bulges slightly between the seams. Where the joins are imperfect, thin water seeps up and flows over the surface in slow glistening films, making the metal slick under your soles if you ever dare step. You don't, because there are places where the plates have been pushed down from below by some pressure, and where the caulk has split a faint line of light shows, not bright but strangely violet, like the glow of heated filaments.

This is a floor that has only been here for a decade or two at most. It hasn't yet time to be worn by the feet that would, if they were human, have been walking on it. Yet there are no footprints anywhere. Only the shallow break of the water and the occasional scratch of unseen things beneath the plates.

This is not the ground of the world, but laid over the world: like a temporary city installed on top of something it isn't ready to inherit.

The water is not a river, but the water that is everywhere when air meets metal through the night. Condensation drips from every edge. The smaller plates are rimmed with a necklace of droplets that coalesce and drop into chain of barely audible ticks into the shallow puddles. You can't see the droplets in the dark, but you hear the sound of them falling off the edges of the world.

You can't see the horizon, either. There is no horizon line. Only a low, uneven silhouette of structures and towers and things that might be towers or might be machines of ambiguous size, all cut black against the black of the sky. There is no moon to backlight them. No stars to throw them into relief. The sky has not been properly present for some time now.

The air is warm, not hot, but a thick stagnant warmth, like breath held in a closed room. You don't feel wind on your face, but sometimes something around you shifts slightly—the condensation on a rail, the surface of a puddle—suggesting a slow air current somewhere above you, not strong enough to be called breeze. You breathe the air and it tastes like steam and rust and the faint chemical of the caulk.

The smell of metal on the verge of rusting, but without rusting yet.

This is not yet old enough to be decay.

---

It is not rained, but it is wet.

The surface of the world is not uniformly covered with water; you are not standing in deep marsh or standing in a river. But the water is everywhere in the sense of being everywhere available to the surface. The air is so close to saturation that you can feel it on your skin as a faint, persistent film.

You look at the nearest structure: a low shed of corrugated metal, maybe twice the height of a person, its roof slanted at one angle to your left and one angle to your right, not quite matching up, the seam between the two halves sealed with the same black rubber that seals the ground plates. The walls of the shed, what you can make out of them in the near-darkness, are half obscured by sheets of water that flow down the surface in slow, uneven paths. The water comes from thin fringes of higher moisture above the shed's edge, where the air, condensing on the metal surface, collects into droplets that run down the walls in sheets. You have not seen rain begin; you have not heard rain end. The water comes from the air.

You are in the region of a machine that was not designed to cause the night to weep itself dry onto metal surfaces. But the machine's heat is wrong, and the temperature of the world is wrong, and so the water comes from the wrong direction.

On the wall of the shed some words were painted once in block capital letters that now only form suggestions of strokes: the last letters of "SYSTEM" and "4" and the suggestion of a number beneath that. You cannot tell the system of anything because the paint has been mostly replaced by the flows of water and by something else that has dragged across it like fingertips or feelers that have not been designed to care about legibility. The letters are smeared into faint lines that might be writing, or might be wounds.

Around the shed, the ground rises very slightly into a low mound of plates and caulk that you realize is not exactly ground. You think of those words they used to use in the Old World: "base load power," "distribution hub," "site services." This is some of the last generation of that architecture, before the world stopped being certain that it should be architecture any longer.

The distance between this and the next building is more space than you're comfortable with. You can't see the ambient detail that the human eye wants to see in less dark places. You have to infer the shape of the next structure from the suggestion of its angles: a series of panels that slope upward and downward, not quite aligned with the planes of the shed. You think of animal housing, or storage bays, or places where they kept things that they didn't want to look at every day.

The outlines of these things suggest not a town but a factory settlement that was not yet finished when the world changed its mind about what it should be.
