At the center stands a single stone tower.

It is not much wider than a cathedral’s nave, slender and rising only four cramped stories above the mirror of the water. The stone is pale gray but never looks dry. The lowest courses are almost green, where thin algae has learned the pattern of the rising tide. Above that, the limestone weeps in slow braided tears, leaving dark tracks that pulse and widen when the rain comes.

Around the tower, the water spreads shallow and flat—a drowned plaza, perhaps a few meters deep at most, deepening only toward the edges. The whole city, if city it can be called, snuggles into this basin like a lost bowl between the shoulders of great hills you never quite see. The air is cool, always faintly chilled, as if the heights around have chosen to keep their frost and share only the cold breath of it.

From the plaza of water, roofs of rough slate and older thatch rise in crooked rings around the tower. Most buildings are half sunk; their ground floors disappeared long ago, replaced by a dark wet shimmer. Wooden walkways stitch the facades together—thick planks worn into smooth humps by feet and weather and time, lashed with rope and reinforced with scavenged metal nails. The nails are the brightest thing here: patches of rust orange on the darker gray of timber.

The water in between is not empty but packed with presence: it is a dense soup of things half-alive and not-alive. The buildings themselves press so close together you can step from one threshold almost to the next without ever crossing open air. The gaps between them are just enough for water to flow and for reflections to shiver into existence.

Mirror-broken and re-broken, the reflections of stone walls, shutters, and windows overlie each other in the water, so that at a glance it seems the city doubles itself, upturned and shimmering beneath the surface. The world feels dense, saturated: not a single inch unoccupied by some presence—whether wood, stone, water, or the faint glow of bioluminescent filaments that lace the submerged foundations like veins of cold light.

Between the dwellings and beneath the walkways, the water is not quiet but still. It moves, but slowly—the slow pulse of tide, of condensation running down stone walls to drip into the surface, of the city’s own breathing. When condensation drops hit, they ring out tiny circles of sound that spread across the plaza and then vanish under the soft creaking of wood.

From above, if there were a sky here to observe from, the tower would be the center of a small knot of habitations, maybe no more than a hamlet’s worth of buildings, hemmed in by the low slope of hills or perhaps by the encircling arms of a long-vanished defensive wall, now only a suggestion under the waterline. The scale of it all is intimate—cathedral-like, even though the houses are plain. The tower itself towers over everything else, but not by much; a few minutes of climbing and you would see its uppermost battlements.

Throughout the day, the light never grows strong. It filters down from above in pale columns, softened by the mist. The sun, if there is one, never cuts directly; the valley of the city was chosen or perhaps abandoned for its shadow, and the mist hangs thick, low, and cold. The light that reaches the water is grayed and milky, but sufficient enough to see clearly.

Vegetation is sparse, but that word feels deceptive. It does not look like a wasteland; rather, every growing thing is chosen carefully for where it can cling. Thin mosses carpet the northern walls of the tower, forming a dark velvet that drinks in condensation and drip by drip turns stone to soil. Occasional tufts of fern grow in crannies where timber and stone join, fed by runoff from the roofs above them.

Below the surface, a few species of pale algae flourish in ropey strands that anchor to the submerged foundations of buildings. They are not lush; they are sparse and yet everywhere, if you look closely. In the dimmer, colder depths beneath the walkways, bioluminescent algae glows in faint constellations of green-blue, outlining the ribs of old beams, like the memory of a ruin’s skeleton.

Fauna is rare but not absent. A pale, blind fish no longer than a finger sometimes slips from the shadow of a pier into the open water, then back again. Its movements are unhurried, almost tentative. It has few predators here; the city’s vertical density makes it hard to hunt from above or below.

If there are birds, they are rare visitors—maybe the occasional silhouette of wings passing through the mist up high, heard more than seen. The sound of their passing is faint enough to be mistaken for the snap of a wet rope in the wind, or a shutter closing.

The soundscape of the place is mostly quiet but layered with small, precise events. The drip from the eaves onto the walkways provides a slow, irregular rhythm. Wood creaks softly as it swells or shrinks in the damp. Somewhere, a low grindstone turns, driven by hand or foot to sharpen tools or grind grain—a steady, low sound that fills the background like the hum of a distant engine.

Voices, when they occur, are muted and close. They pass from doorway to doorway, carried over water that turns every sound round and wraps it in softness. Words heard from one side of the plaza return to the other as faint echoes, changed by water and stone and wood.

Technologically, this is a world of simple things. There are no machines beyond the most basic: hand looms in the upper rooms, pails, winches, rope-and-pulley systems to haul nets from the water. The nails are iron and nothing else; many tools are bone, stone, or wood, hardened in fire or cured by the salt and air. The tower itself, though taller, is built with no greater artifice than the others—just more stone, more labor, more craft.

Metal, where it appears, is precious and dealt with carefully. It is taken from older ruins presumably beneath the water, or traded from caravans that might venture down through the pass—if such a pass exists. The people of the tower melt and reshape what they can, but no more. They are not smiths in the grand sense; they are menders, patchers, preservers.

What is strange here—the unusual thing that sets the place apart from any ordinary village perched above a lake—is the way that water and built space are inseparable. The plaza itself is a sheet of water, but the city’s life happens not on it but around and across it. You can read the history of the settlement in the water’s habits.

When the tide comes in—if there is a tide—the water rises along the tower stones like breath up a chest. It carries with it the faint glow of algae from below, so that for a time the lower stories of the tower are cased in a wavering green light. The wet line climbs higher, and the lower windows of the tower are sealed with thick, warped glass or shuttered against it.

When the water recedes, it leaves behind a thin scrim of salt and algae on the stone, marking the high point of its visit. This line is a map of the water’s memory and the city’s adaptation to it. Over the scale of one human lifetime, the tower has been reshaped by this constant rising and falling.

It is said, quietly, among the few who dwell here, that the tower itself is not merely a building of stone and mortar and simple metal reinforcement. It is something that grew and was built at the same time, or perhaps built upon and into the bones of something older. The weeping stone at its base weeps not evenly, but in patterns that suggest something like a face or a hand reaching upward from below the waterline, if you look long enough in the right light.

Some of the older folk claim that on the coldest nights—when the mist thickens and the water goes still as glass—the bioluminescence beneath the foundations arranges itself into lines of script that no one can quite read. It moves like writing, like a language that flows without consonants or fixed vowels, a script of motion and glow.

Others, newcomers, say that is just the algae drifting and the eye of the viewer shaping it into meaning. But they lock their windows when the water rises too near, all the same.

Ecologically, the place is in active motion. The water changes with the seasons and the tides, if they are indeed tides—perhaps instead the water responds to the pressure of the atmosphere above or to the hidden weight of aquifers below. The temperature rarely stays constant for long; it drifts from cool to colder to cool again, as if testing the limits of human endurance.

The tower’s inhabitants, if there are any, live in the upper floors and the rooftops. They fish from small windows and low balconies, dropping nets or lines. The fish they catch are small and thin and must be prepared carefully to remove a faint bitterness that comes from some algae they eat.

Vegetation is harvested carefully from the walls and roofs. The mosses are dried to make insulation for clothes and bedding; the ferns are cut for medicine and tea; the algae beneath the water is collected by hand, using primitive nets and baskets, and sometimes fermented. It is a hard, sparse life, and yet the density of the urban fabric—the tight packing of every space—suggests a people who have learned to live inside each other’s shadow.

Life moves at the pace of handcrafts, slow and steady. There is no rush to be anywhere else because the water encircles the city, and any land beyond rises swiftly and steeply. To leave the tower’s shadow is to climb into wet stone cliffs and hanging mist, where a fall might mean you vanish into the cloud and never find your way back.

Something, too, under the water—something older than the tower—holds the city’s dwelling place in its hand. 

The foundations that are lined with glowing algae are not all foundations of stone; some of them are bone. Or something like bone. They curve under the waterline like ribs of a long-drowned leviathan whose back has been mistaken, for centuries, for solid bedrock.

Periodically, the water shifts in a way that has no visible cause. The surface of the plaza will develop a slow rotation, a circular motion that twists the reflections of tower and houses around an invisible center point. The walkways creak a bit more loudly then; ropes stretch; and in the tower, someone always checks that the shutters are fast.

Once, perhaps long ago, the city was smaller—maybe no more than a few huts on a hillock above the waterline. The tower might have been built later, or perhaps earlier, by different hands, when it was still landlocked and dry. But the water rose and the land sank and the people found themselves not retreating but rather building upward, into the air, and outward, along the surface of the water itself.

Now the water and the city are one. The walls are wet with it; the air smells of it; the light is filtered through it; and the soundscape of dripping and lapping is never far away.

And yet it is full—not with crowds of people or throngs of animals, but with things to be reckoned with: stone, water, algae, moss, wooden planks, iron nails, glass, bone, mist, reflections, the faint glow of life beneath the surface, and the tower overhead, watching it all, patiently, weeping.
