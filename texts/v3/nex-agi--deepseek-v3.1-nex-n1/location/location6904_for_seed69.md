The sky is never really visible, but if you could climb high enough to see it—and you cannot—you would glimpse only the cloud of a name: Wraithstone.

The sky is made of pipe.

Infinite black iron, ribbed and welded, slung in chains and loops and braids across the upper air of Wraithstone, thick as a forest of branches turned inside out, dense as a ceiling of roots. Its joints sweat rust and water and verdigris; its threads run thick with condensation and heat. The sky is a forest of pipe, and the rain is a constant drip between the gaps.

Wraithstone is a mountain-world, or perhaps a single mountain that is also a world—scale enough to be continental, but folded enough to be a city. The lower air is dense and warm with breath and heat-exchange and the breath of machines. The stone is high and fractured and steep and hollowed. The mountains are as much hole as they are height.

---

The rock is old stone, old enough to have forgotten that it ever was something else—old enough to be carved, not by sudden catastrophe, but by the slow erosion of internal heat and pressure and the constant drip of water from above. The rock is holy stone: Wraithstone.

Here is how stone matters:

Wraithstone is a living stone, or something like it—not stone with a life, but a stone-body whose lives are slower than minds and closer than skin. It holds heat like a memory; it warm-sweats and cold-weeps along its fractures. It holds water like a secret; it releases water when it is tired, or drunk, or frightened, or when it dreams.

For centuries, perhaps millennia, the stone has been breathing itself apart.

The mountain-body is riddled with tunnels, shafts, caverns, flutes, hollows where veins have been eaten away by underground rivers and the slow march of fungi and root and mold and something else—something that has learned to crawl through stone the way light crawls through glass.

---

The air is thick.

The sky is dark, but the air is thick with light.

The pipes themselves glow faintly—patches of phosphorescence on metal where fungus has learned to eat iron and the heat-friction of moving water. The air is dense with condensation and oil droplets and pollen and the spores of things that grow where there is no sun but only heat.

The air smells like rain and metal and rust and warm root and mold and the breath of animals and the exhalations of industry.

---

The vegetation is lush but strange.

There are no trees. There is no sun.

Instead, the stone is forested with pale, thick-trunked fungi columns taller than three people standing on one another’s shoulders. Their caps are heavy and veined with translucent, pale flesh that glows faintly from internal bioluminescence—glow-lamps spawned by forgotten spores.

The fungi-fruits are oil-sacs and water-pouches, swollen, dripping. They sprout with thick flesh like meat-roots, and they drip liquid into carved stone troughs and hollows where the stone has learned to drink them.

On lower ledges and terraces, where rock-lines have cracked into thin ledges like shelves around the mountain, there are forests of moss-riverbeds, moss-kelp: thick, tangled carpets of moss that have learned to grow upright like kelp forests in a dark ocean. They hang down over ledges, dripping long threads laden with slime and water and tiny insects.

The moss grows along the outside of the pipes too.

Wherever pipe meets rock, wherever metal sweat condenses, there are thick mats of moss and fungal paste and algae-blooms, glowing in faint lines like rivers of light in the dark.

The stone itself grows—stone-scale lichens and thermal-sucking fungi that grow in patterns like heat-maps, following the warmth of the hidden rivers beneath the rock and the heat of the pipes that snake through them, forming rose-colored rings and spiral-shaped colonies around warm vents.

---

The water is everywhere and nowhere.

The sky-pipes drip rain constantly, but the rain is not simple rain. It is water mixed with oil and iron and whatever else lives inside the infrastructure of the world. It falls into stone troughs carved into the rock-face and into open gutters that run along the ledges.

The gutters feed into larger channels, channels that run in spirals around the mountain-body, channels that run into tunnels, channels that run into hidden rivers beneath the stone. The water flows in circles and spirals and coils and loops back on itself.

The stone itself weeps.

Wherever rock has cracked, there are thin sheets of water running down stone, like tears on a face. The fractures weep and weep, forming semi-permanent waterfalls, thin as curtains, that fall into pools carved over centuries by the constant drip-drip-drip. The pools are warm and dark and glitter with phosphorescence from tiny organisms swimming in their depths.

The water rises near the surface—sometimes even breaking from stone, like a stone-hurricane bursting upward: a cold-water geyser that erupts from the rock, spewing mist into the dark air, then falls back into itself as a fountain.

The air feels post-rain because it is always post-rain: always dripping, always condensing again on metal surfaces and stone lips and moss-flesh.

---

The mountains are vast.

If you stand on one ledge, you cannot see the other side of the mountain-city-world. You only see the wall going upward, disappearing into darkness and the braided tangle of pipes, and downward, disappearing into mist and the glow of industrial lights below.

The mountain itself is layered, like a city pressed upward.

The higher slopes are narrow, broken, terraced like stacked shelves of stone, each one carved and hollowed into chambers and chambers and more chambers. The middle slopes are broader—broken into wide ledges and terraces where larger communities clamber and cling. The lower slopes, where they exist at all, are a vast fan of carved rock and stone terraces and water-channels and fungus-towers, all sloping downward into mist and darkness.

The mountain itself is large enough to have its own weather.

The warm air rising from the lower slopes meets the colder upper air, and the condensation inside the upper pipes cycles and swirls like clouds trapped in metal. The stone itself exhales—sometimes a warm, humid breath that fogs the air, sometimes a cold rush that chills the skin and makes the moss curl inward.

---

The erosion is old.

The rock-face is carved into smooth, hollow forms like stomachs and lungs and chambers. The stone has long been scoured by water and heat and pressure and time; it has settled into its own shapes, carved like a body sculpted by its own breath.

The stone archways and bridges that connect ledge to ledge are not carved by hand but carved by time—the slow erosion of water running along faults, leaving stone behind in thin ribs like ribs of bone.

The stone itself sometimes looks like faces, or hands, or coiled bodies, pressed into stone and then turned away from you so that you only see half of them.

---

Built marks are few.

There are no true cities here, only scattered structures that cling and grow out of the stone.

There are stone huts built into niches in the rock-face, their walls carved directly out of the mountain itself, their roofs formed from overhanging stone lips. There are stone towers built onto narrow spikes of rock that rise like fingers from the broader ledges—towers that rise only enough to be above the mist-line.

There are faint markings carved into stone: signs like sigils or glyphs or warning marks, worn smooth by the dripping water and softened by moss. There are occasional arches of iron or stone that cross gaps between ledges, bridges without handrails or safety nets, bridges that seem to grow directly out of the rock like bone extensions.
