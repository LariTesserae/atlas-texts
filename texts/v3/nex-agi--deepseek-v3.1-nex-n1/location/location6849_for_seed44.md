Let me call it *Eelbridge*.

---

The air has never been dry.

Not quite rain, not quite fog, but something always between the two: a fine, breathable mist that hangs in the lanes and alleys, making every surface glow as if newly washed. The air smells like wet stone, wet wood, sweet rot from the compost-channels, and the faint tang of ozone from the humming fiberglass conduits that run along every available wall. The heat never crests into true swelter—it stays just warm enough that you never need to think of a coat—but the dampness makes everything feel heavier, slower, as if the world itself is breathing slowly.

Eelbridge sits low in the world, almost sunken.

It isn’t built *on* land so much as suspended just above it. The ground is a shallow, leaf-patterned bowl of water and silt and root, where the city’s foundations are driven down through thick mats of vegetation and deadfall, into the soft, dark mud below. The waterline floats everywhere a few handspan below the streets, so that when you look between the planks of the walkways you can see the surface of the water, black and smooth as glass, except where it is broken by the slow drift of the eelgrass.

The city is small. You could cross it on foot in half an hour if you didn’t mind the crowds and the constant, gentle detourings of its architecture. But “small” doesn’t mean empty. Everywhere there is something: a wall, a stair, a balcony, a crane, a tank, a ladder, a pipe, a trellis, a dripping gutter, a cluster of quarantine tanks humming along a railing, a bundle of cables emerging from the trunk of a witness tree. Everything is built over or under or through something else.

This is a place where everything has been here for a long time.

Walls lean into each other like tired old acquaintances who, long ago, stopped standing up straight and decided to simply prop each other up. The exposed timbers of the older buildings are dark and smooth from years of moisture and touch, their edges gone round and soft, as if the city itself has been worn by the hands and bodies of generations. Mill efflorescences trail from plumbing joints in thin, pale fans of mineral and algae, like delicate lacework. Rust stains vein down steel columns in patterns of old and new orange, each color a record of some shift in humidity or chemistry.

Metal and moss and concrete and living wood have all reached a kind of truce. The city doesn’t grow so much as settle further into itself, each new layer added over the last, until you can read the decades like rings in a cross-section of trunk.

---

The substrate is plant.

Walk the streets of Eelbridge and your feet are rarely on bare earth. The town itself grows on a thick mattress of living plant matter—roots and rhizomes and mycelial threads and fibre-mesh and all the things that have been built into them as support, reinforcement, insulation.

In older districts, the buildings rise from ancient trunks whose canopies form the roof of the city, while their root systems and the entanglements layer upon them form the structural floor. The ground floor in many places is not a floor at all but a lattice of heavy root-branches, covered over with living boards of bioengineered wood that knit themselves into the gaps below. You can feel, underfoot, the faint heartbeat of sap flow and the subtler, low-frequency hum of the industrial systems that tap into those networks.

The walls, too, are not just “plant-covered”: they are plant-made. The city’s builders long ago gave up separating the vegetative from the structural. The walls are living trunks and branches, trained and reinforced with steel and carbon fiber, but the bulk of the mass is cellulose, lignin, and fibrous tissue that continues to grow slowly. The concrete that you see here and there is mostly pockets of ancient infill, or newer composite where stress needed reinforcement—and the plants themselves have grown over, through and around it.

Ceilings are canopies.

In many places, the roof of the walkway is the underside of a balcony garden whose soil is so thick and layered with growth that you can’t see the sky except when rain or leaf drip fall on your upturned face. Ferns and epiphytic orchids and a hundred kinds of engineered mosses cascade from ledges and balconies and the edges of overhead pipes. Vines curl along mechanical cables. The boundary between “building” and “grove” and “machine-room” has blurred to where there is almost no point in maintaining it.

In the residential districts, each dwelling is a small microforest with rooms cut inside the trunk or curled around it like cleats on a mooring line. The trees themselves are the foundation beams, their trunks branching out into floors and balconies and privacy walls. The warmth of human bodies and the glow of lamps inside make the leaves on the exterior walls react slightly brighter than those on the shaded side of the trunk, so that at night you can see, faintly, where people are even from outside.

Even the water itself seems semi-alive with plant life.

In the canals beneath the walkways, dense mats of floating vegetation cover much of the surface—pale water-ferns with their fine roots trailing down, duckweed in thick emerald blankets, bladderwort whose tiny traps create invisible turbulence beneath the waterline. Beneath them, eelgrass and algae and more of the engineered root-mat that keeps the banks from eroding away entirely.

Above the waterline, the roots themselves become the retaining walls, their lattices reinforced by permeable bioplastic panels. When the water rises slightly during the wettest seasons, the city’s streets don’t flood so much as the water just comes higher up the trunks of the trees, lapping at the lower edges of the lift-plumbing and the cooling coils.

Everything is warm.

The temperature stays steady, day-night oscillations barely enough to make you note them. The air is thick with moisture but does not cling to you like mist in a cooler climate would; it just feels like breathing in soup that has only just cooled enough to drink safely. The heat keeps the metabolism of the plant-city high, and keeps the industrial processes below and around and above it churning.

---

Above the rooted canopy, the sky is never seen directly.

You see it reflected in the polished surfaces of water tanks and the pale glass panels of skylights set between the trunks, but if you try to look straight up you see mostly leaf and bark and metal latticework, and the occasional swatch of gray-white cloud that gets caught between them.

This is a cathedral-sized grove-room.

Eelbridge is not large enough to be called a city in the old sense—but it feels like one when you’re inside it. The main “street” is actually a long gallery under a continuous canopy of branches whose trunks have been fused over decades into a single arched structure. The ribs of the vault overhead are the trunks themselves; the ribs of the vault below are the steel beams that support the walkway beneath.

The walls are the living trunks and the structures built against them. The “nave” is lined with small workshops and market stalls whose fronts are open to the humid corridor, so that walking down it feels less like moving down a street and more like traversing a single long, continuous interior whose sides are furnished by different people.

Smaller passages branch off like chapels.

These are narrow side streets where two buildings lean so close together that their balconies almost touch overhead, leaving only a slice of space above. The light here is dimmer and greener than in the main gallery. The wooden floorboards are slightly uneven, each one worn smooth by thousands of footsteps over years, and you have to step carefully because they are always damp and slick in places.

At the edges of the city, where the canopy thins and you can finally glimpse the sky beyond, you realize the whole place is enclosed and defined by a ring of older, more massive trunks whose limbs have been trained and braided together into a continuous treelike ring-wall. This living wall has been reinforced with cable and steel mesh, but from the inside it remains mostly bark and leaf and vein.

Within the ring-wall, everything is dense.

Everywhere there is something to touch or duck under or work around: a low hanging trellis loaded with heavy gourds, a bundle of insulated cables, a cluster of condensation tanks whose sides drip slow water onto the path below. The space between structures is narrow enough that you often brush both walls with your elbows as you walk.

There is very little unused vertical.

Flights of wooden stairs and old iron ladders rise between levels of the city like vines in a tree. Mid-story platforms are filled with small industries and workshops that are themselves layered over homes below and storage under the floor. The air is layered with smells: machine oil from above, cooking from below, wet leaf from everywhere.

---

And yet, despite the density, this is not a chaotic place.

Eelbridge has been established for a long time.

Its structures are not in chaos but in a kind of weary balance. The trees have grown to accommodate the walkways. The walkways have been built to accommodate the trees. Each new beam has been added where someone’s hand or eye or shoulder has repeatedly encountered the same obstacle and decided that enough is enough.

The erosion of time is visible but not destructive.

On the walls of the oldest buildings, you can see where the constant rain from the leaves above has carved shallow channels in the stonework and composite. The channels are not straight—they follow the flow of water over time, the way it always prefers a certain route down the wall. The stone itself has been etched into gentle patterns that you can trace with your fingers.

Wherever metal supports meet the wood of the trunks, you can see the gradual ways in which the tree has grown around the bolts, swallowing them partway into its bark as if trying to take them back into itself. The edges of the metal plates that are meant to brace the trunks are almost softened, like pebbles worn down by river water.

The path itself—where people have walked for generations—shows its own erosion pattern: the center of each board or stone step is slightly lower than the edges, where thousands of feet have worn down the wood.

There is a sense that everything has reached a compromise.

The water does not rush away; it just slowly seeps, condensing on the undersides of leaves and dripping from the edges of gutters. The trees don’t grow wildly but have been trimmed and trained into a stable architecture that supports their mass and that of the city built upon them. The machinery, too, has settled into the slow rhythm of maintenance and occasional repair rather than expansion or upheaval.

---

The city is built; more than that, it is urban.

You might not call it a city in terms of population size, but in terms of organization, complexity, and the way human lives are arranged in it, Eelbridge is unmistakably urban. It has rulers of some kind—though they are almost invisible—inspectors, maintenance crews, systems of allocation and exchange that manifest in subtle signage everywhere.

The infrastructure is dense.

Pipes, wires, and fiber bundles run in thick clusters along one side of every main corridor, protected behind metal or bioplastic panels whose hinged doors are always marked with small symbols indicating what they do: “water-pressure,” “thermal exchange,” “nutrient feed,” “data trunk.” When you walk down the main gallery, you pass beneath a continuous spine of these industrial arteries running overhead like a mechanical backbone along the ceiling.

Housing is stacked.

Most of the homes are small, but they are heavily nested into each other and into the overarching tree structure. A single trunk might support several dwellings at different levels, each with its own small balcony that overlooks the interior canal or the interior gallery. The sound of life is everywhere: people talking above and below, cooking pots scraping, a child practicing a musical instrument somewhere in the latticework.

There are centralized places, too.

The entrance hall to the main gallery is where the city’s most public life happens: a long open floor under the highest part of the canopy, where the floorboards are slightly wider and more evenly worn. Here you find a small market of stands and hanging shelves filled with microgreen trays, cultured algae bricks, vats of fermented broth, and small, bright objects of metal or fired glass.

At the far end, under the largest spread of the canopy, where the light is filtered through a mass of leaves and glass panels, there is a raised platform that functions like a town square or forum. People sit on its steps, eating, talking, and waiting for the water to rise or fall to the next premarked level on the pillars along the canal edge.

---

Eelbridge is not industrial in the smoke-and-stacks sense of the word, but it is deeply industrial beneath the leaf.

Most of the machinery is housed below grade or in the trunkwork itself, where thick roots and the reinforcing mesh of the foundation walls dampen the sound, but you can still feel it through the floor. When you stand in one of the older workshops, you can feel a faint, continuous vibration in the boards beneath your feet—a steady hum that becomes part of the background noise of your thoughts.

The engines are almost everywhere, but they are distant gods.

They live in the lower levels of the city, where the waterline is closer to the floor and the machinery has to sit on raised platforms or sturdy piles of composite stone. The engines are not huge single beasts but a profusion of smaller machines—pumps, condensers, heat exchangers, biolectors that harvest energy from the chemical byproducts of the plant metabolism and the waste systems.

The air carries the smell and occasional subtle taste of ozone from electric motors and the faint hum from transformers and the cracking sound of arcs being made inside protective housings. When a maintenance crew opens one of the access panels in the floor, the smell of warm machine oil and the heat of the engines below wafts up into the cooler air above for a moment.

The production here is scaled.

Most of what is made in Eelbridge is made for Eelbridge itself, or for its near neighbors in the network of small, similar towns strung along the same shallow basin. The workshops are not factories but the small, steady kind of industry that tends to the needs of a living system: irrigation parts, clip-on filters for the air, new components for the water pumps, the occasional replacement section for an aging trunk wall.

In the hydropower alley at the edge of the city, where the main canal narrows to pass under the ring wall and out again, the water runs faster and you can hear the continuous rush of turbines. The walls of that passage are lined with small instrumentation rooms where the hum of the generators and the cool, oily smell of the gearboxes make the air feel thick with the presence of machines.

Most everything is electrified.

Lights flicker on slowly in the evening, not in sudden banks but in a spreading wave as ambient sensors notice the deepening of the light under the canopy. The glass panels set between the trunks and the translucent panels of the composite walls glow faintly from within, as if they are storing the light of the day to release it later. When the night finally comes on the surface of the world outside the ring wall, inside Eelbridge the city still has its own internal twilight of warm artificial light.

---

The light is normal.

Which is to say: there is no perpetual night, no searing artificial sun, no oppressive neon that refuses to let the world sleep. Instead, the light is recognizably like daylight, but sharply filtered.

During the day, the light enters the city under the canopy and the layered glass as a soft, diffuse glow that has been tinted by the leaves of the trees and the tinted glass panels above. The colors of the world are muted and made green at the edges of everything, so that even the bright petals of the flowers in the community balcony boxes seem to be seen through a layer of crushed leaf.

The shadows are abundant but not harsh.

Because the light source comes from many points above and between the trunks and the glass panels, the shadows cast by objects are multiple and faintly overlapping. You see your own shadow as a looser, softer version of itself, duplicated in several directions at once.

At night, the city lights up from within.

The lamps in the workshops and the homes use the same balance of color that the sun would during a clear day at noon, but here they are seen as warm contrasts against the dark foliage of the trees and the dark sky glimpsed above the canopy. When you walk down the main gallery at midnight, the world feels like a continuous luminous corridor of wood and leaf and glass and water.

---

The animals of Eelbridge are there, but they are not the center of the world.

You rarely encounter them outright. Instead, you see signs: a small dark track on the underside of a leaf where a slug has passed over it, the faint rustle among the mats of water hyacinth in the canal beneath the walkway when something small changes position under there.

The birds are the most obvious.

But they, too, are rare in numbers of species if not in absolute individuals. A few varieties of small birds nest in the branches of the trees and in the small spaces between the trunks of the buildings and the rootwork of the canopy above. You can hear their calls, but seldom see them among the leaves and the shadows. The presence of birds is mostly sound and occasional sudden movement of a branch.

There are eels, of course.

Their name is in the city’s moniker for a reason. The canals beneath the walkways are home to long, dark shapes that slip through the water like living shadows. When the light is just right and the water not too covered by floating vegetation, you can see their backs undulating slowly beneath the surface, working their long way against the faint current. Sometimes a child freezes on the bridge, watching one eel or a small group of them passing under the walkway below.

The insects are more plentiful than the vertebrates, but even here their numbers are constrained by the conditions of the city. The air is thick with humidity but not with the swarms you might expect in a wild wetland of this size. The insects are mostly small: midges that hover in thin clouds above the water where the light catches the reflections of their wings, and tiny specialist beetles that get their own names in the maintenance manuals because they help keep the rot-fungi under control on the trunk walls.

---

Eelbridge is not strange by the standards of its world.

There are no impossible geometries of space, no alien skies that twist and turn inside themselves, no laws of physics that are obviously broken or bent. The world operates in the familiar way: water runs down, not up; hot air rises; people need to sleep.

The weirdness of Eelbridge is more subtle.

It comes from the way that plant and machine and settlement and soil have all been permitted to merge into a single hybrid system.

There are no “natural” trees here in the old sense of unaltered life. The trunks of the buildings have been selected and modified and encouraged over generations to fit the architecture of the city. Their bark patterns are regular in ways that bark does not normally grow; their branches spread out in flat planes rather than wild sprays of growth.

There are small, recurrent things that you notice only when you spend longer than a day there.

The water in the main canals reverses flow slowly during the night cycle and then reverses again in the morning, to keep the bottom from silt-locking completely. The hum of the turbines below never changes, but its resonance with the water and the wood around the city seems to shift with the barometric pressure of the outside world.

Some of the older trees are known to have particular “moods,” when the sap flow in them seems to grow stronger or weaker in response to changes in the city’s internal humidity or the way work is being done on their branches. People swear that in certain houses, when the tree’s mood is dark, the lights in the fixtures along the trunks flicker differently.

But none of this is overtly strange. It is just the world that this place has become.

---

The sound of Eelbridge is loud.

Not in the sense of a factory or a concert, but in the sense of continuous presence. There is always something to hear.

Underneath everything else is the water.

It is everywhere in the soundscape: the soft drip from the leaves above onto the path below, the steady drip from the roof edges into the gutters, the occasional heavier splash of something falling from a higher root into the canal, the almost constant, faint trickle from somewhere nearby where a maintenance valve is letting out pressure.

Over the water sound, there is the hum of the machines below.

It is not a single pitch but a complex chord of many different frequencies, depending on where you are in the city. Near the hydropower alley at the edge, the hum is deeper and has more vibration in it; in the center of the main gallery, it is higher and smoother, where all the distant engines blend together into the same background texture.

Then there is the human sound.

People talking in low voices in the narrow corridors between the trunk walls, where their voices echo slightly off the curved surfaces. The clatter of pots and pans from the kitchens above and below. The sound of tools being worked on surfaces in the workshops—metal on metal, metal on wood, the occasional scrape or rasp of a file.

Then the plant sound.

It is subtle but insistent once you notice it: the faint rustle of leaves in the air that always moves between parts of the city, even when there is no wind outside. The soft creaking of the tree trunks and their platforms as they very slowly shift and settle with the temperature changes of the day. The occasional sharp crack from somewhere high above when a branch someone forgot to trim makes itself known by moving against one of the metal supports.

Then the birds and insects.

The calls and the high-pitched zizz of wings somewhere over your head when you are least expecting them. The occasional splash when something falls from high above into the water below.

When you first arrive in Eelbridge, all of this sounds like noise. After a few days, the pattern starts to emerge; and then you understand that the city’s soundscape is itself a kind of language, telling you what is happening with its water and its machines and its people and its trees.

---

Change happens slowly here.

The seasons of the outside world are acknowledged by Eelbridge mainly as changes in humidity and air pressure and the occasional intrusion of a storm from outside the ring wall. Inside the ring wall, the climate is controlled, but in a loose, slow way.

The water level rises and falls over weeks and months rather than days.

The pillars of the canal edge are marked with small, brightly painted rings indicating different levels: “safe maintenance,” “normal operation,” “high water,” “danger.” When the water is approaching one of these thresholds, the maintenance crews begin to open the valves under the floorboards and redirect the flow into the auxiliary basins and storm tanks that are woven into the rootwork below the city.

You can tell the city’s longer cycle by watching the water.

During the wetter half of the year, when the outside basins are full and the pressure on the city’s foundations from the surrounding marshland is higher, the water level comes closer to the bottom edge of the walkways. You can hear it more clearly, smell its deep mineral and root smell more strongly. During the drier seasons, that water recedes again by a few handspan, and you can see more of the black trunks of the root-banks below.

The growth of the trees themselves is slow.

Each year, the new wood of the trunks pushes the support plates and brackets slightly farther from where they were originally bolted on. From the outside of the ring wall, you can see the crowns of the trees lifting and widening slightly over the years, but the change is slow, like watching a continent of leaves drift across its sky.

People change slowly.

Families inhabit the same homes, sometimes for several generations, and the only noticeable change in the dwelling from one decade to the next is a new layer of floorboard over the old, or a different arrangement of the balcony railing where the children have finally grown tall enough to need a higher guardrail.

The machines themselves are maintained, rather than replaced.

When a pump or a generator finally fails beyond its tolerance of patchwork repairs, the replacement models are almost always designed to fit into the same spaces and bolt into the same plates that have been worn into the wood or the concrete floor. This is not because of any lack of innovation, but because the city itself is now old enough that its older parts are considered worth preserving.

---

What you feel when you stand on the waterfront bridge at the center of the main gallery is a sense of deep, uneasy equilibrium.

The water below you, black and smooth as glass, except where the eelgrass breaks it in slow waves. The heat of the air around your face and the cool seeping up from the water below. The faint hum of the city under your feet overlapping the soft sound of dripping from everywhere above.

You can feel the weight of everything above and below you.

Above you, the branches of the trees spreading out to support the homes and the workshops and the balconies and the glass panels. Below you, the rootwork and the pipes and the engines and the tanks of Eelbridge’s industrial heart, all of it humming slowly, like the breathing of some immense animal that has coiled itself under the city and fallen asleep there.

And you realize that this is not a place built upon the land so much as a place that has grown itself into the chance of an existence between the water and the sky.

Eelbridge is warm.

Eelbridge is wet.

Eelbridge is small and dense and full of life made of leaves and wood and water and wire and wet stone.

And if you stand still for long enough on the bridge in the middle of the main gallery, you can feel—through the hum of the machines beneath your feet and the slow, breathing rhythm of its branches above—that the city itself is still alive, still settling, still becoming more exactly itself.
