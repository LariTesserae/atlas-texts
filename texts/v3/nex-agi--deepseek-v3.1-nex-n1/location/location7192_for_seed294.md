The elevator shaft is flooding.

Not water, though Klabbeth doesn’t know what else to call it. It isn’t rain and it isn’t ocean and it isn’t cloud. It is a sky-deep mass of clear fluid that falls and rises and curls sideways in the same breath, as if someone folded the air and filled the crease. The pressure of it hums along her bones. She can feel the weight of the world above them like a hand pressing down on her scalp.

The platform she stands on was not always an open-air shaft, but it is now. The roof of the world cracked open at some point; centuries ago, maybe millennia. The skyline that should be there above her is gone, replaced by this endless column of water-light. The edges of the buildings around the shaft have been softened by time and the flow, until their walls curve into the brightness like the lips of a bowl.

Cold seeps up from the metal under her feet. The temperature here never spikes—never plunges into true ice—but it stays bitter enough that her breath makes faint ghosts in the air before the wet world eats them. Her breath-steam doesn’t behave the way it should; it doesn’t just drift upward and vanish. It lingers, tracing slow loops in front of her face, as if the city itself is exhaling along with her.

She is very high up. That’s what the old records call this place, anyway: High-Up. Klabbeth doesn’t know if anyone else uses the name anymore. The nearest platform is visible two kilometers down and three kilometers across—a tiny flat shape suspended in the haze, like a toy disc someone has left floating on the surface of a pond. From the railings all around her, cables hang in slack coils and sudden, taut lines. They are everywhere, threading the emptiness, connecting platform to platform to pillar in a web so old its rust has been worn down to smooth, dark enamel.

There are no plants here. No moss, no algae clinging to the metal, no grasses pushing out of cracks. There never were. Sometimes the ancients grew their gardens suspended in magnetic tanks or glass-walled sacs, but those gardens are long gone. Now and then, a drifting patch of something like fine, floating ash clings to the railing or a cable: a desiccated remnant of one of those engineered ecosystems. It doesn’t even smell like rot anymore—just cold and metallic and faintly of ozone.

If the world had ever had natural soil, it is far below, under kilometers of water, drowned under the pressure of the sky-sea. This place is made entirely of what the hands of the builders could shape and fix and maintain. Every surface she can touch was forged by beings like her: deck plates, railings, cables, thick pillar-trunks that disappear upward and downward into the same dim, glowing depth. The light is everywhere and nowhere. It leaks from the water-mass overhead, from the structures themselves, and from the slow, luminous figures that sometimes move along the cables.

Those figures are the city’s inhabitants.

They call themselves the Roqqa. Klabbeth doesn’t know if that is their name for themselves or the name the ancients gave them. They are not like anything that grew on the planet’s distant surface, if there ever was one; they are shapes made of slow light and pressure, translucent and faintly iridescent, like a soap bubble stretched into a many-legged thing. Some of them are small, no longer than her forearm. Others are vast, spanning several meters, coiling around one pillar and then the next as if gravity is only a suggestion.

They are abundant, but they are not numerous. The density of the world is sparse—each platform with its own cluster of cables and its own pillar and its own Roqqa, separated by sheets of slow-moving water. The distances between are so great that most platforms look like pale stars strewn across a dim ocean.

The sound of the place is almost quiet.

Almost.

There is a constant low shushhhh, like the breath of something enormous asleep. It comes from the water-column and the cables and the pillars: all of it vibrating together in a murmur that never stops. Now and then, a platform shudders and lets out a groan of metal on metal, but the sound is so thickly dampened by the water that it feels like listening through a wall.

Then, less steady than everything else, there is the turbulence.

The shaft of air-water around the elevator platform is never still. It is pulled and pushed and spun into eddies that have no pattern. Underwater maelstroms and upside-down storms and cascades that flow sideways and backward and in circles. The water hums as it spirals, and in the spirals, things are made and unmade.

Klabbeth watches one of those births happen now.

A few meters away from the platform edge, a stream of water detaches from the main column and begins to rotate, faster and faster, until it is a tight, luminous helix. Within it, she can see shapes coagulating out of nothing—threads, plates, loops of material that are not exactly metal and not exactly glass. The helix tightens. The pressure of it sends a faint tremor through the platform, and Klabbeth’s teeth vibrate together. The air smells of static and brine.

The new thing hangs there for a moment: a knot of skeletal scaffolding, a delicate globe of interlocking rings, shimmering with wetness. Several small Roqqa drift toward it, their translucent limbs stroking through the water as if they are testing the temperature of a bath. One of them—its body marked with bands of turquoise like memory—pulls part of the structure away and wields it as a tool. Klabbeth doesn’t know what the tool is for. She doesn’t know if the Roqqa knows.

Even as she watches, another helix of water twists itself around the structure and wrenches it apart. The pieces dissolve into streamers and then into motes of light and then into the water’s own luminous haze. It’s as if the water itself is trying to build something and forgetting, trying and remembering mid-thought, erasing and starting over.

This is the world’s heart: constant turbulence. A chaotic birth of form and then its sudden, indifferent destruction. New cables and platforms and structural supports are thrown up by the water’s shifting energy, and old ones are worn away by the same relentless movement. Sometimes, the platforms are too crowded to traverse; sometimes, as they drift, they lose half of themselves to a passing vortex of debris.

The Roqqa are part of it. They are not tools of the turbulence; they are its mobile, sensing edge. They are the world’s abundant and restless fauna, and they navigate the storms of creation and erosion as effortlessly as fish once navigated currents in a forgotten ocean.

On Klabbeth’s platform, one of them glides near the railing where she stands. It is roughly the size of a human torso, its body composed of softly overlapping arcs of brightness. Its “limbs” are not limbs exactly, but tendrils of light that it extends like fingers. It brushes a tendril along the worn metal of the rail. The material under its touch blooms with faint color.

The Roqqa are not builders in the old human sense. They don’t fabricate from parts; they don’t assemble tool by tool. They are more like surface catalysts. When they move along the cables and platforms, they leave behind trails of shimmer in the metal—fine, branching lines that are not exactly corrosion and not exactly growth. These are the new structural veins of the system. Sometimes, after a Roqqa has passed along a cable for a few years, the cable’s properties subtly change. It becomes stronger in some places, more flexible in others. It might begin to emit a faint phosphorescence.

The tech of the ancients is gone. No hulking machines remain. No circuits. No panels of glowing buttons or humming generators. Everything that was made is made of structural matter that has survived its makers’ abandonment. The platforms, the pillars, the cables—these are the last artifacts of a tool-using civilization. But they are not dead. They are inhabited by a different kind of intelligence now.

The Roqqa are the tech layer.

Not in the sense of “advanced devices,” but in the sense of a layer that mediates between the world’s physical matter and its chaotic energy. They draw the paths of force. When they move along a cable that has been stressed by some turbulence, they leave behind a trace of themselves. That trace strengthens the cable where it needs to be strengthened and lets it bend where it needs to bend. The city’s structure evolves over decades, centuries, millennia—reshaping itself to match the water’s moods.

They are like biological trade routes, Klabbeth thinks, or like mycelia that have learned to live in steel and light.

In this strange, dense architecture, there are no straight lines. The pillars are curve-thickened, softened by age. The platforms are not perfect circles but asymmetrical petals whose edges have rolled up under centuries of aqueous flow. The cables themselves do not run in neat grids; they are guided by something else. They follow the flows of the water, as if the turbulence itself has taught them where to be.

Klabbeth’s reason for being here is simple and ancient: she is trying to reach the next platform down, where the old charts say there is something of the ancestors.

The maps—she has seen them in a handful of surviving archives that still have the power to show images—were not drawn for this world. They show sharp, right-angled cities with straight streets and skyscrapers that reach up into clear air. There is no water column in the maps. No Roqqa. No cables that dance between towers.

The maps are wrong now.

The world they depict has been submerged by this endless ocean-sky. The streets are gone. The open air is gone. The high places are still high, but they have been reconfigured into this chaotic network of platforms and pillars and cables and swirling water.

The map of the present is not drawn on paper or projected on glass. It is drawn in the behavior of the Roqqa and in the tremor of the cables. When a turbulence swell is coming, the Roqqa change their pattern of movement. They cluster along certain cables and avoid others. The platforms closest to the center of the rising storm emit faint waves of heat through the cold. The pressure changes the way her ears feel.

Navigating this is not like navigating a human-built transit system. It is more like navigating a storm.

Klabbeth steps back from the railing as the water-column around her platform shivers and then breaks into a series of small whirlpools. Each vortex gnaws at the edge of the metal, carving away microscopic flakes that glitter once before dissolving into the water’s brightness. The erosion here is deep and ancient and ongoing. There is nothing that will stop it. The city is not a stable thing; it is just a pattern that persists longer than others within the turbulence.

Far below, another platform spins slowly, as if attached by a hinge that has finally worn through. It doesn’t fall so much as drift, like a leaf riding down a river. Eventually, the water will take it apart, or the Roqqa will use it up.

The fauna here is not only the Roqqa. There are other things.

Klabbeth sees one of the smaller ones now—a creature like a bundle of wire-thin filaments, no longer than her finger, that moves along a cable like a hand of ghostly fingers. It is made of the same translucent pressure-light as the Roqqa, but it is simpler. Its shape is not stable. It flattens and elongates and then coils itself into a tight spiral. When the turbulence swells and a new helix of water tears into being nearby, the wire-things flee along the cables in a wave of silent motion.

They are not predators. They are not prey. They are—like the Roqqa—expressions of the world’s energy. They are the fauna that emerges in the interstices of architecture and water, the same way algae used to emerge in the cracks of pavement when sunlight and water met.

She doesn't know if they are aware of her, or of anything. They just exist.

Klabbeth checks the harness she’s attached between herself and the anchor cable. The fabric is old and brittle, one of the last of her people’s supplies. She doesn’t trust the technology in it—not because she fears it will fail, but because she fears it will succeed in a way that no longer matches the world’s rules. Better, she thinks, to rely on the knots her ancestors tied.

The wind in the air—no, not wind, but water-current—pulls at her hair as she leans out over the edge. The distant platform is still there, a faint, pale eye in the dim. A cable runs from the rail near her boot to somewhere near its horizon. It’s not a straight line; it’s been pulled by centuries of flows and storms. It droops and then rises and then curves into a spiral where it passes through a calmer section of the water column.

Turbulence is coming.

She can feel it in the way her ears throb. A pressure wave is moving through the water, from high up to somewhere below. It’s not visible to her eyes as anything more than a subtle shift in the brightness’s gradient. But the Roqqa know it is coming. They are drawing back from exposed cables and clustering near thicker pillars where they can wait out the storm.

She doesn’t have time for that.
