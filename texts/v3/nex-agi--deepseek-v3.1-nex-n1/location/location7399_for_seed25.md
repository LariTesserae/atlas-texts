The first sign you have of this place is not sight at all but sound: a deep, low, thrumming roar that presses into the bones of your chest and vibrates your teeth. It is not a single tone but a thousand rhythmic pulses overlapping, sliding in and out of phase with one another, forming a shifting chord of noise. Beneath the roar there is a wet, clicking chorus like the endless snapping of innumerable teeth, and above that a high, metallic shimmering, like glass being struck, again and again, by invisible hands. The air is thick with sound, almost solid with it—weighted.

You breathe it before you see it. The air is cool and damp and smells like iron and old stone, with a faint acrid edge like ozone or ammonia after a storm. It has no taste of plant or soil or leaf mold, only mineral and a faint sourness on the tongue. Each breath is slightly heavier than it should be, as if invisible water condensing from your lungs into a cold mist.

Gradually your eyes adjust.

The world is not pitch-black, but it is dark enough that your eyes struggle. There is no sky or sun or moon to orient by—only an uneven, low-slung ceiling of stone that stretches away in all directions until it disappears into a restless gloom. The space is open and vast, but it feels oppressively low, like an immense cathedral whose roof has settled down to within a few body-lengths of the floor. The darkness feels layered, the way smoke might layer over a city; you could almost believe the air is composed of thin, translucent veils, each slightly darker than the next.

The ceiling above you is not smooth stone but a kind of mineralized gridwork, a lacework of ribs and ridges, like the exposed spine of something ancient and enormous. The ribs are pale and glassy, coated with a faint crystalline sheen that throws back whatever light there is as a diffuse, ghostly glow. In places, water condenses along the ridges and drips into the void below; not in rain, but in slow, deliberate drops—each drop falling into darkness and disappearing.

Below you, the ground rises and falls in shallow waves and hollows. It is not soil or sand or forest floor but stone worn smooth by centuries of water—not aggressively carved but gently settled, as if the rock itself had long ago decided on this form and then refused to change. The topography feels old, in equilibrium with itself. There are no sharp edges, no brittle fractures. Instead, everything flows in low, undulating curves: low, rounded hills of stone, cupped basins like bowls waiting to be filled.

In the hollows, water has gathered—not in rivers or lakes, but in scattered, shallow pools that reflect the faint light from above like dark mirrors. Each pool is the size of a room or smaller, some of them no more than a wide puddle. Their surfaces are almost perfectly still except where a drop strikes from above and sets off expanding rings of faint, phosphorescent ripple.

The light does not come from the water itself but from the ceiling. The ribs of the ceiling are coated in a pale phosphorescence, a lingering afterglow of some long-extinguished event or energy source. It makes the stone above look like a network of faint veins traced into the darkness. The glow pulses, very slowly, as if the stone were breathing.

The world itself is strange, but the strangeness lies less in impossible shapes and more in the absence of what you expect to see. There is no vegetation. No trees, no shrubs, no moss or lichen, no roots creeping from stone, no leaves drifting on water. There is no wood at all, no bark, no litter of plant matter underfoot. The floors are smooth stone and water. The air is empty of pollen or dust; no winged seeds ride on the breeze because there is no breeze and no seeds.

The invisible life, however, is overwhelming.

You cannot see it clearly at first because your eyes are still trying—and failing—to find larger forms. But when you stop searching for animals and simply look at what the light reveals, you begin to register.

The ceiling crawls.

From ribs to ridge, every scrap of stone is encrusted with a carpet of moving bodies. They are not insects or birds or even bats, but something else: thin, segmented creatures whose bodies are almost translucent, like flexible shards of glass or chitin. Some are long and thin as a finger, others shorter and broader than your hand. They grasp the stone with rows of tiny hooks and legs, and they move in waves, like a tide of shards sliding across the ceiling. The metallic shimmering sound comes from their contact with the glassy stone—from countless limbs and bodies striking and dragging across crystallized surfaces.

When you look at the water you begin to understand, and the roar begins to resolve.

The pools are not empty. They seethe.

Each surface is broken by a constant motion of shapes rising and falling, breathing, breaking, and submerging. The creatures are too many and too close to count easily. They are not fish but something else—something that has chosen to live in the water but not to be shaped by it like fish. Their bodies are long and flexible, with thick segments of translucent shell. Their limbs are fins or flukes or flattened, jointed oars, depending on how you look at them. They roll in the water like lengths of bent glass, breathing in a rhythmic, undulating pattern: a long inward rush of current through narrow openings along their sides, then a sudden, forceful expulsion that pushes water out behind them like tiny jets.

The pool itself reveals the deception of scale. The water appears random only if you see it from above. But as you watch, you realize that each body is synchronized to thousands of others, in a delicate, staggered ripple. When one body exhales, its neighbor three segments away does the same, and then the next. The result is a coordinated wave of exhaled water that passes across the pool like a wind over water—and the sound is that wave passing through the entire chamber, echoing and rebounding from the stone until the whole space becomes one huge, resonant tube.

The roar you hear is not the roar of a storm or a waterfall. It is the breath of the water.

The stuttering clicks and pops you hear are not distant teeth but the near and constant collisions of shell on shell, as creatures in the water and ceiling jostle against one another. The pool itself is not a chaotic mass of individual bodies but a tight, concerting layer of overlapping life forms, each pressed close enough to the next that their shells touch. The water itself is almost black with their bodies—so dense you cannot see the bottom of even the shallowest pools.

The creatures themselves are strange. They have no obvious eyes. No mouths or jaws you can recognize, at least not in the sense you know. On their undersides, where a mouth might be, there are instead arrays of thin, glowing filaments, like iridescent threads that hang beneath them and drift in the water or in the humid air. These filaments gather faint debris from the water and air—not organic matter in the way you think of “food,” but something more like energy or condensation or faint chemical residues glinting with the faint ceiling light far overhead.

If you were to look closely at one individual creature, you would notice that its shell is not entirely opaque. Within its glassy segments, faint patterns of light and dark ripple slowly, like lazy smoke trapped inside ice. The patterns are not random but repeating—simple shapes folding over themselves again and again. It is not intelligence as you know it, but it is not entirely mindless either. These are bodies whose very form is a living memory of some earlier process, perhaps a long-extinguished eruption or a long-finished cycle of the world.

The air itself is thick and saturated. Not with foliage but with presence. The humidity hangs just this side of condensation, forming a faint film on exposed stone and your skin if you have any. It is the cool of damp underground rather than the cool of a winter night or mountain air. There is no wind to speak of—only the slow, massive pressure wave of the breath in the water and the occasional stirring when the creatures on the ceiling shift in a sudden ripple.

The ceiling is not the only surface. The low walls that curve out of sight and the low ridges of stone between pools are also covered with life. But here, instead of the moving glassy shards, the stone is coated with something that looks at first like frost or mold or a thin, pale lichen. But there is no plant in this world—and you only realize its nature when you see it move.

The coating is a thin, crystalline growth of something else—not alive in the way you recognize, but not entirely inert either. It is like a thin film of frozen fog or fungal material that has found itself halfway between living and mineralized. It does not breathe or grow, but it pulses and shifts subtle color with the faint light from above. Here and there, a faint discharge of energy sparks through the film where a drop of water has finally gathered enough weight to fall from a ceiling rib and strike it, sending a tiny flare of light along the crystalline surface. The sound of those drops is not the loud splash of liquid falling into water but the sharp crack of crystal on crystal.

The fauna is not limited to two kinds. There are others as well, though they are harder to see because they inhabit not the surface of the pools but the water’s edge and the stone at the water’s edge.

In the narrow margins where stone meets water, there are clusters of smaller, translucent forms that are not like either of the larger kinds you first saw. They are almost worm-shaped, but composed of dozens of transparent rings. Each ring holds a faint, glowing fluid inside. When they move, they extend like telescope cylinders or accordions, stretching along the stone until something large in the water above them exhales and the water surges, and they contract quickly inward again with a faint clicking sound like the closing of many tiny rings.

The world itself is not static. The processes of its life are not dramatic but they are active and layered. The ceiling creatures shift slowly, sliding in their endless carpet over the stone ribs, following the faint light as if grazing on invisible energy. The water creatures breathe in concert, creating not just a roar but a pressure pattern that ripples across the chamber. The thin film of crystal-mineral-fungus on the walls continues to spread and recede, very slowly, as if each day it were pushed by the faint energy of falling drops and then pushed back by the cool dampness of the air.

The erosion itself is long in balance with the space. The stone does not seem to be wearing quickly away. There are no high waterfalls plundering the rock in a violent rush, but instead low, gentle slopes where water ultimately condenses and pools and pools where it is kept by the low elevation of the ground. The air feels old, as if this place has been with this particular balance of dampness and dark and cool temperature and sound and life for a long time.

The temperature is not cold enough to freeze the water, but neither is it warm enough to feel comfortable. It is the cool of a cave cellar or a deep cellar before the dawn of the world’s day. There is no chill wind to accelerate you, but neither does the heat of your own body have much space to linger before it is stripped away into the damp stone and air.

The soundscape is not chaotic noise but a complex, layered pattern of frequencies and pulses that your brain keeps trying to interpret. It is not music or speech or even machinery, but something like all of those at once. There is a low bass rumble that is the base breathing of the water creatures; there is the higher, clicking chatter of limbs and bodies and rings of smaller creatures; and there is the faint, crystalline shimmering from the ceiling and walls where the crystal-film grows and responds to faint shocks of water and energy.

If you stand still enough long enough, you might notice that your breathing begins to slide unconsciously into the pattern of the water. The big roar is not every breath of every water creature at once. It is a wave of exhales that passes through the chamber like a pulse, and your own lungs begin to follow it, drawn out by the gravity of its sound.

The rules of this world are not laws you would understand like gravity or thermodynamics or even chemistry as you know it. But they are clear:

—Water condenses from the dark above and falls into the world below. It gathers not in rivers but in low, stable pools whose water is thick and full with other bodies rather than soil or sediment.

—There is no sun or external light. The only light is faint and phosphorescent and comes from the ceiling and the faint filaments within the water creatures themselves. It is not enough to see by in the full, bright sense you know from other worlds; it is only enough to suggest form and movement.

—There is no plant life. There is no photosynthesis or rooted growth or decay of leaves. There is no wood or bark or fiber or pollen or seed or root or sap. There is only stone and water and air and a thin film of something that is not quite plant and not quite mineral, and a vast web of fauna that has never needed to bend around the presence of leaves.

—The temperature is cool. It does not vary greatly. There are no seasons or days or nights. There may be cycles that are too long for you to perceive in a short span of time—slow changes in the energy of the ceiling, or tiny shifts in the erosion patterns—but they are not the cycles of sun and season.

—The elevation is low. The ceiling is low and the landforms are gentle and undulating. The water collects in depressions but does not rush away to distant valleys. It is held here by the surrounding shape of the world and by the low ceiling.

—The erosion is not violent but gentle and established. It has shaped the stone into long, smooth waves and niches that support the present density of life rather than threaten to change it.

—The scale is open but bounded. You cannot see the edges of the chamber from the center of the floor, but you can feel the boundaries of the ceiling above you and the low walls beyond the pools. The whole place feels like one region of a larger system, perhaps one of many similar chambers set together like a honeycomb or bone marrow.

—The density is almost overwhelming, not because of structures or crowds of people, but because of bodies—every surface of ceiling and wall and water and many edges of stone are covered and moving. The thin air feels thick with presence.

—There is no built or constructed structure. No walls or houses or paths or tools or technology of any kind. No rooves or roads or bridges. No signs or artifacts or ruins or machines or devices. No handprints or tools or clothes or instruments or cables or wires or fragments of any kind. There are only natural shapes and the bodies and creatures that have organized themselves into them.

—There is nothing you would recognize as technology or culture. There is no language, no writing, no fire, no metalwork, no weaving or pottery or drawing or song or dance or architecture or any of the things born of human or other tool-making minds. There is only the sound of the creatures themselves breathing and moving and clicking and shimmering, and the faint glow of their own internal forms.

The darkness feels strangely like a sheltering rather than a threat. It is cool and damp and heavy, but it is also thick with presence. The absence of plant life and the presence of overwhelming fauna have not created competition or destruction or chaos but rather an intricate, layered equilibrium of bodies and breath and sound.

The place does not change quickly; but it changes constantly behind its sameness mask.

The ceiling does not change shape but the creatures upon it are slowly, endlessly shifting.

The water level does not rise or fall noticeably but the water is always in motion, breathing.

The stone does not crack or crumble but it wears so slowly that you would never perceive it.

The sound does not fade or silence itself but it continues, forever.

The light does not brighten or go out but it persists faintly.

Time:

The place does not know time.

Time: it knows something else instead of you.
