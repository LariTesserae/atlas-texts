At the center of the Last Table of Deimos, the sanctuary of Nesh rises in a whisper of stone and silk and breath.

The world beyond the rim of the tableland is a long, flat night of stone and dust and meaningless horizon—the kind of place where rivers are myths and storms are preserved in the geometry of crystals. But up here, on the highest tier of an ancient, dish-shaped plateau, the air is warm and thick and alive with sound and motion and the deep, green weight of foliage.

---

### The Pavilion of Nesh

The whole plateau is a single table, perhaps a kilometer across at the widest, but the city itself—if you can call it that—is no more than a cathedral of living space. The urban fabric of Nesh is not a plan of streets and districts but a single, continuous pavilion that circles the inner edge of the table: a long, curved hall of stone and wood and growing things, its roof so low that the tallest adults must fall to a near-crouch if they walk the centerline.

From the outer rim, where the wind of the greater world bores thin and relentless against the pale retaining wall of the table’s lip, the pavilion looks like a long, low ring of darker stone beneath the canopy of Nesh’s one forest.

Step into the forest’s margin, and the world changes.

The air is warm and mildly humid, not because of water but because of the thick exhalations of Neesh moss—the deep, springy mat that lines the ground like a living carpet. Neesh feeds on the fine, dustlike rain of minerals that the wind carries from the world below the table; it is the city’s “river,” its source. The moss is a dark iridescent-green, soft and dense to the touch, and everywhere it is underlain by a thin lattice of living ironstone, which the Neshians call “the bones of the table.” This bone lattice is not random but fine and precise, like a fossil lace of ancient coral, or a long-crystallized memory of a world’s old fluid skin.

It is this lattice of ironstone that gives Nesh its shape. The pavilion is not built of cut blocks stacked on a flat plain; it is grown from the bone lattice of the plateau into an ordered city of niches and chambers and walkways, all rising within and upon the living stone itself.

The walls of the pavilion rise from the forest floor in gentle curves, like the ribs of a buried ship whose hull is the tableland itself. The ceilings are low, never more than twice or thrice a person’s height, and the rooms are shallow chambers carved into the stone, rather than houses seen on other worlds. They do not build “up” so much as “in” and “along.” The urban density of Nesh is not tall, but deep and layered and woven into the stone.

### Lush and Dry

The vegetation of Nesh is not the wet-lush of a swamp world, but the dry-lush of a sealed garden whose water cycle runs on blood and breath and the slow, sealed chemistry of the moss. There are no rivers here, no lakes, no visible wells. The only phase of water that appears in Nesh is the fine sheen of dew on the surfaces of certain leaf-shaped stone plates the Neshians call windleaves.

These windleaves are not leaves but long, flat-curves of stone that emerge from the bone lattice at the edges of the pavilion’s niches and along the inner rim of the tableland. They are thin and translucent at the tips, and they hum faintly in the wind, subtly channeling it inward and downward through hidden networks of galleries and roots. The wind of the world below the table is faint here at the summit, but Nesh has been shaped for millennia to catch and treat and refine every particle of motion that brushes over the plateau.

On the inner surfaces of the windleaves, when the night is deep and the stone of the table cools itself by the brutal emptiness of the world below, thin dew-skins of moisture bloom from condensation and exhalation of Neesh moss and fauna and people. This is enough—barely, with great care—to sustain the delicate forest canopy of the city: shallow, bowl-shaped trees of limestone and fibrous minerals whose “leaves” are half-plant, half-stone, and whose “bark” is a living matrix of town and bone.

### The Forest

The forest above is thick and layered and almost uncomfortably dense but not tall. Most trees are no higher than two or three people; their “canopies” are overlapping, shallow bowls of leaf-shaped stone plates, each lined with mosses and filaments of colored minerals, and each with a small, humming axis of bone lattice at the center.

Beneath these canopies, the pavilion is arranged in a series of interlocking rooms and halls and small niches, each shading into the next without sharp walls. The “buildings” of Nesh are not discrete houses but rather carved chambers of the bone lattice, each niche framed by a low stone rim that the Neshians call lintels. The lintels are not strictly necessary for structural support—the ironstone lattice is strong enough—but they mark the rhythms of social and sacred space.

Within each niche chamber, the moss floor is often carved into shallow basins, or raised into subtle platforms, depending on the era of its formation. The chambers are small and numerous, each holding enough space for a few people to sit or work or sleep together. The term “urban” here is not an exaggeration: the entire pavilion hosts considerable population density by leaning into the dimensions of the world’s height—which is already shallow—but with the internal depth of a long-churned inner city.

### Built and Dense

The interior of the pavilion feels at once primitive and strange: there are no metals shaped in intricate ways, no mechanical engines, no glowing screens. The Neshians are capable of simple metalwork—bronze and worked iron and copper—mostly in the form of small tools and decorations embedded into the bone lattice of their walls. But they are cautious of metal; they fear it will draw too much of the world’s sharp wind into their sealed garden.

So instead they build with what they are: they are their own infrastructure.

Volition-walking, the Neshians call it—a long, slow promenade of many people along the central hall of the pavilion, each carrying a chosen mass of moss or mineral or shaped ironstone, and laying their hands upon the cool walls to gently “reindex” the lattice, guiding the slow growth of the stone into whatever form they desire. Over the course of centuries, this has produced the urban pattern that now exists: layer upon layer of chambers and passages and niches and terraces, all of them curving gently along the inner ring of the table, with little “dead” space between them.

The population is not enormous by the standards of a wide-world city, but the available space is small, and the surface of interaction is dense. Many people, in a small area, with very little “void” between them. The pavilion is never crowded by rush, exactly—the pace of life is too slow for that—but it is always near-full with movement and touch and small gestures of work and play.

### Fauna and Light and Sound

In the dim light of Nesh—the plateau of Deimos lies under a canopy of night sky almost always, but starlight is sufficient for the stone-leaf trees—the fauna of the table city are more felt than seen clearly. They are not large; but they are abundant and diverse and everywhere.

Soft-skinned, small-scale invertebrates scuttle in the warm moss mats between the lintels of the chambers. They are not insects or mammals in the old-world sense; they are slow, many-limbed creatures with thin, mineralized shells at their base and padded, breathing limbs at their tips. They feed on Neesh moss and the dust carried from the world below, and in turn they leave trails of excrement and secretions and shed shells that reinforce the bone lattice and the “soil” of the city.

The birds of the table are not birds of air and sky but “air-fish,” slender and long-winged but a bit clumsy and loud, their voices not songs but low metallic croaks and chirrings and the occasional piercing tone of signal. They live in the shallow reaches of the forest canopy, where they pluck at moss and insects and small mineral nodules from the tree branches.

Everywhere there is sound: the low, windlike chorus of air passing through the windleaf lattice of the forest and the city walls; the constant murmuring of the Neshians themselves, who speak rarely in silence and always in a low communal hum of commentary and question and response; the soft crack of settling stone and the shifting of the bone lattice as the living city continues to creep into new shapes; the distant, far-off howling of the wind that the table of Deimos has never succeeded in fully quieting.

It is a loud world, though not chaotic in its noise. The sounds are layered and in many registers, but they are long-patterned and slow-changing. The sound of Nesh is not violent noise but a ceaseless, low-level orchestration of the city’s deep life.

### Time and Erosion

The city of Nesh is not just “old” in the sense of having been inhabited for a long time; it is, in many ways, the last of itself, and by now it has already settled into the forms that it will keep until its end.

The world of clouds below the table has been dead for many eras of history; there is no longer anything to erode the plateau of Deimos in the sense of tectonic novelty or storm-driven violence. The edges of the table are not jagged but smooth and rounded with long, slow wind-wear and the gentle, uncomplaining decay of mineral matter over time. The bone lattice of the city itself is not fresh but finely polished by the passage of hands and feet and backs and by the long-term, slow motion of waterless dew and warm air and the breathing of many lifeforms.

Erosion here is a near-steady state of gradual polishing. The bone lattice loses trivial matter on the surface of passages and lintels only to be redeposited in the carpet of moss and in the rootlets of the stone-leaf trees. The “walls” of the city chambers are not new edifices but slowly migrating patterns of crystal and moss and animal and air and people; the chambers of Nesh have been rearranging themselves for several thousand years of continuous history.

And yet the change is slow enough to be invisible on the timescale of a single generation. The adults of Nesh know the shape of the city by memory and can tell when the lintel of a chamber has been smoothed by a few millimeters more than they remember as children, but they do not expect to witness the growth of entirely new chambers or the collapse of old sectors in their lifetimes.

### Warm and Slow and Normal

The temperature of the plateau city is always within a narrow band of warm stability—not so hot as to deeply discomfort, but warm enough to keep the Neesh moss and the fauna in a state of constant low metabolism. The subtle chemistry of Nesh requires this warmth; the dew-skins and the thin films of moisture that drift on the surfaces of the windleaf stones are affected by even slight shifts in temperature.

Despite the strangeness implicit in the details of this world, the lived experience of Nesh is not exaggeratedly “weird.” The Neshians are not transparent beings or crystalline minds or shapes of pure energy. They are humanoid—two-legged, standing upright when they are in the open, but more often moving on all fours or seated on the moss within the chambers—and they have hands and eyes and voices and relational rhythms that visitors from other worlds would find understandable and even “normal.”

Their bodies are adapted to this world—they can breathe the thin-air of the table and subsist on the dew and the minerals of the moss and the fauna of the forest, but their adaptation is not cartoonish or bizarre. It is simply a long, slow lifestyle of careful nutrition and deep respect for the sealed garden in which they live.

### The Open Center of the Table

Within the ring of the pavilion and forest, the vast center of the table of Deimos is not built up but left deliberately open to the sky.

This central area is not a “park” in the sense of a planned amenity but a holdover from the earliest functioning of the table plateau as a whole—when the world below was still being shaped by the last, great age of geologic change. The moss of the center is not trimmed to the level of the urban forest; it is allowed to grow into taller and more complex mats of mineral and moss and occasional stone-leaf trees that are not shaped by the volition-walk of the people but left to the wind and the movement of air and animals.

In the center of the table, at the very middle of this plateau on the edge of the world’s sky, there is a single, low-walled structure of the bone lattice—a circular chamber of the same composition as the pavilion but smaller and more deeply folded into itself. The Neshians call it the “memory of the table,” and it is not a place where they store records or artifacts but rather where they go to listen to the faint, long note of stone and growth and wind and the world’s last history.

### A Final Form of Place

Taken in its whole, the plateau of Deimos and the city of Nesh are not spaces of dramatic change or future-construction. They are not places of revolution or of new beginning but rather of final settling and of long-term maintenance of the last possible shape of a world.

The “urban” character of Nesh is not an expression of growth or expansion but of the close, layered, many-presence-per-volume kind of settlement that emerges when there is no more frontier to move into, and when the only way forward is to interlace lives more deeply with each other and with the stone and the forest and the bone lattice and the air of the table. They do not build “out” or “up” but “through” and “along,” and at the end of all that effort, the city’s shape is the same long, low curve of chambers and niches and chambers and niches and chambers and niches and…

Climb back to the rim of the table and look down upon the world of the world below, and the city of Nesh is a small, dark, ring-shaped forest upon a high plateau in the night sky. It does not glitter with the lights of technology or shine with the reflected glare of water; but in the starlight and in the faint glow of the world below’s thin horizon, you can see the long curve of the pavilion like a hoop of slightly darker stone and the slow, almost imperceptible motion of people and animals and the slow shifting of the stone-leaf trees beneath the low sky.
