Far below the snowline, in the root-dark of an old mountain range, the machine works.

You know it is there before you see it—by sound, and by the slow, cold wash of air that carries that sound, pushing down the tunnel ahead like something liquid and heavy. The corridor is a low, vaulted passage braced with ribs of blackened steel and the trunks of trees that gave up growing long before the machines were a dream; in places, resinous wood has been replaced by plates of iron, and in others, the roots have been left to lace themselves through the stone. The floor is uneven, polished in faint crescents where generations of boots have taken the same half-step, but the edges are still sharp, where the drill first tore its way into the mountain and then out of use.

The walls are wet with a slow, cold slickness that doesn't quite form true droplets. It is not a rain, but a condensation that has its own patient schedule: a thin sheen of mineral water, beading on the ribs of the tunnel and soaking into the moss that has taken those ribs as its world. The moss is everywhere, in a sense, but still sparse—crystals of dark green and iron-gold pressed into the seams of the rock, a thin fur of lichen on the supports, fragile traceries of white algae floating where seeping water has hollowed tiny pools into the stone. The vegetation is not ornamental; it has been recruited into the structure of the place itself. The builders never painted the walls; the moss did that for them, slowly and quietly.

And then, between the stations of the supports, the passages are bare. The great machine is not planted here to be beautiful; it is here to be deep. The scale of the place is not the intimate closeness of a cellar or a narrow mine shaft, but something larger and more open—a long succession of vaults linked by necklike corridors, a main gallery that could be crossed by a small train of wagons, if wagons were still being used. Instead, the rails are buried under a thin, cool layer of dust and the occasional carpet of low moss. The system of tracks and switch points and holding bays suggests that once, before the mosses and algae, before the condensation had time to learn the shape of the tunnel, this place was full of movement and intent.

The temperature never rises to what you could call warmth. The air holds at a cold just above freezing, where the breath in your lungs is a visible thing and the metal of the supports aches to the touch. The sound of the machine sits at the edge of hearing at first, then deepens as you move forward: a low, grinding tremor of gears turning somewhere else, in shafts of stone and steel that you never learn to see directly, but whose presence you can feel in the floor. You move into it, and the roar of the machine builds itself in layers.

The nearest layer is a steady mechanical rumble that never speeds up or slows down appreciably. It seems like the background heartbeat of the mountain, but it is not natural; underneath the rumble is a higher, metallic ringing of chains on drums, of something rotating against a sleepless resistance. The tunnel amplifies every sound, turning it into a long, continuous note of pressure and strain and metallic grinding, until your own thoughts are pushed down to the corners of your mind.

Beyond the constant deep rumble are the sounds that turn and fade and come back: a distant, rhythmic hammer of hydraulics, like a heart beating inside a wall of iron; the sudden release of compressed air in a hiss that makes the moss shiver; and, at the very edge of sensation, the low, resonant boom of a massive valve opening somewhere deeper in the system, where the water pressure is a factor and the cold metal has been taught to obey the laws of pressure and time.

The moisture is everywhere but fleeting. It appears in unexpected places: a thin line of water that follows the seam between two plates of wall, trailing along and then stopping, as though the cavern has taken only the necessary sip of a river that is flowing elsewhere; in the shallow depressions of the cross ties where the rust has been worn away by the tires of carts that no longer come; in the occasional film of water that collects on a horizontal ledge of rock and trembles with every bass note of the machine's motors. There is nothing here that could be called a river, but something like an underground river’s memory—small offcuts of a larger wetness stealing into the machine spaces.

The vegetation, in its total, ancient patience, has been incorporated into the world in a way that makes it architecture as well as ecology. The supports of the main tunnel are half steel and half tree, and where the steel has corroded, the old beams have been replaced by new growths of wood from the tree roots that have been slowly trained along the same line. The work of centuries has been to make these supports grow into the shape the engineers need: straight trunks rising into the vault as if columns in a cathedral, the bark of the tree interlocked with the boltheads of the plates and struts of iron.

In the narrower side galleries, where the light is less evenly distributed, the situation is more extreme: small side chambers that are little more than pockets of the main machine space are defined by the walls of a single old tree, whose trunk has been split and shaped and then allowed to close over the years, forming a room of living wood. The door of such a chamber is simply a place where the tree’s growth was interrupted to allow a gap, and when the door of iron and glass that once sealed it finally rusted away, the tree itself supplied the barrier of fresh growth and a thin bark of moss that seals the room from drafts.

The ceiling of the main gallery is not bare rock, but a lattice of thick branches and fibrous root matter that has been trained into a canopy over time. The roots of huge trees above the tunnel system have been coaxed down into the space, where they are woven into a mesh that supports the soil and the immense machine suspended from it. In the old, damp places of the world, this may be the only garden where the tree and the machine have been married into the same substrate.

Under the canopy of roots and above the floor, the main machine hangs from its own web of supports, like a suspended factory of strange and gentle purpose that is no longer quite clearly industrial. The main drum of the machine is a vast cylinder of iron and copper, thick with the corrosion that time has allowed, but still turning under the influence of hidden sources of power and the water that is pumped from deeper in the mountain, where natural springs have been given into custody of the system.

The machine of this hidden chamber is not elaborate in its parts. It has a central drum, which turns slowly and without end, and from which wide chains of articulated links curve down into the rock floor, where they are drawn into well shafts that are deeper than the eye can see. The chains bring up nothing; whatever is being taken from the mountain’s root is quiet and invisible and does not appear to change the air of the chamber. The movement of the machine is not a show—it is a process, and the process itself is almost the only point. The technology of the place is industrial, but no longer clearly industrial in the sense of factories producing for people; it is a scaled, mechanized garden of growth and pressure and water.

The lights are few and scattered, and they are only barely enough to give form to the space. The glass of fixtures is thick with dust and the condensation that has years of processes to go through before it will be washed away by any cleaning; the bulbs of the industrial lamps are dimmed, but still glass, and in their encrusted glow you can make out the weight of the machine, the slow advance of the moss, and the occasional glint of metal in the supports. The light of any one lamp does not travel far; between the lamps there are zones of shadow that nevertheless belong to the machine’s world, places where the moss and the humidity and the sound of the gears continue the work of being.

Animals do not belong here in the way of forests and fields above, but they are here in small degrees and at long intervals. Infrequent is the small, pale rodent that will venture from some hidden crack in the wall to run the edge of the railway track where the thin dust gives the only texture of soil to be found. The bird in the under-chambers of the system is a rare and hollow event; a stray of some species that has flown down the wrong passage, and now finds itself in a world where its wings are too large and its world of sound too small to be able to explain the place of the machine by the rules of its sky.

The human presence is scattered in abandoned and forgotten structures that are themselves only half of the story. The built of this place is not a city, but a series of small installations that have been left to interact with the greater and quieter grammar of the tree-mountain and its machine. The old control station is a small room built of brick and iron studs that juts into the main gallery like a misplaced closet of some ancient house; the door of iron has lost half of its panel, and the inside is filled with the parchment of old diagrams and the wooden ribs of a desk where the finish of the varnish has been taken away by time and the humidity.

In the corner of the control room, a small stove, once of iron and now only of the rust that has eaten into it, suggests the faint, abandoned idea of warmth. The wiring of the switchboard and the panels of the gauges and the wooden of the chair are all covered by the same thin layer of dust that has been accommodating the slow centuries of turning of the machine without needing to change.

Other artifacts are scattered and more functional: a small, freestanding workbench of steel and wood, half of which has been taken for other purposes; a single, heavy-duty trolley on rails of steel that has been left to silence at the end of a spur track; and, in one of the tree-shaped rooms, a tiny bed of stone and straw, as if someone at some point decided to sleep in the world of the machine and never moved to the surface as more than an occasional thought.

The structure of the world as this place is built is not of the dramatic and the strange; the rules here are physical, and geological, and industrial. The machine of the mountain is not here to break the laws of nature; it is here to use them very hard and continuously for some long-duration purpose that has been forgotten by much of the world above but not by the machine itself. Water is pumped from the springs and the underground riverlets of the mountain; in this process it is used to move the mechanics of the drum and the chains of the system; and when the water has been used, it is released back into the rock along the small condensation channels that have been taught to the stone by time and pressure.

The temperature of the place is held by the same processes of water and pressure and the machine’s own work to the cold of the mountain’s interior, with the thin variations of some days of the year, when the outside air of the world above the mountain is slightly warmer or slightly colder, and the change flows down the shafts and into the chamber as a faint breeze. The erosion of the stone is not aggressive; the edges of the tunnel where the drill first made its way are still defined by the sharpness of the machine’s cutting and the relative youth of the system on the scale of geological time, but the water and the particles of rust and dust and the moss and the animals of the small kind have been working at them in a way that suggests centuries of slow, gradual change are still in the middle of their sentence.

The sound of the environment is not the sound of nature, but nature of the machine and the mountain joined together. The continous grinding of the gears and the rotating of the drum and the movement of the chains in the rock of the floor, and the water being pushed and released and forced into its new paths of erosion and corrosion, are all part of this same deafening, but dimly lit, environment of the underground.

The change in the place is slow and not obvious to the eye of the visitor who will stay only minutes in the machine of the mountain. The time of the tree’s growth of the supports into the new of course of the iron and the stone, the time of the moss’s advance of a new generation of itself into a new seam of the wall, and the time of the erosion of the rust of the old supports into the dust of the floor, are all of the same long and patient and continuous movement of processes and cycles.

In the largest of the tree-chambers, where the trunk of the ancient tree permits the carving of a small observatory within the machine of the mountain, the air of the world is still damp and the dimensions of the space are still open and the machine of the drum is still turning, but the tree itself has a more direct presence. The walls of the room are of living wood, and in the cut of the window of the room of the tree, the tree of the world has been allowed to see the machine of its world, and to be part of the same system of water and pressure and sound and light.

At the same time, the world of the tree of the mountain and the machine of the mountain is not crowded with the factories of the world of the surface of the earth; in the world beneath the mountain of the tree is the sparsity of the supports and the rails of the tracks and the lamps of the light and the trees of the support and the rocks of the wall, and in the long of the space of the gallery, the mind of the visitor is able to move between the elements of the world without the problem of the noise of the confounding of the world.

The machine of the mountain of the tree of the world under the world of the surface continues to work, in the cold of the rock of the mountain of the world of the tree and the water of the world of the under of the world of the mountain of the tree and the vegetation of the world of the tree of the machine of the world of the mountain of the tree of the world.
