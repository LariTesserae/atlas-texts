The world is a low basin cupped between the knuckles of bluestone ridges, the water’s faint gleam showing in every gap between the buildings, like the city has been pushed into a shallow pool and never lifted out. The lagoon is small—you can stand near the edge of the district and see the other side of it, a kilometer of chimneys and low domes and terraced rooftops rising above a wet tangle of metalwork piers. The houses are carved from the same stone as the hillsides, and the same water that seeps from the cliffs has been staining the walls for centuries, so every building is a vertical marsh of moss-green, rust-orange, and dull blue, where mineral streaks rivulet down in slow rainbows that never end. The air is heavy and hot enough to press the droplets in your hair back onto your neck before you’ve taken five steps from the harbor.

If you look at the city by itself, it seems like every inch of earth is built over, but from the low slope above the basin you can see gaps between the districts, where the ground sinks into the true lagoon. The islands are small and irregular, the canals that separate them narrow and dark. The city is not dense in the usual way; people are occupied by the water between the buildings, not the structures themselves. The “streets” are almost all bridges of stone or glassy brick, or low suspended lattices of corroded ironwork that you cross by bouncing your weight in the right way. The spaces between the districts are empty of anything solid—just the dark, thick mirror of the lagoon, and then the quiet, vertical architecture of reeds and aquatic plants that have taken root on the pilings below. You can see them even from the rim of the basin, a ring of dark green trapped halfway between the surface of the water and the bottom of the city.

When you climb the few steps down to the first of the floating platforms that mark the entrance of the district, you notice the sound of rain everywhere. You look up, but the sky is only a low, flat ceiling of storm cloud, the kind of dense grey that never seems to move. The rain doesn’t come from the clouds. It emerges from the city. The air is just thick enough to condense on the metalwork spines of the buildings: the gutters that are also chimneys, the stone pillars that have been ribbed with metal fins to draw the fog out of the lagoon and push it upward. The fog accumulates around the mechanical roofs of the workshops, and as it cools the roofs begin to drip. The drips fall into metal channels that are tilted to cast the rainwater down and sideways, cascading from terrace to terrace until it reaches a lower roof where it can be gathered again or simply allowed to fall back into the lagoon.

The result is a constant clatter of water striking metal, stone, and depressed glass all over the district. It is not a storm, but it is not soft rain either. It is an industrial rain of small, precise volumes of water falling on exactly those surfaces that have been designed to catch and amplify the sound. The same metalwork that condenses the mist also generates the noise of the city: rhythmic dripping from the cup of one gutter into the next, the rush of a thin waterrace down a vertical pipe, the low boom of a larger cistern somewhere being filled by a sudden torrent of runoff. The irregular patterns of the dripping are almost like a machine code. You can’t understand it, but you feel the logic of it somewhere in the back of your teeth.

Because the city is small and the basin is low, the architecture of the district is focused on working with the water rather than walling it out. The buildings are sliced from the same escarpment of cavernous rock that forms the basin’s rim, but they’ve been carved through the inside and rotated onto the lagoon, so far more of the stonework is actually underwater than is visible above the surface. The walls above the waterline are thick and hollow, with long galleries and tunnels that you can’t see from the street. Underwater, they split into networks of shafts and chambers that the builders have filled with machines of globed and jointed ceramic and glass, the shells of ancient engines that have since been replaced with more silent versions but still partially remain.

The erosion is the reason the city looks like it has been here forever, and yet it’s not the ocean that has worn the stone into the strange shapes you see now. It’s the water from the sky itself. The people of the district have been directing the rainwater for so long that the surfaces of the buildings have been shaped by it as if they were riverbeds. The soft bluish stone of the walls is carved into smooth grooves and cup-like basins where the diverted drops have been taught to form regular patterns that they repeat hour after hour. The longer the rain has been trained on a particular place, the more intricate the rockwork becomes. Some walls are completely covered in shallow spirals or branching patterns like the veins of leaves, so that the rain cascades down them in curtains of falling thread that never seem to break.

The temperature makes the water’s presence everywhere in the air. On the plateau above the basin you might be able to forget the lagoon for a moment, but here you can’t take a breath without the knowledge of it entering your lungs. The air is thick, and it smells of wet stone and freshly oiled metalwork. The humidity of the lagoon condenses on any surface that is even slightly colder. The ironwork stairs of the platforms are always slick with droplets of condensation that form, then roll down the railings and drip into the water below. When you set your hand on the stone wall of a building, you feel the film of moisture that has already soaked into the surface, and if you leave your palm there for a minute, the water under your skin will warm the patch of stone and you will see a faint glow of heat where you had been touching the rock.

The vegetation manages to be both lush and sparse at the same time. The streets are nothing like the crowded, jungle-covered boulevards of a typical rainforest city; the trees and large plants are confined mainly to the thin edges of the districts where the buildings plant their feet into the rock of the basin. The plants are mostly the slow, deliberate forms of the deep basin: low stone-mosses that have been woven into the limestone of the walls, the crowns of thin reeds that drink the fog from the lagoon’s surface, the occasional fan of tall, pale ferns that have found their way into a shaded courtyard where they can stand in the dampness without the full heat of the sun. Wherever there are gaps of exposed soil, the mosses have been allowed to grow over or around the buildings, so the walls appear to be the same texture as the ground on which they stand.

The industrial aspect of the district comes not from the sky of smoke and roaring engines of the usual factory city, but from the constant ritual of the waterworks. The engines here are small and specialized, and they are built to expose the water of the lagoon to the heat of still and subtle fires rather than to boil it with force. The factories are half workshops of hand-worked metal and glass and half enclosed chambers where the water of the lagoon passes slowly through ceramic filters that separate the minerals from the moisture. The separated minerals are then worked into beads of glazed ceramic or black glass, which are used as the building’s primary “product” and trade good with the plateau towns above.

The engines of the district are not driven by the same thermodynamic frenzy you might find in a valley of coal mines and blast furnaces. Instead they are powered by the difference in temperature between the water of the lagoon and the heat of the condensed cloud above the basin, which the city pulls down and concentrates in the chimneys. The waterworks are the most elaborate parts of the buildings, and from the district you can see them as the thin vertical ribs of metalwork that are always the brightest part of the skyline. They branch out of the stone like the skeletons of giant fish, and when they catch the mirrored surface of the lagoon at the right angle, they look like they are floating above the city in the fog.

The sound is the most unsettling thing about the place. You expect the roar of rain, or the distant hum of machinery, but you don’t expect the sound to be so precise and so clearly “engineered.” You are not hearing the noise of a storm so much as the echo of an entire factory of pipes that have been tuned to each other. The different rhythms of dripping from the roofs overlap in the way of a machine leaving the impression of pattern on your mind even when you know you can’t predict the next drop. The metal of the cisterns and the channels has been chosen to ring at particular frequencies when wet, and over the drone of the rain you can begin to distinguish the metallic ringing of one particular channel of water from the softer splash of another.

The people of the district use the sound of the rain as a regulatory system as much as they use the runoff of the water. The acoustic logic of the city was designed, in the same way that the layout of a machine’s internal gears might be designed to work together in a particular pattern. The eavesdrop roof of one workshop is designed to catch the rain and pour it onto the metal of the next workshop in a sequence that tells the workers inside the rate of condensation of the lagoon. If the sound of the rain on the metalwork changes to a higher, more dissonant pitch, they know that some of the channels have been blocked by the silt from the lagoon and they need to go out onto the waterworks to adjust them.

The absence of animal life is strange, because the city feels like it should be full of it. The moss on the walls is thick enough to provide a home for insects, and the lagoon is full of the slow processes of plant and microbial life that usually draw the birds of a wetland; the air should be filled with their calls and the shadows of their wings. But there is nothing. The only sounds you hear are the water and the metalwork, and the only moving forms you see are the shadows of the clouds themselves and the occasional slow motion of a plant frond turning toward the faint light of the sky. You are aware of the absence as a kind of faint pressure on the atmosphere of the district, like the silence in the middle of a storm when you never quite hear the thunder you expected.

The people of the district have not eliminated the animals of the lagoon out of some kind of fear of them, but because they accepted the contrast of a world without small animals into the design of their environment as a kind of structural constraint, the way you might choose to build with a single material to limit the complexity of the structure. Over the centuries of work the city has been “weeded” of the living forms that would most complicate the waterworks, and the remaining community of moss and bacteria and plant matter has been integrated into the machine of the district itself. The mosses act as filters and the plants as the living tissue of the walls, drawing the water of the lagoon upward and keeping the stone of the buildings from collapsing into the channels of the runoff.

The process of erosion is the primary movement of the place. The city is not young enough to be still settling, and it is too old to be in the first stages of decay. The landforms of the basin are stable enough to support the long, slow process of the water sculpting new stone, and the buildings have been in the same configuration for so long that the rain of the district has been carving the walls of the same terraces for the lifetime of several generations of the inhabitants. The erosion doesn’t show itself as a sudden threat of collapse or crumbling; instead you see the long-term effects of centuries of condensation on the surfaces of the stonework, where the edges of the carvings have been softly rounded by the constant passage of water droplets along the channels of the same path.

The lighting of the district is the first of many things about the place that is “normalized” rather than truly dim. The sky is the uniform flat of a storm that never breaks, and the reflected lagoon fills the streets of the city below the canopy of clouds with a faint greenish gloom that never quite darkens to night and never brightens to day. The shadows of the buildings are almost always cast horizontally along the water of the canals by the low angle of the sun through the clouds, and they are long enough that you can see the entire shape of a district’s skyline stretched out on the water of the lagoon as you cross the bridge from one district to the next.

The buildings of the district have been designed to respond to that particular kind of dimness. The roofs of the workshops are not topped with chimneys of smoke so much as with the glass of skylights that have been frosted and narrowed to filter the light of the sky into the interior of the buildings without the sharpness of direct sun. The people of the district have been living in the medium of the same filtered light for centuries, and their eyes have adjusted to the slightly shadowed reading of stonework surfaces that you would struggle to see clearly at the same distance.

The “weirdness” of the district is not the kind of obvious strange that shows up in the images of a city built of impossible geometry or flying cars of glass. The strangeness is the way the world itself has been folded into a machine that you can only see the edges of. The water of the lagoon, the heat of the air, the erosion of the stone, the condensation of the mist, the growth of the mosses on the walls—all of these are the working parts of the same system of the city, and they are not the “background” of the place but the central mechanism of it. The city is not built on the lagoon; the lagoon is built through the city.

The buildings of the district are not placed in a way that makes sense from the perspective of land-based architecture. You cross the first of the bridges into the heart of the district and you notice the way you are gently turned away from certain streets not by a sign or wall, but by the slope of the ground and the angle of the runoff channels that have been carved into the stone to discourage the water of the lagoon from reaching certain areas. You stand on the edge of the district and you see the water of the lagoon lapping at the stonework of the same terrace you are standing on, but you do not feel the fear of the sea; the water of the lagoon is not trying to swallow the city. It is being drawn into it.

The process is the place, and you are watching the world of the district being the machinery of the world at the same time. The sound of the rain on the metalwork, the sound of the runoff in the stone channels, the sound of the condensate of the lagoon dripping into the top of the same cistern that the workshops of the district have been draining from the bottom for the last century—all of these are the same continuous operation of the same machine of the city.

The basin of the district is not large. If you climb the narrow stone stairs of the cliff that you came down when you entered the city, you can look back and see the entire lagoon of the district filling the bowl of the stone, the metal of the waterworks ribs rising above the rooftops like the ribs of a mechanical creature that has submerged itself in the water of the world and only left its skeleton above the surface. The city of the district is not huge in the way of the metropolis of the distant plateau, but it is the entire world of the lagoon.

When you finally step back from the edge of the water and you climb the stone of the basin up the last of the stairs to the rim of the world above the city of the district, you remember the ceiling of the cloud that you had been walking under for the last of the journey down the terraces of the cliff. The world of the district of the lagoon is the world of the machine that the people of the city have been building of the same material of the world of the lagoon of the district.

The world of the district of the lagoon is the world of the machine that the people of the city have been building of the same water of the world.
