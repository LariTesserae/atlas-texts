The air is a thick black velvet, saturated.

You breathe it and it feels like breathing the underside of water: heavy, humid, pressing in on your lungs, but it doesn’t drown. It’s warm. Not sweltering—just enough to keep your skin always a little damp, the way a forest floor stays warm after the rain. The warmth hangs so evenly that you can’t tell if the heat is coming from above or below; you only feel it everywhere at once.

There is no sun.

There is no sky, only a ceiling of plant matter that you can’t see but you know is there—because you move through a world made of interwoven stem and vine and trunk and leaf and root and flower and seed and bark and fiber and living wood and living pulp, all growing through and around and inside and over each other until the distinction between ground and air becomes meaningless.

---

### Ground and Water

You walk on a soft surface that gives just a little under each footstep.

The ground is not dirt or rock or sand. It’s a mat of roots and rhizomes, tightly knit into a living mattress. The deeper layers between your feet and the bedrock below are a tangle of fine white filaments, thick rope-like roots, and decaying plant matter constantly being broken down and replaced by new growth. The oldest layers are already compressed into something half-peat, half-fibre; but “young” means this process has only just begun. The root system has not yet fully consolidated its strata, so the ground is uneven in places, laced with hidden pockets where old roots rotted out faster than new ones could colonize.

Everywhere, water is near the surface.

The air is wet enough that you feel condensation on your cheeks and lips. Beads of moisture gather slowly on the surfaces of your gear—not as dripping rain, but as a faint, stubborn sweat born from the air itself. When you step down, your boot soles sink into the root-mat until they feel a faint resistance: the water table. You’re not in deep water; you’re at the edge of it, where the ground is saturated but not quite submerged. The soil beneath your feet is a living sponge.

Small depressions in the root-mat collect shallow pools of black water. No waves, no ripples—only the smooth, lens-like surface reflecting a faint sheen from whatever unseen light source clings to the world’s distant edge. The water is utterly still. It’s not stagnant in the sense of filth; it feels like a distilled, condensed essence of the plant world around it—a faintly oily, translucent resin-water.

In places, the water table rises up and through the root-mass, creating rounded hollows: shallow basins the size of your hand, or your torso, or even bigger than a room, where smooth water lies like dark glass. The plant material around these basins glows faintly at the edges—not with bright luminescence, but with a low, ambient self-emission of light, just enough to outline shapes and nothing else, leaving most of the world wrapped in a deep, saturated dim.

---

### The Vegetation That Is Everything

There is no clear “forest” here.

There is a plant-system.

Everywhere you look—or what you can see in the gloom—is living plant matter. It’s not just that trees grow densely: you’re inside a single colossal organism, or perhaps countless organisms that have long since merged into one continuous living tissue. The environment is a single architecture made of cellulose.

Above you, the stems of half-visible trees rise into the darkness, their heights lost beyond perception. Their trunks are not truly trunks as you understand them. They’re more like columns, or pillars of growth, fused into neighboring trunks until the whole canopy becomes a ceiling ten, or twenty, or thirty meters above—impossible to tell. The bark of these pillars is smooth in some places, ridged in others, like softly sculpted bone. It’s not brown; it’s a deep, dimly absorptive grey-green that you think might have faint iridescence when you’re not looking at it directly.

From the trunks and columns and logs and fallen stems, countless forms of vegetation branch out, filling every space that isn’t already occupied:

- Broad leaves, thick and succulent, dripping condensation with faint ticking sounds when the droplets finally slide free.
- Lighter ferns and moss-like growths that spill down the columns like living hair, covering the surfaces in a soft velvet.
- Stiff, woody vines that serve as ladders or scaffolds, threading through the air horizontally.
- Epiphytic gardens of strange plants that grow from nowhere, their roots holding in tiny air pockets where soil and moss have collected in the crevices of the greater architecture.

The entire system is a kind of eternal plant. This is total vegetation: not a “forest” ecosystem but a plant-ecosystem, where plants are substrate and medium and organism and atmosphere all at once. There is no bare ground, no exposed stone—only age layers of growth that have themselves become the ground.

In the perpetual darkness, the plants have solved their need for light in two ways.

First, there is a subtle, unmoving source in the distance—too faint for you to pinpoint. It may be the edge of the system, where some unknown sky or surface or boundary leaks in a dull trace of radiation. Or it may be a locus of photosynthesis that rises, somewhere else, on a scale you can’t comprehend.

Second, the plants here glow. They glow faintly and relentlessly. Not enough to brighten anything—only enough to outline each other, to give shape and depth and curvature to their world. You stand in a sea of silhouettes and delicate edges that are self-illuminated at the lowest threshold of visibility.

---

### Scale with No Sky

The scale is vast.

You cannot feel the boundaries of this place. You move forward, and the plant architecture around you shifts, but it doesn’t end. The pillars stretch into the darkness like columns in a cathedral whose ceiling and far walls are erased. The distances between supports—tens of meters, perhaps—are faint black channels between living trunks, and beyond those, more channels.

You cannot see wind or movement. You cannot see weather.

And yet, you know there is weather of a kind here.

The air has its own climate—faint, invisible, but persistent. Tiny invisible currents steer the condensation from your skin to your sleeve, or from the surface of a leaf to its neighbor. The heat of your body is only a small disturbance in a warm, stable ocean of air. The system’s thermal gradients are so weak that the air above you may be the same temperature as the air at your shins. The result is that nothing else seems to move.

In the distance, invisible pressures might generate plant-weather: silent shifts of humidity and temperature that make the vegetation breathe differently. You feel, on some edge of your awareness, a faint suggestion of great volumes of air moving on a scale you cannot perceive—changes of state that take decades or centuries or millennia to cycle.

The scale of this world is not just in kilometers but in thickness. The roof you cannot see is not a thin canopy; it is a thick, dense roof of intertwined plant tissue that extends upward and outward and inward and onward and onward and onward—maybe continents-wide, or maybe planet-wide. You are inside a living skin that has wrapped itself around the entire world.

Nothing is “young” in the sense of fragile. The system is young only in the sense that its development is still early—its features haven’t been eroded or smoothed. The columns of trunk are still sharp-edged where they fused; their furrows and ridges and structural joints are fresh and defined. Growth itself hasn’t yet worn down the sharpness of its own architecture.

---

### No Animals, Only Machines

The world is not empty, but animals are absent.

In the living gloom you hear, faintly, the sound of machinery.

It is not a loud roar or a rhythmic pounding. It’s a distant murmur of forces—massive fans moving colossal air, or pumps moving water through hidden veins in the plant-world, or great engines driving the circulation of this whole ecosystem. The sound is not from any one direction; it comes from everywhere and nowhere, like hearing the ocean in a shell.

Somewhere beyond the pillars of wood, power stations and filtration plants and engine halls and chemical factories sit embedded in the vegetation. They are not built by you; they are built by a system that has assimilated the idea of “factory” the way some other world might assimilate the idea of “predator.”

The industrial apparatus is the plant world’s own circulatory system.

Conveyor belts—not metal, but woven fibrous tracks—travel through root tunnels, carrying biomass from one region of the system to another. Massive pumps draw the dark water up from deep reservoirs, not through pipes but through engineered plant trunks that have become pipes themselves, lined with smooth, hardened tissue.

Somewhere, there are engines—not with engines in the traditional sense, but plants whose inner tissues have been repurposed into biological machinery that burns fuel or breaks chemical bonds to generate force. They have been genetically engineered, or evolved by some other path than your own, into production systems.

Electricity is here. You feel it less than you hear it—high, faint hums along the root-trunk corridors, or microscopic statics between you and the nearest leaf edge. The sensitive plants in your world might “remember” touch; the plants here have learned to be sensitive to voltage. They use it as a nervous system, or as a control system, or as a language.

The industrial tech is everywhere—just not in the form of smelting furnaces and smoke stacks. You never see smoke or flame or sparks. What you have instead is a dim, humming, eternal industry of living matter: plant factories that never stop.

Feeding what?

Feeding itself. The entire system is a factory of life.

---

### Silence, Almost

There is no animal noise.

No buzzing of insects, no chirping of birds or crying of unseen creatures, no distant rustles of movement through underbrush. There is no decay of corpses in the usual sense—no carrion flies, no scavengers. The system’s dead matter is broken down by enzymes and fungi and bacteria and chemical processes, not by teeth or claws or jaws.

The silence is not total, but it is nearly.

You hear:

- The faint creaking of fibers inside the pillars of wood—a slow, structural sound of growth under its own weight.
- The tiny drip of condensation breaking from the underside of a leaf and striking the surface of a dark pool.
- A distant fragment of machinery from some hidden plant-factory, a hum like a low electric transformer.
- The faint, ghostly hiss of ventilation far above—air moving through openings you cannot see.

Your own breathing sounds loud in this world.

You become the only animal in the universe.

---

### Dynamic and Yet Static

Despite the soft creaking and the faint hum of machinery, the world feels like it is not changing.

The growth is slow—so slow that you cannot perceive motion. You can see the results of growth: a trunk diverges here, a new shoot pushes out there, the surface of a water basin is slightly more than half-covered in a fibrous mat that wasn’t there yesterday. But you never see the growing happen, not like you would watch a vine tendril curl.

The system’s equilibrium is almost perfect.

- Temperatures have been engineered to be steady.
- Humidity is maintained at just the point where condensation forms on surfaces without rainfall.
- Water levels in the basins and below the root-mat stay stable.
- The machinery hums, but you never hear it stop or start.

Time exists here, but it feels thin, like a thin film over a deep, almost motionless ocean. The young world has not yet been worn by erosion; there are no deep valleys reflected by their rivers, no rounded hills smoothed by wind. The pillars of wood are still sharp and definite in their geometry. The edges where the plants have met and fused are still clean, like fresh scars.

The future is here, but the future does not rush.

As you walk forward through the warm, saturated dark, the faint outlines of the pillars beckon you onward. Their glowing edges are not enough to show you what lies ahead; only enough to suggest that the same pattern—fused, growing architecture of living matter, humming quietly with its own industrial rhythm—extends forever.

---

### Final impression

You are in a world that has never met animals and does not need them.

It is a plant-world, still young and sharp-edged, but already mature enough to have engineered its own climate and its own machines. It is warm and wet and dense and stable and dark—a vast continent of cellulose and biochemistry and subtle electricity and slow, invisible weather.

You move through it as a disturbance in an otherwise perfect equilibrium of living matter.

And you know, with the certainty that you recognize your own breath, that this system will continue, unchanged, almost long after you are gone.
