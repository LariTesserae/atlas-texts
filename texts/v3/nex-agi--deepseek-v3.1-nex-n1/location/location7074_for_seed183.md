The Caverns of Ceaseless Rain hang beneath a sky that doesn’t remember how to stop raining.

You are not on the surface anymore—if there is a surface. The world is this: endless interior vaults of stone, suspended in a vertical landscape where the floor keeps falling away into the next floor below. You stand on a ledge halfway along the cliff of a cavern so large it aches to look across it. The distant ceiling is a sky of inverted stone, and from that sky it rains upwards.

---

## Terrain and Substance

This is a mountain turned inside out.

The rock is not gray so much as translucent—the way that ice or thin alabaster holds light without showing color. You can see currents in the stone, ribbons of milky quartz and pale schist that bend like rivers frozen in time. The cavern walls, the floor, the stalactites and columns are all varieties of this glassy stone, and it picks up the light from below and carries it in thin veins like slow lightning.

The floor of the great cavern is not a floor you can walk to; there are no “steps” down there. The floor is a continuous plane of water, perfectly still and impossibly black, stretched like taut skin across the gorge below your ledge. If you look long enough, the surface of the water seems to breathe.

Above you, the ceiling of the cavern is not the ceiling of the world. It is broken into irregular “windows” where higher chambers of the same glass-rock open above this one. You see through them to more floors, more shafts, more levels of this inverted mountain, and beyond that:

Nothing.

The ceiling gives out into a featureless white that is not sky or foam but the absence of definition. It looks like physics itself forgot how to continue and just stopped.

From the holes in the ceiling, rain falls up.

---

## The Upward Rain

Water runs along the cavern ceiling in thin, clear streams that refuse to drip down. They race along the ceiling’s contours like silver snakes, then—when they reach the ceiling holes where layers above begin—they leap into the air and climb into the next chamber of rock as a fine, upward-splashing rain.

The spray falls upwards toward you and then past you, rivulets curling against gravity and drifting along invisible walls like air currents. You can feel the moisture on your skin when it passes; not a heavy downpour but a constant, cool misting that feels as if the atmosphere itself is weeping.

Below you, the black floor-lakes seem to bleed the same water in reverse, feeding thin waterfalls that climb the walls and then flow along ceilings until they reach the ceiling holes and rise again. The whole system is a closed cycle of water chasing itself through the hollows of the world.

The constant sound of the rain moving along the ceiling and walls is a low white roar that never ceases, but it doesn’t overwhelm your thoughts—just becomes the background noise of the world itself, like breathing.

---

## Atmosphere and Light

The air is cool, almost cold, heavy with condensation. It smells like wet stone and a faint, metallic ozone, as if lightning is always about to announce its arrival but never quite arrives.

The humidity is high enough that you see your breath faintly shimmer, not just from temperature but because moisture hangs around you like a cloud insisting on being visible. If you stand still too long, tiny droplets of water gather on your skin and clothes without ever seeming to fall from anything—just condensation pulled from a saturated atmosphere.

There is no visible sun or lantern or torch, but the cavern is bright—almost too bright in the places where stone channels the light into your eyes. It comes from everywhere: thin luminous lines in the glass-rock, like long veins of phosphorescent algae inside the stone itself. These glowing veins flicker very slowly, like distant breathing.

The light doesn’t fall evenly; the vast cavern is full of bright arcs and deep shadows, where luminescent lines curve around pillars and archways of stone. The effect is like being inside a cathedral of circuitry whose wires are molten stone that has learned to glow.

---

## Sound

The most noticeable thing is not the silence, but the loudness of non-living things.

The upward rain is a constant chorus of water moving along every ceiling, every wall, every artificial channel you do not see until you look closer. It sounds like distant applause, or the ocean in a shell, but louder, more present than either.

The cavern itself occasionally sings: thin, high-pitched tones like struck glass that ripple across the vaults when water strikes a particular column just wrong—or just right. These notes are not random. They move across the cavern in patterns, like a melody being played too slowly to be recognized, but once you notice it, you cannot stop hearing.

Then there are the shapes.

---

## The Clause-Walls and the Meshing of Reality

The walls of the cavern are not plain stone. They are covered—in places, completely saturated—with text carved into the rock itself.

The letters are small but not microscopic; you can see them individually if you look. They are strange symbols that sometimes slide into your mind like a language you almost remember but not quite. When you don’t look directly at them, the edges of your vision catch them moving, rearranging themselves like lines being rewritten on a page, but when you turn your head, everything stills.

These are clause-walls. The principles of the world are written here.

You can feel the weight of them without reading. If you stand too close to one, you become aware of certain allowed things and not others. You might feel, for example, that walking on the ceiling is impossible—except then your eyes drift across a phrase that says something else, and suddenly you’re aware of a ceiling-handrail that wasn’t there before, or a faint, glowing ledge that circles the ceiling like a path.

Conversely, if you read a clause insisting that “no sound can exist here,” the song around you might soften abruptly into a muffled, underwater quality until the line shifts again.

The reality breakdown is gentle here, but real: the world keeps adjusting itself around the edges. You don’t see mountains rise or fall, but viewpoints shift. You swear you were looking at a wall that was seventy meters away, and then the next time you glance, it’s clearly three hundred. You blink, and a pillar that wasn’t there before is now serving as a bridge between your ledge and another that you would not have been able to reach before.

The density of the world—the sheer number of “lines of code” carved into the rock—gives the place a crowded, almost oppressive feeling of everything being near-touch, everything layered with invisible meaning. You feel like you’re surrounded by a forest of rules you can’t see but can occasionally brush against.

---

## No Plants, No Animals

The world you stand in is not biologically dead; it is simply biologically indifferent.

There are no plants here. Nothing green grows from the stone floor, or hangs from the ceiling like vines, or catches in the crevices where water might once have nurtured soil. The stone is smooth, polished by time and water, but only where it hasn’t been carved.

There are no animals—no insects, no birds, no bats. No movement of life except the water and the flickering light within the stone. You listen not for the absence of them but for the noise of their absence: the cavern should hum with the sound of wings and the chirping of cave creatures, but instead only glass-rock and water and the occasional singing of the stone itself echo.

If you press your hand to the wall, you swear you feel a faint pulse beneath the stone, like a heartbeat in slow motion—not organic, but not mechanical either. Just a pattern occupying itself.

---

## Built Marks and Remains

This place is not empty of people, but neither is it populated.

The “built” parameter floats here like a half-remembered dream. There are structures, but all of them are partial, incomplete:

- On a distant ledge across the cavern, you see a lone wall of stone, clearly worked by human hands. It stands without any roof to support, curving into the air and then stopping abruptly, as if someone started building a cathedral or fortification and then forgot to finish the thought.
- Carved staircases disappear into mountainside walls that have no entrances or exits at that level—they simply end in midair, or merge into smooth stone where the door should have been.
- On the floor below, you see faint geometric shapes—pavements, circles of stones, maybe—partially submerged under the black water, like sigils drawn by a culture that treasured exactness more than they remembered the name of the sky.

The tools that made this are primitive, by any measure you can see: chisel marks, hand axes, maybe some simple awls. There is no sign of metal machinery; no pulleys or gears or pipes. The cuts are clean and precise but not high-tech; it feels like someone spent lifetimes with a stone tool and infinite patience.

A few scattered artifacts remain on the ledges around you:

- A small stone basin with characters carved into its inner edge, like instructions for rain that never falls down into it.
- A half-finished statue of some kind of figure, but only the hands and knees are detailed enough to recognize; the rest melts into unshaped rock.
- Round holes carved into the rock floor, like post sockets that might once have held timber supports—but now, empty, they become channels through which rain water flows upward in thin columns instead of falling down.

These structures feel like they belong to several different civilizations, or several different phases of the same civilization, but none of them line up in time. You might see roofless walls standing beside perfectly intact circular foundations of stone that never touch any surface above them.

---

## Ecology and Rules of the World

This is not an ecosystem in the usual sense. There is no food chain, no predator-prey, no photosynthesis. There is only:

- Water cycling endlessly through itself.
- Light carrying patterns through the stone.
- Stone holding itself in the inverse of a mountain.

And the rules. The rules are everywhere.

Each clause-wall carries a sentence or two of the world’s physics. There are clauses like:

- “No sound travels faster than water can.”
- “Every path must intersect twice with itself.”
- “The memory of touch cannot exceed the length of one breath.”

You don’t read these in the language you speak now, but your mind parses them anyway. The boundaries of what is possible shift subtly as you inhabit them. You might notice that you cannot remember more than one step of your journey before this one, and then you realize that clause is active somewhere nearby.

The world itself is active. Processes are everywhere: the rain pursuing its upward cycle, the light veins in the stone slowly migrating their patterns like tectonic plates shifting in millimeters per year, the reality of distance and possibility reshaping itself according to the carved laws around you.

It feels both in balance and constantly revising itself. The erosion that has smoothed these walls and ledges is not violent or sudden. It is the kind of erosion that happens when a world spends long, slow eons settling into its own shape, until every stone knows exactly where it is supposed to be swallowed and where it is supposed to remain.

---

## The Far Chasm and the Boundary of Nothing

At the far end of the cavern, where the ceiling finally lowers and the floor rises to meet it, there is a narrow slit in the rock like a doorway into the next chamber of the mountain—but beyond that “door” is not rock.

Beyond that frame is a white expanse that doesn’t match the ceiling’s mere no-sky. It looks like the paper the world is written on before any words are laid down.

You cannot tell whether it is a surface, or a volume, or an absence. You just know that if you walked there you would not misunderstand what it is; you would either immediately understand and never return, or you would be erased on the way.

The upward rain keeps flowing from that edge into the ceiling above you, as though the empty white beyond is still somehow water-bearing and willing to lend itself to the world’s cycle.

---

## You on This Ledge

You stand on a ledge halfway along the cliff. The rock beneath your feet is polished smooth by time and moisture and the occasional invisible hand of someone who decided this ledge should be exactly here for exactly this purpose.

You can feel the faint vibration of the rain moving along the ceiling above you, like the world breathing.

You look at the clause-covered wall behind you, and without reading you know that if you choose to step closer to it, the world will rearrange itself around you slightly. You might step and find a new path carved into stone that wasn’t there before, or a ceiling that becomes your floor, or a ceiling-rain you can walk into.

You might also find a clause that says something like: “You cannot go back,” and then you will have to live with the fact that you did not notice when the world decided you didn’t need your past anymore.

The air is cool, wet, bright.

The Caverns of Ceaseless Rain hang beneath a sky that doesn’t remember how to stop raining; and you, standing on this ledge, are a small presence in a world of dense, saturated reality, where geometry has been told to do something more interesting than obey itself.

---

You can step forward into the rain, or along the wall to where the ledges wander into the next chamber of this inverted mountain.
