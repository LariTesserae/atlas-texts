A heat-glaze of horizon wobbles over the flats, the air trembling in verticals like glass about to shiver. You are standing on a high mesa of black glass that stretches away on all sides, a polished plate of obsidian fused into a seamless plain by machine ages now forgotten or entombed. The sky is a blistering, pale cobalt, featureless but not empty—something behind it blurs through the blue, like an industrial city pressed into the skyline and then out of focus.

There is no water.

There is none.

Not a puddle, not a damp stone, not a trace of frost on the underside of anything. The air is so dry that when you breathe in through your nose, the mucus membranes inside buck and crisp and ache. You can feel the moisture evaporating from your lungs and skin, the heat of this place leaning against you like a furnace door that has no off switch.

And yet.

The world is loud with fauna.

From the cracks that run in thin, hairline fractures across the mesa floor, rising from the deeps below the crust of black glass, a swarm of voices climbs up.

They are not insects, though they move like insects—the idea of countless leg-joints and antennae, the skitter of digits on a surface too hard to be stone. The sound is a dense roar, a confusion of chattering, hissing, clicking, the constant background of a machine-room floor and a slaughterhouse floor and a call-center floor all rolled into one. They are not animals you see easily; the landscape feels empty at a glance. But stare at the edge of a fracture for a few heartbeats, and you will begin to see them.

The first thing you notice is their outlines—like they are drawn with slightly wrong geometry on the world. They come in two varieties:

The first: the Slow Feeders

They come up from the fractures in the morning, when the temperature is still just below lethal. They are tank-heavy things, armored in heat-shedding plates that resemble the cooling fins of a generator block. Their legs are like hydraulic arms, each moving on a track rather than a joint, and every time one foot lifts and swings forward it is more than legs—you are watching a machine-like body walk, but the body is not built; it grew this way, over centuries of industrial heat-states.

They call them Slow Feeders because they move at a pace that would bore you to watch if you were not afraid of them. They drift across the mesa glass—not stepping, not exactly. Their feet touch the surface for long minutes at a time, and where they linger, the heat they carry away is slowly, slowly replenished from the buried veins beneath. They are eating heat. Their bodies are black iridescence, the same color as the mesa floor, but something like engine oil shimmers on their skin when they heat-shed, so you mostly see them as smears of distortion in the air.

Where they are, the temperature drops slightly. You can feel it—like a hand raised from a hot engine block and into merely unbearable air. They line the horizon in dozens, their silhouettes briefly turning into a stack of cooling towers on an industrial skyline, before your eyes correct it and they resolve back into living bodies. It is impossible to look at them without architecture sliding into your mind. Their ribs might be the girders of a factory roof. Their eyes, if you ever see them clearly enough, will be glass lenses of cooling systems.

The second thing: the Fast Feeders

They boil around the Slow Feeders like sparks of static electricity. They are small—cat-sized or smaller—but they fill the volume around the larger bodies. They are winged, but they do not fly like birds; they fly like broken machines, like shards of glass thrown erratically from the back of a truck that is taking a corner too fast. Their wings are bright-edged plates of hardened metal—not forged metal, but the metal-like substance of their bodyplates.

They hover or jitter in place, whipping past your head so close you feel the heat-off their skin and the sound of their wings shreds your hearing like a bad speaker. They are not attracted to you; they are attracted to motion and temperature differentials. You, being cooler than anything around, are a sudden cold sink in a world of hot. They do not touch you, but they hover inches away from your skin, their wings vibrating so fast they sound like chainsaws starting.

They do not bite or sting. They drink heat variance.

When you’re held in the center of their cloud, you experience something like multiple realities at once. Your body is in a hot, dry, high-altitude world of black glass. But each time a Fast Feeder swings close to your skin, a different temperature pattern flickers into your nerves—an ice bath, a cold night wind, a damp cellar floor, a rain-slicked street. The heat doesn't actually leave you; it's just that your body momentarily believes it's somewhere else while the temperature information around you is rewritten.

These are not illusions of the mind, but glitches of the environment.

The temperature of the world is stable. The temperature of your flesh is stable. The temperature flux in the space between you and them is not, and the gap—where reality breaks down—is where they live.

The animals are the dominant reality, not because of their bodies filling the space, but because their presence changes how temperature works.

Wherever a Slow Feeder stands for long enough—and they stand for hours or days—the temperature lines around them warp gently. The heat-wind of the floor is pulled toward them like iron filings toward a magnet. They are eating heat in the sense of converting temperature into something else, but the temperature of the world itself doesn't drop; it's just that, around them, the temperature gradient becomes even more weakly dynamic than elsewhere. Time stretches.

- You see the floor beneath you as a floor, but shot through with the faint lines of a factory floor layout—walls that aren't there and shadows that are.
