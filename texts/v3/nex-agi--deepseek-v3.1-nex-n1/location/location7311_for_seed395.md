The city of Jelibeth rises from the throat of the world, a knot of stone and soil and half-sleep in the low warmth before dawn.

It is vast not only because it is old—though it is very old—but because it is hungry: a sprawling, low-slung thing of brick and lime and bone and root, chewing the horizon into itself. The terrain is almost flat, a slight bowl, a shallow pan whose rim is lost in milk haze, so that the skyline is not a skyline but a suggestion of more, a faint ink of towers where visibility falters. The air is weighty without being wet; the ground is warm to the touch, even at night, as if the crust below it still remembers the furnace of its making.

The city itself is the only geology of note here. There is no river to speak of, no riverbed at all, not the faintest scratch of a channel that the land might once have given into. There is no sweeping of gullies or alluvial fans, no carving of watercourses. The few low rises are modest and unformed, like a child’s half-made lump of clay beneath a steady hand. They are hummocks of pale clay and chalk, thrust up by the pressure of the city’s growth and not yet levered into proper slopes or low cliffs. The cobbles of the outer streets are level with the root of these infant hills, so that at the margins of Jelibeth, buildings and roads and hillside emerge from the same blunt, almost-planar surface, as though the ground were pushing out new teeth and the city were already gumming them into shape.

This is not a desert in the old sense. There are no sand dunes eroding into shifting beauty, no salt-pans slicking the sky into their surface. It is simply dry. There is no free water to move into or from, no surface channel to guide runoff or carve hollows. The water is a locked thing: present enough to keep the city alive, but not in any form that can be called weather. The ground’s pores are saturated with something else—a thin, clear syrup that tastes like root and metal and is not strictly water—but it does not drip from the sky or wander in the overground. It is kept, it is stored in the walls of cisterns and the sponges of the greenery, and the city, in its emergent, half-formed way, is not yet shaped by water’s will.

Instead of rivers, there are salt-scars: faint smudges of white stain where the occasional, rare condensation of the night bleaches the stone. These are not yet channels of erosion; they are ghost marks, like the first sketched lines of an artist who has not yet decided where the picture will go. In the half-light before day, these salt-scripts catch faint glints, as if the city itself is trying to write its name into the stone before the sun can burn it out.

The buildings are not tall, but they are everywhere. There is no room between presences: no place where you could look and not see something made or claimed by the city. The architecture is dense without being high, stacked and interlocked, like something that has grown from below rather than been planned from above. Stone is pale and porous, tan chalk and soft bone-white, taken from low quarries at the city’s edge where the ground first began to lift into possible hills. The roofs are flat and thick, not with tile but with a dense, low mat of vegetation that clings to the lime-stone and forms a second, living ground plane above the streets.

The materials of Jelibeth are humble and immediate: clay mortar, chalk blocks, chalk-white plaster, and the ribs of the canals. Canals there are, though not for water as such. They are shallow, broad-bottomed channels lined with the same porous stone as the buildings, and they are almost always damp with night sweat and the breath of the city. They are not filled with clean water; they are lined with a thick, clear gel that keeps from evaporating and stays just enough to support the mat of green that binds them: pale, rubbery fronds in low ropes that press into the stone at the edges and creep into the joints of the walls where they are allowed.

Within the city, the streets are not precisely planned. They bend like capillaries in a body, dividing and rejoining without the need of a mapmaker’s rule. One might be broad and straight enough for three carts to pass side by side—if carts were to be seen here—but in the next block it is pinched down by the constant pressure of new building walls, new terraces, so that it is a narrow alley between two high houses of pale chalk that seem to lean into each other’s shadows. Stone is broken down and reused from the inside out: new thresholds are made from old lintels; the walls of the old houses are not demolished but mined into for new chambers, and new rooms are hollowed out of the soft stone. The city, in this sense, is eroding itself—but slowly, with the deep care of something that knows it is not yet finished forming.

The climate is warm but not harsh. It has not yet learned to be extreme. The nights are gentle with a low, distant heat radiating from the ground itself, as if the world’s core were still paring down to its right temperature. The days are warmer than the nights but not blazingly so; there is no sun that can burn out the sky to white. There is only the normal light of a clear, low sun over a low landscape, bright enough to show the world without being theatrical. It is not tilted toward any mood of harsh or soft; it is only, self-evidently, day.

The soil of Jelibeth is thin and raw in the floors of its courtyards and the margins of its half-built hillsides, but where it is present, it is thick with life. The rule of the world is not water but sap: clear, thin, and mildly bitter to the tongue, circulating through the city as if it were a single plant body. The plant life is all low, fibrous, and ropy, rather than tall and trunklike. The roofs and the balconies are not crowned with towering trees but with thick mats of vegetation that weave into each other. The fronds emerge from cracks in the plaster, descend from gutters that never catch actual rain, and knit into the walls and the niches of windowsills. There is no true tree to be seen here, not yet—but the vegetation is lush enough to form the city’s shadow canopy. The colors are pale green, silvered, pale yellow like the underside of leaves, and the faint blush of a new shoot that has not yet decided its color.

The animal life is abundant without being noisy. The world of Jelibeth is quiet. The steps of living creatures are not yet accompanied by the clatter and speech of tools and metalflesh and the grinding of wheels. There are no machines, no devices of metal or gearwork to be found: not a wheel, nor a lock, nor a simple mechanism of lever and pivot. There is no metalwork to speak of except the faint, trace glints of some thin ore in the stone walls, which seem to capture the light and hold it as if they were only halfway to being what they might become.

Yet there is no sense of absence or lack. The city is simply not of that order of things. It is biological, not mechanical; it is ceramic and chalk and sap and fur and chitin and soft, steady breath. It has not yet learned any other language of existence.

The fauna, in this sense, are as much part of the architecture as the plants are. The streets are pressed with them: innumerable bodies moving without drama or complaint, like the cells of the city’s circulatory system. The greatest are the low-slung mound-beasts, whose backs are flattened to carry the paved stones of new roads and plazas, and whose paths are sluggish and slow. They are not burdened; they are not yoked to axle or rein. They merely choose their routes by the subtle pressure of the sap beneath the ground, and each building donated to the city—whether it was once a home or a warehouse or a simple alcove—has been negotiated by them and given into their body, as if they were the city’s true foundation and bricklayer at once.

There are smaller things too: creatures with broad bodies and low legs, whose fur is not like hair but more like a dense, downy mat of lichen or moss, and whose color shifts from tan to pale chalk-white to the faintest of greens when they are nesting among the roofs. They slip along the walls, not like climbing animals but like the creeps of a growing vine, and the vegetation of the roofs parts for them with a small, soundless sigh, accepting them into the weave of the city’s surface.

Birds, if birds are the right word for them, circle the low sky in slow, broad arcs, not crying out but drifting with a faint rustling of their edges like the leaves of a plant being gently turned over. Their feathers are filamentous: not like quills or plumes, but like thin green threads that have learned to move air into themselves so they might float upon it. They are not loud; they call out with soft clicks and low, contented murmurs that are not songs but more like the sound of a settling joint.

There is abundance of every other kind of life: swarms of small, hard-shelled insects that echo the color of the stone walls and not the ground, so that they are not easy to see until they are moving; things that burrow into the soft chalk of the roadsides and not the soil; tiny things with no limbs to speak of, whose bodies are pale translucent sacs and whose slow migration across a shaded courtyard wall is like the faintest of cleansing breaths pulling the stone into something else.

It is all this movement that gives the place its sense of being far more than itself. The erosion of Jelibeth is not yet geological in the grand sense; it is not yet shaped by the great laws of wind and rain and river and ice. But it is active. The city is being slowly formed under its own skin. The hills are each a small history of pressure: the pressure of new construction always from below, the new chambers of the city hollowing their stone, and the pressure of the mound-beasts moving their roads and new foundations into place. The ground gives itself up to this process not grudgingly but with the same slow, contented sleepiness of a child rolling into warmth before it fully wakes.
