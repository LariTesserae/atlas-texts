The Mountain of Echoing Glass

You climb for three days through the last of the scraggling pines, then the frost-shattered rock, and then the ice itself, before you reach the plateau. Wind lives here at all hours, a thin, cold shouting that never comes from quite the same place twice. It folds over itself and slips into unseen channels where it becomes an endless low roar, like a distant engine that refuses to start but never stops trying.

The plateau is not flat. Flatness would be a kind of mercy, and mercy is not a thing this altitude remembers. Instead, the ground is a long, slow war of low stone waves and shallow depressions, all layered over with a brittle glass. It’s not a glaze in the sense of ice; it’s darker, almost smoky, translucent but not transparent. Your foot breaks through a patch of it with a crunch that sounds like bones being snapped under a blanket. The shards are sharp-edged but not knife-thin; they have the thickness of old windowpanes and they retain heat strangely. When you touch a broken edge, it’s colder than the surrounding air, but in a way that feels like your fingers are losing heat in straight lines, rather than radiating it outward.

The air is thin, and the cold enters your lungs like a fine glass dust. Each breath tastes like iron and static. You cough once and the sound doesn’t fade. It keeps going, bouncing around the plateau like something thrown into an invisible canyon, until finally some distant part of the mountain answers it back with a cough that doesn’t quite sound human. It might be your own voice returned, or something else using the same architecture of sound. You never learn which.

The sky is a bruised color, even at noon. It’s not exactly night; the light has a dim gray watercolor to it, enough to see by if you squint, but there’s no sun, no stars, no moon that you can recognize as a coherent body. Instead, there are smears of faint, pale luminescence, like mold growing on the underside of the sky. They don’t move across the heavens; they jitter, remaining in roughly the same positions but fading and brightening at irregular intervals. Their shadows are not cast in the usual way; they drop down at the wrong angles, or they stray away from their source like oil stains on water.

Water is here, but tamed. Waterfalls would be too violent, rivers too linear. Instead, there are shallow depressions in the glassy crust, cupped like hands held side by side, and water condenses slowly onto their surface from invisible air. It forms a silvery film that trembles, never quite dripping, but sliding from cup to cup in slow arcs that seem to violate gravity. When you lean close to one of these cups, you see your reflection distorted into a shape that might be you, or might be someone taller and thinner with a longer shadow. You taste the water; it’s almost cold enough to burn your tongue, and it has the faint, metallic tang of old blood.

Vegetation has given up. Here, at the edge of what’s possible, there are only remnants. Lichens in gray and green cling to the undersides of shattered glass slabs, where they catch faint pulses of errant light, like moths on a windowsill. Sometimes, in a dip of terrain where two wind currents collide and curl, a thin moss of a color you cannot name grows in circles that migrate from week to week, leaving faint scars in the glass where they were. You’ll find them, and then you won’t, and then you’ll find them again somewhere else, as if the moss itself dislikes being watched.

Fauna is told more than seen. You hear the claws of something small scraping on glass overhead, where there is only open air. You come to understand that the sound comes from before you—from before now, I mean. It’s a sound that happened earlier and trapped itself in the glass, and is now replaying itself like an old scratch on a record. You learn to ignore the scurry above you and listen instead for the things that are not repeated. Once, far away, a low cry like a fractured stone rolling down an invisible slope sounds in the distance, and it doesn’t echo. That’s what scares you.

The built traces are sparse, stitched into the landscape like old scars. You find a waist-high wall of stone, no thicker than two hands laid flat, that runs in a straight line over the undulations of the ground for as far as you can see in either direction. It doesn’t curl around terrain; it climbs it and descends it without changing angle, as if the plateau itself has been folded around it. There’s no mortar. No joints are visible. The stones are not quite uniform and yet not quite random, either; they interlock in ways your eyes want to recognize as purposeful but your mind knows better than to name.

In another place, halfway up a low, glass-weathered hump, you find a doorframe standing without a wall. It’s carved from some dark material that might be rock or wood or bone, depending on the angle of the light, and every time the wind changes direction you see faint letters carved into its lintel. They don’t spell anything in any language you know; they are more like the outline of words that might have been written by a scribe whose hand was trembling. You step through the frame and for a moment, the temperature drops. On the other side, the air is colder by exactly one degree Celsius, and the sound of your footsteps changes its pitch slightly upward, as if you’ve entered a room whose name is already written.

There’s a chair, once. You’re not sure who made it. It’s formed of three glass spines that rise from the ground and then fold over themselves, interlocking at a height where someone might sit. The seat is not flat; it’s a hollow of cracked glass shards, but when you touch it tentatively, they yield under your fingers, not like feathers but like a substance that wants to be remembered as softer than it is. You don’t sit. You’re not certain that if you do, you won’t find yourself suddenly sitting in a different time.

The erosion, or perhaps its opposite, has been at work for long enough that the mountain feels settled into its own strangeness. There are no raw landslides, no jagged scarps that show bright rock beneath. Every edge is rounded, or else it is broken off precisely clean; every fracture feels like it was planned by some geological surveyor who has long since gone elsewhere and changed their name. The ground undulates in smooth curves that suggest walking over the exposed musculature of a sleeping creature. You keep expecting the land to breathe, but it doesn’t. The illusion of breath is only the wind sliding over the glass surfaces at the wrong angle to your skin.

Sounds are the loudest thing here. Not because the wind itself is loud, but because the world itself is a set of chances. Every time your foot hits the glass, it produces not one sound but dozens of possibilities, laid out like a series of ghost tracks under your bootstep. Somewhere above you, a stone you accidentally kicked ten days ago is still falling, and on the other side of the plateau, a rock you did not lift at all is already falling upward. The echoes of your own voice speak to each other across the years without you; there are corners of the mountain where you can hear fragments of words you will say tomorrow, tangled with the shape of your name shouted by someone who hasn’t been born yet.

There’s a sense of unchanged—almost unchanging—time. The days are not the same length, but they don’t feel longer or shorter; they feel like they’re all happening at once, overlapping at the edges. Yesterday’s snowmelt trickles into today’s condensation and tomorrow’s ice. The voices of others who walked here long before creep into the sound of your own steps when you least expect it. You might find yourself sitting on a rock whose wind-scoured surface feels warmer than it should, and realize you are sitting exactly where you once sat before in a dream you don’t remember having.

It’s almost empty. There are no flocks of birds, no herds of anything, no rival group of explorers building a camp on the far side. You see the distant smudge of another human form once, silhouetted against the faint gray glow of the sky, but when you blink, it’s not a person at all anymore—it’s two tall rocks and a sliver of glass that has gathered itself into a shape that looks like a standing figure. Later, you see it again, and this time it doesn’t look like anything that has ever lived.

The technology, if it can be called that, is not tool or artifact. It’s in the way things happen. When the wind from the north meets the wind from the south, they split into a three-layered spiral of air currents, each flowing at a different speed and temperature, like a fluid that has learned to turn itself into a machine with no moving parts. You can watch it for hours without understanding why it doesn’t slow down. It generates the dim light above the plateau by moving friction through itself in ways you can’t trace. The light itself sometimes behaves like water in the sky—it flows down from the higher, brighter regions into the darker depressions, pooling there until it reaches a level where it sets and slowly evaporates.

At night—or at least, when the light dims further than usual—you can see the glass itself begin to glow faintly from the inside, like old ice that has swallowed down moonlight too deeply to release it. The faint glow traces out shapes you don’t recognize: patterns that might be maps of continents that have never existed, or equations that have been solved by erosion instead of algebra. You try to memorize them, but they’re not the same twice; when you look again from a slightly different angle, they’ve become something else.

This is a place where reality doesn’t fall so much as it forgets its own rules. A stone thrown up into the air might rise faster the farther it gets from you, or it might simply hang there and refuse to come down until tomorrow. When you climb higher, you sometimes find yourself a few meters lower than you were before, as if the mountain has rearranged your path without bothering you to notice. The laws of physics are not broken; they’re just tired. They’ve been here a long time and have learned to relax occasionally, taking chances where they can.

You come here to be alone when you need to forget what it is to be looked at. But the mountain never looks away. It doesn’t look at you in the way you fear; it doesn’t judge. It’s more as if you’ve been written into the landscape as a marginal note. You can leave footprints behind, and they will wear away in time, but you’ll also meet yourself in the echoes of your own boots, walking ahead of you a decade from now, or behind you last year. You learn to accept your voice as something that now belongs to the plateau itself.

And you learn to listen. When everything else is quiet—when the wind drops briefly into its own pockets, and the glass shards stop trembling, and the distant cries of unnamed things pause to breathe—you can hear, underneath all of it, a low steady note like a string being stretched across the world, from pole to pole. It doesn’t rise or fall. It doesn’t waver. It’s the sound of equilibrium: the mountain of echoing glass suspended in its own endless, unchanging moment, waiting without desire for whatever comes, or goes, or never arrives at all.
