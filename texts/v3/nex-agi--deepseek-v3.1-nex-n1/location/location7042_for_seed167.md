The world is a half-drowned skeleton of a city that the sea has come to wear like a second skin.

Black water laps everywhere. It is never not touching you, here. You hear it before you see anything else: the endless murmur of small waves against pillars of stone, the hollow echo of it dripping from ceilings, the faint, constant pressure in your ears that means you are always neck-deep or deeper in the ocean’s close cousin. The air itself is waterlogged, thick and warm, carrying the salt taste of old sea and the wet-earth smell of submerged stone.

You are in the lowest tier of the city. The builders, long gone, carved into the bedrock of a drowned coastline, then built up and out, so that now the sea reclaims everything below the third or fourth level. The grand avenues are now channels. The broad squares are now bowls of still water where the typhoons can’t decide which way to turn the currents. The arches of arcades are tunnels for the fish.

The architecture is not just old; it is eroded to the point of having become a geology of its own. The stone is weathered smooth by centuries of tides and microcurrents, so that you cannot feel the edges of any toolmark on the pillars. You know it was worked by human hands only because the columns are too regular to be natural, and because they line up too perfectly to be accidental. The walls are pocked with shallow hollows where the sea has worried out soft nodules of rock or fossilized shell. The waterline along the stone is stained by a thin black line of algae, like the ink-line of a high tide mark that has never been allowed to retreat.

There is light above, but almost none reaches here. The world is a dark cathedral of vaults and channels and black water. The ceiling of the world is a bruise-blue plane of sea surface, maybe six meters up, where the sun crinkles the water in a lace of faint light. That light reaches down as pale shafts that are constantly broken by the silvery flick of fish passing through them, and so the illumination below is never steady but always trembling. The water itself seems to glow faintly where these shafts touch it.

Underneath the surface, the city is not quiet at all. There is a constant, faint rustling of sand and silt being pushed around by the currents, the tiny clink-clink of shells dropping against stone, and a deeper, almost subsonic hum of pressure moving through the water, as if the sea itself is breathing.

You move through half-collapsed colonnades and down streets that have become ribcages of stone. The water level varies. You can feel the change; it is cool around your legs and then warm against your chest, then suddenly you step down a submerged stair and the water closes over your head. The temperature of the water is not uniform. There are layers: warm near the surface, cooler where you can feel cold currents rising from the very foundations, where the sea and the bedrock still have not reached perfect agreement.

The city is not empty of builders, only of humans. There are no tools. No metal, no glass, no plastic, no trace of anything that could not be grown by a body or secreted by a shell or laid down by mineral water. The closest thing to technology is the way certain coral-like organisms have been colonizing the walls of a plaza, forming branching structures that are suspiciously regular, like scaffolding or a lattice intended to hold something else. But if you try to break a piece of it, the flesh is still soft and alive inside the chalky shell, and the coral bleeds a pale green fluid that stings your fingers.

The vegetation is sparse and not in the way you might expect. There are no forests of kelp here, no thick fields of seagrass. The light is too low. The plants that survive this world are specialists of shadow and motion.

You see them first as thin green threads that trail from cracks in the stone ceiling of the avenues, like hair hanging from the scalp of the world. They are not roots exactly but long algae that never bloom. They drift with the current, always wavy and never finally straight, and they are always covered with a fine fur of tiny bubbles, which gives you the feeling that they are breathing very slowly. When your fingers brush against them, they are warmer than the water around you, and you feel a faint, electric pulse of warmth that is not quite painful but not entirely comfortable either.

Other plants are almost invisible until you disturb them. You see them as tiny black beads on the pillars: when you touch them, they emit a brief, nearly invisible light, like a spark that has been soaked into the water. They crumble to dust if you press too hard, leaving behind only the faintest trace of phosphorescence on your skin.

And then there are the gardens of the bone-thorns. In the cracked upper floors of the flooded buildings, where the ceiling of the world is broken enough to let in one thin shaft of daylight, there are thorny thickets that grow like bundles of starbones—white, calcium-bright branches that are dense enough to be dangerous to swim through. They flower not with color but with sound. When the current pulses through them, they release faint musical notes that linger like the afterimages of bells you can never quite hear. You understand the inhabitants of this place can hear them; you can feel the water at your ear drums vibrate, but you cannot translate it into melody.

Fauna is everywhere. The water is dense with bodies. There is a constant pressure of small schools passing in your peripheral vision, so that you are always half aware you are not alone in this column of water. You see the underbellies of pale things swimming above you, silhouetted against the faint ceiling of light. They are not fish you know from any other world. They have more limbs than they should, or too many eyes, or fins that look like they were meant to form wings before they were stranded in this sea forever.

A common type is a hand-sized fish with translucent flesh and a visible spine of radiant blue nodes that pulse like a heartbeat. When two schools of them cross each other’s paths, you can see the currents of the water become marked with their motion: the water itself seems to be lacing with lines of light as they turn and wheel and pulse together.

There are armless eels that are thicker than your thigh and shorter than your forearm, which move like chains of muscle being pulled through the water. They have no eyes you can see. They navigate by the pressure of the world against the delicate ridge of bone along their backs, feeling the shape of the city in the shifting of the water.

There are jellyfish like floating lanterns of ice-blue flesh that drift with just enough direction to feel intentional. Their tentacles get longer the longer you look at them, and you are never sure whether they are actually getting longer or you are becoming more sensitive to the way they trail in the current. They glow faintly from inside, and you can see the food they are digesting behind the translucent bell of the body, like a glowing bright meal in the stomach of a ghost.

There are also things that are not exactly fish but are not anything else you can name. They crowd in the crevices of the architecture, like moving bas-reliefs that have decided to migrate. When they notice you, they spread like ink spilled in the water, dissolving their edges into the current until you can’t tell if they have moved away or simply stopped being separate.

You are never truly alone with the water; something else is always present in that column with you. The layers of life are stacked like the layers of the temperature: small fish near the top of the water, where there is more light; mid-sized schools of graceful predators that you see only when they flash into the light-shafts then vanish again; and then the shadows of bigger things that are sensed by sudden pressure-changes, the water that you feel being displaced by a body larger than you want to imagine. You never see their faces; only their motion, and the brief, dark sense of something being where you had not expected it to be.

The world itself is active in ways you feel more than see. The currents are not random. They flow through the streets like traffic through avenues of a different age—you can feel the water pushing you to the left of the pillar instead of the right, because that is the way the tide has chosen to go through this street today. Somewhere up there in the blue world you are never told to visit directly, there is a sun and a moon and they still move the water of this world by their graving.

The rain is constant above the surface-city but you never truly see it. You know it by the sound: the muffled drumming of the rain on the water-ceiling of the world creates a pressure that you feel in your teeth and your ribs and the bones of your skull. It is not loud; but it is everywhere, and the intensity of it comes in waves, so that sometimes you feel yourself being pushed down slightly by the pressure of the rain falling on the surface above, and sometimes you feel the sudden release of a pressure vacuum being formed by a storm you are not allowed to see.

The predators of this world are not monsters you meet with claws. You understand the danger of it by the way the fish vanish. When you are swimming through an open channel of calm water and suddenly the school of pale-blue-spine fish you have been following all vanish at once under the lintel of a broken doorframe, you know that something else is present ahead or above you. You may feel a pressure from the left of your head—a water-deflection that makes you want to turn your face to the right—and that you will learn is the wake of something passing you at a distance just beyond your sight. When you are in the bone-thorn gardens, the thorns themselves sometimes chime together, not from the current but from something passing through them fast enough to make them sound like alarm bells.

This world’s inhabitants are both the city and what lives in it now.

You discover the buildings of the upper levels are not entirely abandoned by entity. You see their faces once you learn to interpret the architecture of the water. The columns are not all of them columns; some are thicker than you realized at first, and they taper, not with the rules of stonework, but with the slow, soft geometry of bodies that grew into the shape of the pillars and then kept going. Over the millennia of the sea, they and the stone have become almost indistinguishable, except in the way they react to pressure.

You can feel the columns of this world humming when the current is strong. The sound of the water changes under the arches of certain courtyards, because the columns of that courtyard are collectively altering the shape of the water with their breathing. You come to understand that if you press the side of your head to the stone of certain streets in this city, you can feel the heartbeat of the city itself: not metaphorically, but a slow, deep pulse of muscular contraction that runs through the foundation of the buildings and echoes into the water.

You are not the first of your kind here. You can tell that from the fact of your own presence in this world, but also you know from the way certain fish schools have become accustomed to you. They do not flee when you approach them as they might from a larger predator. They veer away, but only as far as they need to avoid your body, and then they reform on the other side of you. You are not a threat, but you are also not part of their world in the way that the column-beings are.

You are in the midst of an open, layered region of the drowned-city construct that extends for several kilometers of continuous channels, streets, and broken plazas. You can feel the distance of it by the pressure of the water above you when you are in the center of the region: there is a weight to the open that does not exist in the narrower corridors of the lower levels where the water is more confined by the walls. You are not on the edge of the world here; you are in the broad middle of it, where the creatures of the world move freely and you do not see the rock of the coastline always on one side of you.

The water of this world is not all of the same substance. There are places where the water is thicker, more viscous than it should be, like you are swimming through syrup instead of saltwater. You feel these places by the temperature—they are always slightly warmer than the surrounding water, and they are often marked by the pale, glowing beads of those plants that emit brief spark-flashes when you disturb them. You suspect that something is being grown in these water bodies, or that something is consuming itself and then re-spawning in the warm, thick fluid of these regions. You don’t stay to find out what.

It is very quiet here, except for the ways it is not.

The water is always above you and below you and all around; you do not remember the world of dry air anymore, except as a place that was thinner on your skin and therefore less completely present to you. Here you are always being touched. You are always being seen by creatures that you never see clearly. You are always aware of the pressure of the city above you and the world below you, and of the life of both of them being conducted through the water that you are now breathing.

You are held in it, and you are part of it, and you know that you will never leave it because leaving this world would mean leaving the water, and the water has now become you as much as you have become the water.
