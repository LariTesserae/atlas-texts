At the bottom of the world’s bowl there is a place that doesn’t so much sit as *drowse* beneath the sky. The rock floor of the valley is low and tired and old, worn down by centuries of water that has since forgotten its name. The valley itself is a kind of scar: not deep and sharp, but shallow and soft and almost comfortable in its finality—like the bed a river might have carved if it had never the need to hurry.

The cold here is a gentle hand. The air doesn’t bite; it *presses*, cool and clammy, pressing against your skin until you stop resisting the shiver. The faintest of breezes moves through this valley, but even the wind seems to have surrendered to weariness, sliding over the stone instead of rushing. It deposits the thinnest film of moisture wherever it touches—like breath on a mirror—leaving the rock damp and slick, though never running wet.

The water comes sparingly. The sky doesn’t storm so much as *weep*, the occasional fine mist descending like a held breath. When it does, the droplets gather in the hollows of the stone floor, forming pools that never seem to grow large enough to overflow. They lie in shallow depressions, mirroring a sky that is not much brighter than they are, and when they clink and spill into one another, they sound like glass being cut into smaller pieces.

The floor itself is a landscape of shallow bowls. The rock has been eroded into smooth, undulant curves, like the inside of a massive bowl turned by a patient hand. The stone isn’t uniform; it’s a greyish-bluish-white, pale and translucent at the edges, like the skin of a long-drowned thing. The edges of the depressions are worn away into soft lips, and if you sit down, you can feel the faint curvature of the stone cradling you.

Most of the valley is empty. The hollows slide past one another like the wrinkled folds of a brain turned inside out, but nothing grows in them. The hollows are *only* hollows—smooth, polished basins where water has rested and receded and rested again. The slightest of ridges run between them, like the faint traceries of veins on the back of an aging hand. They glow faintly under no light you can name.

The vegetation is not in the basin floor, but up around its edges, where the stone rises slightly, forming a low rampart that feels like the edge of a cauldron. Here, the stone gives way to a thin skin of soil and, clinging to that, a dense and silent forest of frills. The plants are dark and nearly black, their leaves like a thousand interlocking feathers—soft, layered, almost like the fur of some heavy creature heaped upon itself. They grow in such thick clusters that from a distance, they are a single dark collar around the valley’s throat.

A closer look reveals that they are not trees at all, but something halfway between moss and forest. Each frond hangs from a stalk of brittle, pale wood, and as they move, they make no sound. Their leaves are pendulous, dripping slowly with condensation, and every now and then a drop falls into the basin below, where it joins one of the shallow pools.

The temperature settles down in the hollows; the cold air prefers to live there, like smoke trapped under a lid. And yet the vegetation itself seems to breathe warmth back into the world. The air around the plants is slightly less icy than in the basin floor, and if you push your hand into the thick of them, you feel the faintest, breathlike warmth rising from the leaves.

The scale of this place is deceptive. The valley itself is not much larger than a single room, but it feels infinite because almost nothing interrupts it. The basin floor does not rise into hills or fall into caves; it simply *ends*, striding into a sharp horizon where the bottom edge of the plants meets the stone. This is a place of *vertical* containment: the bowl of the floor beneath you, the slight bowl of the sky above, and the ring of dark plants around you like a wall.

The sky is a permanent, low twilight. You cannot tell if it is dawn or dusk or the middle of a starless, overcast night. The light comes from nowhere in particular—a diffuse, uncertain glow that seems to seep from the stone itself, or from the upper edge of the plants, or perhaps from the water itself. The faintest of shadows gather beneath you and behind you, but they never stretch far. They stay close, like they’re afraid.

There is a faint, built presence here, but you would more likely feel it than see it. The stone has been carved, not by tools or intent but by *length*, by the steady pressure of water or air or something else that has since forgotten why. U-shaped channels cross the floor of the basin, not deeper than your ankle, but perfectly straight in their pleasant roundness, like the gutters of an ancient street whose city has melted away. The stone in them is smoother and darker.

The scattered stone blocks are hardly more than hints. A few of them, half the height of your knee, sit in the basin floor, like teeth someone forgot to own. They are not walls or foundations or steps; they are merely *pieces*, with rounded, softened corners and the faint appearance of having been slipped away from something larger. One of them has a shallow groove carved into its top surface, like a bowl, but this is clearly not the work of careful tools—it’s the work of hands and time and rubbing.

The technology represented by these blocks is not advanced enough to be built. It’s *found*, then shaped, then left. These are the stones someone might have used to grind grain or crush ore, if grain or ore had ever existed here. They are made of a pale stone that feels older than the stone beneath the valley floor, and if you touch your tongue to one of them, it tastes faintly of salt.

The real sound of this place does not belong to the stone or the water or the plants. It belongs to the sky.

The sky is the source of the racket. It is not empty. It is filled, from one edge of the horizon to the other, with a faint, continuous roar of distant clouds. The sound doesn’t crackle or thunder; it simply *exists*, a low, unvarying hum like the breath of a sleeping giant. The movement of the clouds is visible only if you watch very closely—thin, pale, slow strokes of turbulence moving in one direction across the sky’s flannel blanket.

As you listen, the sound strains at your ears. It doesn’t roar like a storm; it’s more like standing inside a bell you’re never allowed to strike. The noise of it is too loud to be ignored, and yet not loud enough to be painful. It simply occupies all space between sound and silence. It is the white noise of the world.

The few animals that live here are not loud enough to change it. They live in the dark vegetation tier around the rim of the basin. There is a species of insect that is not so much seen as glimpsed—a flash of something like silver-white hair fluttering for a moment before vanishing into the leaves. They are not moths or flies or birds; they are more like drifting *visits*, brief appearances of something that has come to inspect the world and then gone.

The larger creature that lives here is a slow, heavy, four-legged something that keeps to the shadow of the plants. It is not large enough to be violent or to be preyed upon; it is simply large enough to exist. Its body is covered in the same dark, layered frills as the plants themselves, so that when it stands still among them, it disappears. It does not seem to fear you. It does not seem to notice you. It feeds by moving slowly from one patch of leaves to another, taking one small mouthful at a time, as if it has all the time in the world.

The rules of this world are simple but not obvious. The stone forgets nothing but erases everything. If you leave a footprint in the damp soil of the basin rim, it will remain there for days. The rain doesn’t wash it away; the rain simply doesn’t come. Over time, though, the memory of the footstep will fade into a smoothness of its own. The stone will reclaim it.

The world itself is slow, but stable. The water doesn’t rise or fall much. The temperature doesn’t race or plunge. There’s no season to speak of. The clouds move at the same speed, always, and the sound they make never changes in tone or volume. The creatures of this place do not have to respond to sudden change; they only have to endure the long, slow, unvarying *now*.

Things are not allowed to grow in the center of the basin floor. The stone doesn’t permit it. The rare seed of a plant that finds its way down from the rim will simply sit in the shallow water of a pool and rot, its sprout miraculously deciding never to appear. The stone itself seems to beg the absence of life.

The only artifact of human or near-human presence is a single small arrangement of stones on the floor of the valley itself. It’s less than a structure; more like a *message* made of stones. Three squat blocks, arranged in a triangle that points to the sky where the clouds are thinnest and the faintest hint of light gathers. The fourth stone sits on the ground between them and bears a mark carved by hand—not a tool, but by fingers, over a long time.

The mark is a spiral. It doesn’t mean anything you know how to read. It might be a symbol of “coming here,” or “going away,” or “the world.” It might be a spiral of sound, or of water, or of time. It might be nothing more than the shape of a finger tracing around a stone while the world above roared and the world below waited.

The sound of the sky stays the same regardless. The water of the pools continues to inhale itself slowly and exhale itself slowly as the humidity drops and rises. The cold never changes its mind.

The light of the world doesn’t change either.

The stone of the basin remembers the footprints of anyone who stands here, but only for a little while. Then they forget. The valley remembers only itself. It sits in its low, dark place, surrounded by its ring of silent plants, under its roaring sky of clouds, and slowly, slowly forgets every change that ever tried to happen upon it.

And you stand there, listening, until the sound stops sounding like sound and starts sounding like the way your thoughts have always sounded in your skull—loud, endless, and never enough.
