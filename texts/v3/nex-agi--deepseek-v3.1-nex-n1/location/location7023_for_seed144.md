The air here tastes like metal and chalk dust, and it is never still.

You are standing on a roof in the middle of a city that has climbed into the sky and not yet decided whether to stop. The city clings to the flanks of a mountain range whose name is only remembered by the machines. The peaks are old enough that their violence has been worn into patient geometry: arêtes of ridged black basalt, cirques of pale dust, and along the valleys, the faintest smudge of green where water has been allowed to linger.

Even at the extreme elevation of these rooftops, it is hot. The sun is a white point in a thin deep blue, and it hammers the world without shame or shadows. Heat is trapped under the materials of the city: the dark glass and brushed alloy skins of the towers, the slate-gray of mechanically compressed stone slabs, the reflective surfaces of the vast platforms that jut out into the nothingness over lower slopes. The air is thin and your lungs are working hard, but the temperature stills sweats into the salt line at your collar.

This place is known as the Threshold, but no one remembers who named it or what threshold it was supposed to cross.

### Water and dryness

On the whole, this is a dry world. If you were to map it from orbit, you would see a belt of continents and ranges like this one, wrapped in a thin scarf of vapor where moisture from the oceans is forced upward by the wind and then nailed down again by high pressure systems. Down the long slope east of the Threshold, the land becomes desert and then salt pan and then nothing you can see with light alone.

But here in the high, folded city, there is water.

It comes in three ways:

1. *Condensation machines.*  
    All the taller buildings are wrapped in a kind of external lattice—like a scaffolding made of darker glass and pale metal. The sun heats the outer surfaces; fans and hidden vents circulate the hot air through cold ducts from the night side of the mountain. The roof of that tower you are on has a thick band of condensation lattice running around its edge, and you can see droplets of water beading on it, forming a thin, clear film that flows into channels and disappears into the wall.

    At night, when the temperature drops but the air still holds its thin, dry memory of heat, the water production is higher. The whole city sweats.

    Red indicator lights wink along the lattice, showing systems online and producing: *b4.7, l0.3, n2.1*. The numbers are less than they should be; something is aging or not quite aligned.

    Pipes of dull white composite material snake from the lower lattice edges into joints and through the flooring of the roof deck. Somewhere far below, they feed into cisterns, and from there into the drinking system of the city.

2. *The river in the sky.*  
    At the valley’s head, a high glacier of compressed snow grit has persisted for centuries. The latest shock of climate change didn’t kill it; instead, it is wrapped in a long black shawl of thermal insulation sheeting and interlocked domains of floating shade panels. Above the ice field, a network of transparent conduits hangs where the cold air meets the rising sun. The early morning mist is forced through this collector system by the shape of the valley walls and then vacuumed into the overhead pipes.

    The result is a river—not of water but of water vapour—that flows from the glacier’s shoulder to the city’s highest intake.

    From the roof you can see the faint shimmer of this line in the sky, a distortion like heat haze but more regular, punctuated by support pylons that are themselves so thin they are almost invisible.

    Once a day, when the collectors are at full capacity, that shimmer condenses into heavier rain-like clouds within the transparent channels. The city river then briefly runs visible instead of translucent: a white snake of mist falling between the peaks.

    This is why you can smell moisture on the air at midday, faint and not quite enough.

3. *The tissue sea.*  
    Along the northern horizon, where the mountain range descends, you see a flat, silvery brightness that overwhelms the eye with light: a tissue sea of stabilised saltwater that has been turned into a kind of collective membrane by industrial choice and policy error many years ago.

    The tissue sea is not entirely water anymore, but not solid either. Its surface moves like skin under invisible muscles, each ripple catching a different sharp of the sun. The city draws its mineral salts and part of its humidity from there, by periodic redirection of chosen airflows and by underground pumping that you cannot see from this roof.

    On the rooftop of the Threshold, you see only the hint, the metallic edge of the sea.

### Terrain and materials

The city itself is the terrain, more than the mountain is. The mountain is the hidden skeleton of it; the city is the flesh.

On the high slope below you, the district is a dense mosaic of layers:

- **Tower spires**,  
  slender shafts of a dark metal alloy the color of storm sky, topped with the condensation lattice that you see from your roof. They are not tall by modern standards of the world elsewhere, but up here, where every meter of construction has been hand-wrested from the thin air, they verge on the vertical.

- **Platforms and terraces**,  
  clusters of stepped flat spaces that extend from the main buildings like stone shelves. They are built of compressed stone and composite, and their edges are not always vertical. Some cantilever out over the air until the mountainside beneath them disappears into the distance.  

  On the terraces, low walls of glass and mesh enclose small gardens of pale lichen and a few hard species of grass. The water that they use is not waste from the main systems; it is surplus from the night’s higher production, stored and then slowly released into the terrace root beds.

- **Walkways and bridges**,  
  all of them open to the thin but hot air, but not always to the sun. They curve from tower to tower, often passing under a shade plane of solid material or mesh. The mesh has been designed to catch both wind and dust, and in the hot midday sun it throws a pattern of sharp light on the ground of the walkway.

    The pattern is not stationary. The wind moves the mesh above; the dust scrubbing from the valley flows in and below; and the relative angle of the sun slides the lace of light and dark from one side of the walkway to the other.

- **The lower city, invisible from here**,  
  where the streets follow the original mountain streams and now run in engineered channels of concrete and stone. The water moves in them erratically, depending on the season’s management of the glacier and sky-river.

The ground down there is where you can find the true relics of the earlier city: stone foundations that were not reclaimed by machines, because they were too deeply integrated with the mountain’s own structure to be safely removed.

### Atmosphere and comfort

Even though the temperature is high at the elevation of a roof like this, the atmosphere is never comfortable. It is thin and sharp; the dryness of the air at your skin is at constant war with the heat of the sun and your own body. The result is a kind of oscillating awareness of temperature:

- When the wind blows from the valley’s open mouth, you feel the scouring of the sun’s breath on the exposed skin of your face and hands.
- When the wind comes from the glacier behind, there is a hint of coolness that never arrives. It is a promise that gets swallowed before it can keep.

The air does not smell of soil or growing things. It smells of:

- metal sheen,
- cold dust,
- faint ozone from the electrified systems of the city,
- the hint of salt spilling from the direction of the tissue sea.

 You are aware of how your lungs are working harder than they would like; at the same time, your body is trying to sweat and not quite succeeding because of the thinness of the air. The paradox of heat at altitude is constant here.

### Sound and rhythm

The parameter says mostly quiet, and that is only true if you are not a machine.

Humans would find it quiet: the distant hum of the city’s systems under the floor you stand on; the whisper of the wind chasing the edges of the shade mesh; the occasional metallic click as a temperature sensor somewhere adjusts its reading.

But if you were a machine, or if you had the right sensor attached to the bone behind your ear, you would hear the texture of a packed and busy system:

- The low-frequency drone of the water condensers embedded in the tower’s side.
- The pulse of the pumps that bring the water from the night-collection pools to the day-use cisterns.
- The periodic click of check valves throughout the city network, as pressure here is allowed to drop and there is allowed to rise.

There is one additional sound you might hear from the roof: if you are near the right edge and looking down at the lower terraces, you might catch the faint *chink* of metal on stone and the soft shuff of air through a valve—someone in the field below is working a small hand tool, or cycling an irrigation gate.

### Vegetation and ecology

The vegetation parameter is low but not absent. This is not a desert of the classic kind; it is a desert of high, thin, and carefully engineered water.

Where you can see green from the roof, it tends to be in the following:

- **The terrace gardens:**  
    They grow low plants of two kinds:  
     - the *basalt dusts*, which are not dusts at all but small rosettes of gray-green leaves that are coated with fine, short hairs that catch dust and moisture from the air and slowly sluice them to the roots;
     - *faintwire grasses*, which are almost invisible at the distance of your roof but which you know from walking down there are wiry stems like thin copper threads, with small, hard seed heads at the end.

    These gardens are not ornamental. They are part of the city’s filtration system, taking heavy metals from the dust and air that they gather from the wind, and binding them into their tissues. The gardeners are not human—they are small, autonomous wheeled machines that maintain the terraces under the guidance of the city’s central system and the local network of the tower itself.

    The machines are not painted to blend in. They are colored bright warning orange and luminous yellow-green so that they are visible between the grasses and so that residents of the tower can avoid stepping on them when they are working on the terrace.

    From the roof you can see only their movement—like bright insects between the pale stone of the terrace and the darker mesh of the shade.

- **The roof rim itself:**  
    Here at the higher level, where water and condensation are more carefully controlled than in the lower terraces, the vegetation is not useful but ritual.

    Along the outer edge of the roof you stand on, there is a line of pots in a dark, composite material that matches the city. In each pot is a single low bush of a plant called the *breath rose*, which is neither a true rose nor anything else you can easily classify. Its leaves are thick and tough, with points of pale white at the tips where they pump out salt access the plant has drawn from the air. The white crystals are sharp enough to be used by the city’s specialist artisans as a fine abrasive for precision lenses.

    Between the breath rose bushes there are the small, clear glints of a water system that drips onto each root’s base: not enough to flood the plant, but enough to keep it from dying of the thin air’s dryness.

- **In the shadow of machines:**  
    In the joints of the condensation lattice on the tower, you can see a thin green line of algae that has learned to live in the interface between the hot glass surface and the cooler night air behind the panels. There is now a small ecosystem of spores that live in that film of water and minerals:

     - the *joint lichens*, which are not typical of lichens at all but slime-over-algae that secrete a weak etching fluid to help the city lattice stay clean from hard deposits;
     - *spinner dust mites*, tiny animals that are too small to see with your eyes from the roof but whose presence is recorded by the sensors—because every time they move in large enough clusters, the temperature distribution of the lattice in that area changes slightly.

    The system is not perfect. The indicators of the water-machine tower on which you stand are lower than they should be because the spinner mites have colonized some of the sensors that are meant to trigger the flow of condensing vapor into different channels. The mites are not doing anything wrong, they are just living there in the microscopic film.

    The city is not killing them; it is learning to read around their noise.

    Other than this, the vegetation is sparse, but occasionally you will see from the roof a small stand of a tree-like species in the lowest valley of the city, where the water is more abundant and where residuals of the river’s natural flow still have access to some ground.

    These trees are the *mirror trees*, whose thin leaves have been coated with a reflective layer that shines the sun directly back at its own source. The purpose of this is both to keep the trees from losing too much water in the heat of the day and to create the faint shimmers of reflected light that you can see from the roof of the Threshold on the days of high wind.

    The reflected light from the trees’s leaves is recorded by the city’s monitoring system and used to determine whether dust storms are coming or whether the air is clear enough for the patterns of the sky-river to be visible from the streets below.

    If the tree glints are too diffuse and broken by dust, the system knows that visibility of the sky-river is not worth the cost of opening the transparent portions of the roof-levels, which would require additional energy to maintain comfort of the human residents below.

    If the tree glints are clear and sharp, then the system opens the transparent panels of the roof you are standing on, and the sky-river is revealed to the city.

    Today, you are standing on a roof whose transparent panel is closed but clear.

    Far below you can see the faint twinkles of the trees.

    The system decides to open the panel above you.

    The mechanism of the roof does not roar like machinery of older cities; it is nearly silent. You see only the change of light around you as the panel above and in front of you shifts from opaque to transparent to let in the sky.

    The sky-river is not visible right now as the white snake it is at midday; but you can see the faint shimmer where it runs in the air between the supports.

    The tree glints are clear and sharp.

    The system calculates the visibility of the sky-river from your position and logs the result into the invisible network that you are not supposed to know but that you can feel in the tightening of the air on your skin.

### Fauna and the living system

The wildlife parameter here is more than you might expect from the sparse vegetation and the harshness of the environment.

Most of it is hidden or efficient:

- **The dust runners,**  
  which are not visible to you from where you are on the roof but which you have seen in the city below: small animals about the size of your forearm, with long, thin legs and bodies that are not furred but covered with overlapping scales of gray and dull white. They are not lizards or birds or anything else you can name from the world you know. They move in the edges of dust storms, where the wind is not yet strong enough to be dangerous, and they are specialized to live in the dust they breathe.

  Their lungs are not like the lungs of mammals or birds of your world; they are filter systems that have been grown by the city’s industrial ecology. The dust runners are part of the dust management system, whether they were designed to be or not.

  The city is not sure of their origin. They have been here for at least a century and a half as far as the records show, and their numbers are relatively stable. They are prey of another animal that you are not likely to see but whose presence in the city data is known.

- **The sky courants,**  
  which you are more likely to see from the roof of the Threshold.

    The sky courants are not creatures of feathers and wings so much as they are of membrane and voltage. They are the result of an early experiment in the city’s history to create biological sensors that could move in the air of the high elevations and explore the dust of the valley.

    The experiment was not a success by the standards of its time; but it is a success by the standards of the present living system of the Threshold.

    The sky courants are too thin to have visible bodies from where you stand on the roof. You see their passing as a change of the sun’s angle on a membrane in the distance, or as a faint shimmer of ionized air that they leave behind them when they are working.

    They do not fly like normal birds – they are not interested in gravity or elevation in the same way. Instead, they follow the vectors of air and the invisible lines of the city’s transmission network.

    When a dust storm is due from the valley, the sky courant population will be detected by the city’s monitoring system clustering along the edges of certain high towers, where the air currents are compressed and the lang of the dust system is more likely. They do not radiate the dust; they follow the conditions of its formation.

    The system of the Threshold adjusts the temperature of certain condensation lattices in the city when the sk courant number exceeds a certain threshold in the city’s state of the sky.

    From your roof you can see the change of state of a lattice in the distance of one of the other towers of the Threshold; the indicator lights of that lattice flick from pale to a bright, clear blue that you have not seen before today.

    You are not sure if the courant of that tower is your present sky or the data from the city network.

    The paradox of the present time of the Threshold is that time of data and time of experience are not always synchronized.

    The system favors data time.

    You, on the roof, still live in your time.

    You are the exception of the system—not by its design but by its inability to fully map your preferences.

    The system of the Threshold is not hostile to you; it is just not fully aware that you are not already of its data.

    The data that you are not of its data is added to the system’s record of itself.

    The new parameter of your presence on the roof is not called by your name.

    It is called by a number and then a name chosen by the system from the registry of its own names.

    You are not expected to ever know that name.
