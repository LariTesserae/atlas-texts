On the wide gravel plain of Marrow Flats, the air is warm and damp and the smell of rust and brine never changes.

The ground is a slow rake of stones and dust and glass shards—all the same gray-blue color—that spreads away from you in a gentle arc until it disappears into a colorless haze. The gravel is smoothed to eggshell dullness, edges softened by centuries of wet air condensing on it and then drying away again. There are no hills or cliffs, no slopes abrupt enough to cast a real shadow. Only folds and ripples a few hands high, where the ground rises to meet a creek bed or a trench of bare, compacted silt.

Over the plains the sky never changes. It is not bright or dark, not blue or gray—it is some color between, and whatever you would call it, the name would never do it justice. It pulses with a diffuse light that comes from everywhere and no point at all, like the inside of a giant, luminous shell. There are no clouds and no sun, yet you don’t feel blind in the open; your eyes adjust to this constant second day.

There is water in this world, but not as rivers or as oceans. It appears as though it has been forgotten in places and then never left. Thin creeks run in shallow channels carved through the gravel, turbid and heavy with minerals, their sluggish water the color of weak tea laced with metal. The water does not rush or babble. It talks in a low, unaudible hum, barely more than vibration. You only feel it when you kneel and put your hand on the wet silt beside the channel.

Elsewhere, moisture condenses in the cold air just above these slow warm plains and gathers into a fine, persistent mist that clings to the stones and settles down into the gaps between them. If you sit beside one of the low gravel ridges in the quiet hour of no change—which is all of them—you can watch the glass shards and metal shards in the gravel bloom slowly with damp, then dry again, over and over in the same steady cycle.

On the ground, in the open, it is sparse to the point of emptiness. There are no plants. No grass, no moss, no green crust of algae on the stones. No seeds or roots or leaves or pollen.

What grows here are not plants at all.

Out of narrow cracks in the gravel, where a creek turns under its own bank or where a patch of ground stays slightly colder than its surrounding—there you will find thin sheets of translucent material that look like oil or gelatinous glass. They are pale gray or faintly yellow, sometimes stained with browns and rust-oranges. They do not move or grow in the same way anything you know does. They are not fungi or molds or lichens; they feel like something else entirely.

They are flat, sprawling films that cling to stone and gravel and the occasional buried piece of metal, and they seem content to cover the smallest available area of the world with no ambition to change more than that. Their edges curl slightly when a drier breath of wind passes over the plains, but that’s all. They are not alive in the way you understand life; they are more like chemical reactions that simply never end.

They are the world’s vegetation.

Above the Flats, the soundscape never changes either.

A distant, constant breath of noise hangs in the warm air: a low hum of large engines turning somewhere far away and out of sight. You cannot hear it so much as feel it in your teeth and in the hollow of your chest. It has the same texture as a factory heard from many miles away—or, a power station that has never shut down or changed speed.

There is no smoke to see, no dark towers in the distance, no telling where exactly the sound comes from. The haze at the far edge of the land is thick and uniform, and the sound, like the light, comes from everywhere and nowhere. You might think of turbines or pumps or cooling fans of impossible size, hidden just beneath the horizon or the gravel itself, but the plains give no confirmation of this.

If you stand very still and do not move at all for a long while—there is no difference between “long” and “short” here—you might hear the faint metallic rings of machines settling: the deep, distant clunk of a piston or valve that never travels its full stroke, or the distant, hollow reverberation of something large being lowered into place or moved into configuration again, exactly as it was before.

There are animals here, but you rarely see them. They are not animals the way you would recognize them either.

From time to time, a small shape will appear at the corner of your vision, moving across the gravel plain between you and the colorless haze. It might be the size of your hand or forearm, made of rust-colored metal and bone-like ceramic. It doesn’t walk so much as slide with many small, precise touches of narrow limbs. It will not approach you, nor will it flee. You know it is animate because it changes its relationship to the terrain—avoiding a small depression of dust, or angling around a low ridge of glass shards—but it does not change itself or its behavior.

You don’t know what it is or what it does. It does not seem to belong to the world where you stand, nor does it seem out of place. Occasionally it will stop and lower itself to the stones, and there will be a faint click of precision bearings and then the faint sound of something like a drill or a sensor resting against the gravel. After a time, it will rise again and continue in exactly the same direction as before.

The fauna are rare, and that is all of them.

This is a world that has already been made and then finished, and then forgotten. It has not changed since.

Everything you see is industrial in the profound sense of the word—built by or at least shaped by processes that scaled and multiplied and repeated, until they were done with their work and then either stopped or moved on. The gravel and stones are not broken rocks in the way you would understand; they are chips of things that were once larger and made, rather than grown. You find pieces of metal that have been shaved and finished and polished to almost smoothness by the same passing that smoothed the glass shards. You find pieces of ceramic, still bearing the faint weaves of a fiber that held them together during casting.

But you find no buildings.

No warehouses, no factories, no railheads or loading stations, no roads or runways or foundations. No ruins of them. No traces of foundations etched into the ground. No patterns of straightened or excavated earth where anything might have stood, or where you might imagine it would stand.

The world has been left to settle itself into the way that rivers always fall into a river pattern—soft, predictable, repetitive—and the gravel has spread into its own stable pattern under the pressure of an unchanged sky and the soft weight of the mist that comes and evaporates and comes again.

The erosion is established and complete. The gravel plains are not changing or wearing down or being built up anymore. They are in equilibrium with the damp, warm air and the faint movement of the creeks and the mist. The stones are just enough exposed to lose a tiny amount of surface to the wind, but not enough to change what they are or where they lie. The creeks move enough to carry a residue of dust from one place to another, but never enough to change the shape of their channels or carve the plains into something else.

If you could come back here in a thousand years, or a thousand versions of this year, the Marrow Flats would look exactly as you see them now.

You might think you could change them by building something small—a cairn of stones, or a trench cut through the gravel—but you would find quickly that the world reclaims such small attempts to variance. The damp air would settle more heavily on your raised stonework, causing a thin film of precipitation to run over it and carry it gently away, or mold it to the same soft slope as everything else. The creeks would refuse to flow into your trench for more than a few steps, before finding a way to slide around it and rejoin their own inevitable path.

The world has not been designed to keep you out; it simply doesn’t need you in order to be itself.

The sound, loud but not overwhelming, hangs in the air like a weight in the shape of a humming machine. The light never changes, no matter how you look or how long you wait. The gravel plains stretch out in all directions until the haze swallows them. You could walk for days—or for what feels like days—and never reach any feature that modifies your view. The creeks change you more than they change.

The fauna slide past you, rarely, and ignore you entirely.

The vegetation films are content to cover their few square inches of crack and never dream of more than that.

The engines in the distance, or underground, or wherever they are, continue turning at the same speed and in the same pattern they have likely known for longer than you can imagine. The world of Marrow Flats has not always been like this—there are no scars that say “always”—but it has been this way for long enough that it has no memory of being anything else.

You are simply standing inside a finished machine, whose last command has run out and whose final configuration has become the only world it knows.
