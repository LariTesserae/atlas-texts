The world is a room submerged under something else.

Warmth comes first, then brightness. It is almost body-hot—feverish to the air of your old life, gentle now to your skin as if you were stepping back into amniotic fluid. The light is everywhere and unsparing: white bleached to the color of itself, reflecting and re-reflecting from every surface until you cannot see where it starts or ends.

When you look down you see the water, but not the way you remember oceans.

It is a floor three inches deep.

Step and you enter a ceiling three inches deep.

Move far enough and you realize the floor was ceiling all along; the room was inverted, you the intruder walking across the sky of another world. The water is so clear you can see every detail of the world below through it, as if through a window, except the window moves in slow, thick ripples when you touch it.

The room itself is not large—maybe ten strides wide, eight long, if a stride were still something you could measure in the old world. You could call that a pocket, a hidden space, something that should be nested inside of something else, but there is no outside that you can find. No doors, no cracks, no seams in the walls where tools might have been forgotten.

The walls are gray.

At first they seem like stone, the same way the ocean once seemed like water—familiar, then not. On inspection they are too smooth, too regular, the same dull, unreflecting tone of poured concrete or fired ceramic, but they are neither of those things. They are not made of anything you have a name for, and yet you sense the intention of it: poured, shaped, allowed to set. This place is made.

Between the gray floor and the gray ceiling and the gray walls, there is not much else.

Light passes through the water layer, and through the film of water on every wall. You notice the walls are not truly vertical; they lean inward just enough to make you feel how they might want to close like a jaw. You notice the air is not truly air: it smells faintly of wet clay and old salt and something else, like green leaves before you have named them as vegetation.

When you let your eyes unfocus, you see the grown things.

They are sparse, almost not there at all. At first you think they are cracks in the world—fine hairline fractures where the ceiling has begun to fail. Then you see that the cracks are lines of growth, too thin and regular to be accidental.

They are not plants.

You are under the water layer when you first understand them; you are looking up at their undersides through the clear medium. The structures are dark filaments, branching like a diagram of river deltas or human lungs, clinging to the ceiling with the patient immobility of coral that has never known sunlight. The filaments are so thin they could be drawn in ink, and they are not alive in the usual sense; but they are most definitely not the material of the room.

They were not poured here.

They have been, and you spend the next few heartbeats not sure which of those words is true.

They are also the only color you can see. The filaments that are closest to the actual room material are gray-black and almost indistinguishable from it, but as they branch out into the water layer—they are not in the water, they are under the water layer, right under the surface facing you—they slowly lighten to a faint algae green, then to a pale yellow, then, at their very tips, to filaments that are almost white.

The thinnest endings of the structures pierce the water layer itself.

You watch the place where a tip of one white filament touches the bottom of the water ceiling. In that infinitesimal point of contact, tiny bubbles form and detach, rising toward you with the delayed gravity of dreams. They enter your world of air and warmth and sound indistinguishable.

Sound you notice now, because it is loud.

At first you thought it was silence, or the blood in your ears, the same way you thought the walls were stone. The room is not loud the way an ocean storm is loud; it is loud like being inside a machine whose heart you never hear. You have become that heart. The sound is a low, unchanging, percussive thing—a deep and distant thump that you cannot feel in your feet but only in your chest, where your own rhythm presses to match it.

The thump comes at the same interval every time, like something measured with a tool people might have once used before they had metal.

Thump.

You wait.

Thump.

You can't find the source. The sound comes from the walls, and from the water ceiling, and from the floor, and from inside the bone box of your skull where you also cannot find doors.

When you look at the structures again, you notice that the thump does something to them.

Once per cycle, the filaments pulse slightly, the same way a muscle might under skin. The color and light do not change; the world does not warm or sharpen or dim. Instead the knots of filament where several branches join together swell slightly, then relax.

In the water layer above, you see the tiny bubble that you watched before finally reach its surface.

You think it will pop into the air you breathe, but it doesn't. Instead it makes contact with the lowest of the thin structures—the ones that cross from one wall to another, not the floor or ceiling—and you realize that those are not cracks at all but the thinnest strands of filament you have seen yet: a single thread of fiberglass, maybe, or spiderweb that has been saturated with resin.

The bubble sticks to the strand of filament.

Time passes.

You don't know how much. The light refuses to change; the room doesn't shift to the old madness of day and night. Instead there is only the unchanging, overbright world, and the thump, and the water layer ticking slowly.

Eventually you notice that the strand where the bubble stuck has thickened slightly.

It is not “eventually” in the way you might mean, outside. There is no outside, here. There are only rests between the thumps, and then you look at the strand again and cannot remember if it was always that thick. You cannot remember, either, if the room was always this size.

The erosion of memory is not the same as erosion of stone, but it is the only erosion you can find. The walls are not worn away; nothing has rounded; there are no smooth channels of water cutting down slopes into deltas.
