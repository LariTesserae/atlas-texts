The world is sound underwater.  

Not sound *like* underwater—the dull muffling of a diver’s ear—but the world as sound that has sunk, heavy and saturated, into a medium far thicker than air.  

You hear it before you see it: a deep, low-frequency roar that rises continuously just out of tune, like a thousand industrial generators arguing with each other. The roar is threaded through with higher frequencies—a metallic dendritic ringing, like cables being struck by hammers, like glass being made in mid-air, like turbines being born and torn apart in the span of a breath. The sound is *deafening*, but oddly coherent; it is not random noise. It’s organized—like a storm, but one that has learned counterpoint.

The air itself is aqueous. Not water in the sense of rain or ocean waves but water as atmosphere: a humid, heavy fog that clings like skin, condensing into hot droplets on your clothes and your face. The medium is nearly liquid, and visibility is poor—your world is reduced to a radius of ten or twenty meters. Beyond that, the world fades into white-gray noise. The moisture feels like warm breath on your neck. It tastes faintly of salt and ozone, like factory air made into breath.

This is a lowland world. The ground beneath your feet is barely there at all—more of an idea than a definite surface. You stand on a shallowly submerged stone platform that feels newly carved, its edges sharp and precise, not blunted by time. The water rises almost to your knees, but it doesn’t behave like ordinary water. It’s layered: thick, warm, translucent liquid that glows faintly from beneath, like bioluminescent fog trapped in glass. The light doesn’t come from above; there is no sun or moon. Instead, faint haloes bloom beneath the surface in irregular intervals—pale blue-green streaks that pulse and fade like faulty neon signs at the bottom of the sea.

### Terrain and Materials

The landscape is a young archipelago—or rather, a place that might once become an archipelago, if time ever settles into linearity here.

Jagged islands of stone and metal rise abruptly from the shallow water. They don’t look carved by rivers or worn by waves; they look *sprung*, like newly forged teeth emerging from a giant’s jaw. The rocks are sharp-edged and crystalline, their internal surfaces shot through with iridescent veins that flicker between black and electric blue, depending on how the light beneath them chooses to interpret color.

Between the islands are narrow channels where water runs deeper, forming improvised lanes that flow into nowhere. The water doesn’t always flow in the same direction; sometimes it flows in circles around small, emergent landforms that seem almost to purposefully create themselves at your approach. You see a stone mound rise a few centimeters higher as you watch it, like it’s responding to your vision—but then it collapses again into frothing water as the sound of a distant generator falters and rephrases itself.

Metal structures are scattered through the islands like partial skeletons—graceful curves of steel and iron that twist into space before suddenly stopping, as if someone forgot to finish designing them. Here and there, a spindly tower rises ten or fifteen meters, then ends abruptly in mid-air, its edges blazing like molten metal momentarily frozen. The structures glow faintly along their rims, casting uneven reflections onto the water beneath them.

The ground is uneven, composed of layered basalt and metal slag fused together into a new kind of composite. It’s not old enough to have a name or history of weathering; it feels as if it were made yesterday. Or perhaps today. Or tomorrow.

### Atmosphere and Light

The light is low and diffuse, like the world is lit from inside a storm lantern. Each fleeting light source feels like a match struck underwater—brief, intense, and doomed to fizzle out.

Above the horizon, the sky—if it exists—doesn’t show itself. Replacing it is a ceiling of dense, streaming moisture that behaves like vertical rain that never hits the ground. Water droplets hang suspended mid-air, forming curtains of atmosphere that drip and reform and drip again. The water droplets occasionally synchronize into strange patterns: a wave of condensation running across the air, like a signal moving through a cloud.

The air feels thick enough to part your hand through. Clouds of warm mist roll over the stone platforms in slow waves, sometimes condensing into sudden drizzles that fall upside-down in mid-channel, suspended droplets flickering in and out of visibility like faulty pixels.

The temperature is warm but not oppressive—like a fevered world breathing against your skin. Temperature gradients are unstable; you feel a sudden hot rush of air near a metal tower, then a cooler one as you step away from it, as if the heat itself is forming momentary fluid pockets the way air does over fires.

There is no day or night. There are only rhythmic fluctuations in the glow beneath the water: periods of collective dimming when the sound seems to lower a few semitone, then a sudden surge of brilliance and volume when color bleaches, and the world becomes briefly a blinding white noise of light and sound.

### Vegetation—If You Can Call It That

The world is lush with *grown* things, but not with plants as you know them.

Cabled vines of a translucent, flexible material grow from the rock platforms. They don’t look organic—more like industrial tubing grown into roots. The vines are glassy and dark green, threaded with faint yellow luminescence that pulses like veins in time with the underlight glow. They branch out in intricate, precise geometrical patterns, like architectural diagrams drawn into living matter.

The vines curl into columns that meet themselves in mid-air and begin forming canopies, like scaffolding of a future forest. Each leaflet is a thin membrane of translucent material that quivers with the roar of the world. When a droplet of water hits them, they don’t drip—instead, they emulsify the droplet into a luminous haze that seems to *feed* the leaf itself, like fuel taken into a system.
