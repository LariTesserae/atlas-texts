In cool darkness a world of water rests inside a shell of built matter.

Above you rises a ceiling so low and unbroken it seems to be the sky: a smooth plane of pale gray stone, faintly luminous with trapped bioluminescence, barely visible in the dim. It curves slightly down at the far edges into the slow, massive ribs of a wall that must run for kilometers with no visible break, making clear this whole place is a container, an artifact made by something now long gone.

That wall is the oldest surface you see. It is not sculpted stone but a single knotted architecture of root, stem and bone that has long since forgotten where it begins or ends. Branches, some thick as city streets, weave into one another and then fuse into a seamless shell of dull, pale wood-skin, hard and polished by time into something like petrified tree. The joints are smoothed by eras. No knothole or seam is sharp anymore; the edges look like long, low hills of bone, and everything slopes gently, as if the room itself is slowly relaxing into its final sleep.

The water itself is the main body of the world. It is salt-thick and cool, almost motionless, but you feel its slow weight through every small shift you make. It fills this space so deeply that you cannot see the bottom—if there is one. Instead of the floor of a basin, you see only down and down and down into liquid black, where the faint luminescence of the ceiling breaks on particles and dust, and then nothing. The chamber feels like an ocean locked inside a shell.

This is not a stormy ocean. It is not a violent ocean. It is a tired ocean. It has been here for so long that its currents have settled down into a few lazy loops, like the thoughts an old mind runs over and over again. There are no waves here, no sudden shifts of temperature or pressure, no gradients to be balanced out. The water is in perfect equilibrium with the room around it. It doesn't carve new channels into anything; nothing has much to give away anymore. The erosion is long over and what is left is the final shape of things.

Everywhere, plants are the world material.

The ceiling you are under isn't stone but something else: a thin, tightly-woven mat of algal filaments and mosses, pressed into a hard membrane by weight and time and absence of change. You can see darker, older threads in the faint glow, like scars in the sky, where patches of the system must have died and been replaced centuries or millennia ago, but there are no sharp changes of species or pattern. One form of vegetation steps into the space another left, and the pattern never breaks.

Hanging down from that ceiling are thick braids of something that might be roots, or might be leaves, or might be both. They are not like any forest you have seen. They are not small trees reaching down; instead, they are like long rivers of dark matter, descending into the murky depths below. They don't branch very much. They simply flow, from one root to another root, until they all merge and become part of the walls of the room. The architecture of the world is clearly, unnervingly alive, but so slow that you cannot feel it change at all.

There are gaps in this root-river forest, but you are not aware of them until you move into them. The rarity of those spaces makes sense of the sparse parameter. The living root-columns are not absolutely everywhere; there are open stretches of water between them where nothing else reaches to fill the volume. But you're not alone in them.

The fish are the reason the sound is so loud.

Once you pause to listen, the sound is not the noise of water itself—which flows quietly if you do not force it—but the sound of countless teeth, bones, and shells grinding against each other in the dark. You keep hearing it as you go deeper, and the longer you listen, the more you understand it.

There are schools of small fish so thick and numerous that they fill entire regions like dark clouds drifting from roof to riverbed root to invisible wall. They are not especially colorful, but you can't see color anyway in this dim light. Instead, you see their shape as a disturbance of the texture of the world itself, like a moving shadow of complex grain. The sound of their feeding is not the splash of water, but the crunch of tiny mouths scraping and chewing on the surfaces of the algae root, on the slow-flow of growth matter, and sometimes on each other—when one of the bigger fish slip through the cloud.

Some of the fish are much longer than you are, but they move like drifting logs rather than like predators of prey. They are all slow muscle. There are eel-like things with wide heads and open mouths that simply float through the water, letting the smaller forms of life pluck parasites from their skin in exchange for nothing more than tolerance of being near them. They are in the middle of a food chain that has been in stable motion for so long that no step of it is in any hurry to complete.

Some of the fish have teeth on their outside—shell-like ridges of enamel-like bone that clatter against each other as they swim close to the root walls. It is that sound, more than anything else, that makes up the roar of the world: the soft, constant *click-click-click*, the sound of thousands of tiny shells scraping old root-bark polished by ages of eating.

The sound is not harsh or distressing. It is more like the deep sound of a bronze machine that has been running in the background of an age-old city for so long that you no longer notice it until it is your turn to sleep near it. It is so loud because you have never had anything to interrupt it.

The temperature is cool, but not cold enough to be a shock. It is exactly the same everywhere you move. This is not a place designed by accident or carelessness to be cool. It has been designed to be exactly this temperature, and to maintain it forever, without the need for any control device. The material itself does the work of temperature exchange so smoothly that no machine is needed.

The elevation of the room is strange because you feel yourself high over the unseen floor, but you are not in open sky. In the few open spaces between massive root forests and wall ribs, you can feel yourself exposed over a deep drop, but this height doesn't open onto sky; it opens onto more water. You are not at the top of the world, but somewhere deep within its body, and the height above you is very much like the height below you.

Still, in the middle of that open space, you feel like you are standing at the ceiling of a deep pit with no bottom. The feeling of being elevated over the abyss is strong, even though you are not outside the planet itself, but inside a structure that has taken its place.

There are small structures on the root surfaces that you initially think must be artificial tools: neat rows of little holes drilled into the root walls, perfectly aligned; narrow flattened channels that seem to have been carefully smoothed by tools; bridges of welded root between larger root stems.

As you get closer and let your imagination run along the lines of what you see, you realize that none of these things require technology at all.

The holes are worm-burrows, rows and rows of them, each one exactly the same size and shape as the others because they are made by the same body repeating the same instinct over and over again. The worms leave a thin mucus of some sort in the walls of their holes, and after time, that mucus hardens into the same kind of shell-skin as you saw on the fish, and turns into a permanent lining of the room. The line of holes is hard to see from far away; it is only when you are near enough that you can see how each opening is a perfectly circular hole in the world root, like a window into nothing.

The channels along the roots are not cut; they are grown by the tree itself into something like gutters or troughs. The way of the world is such that the water flow, though slow, rests along these lines, and the fish, without being aware of it, lay their eggs in the troughs that the water has already chosen for them. The troughs are not geometric. They flow like the lines of a map of a river system on the surface of a planet, but they are spiraled around the roots of a tree whose branches are also the walls of the world.

Some bridges between root-stems are not grown by the tree, but by other kinds of organisms. You notice that a thin, web-like structure hangs between one root and another, and at first you think it might be spider-silk. But as you look closer, you see that it is not made of threads. It is made of tiny white-shelled animals, each no longer than your fingernail, each linked by its shell to the next in a long welded chain. The animals are all alive, but all of them are also holding on to each other so tightly they have fused into a single structure of chain.

From time to time, one of the animals in the chain dies, and its shell is broken by the pressure of the water around it. But then the next generation takes up its position, and the new child shell grows into the shape of the broken one, so the chain never loses its pattern. The bridge of animals is not a tool in the sense of something you would pick up and use with your hand, but it is clearly a tool in the sense of a function that has been organized by life itself and not by any machine mind.

Nothing else in the room requires technology of any kind; there are no gears, no engines, no panels of any kind to be controlled. There is not sudden switch-on of light or change of temperature that you have ever seen or heard of in this world's history. The system started itself and then slowly warmed into full shape, and once it reached full shape, it stopped adding.

The sound of the world is not a sound you need to hear in order to understand yourself. It is not a message. It is simply the sound of matter moving against matter, as it has been for perhaps longer than you can imagine.

We are at the near end of time. The water has chosen its shape and the roots of the world have chosen their arrangement of support and food. The fish, in their infinite numbers and forms, have been the same mix of the same types for so long that no new species has been added to the room in memory.

The sparse nature of the space exists between the root rivers and fish schools. There are open pools of water between these root-pillars, but open here doesn't mean empty. It means open enough to swim through without being caught by massive root-forest, or pulled into the structure of the shell-bridges of animals.

You can't see the far end of the room from the middle, but you can feel the roof of it everywhere, and the walls occasionally curve in close enough to make clear how narrow the channel is between their root-skin.

The region is not microscopic. It is not something you can hold in your hand. It is more like a valley, or a town-sized cavern of wood and bone and water, all pulled into shape by long, slow forces of growth and time and patient erosion.

The erosion in this world is not of stone by water. Nothing is stone anymore except in memory. The erosion is of the soft structures of living matter. The roots of the world, being the oldest living matter here, have been eaten by generations of animals and micro-films, so that their surfaces are polished smooth. You can't see any sharp edges on any of the older roots.

In the newer root structures, which are not young by any measure of time on your own world, there are still edges and corners of a sort. You see, where a root-strand has forced itself through a layer of older material and become part of the wall, that there are still soft lines where different matter types meet. But even those lines are softened by time, and the fish chew on them in such slow, careful waves that everything gets slowly made into the same substance.

There are no seasons in this world. There is no time of day when light is stronger or weaker than it is now. There is no time when the temperature is more or less cool than it is now. There is no time when the water pressure or chemistry of the room's fluid is different than it is now. The world of water and plants and roots and roof-shell is not in a cycle of change, but in a cycle of repeated repetition of the same state.

The dynamic changes of the world of open sky and open surface, where cycles of birth and erosion and growth and collapse and rebuilding and changing climate are normal, were things that happened before this room became itself. Now it is too late for them to happen again.

The room of water and living root is not a young world. It is not old in the sense of being near the end of its life span; it is old in the sense that it has settled into a form it will hold forever if not disturbed by outside force. The forces of growth and dissolution and erosion and rebuilding and change of any kind have been brought into such perfect balance that they might as well be frozen.

Yet you can't see the end of that room of water.

There are long distances between you and the visible wall of root and shell. You are not at the edge of the region; you are far enough from the edge of it that you can't see clearly where any of the sides of the room meet. You see only roof of the world and root of the world and open-water of the world, and the sound of fish eating the shell-skin of the room.

You can't move across the surface of the planet's sea toward open air and different coasts and different cities and different types of forest and open sky and changing weather and open sun and moon and stars and seasons and change of day into night.
