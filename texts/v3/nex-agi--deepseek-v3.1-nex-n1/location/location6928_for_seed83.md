At the end of a disused spur tunnel, under the weight of the city, the Engine Room sits like a heart buried too deep to be found.

Calling it a room is an act of mercy to the tongue; in truth it is a chamber only in the sense that the body has chambers: not a room but a container, something made to hold something else. The ceiling is not true stone but an engineered shell of interlocking ribs: vault after vault of ridged concrete drawn into scallops and hollows, meeting like the palms of two hands pressed together in prayer. The arches are black with the years of controlled heat. The feeling of the place is not ruinous but finished—settled, as an old tree might settle into its shape, or an old bone into its ache.

It is hot. Not the hot of an open hearth or a sunlit day, but the slow, close hot of something kept at a low boil for a long time. The air does not move so much as it thickens and rests; that heat hasn’t changed in decades, maybe centuries. It is carried up by worn stone steps and the coiled rails of the service ladder and, when there are still men to run it, the steep stairway of the inspection shaft, but it never quite escapes the labyrinth of the tunnel network. It is the heat of a system that knows only to persist.

The water here is never bold enough to be rain or river; it is the ghost of water. The main tunnel that feeds into the Engine Room has a metal gutter set along its floor, maybe the width of a man’s forearm, and in this gutter a thin current of pale, clear liquid flows, always. It does not splash, or bubble, or hurry anywhere. It simply seeps from the thin crack between the wall’s last brick and the first iron plate of the engine chamber, tracing a line of polished rust in its bed. The gutter itself is not natural—the course of the water follows the path of least resistance, but the path itself has been painstakingly carved into the world.

The condensation of years paints the walls of the Engine Room itself. The ceiling is not so wet as to drip, but not dry enough to be free of its own sweat: a thin film of moisture clings to the concrete, faintly shimmering where the light catches the curve of the vaults. In the corners of the highest arches, where the heat of the machine lingers longest and the stone above has not forgotten the cold mass of the earth around it, there are dark stains in the shapes of fingers or tears. Everywhere you are aware of water being recycled, not lost or added to but preserved in faint, invisible cycles.

If there is vegetation, it does not look like it. On the lower portions of the wall, where brick and concrete give way to iron plating and the occasional rivet, the moisture is old enough and regular enough that there is sometimes a thin, slick film of something like mildew or algae, not green enough to be brave but the color of weak tea and the texture of wet paper. It never spreads to cover the walls, but neither does it entirely disappear. It exists the way the damp of a cellar exists: undeniable, not quite alive enough to be called a presence, and not quite dead enough to be ignored.

There is not much else alive that walks or crawls here. The occasional engineer might come down the ladder, or the quarterly inspector, whose pens are always one ink color away from running dry. They might bring a lantern with a battery of iron and glass and the faint chemical whiff of wonders that are not coal, yet not quite magic. If there are animals, they are not many: a thin, translucent millipede edging along the gutter, its body not quite opaque, the world around it visible as a haze through its shell; maybe a spider that has legs so long they seem like extensions of the rust itself.

The engine itself is what fills the room, not merely because of its size but because of the absence of anything else to take the eye’s attention. It is not one of those monstrous boilers that might occupy the machine halls of the above-world; you are below the surface of all that. The scale of the Engine Room is the scale of a cathedral’s transept, maybe twice or three times the length of a railcar, but tight in the way of things made to be knelt and crawled around. The engine itself is not colossal in the absurd, sense-defying sense; but it is dense enough, layered enough, that if you stood before it, you would not know where its machine ended and the world’s machine began.

It is built of iron and brass and some other alloys that do not define themselves easily in the faint light of the gas jets that line the wall. The body of the engine is not a single block but a stack of modules: each module might be the size of a large trunk if trunks were square and welded to one another with the brutal efficiency of a world that has outgrown its own need for ornament. There are no flourishes or decorative scrolls here; the only decoration is the pattern of the bolts themselves, the rows of rivets marching like teeth into the shadow of the next machine-piece.

Valves and glass pressure gauges protrude from the engine’s surface at strange intervals. The gauges have not been broken or cracked for many years; whatever fragile thing they measured might still be known to themselves, but not seen by the old world. Strange inscriptions might once have been written on their labels, but the heat and the damp and the time have turned the ink into stains that blur toward the center of the glass. Maybe they are in the language of the city’s artificers, maybe in some mechanical language meant not for humans but for the machine itself; maybe they are images of hands and not letters at all.

The plates of the engine have not corroded in the conventional sense of the word. There is no violent peeling of rust or chalk of disrepair; rather the metal is dulled the way old silver might be dulled by time and not neglect. The heat of the machine has been the same for so long that the surface of the iron and brass and steel might as well be the surface of any stone that has been wearing the same route for centuries. The balance of heat and damp and pressure might almost be an environment in itself, not quite oxygen and not quite nitrogen, but something else altogether that slowly changes the metal at a scale the human eye cannot grok.

Sound is not absent here, but it is thin. The engine might, at long intervals, be sensed to hum—not a mechanical noise so much as a low, continuous pressure against the eardrums that comes not so much from the air as the bone itself. Maybe once every two minutes, or maybe not. Maybe it is the sound of the city itself settling into the bedrock of the world, or maybe there is a flywheel deep inside that engine that still turns to some undiscovered rhythm. The sound never swells enough to be called a noise. It is merely the presence of sound the way moisture might be the presence of water.

The world this Engine Room serves is not obvious, but the lines of its service are not entirely hidden. The narrow rails that run into and out of the chamber are not for the convenience of men or heavy matériel; they are for the slow, deliberate, and not-quite-human maintenance of the machine itself.
