Deep in the warm water of an ancient sea, there is a buried machine the size of a small room.

It has been running for longer than anyone can remember. Longer than the language in which “memory” is spoken. The great ocean above and around it has been liquid for so long that all its peaks have been ground down to smooth currents and low ridges, and the world’s edges are so far-flung and faint that this place is, to the world, nothing but a speck of pressure in the dark.

Yet here, inside the machine, everything remains.

***

You don’t so much see the chamber from the outside as feel it from the inside: a sudden hunger of structured space made in the midst of unstructured sea. All the world around it is dark water, thick with wandering salts and minerals, but the machine persists, a vertical cylinder of worn ceramic plating and thick ribs of alloy, bolted together with seams grown into one another by the years. Each plate is inscribed with concentric patterns of channels and grooves, like a tree ring but smaller than a hair’s width, stamped by engines whose names are no longer written anywhere at all.

The chamber itself is not large—maybe four meters tall and three wide. It is not a room designed for people to stand in. It is a culture tank for something else: a place of staying and renewing, rather than passing through.

It is lit by a ring of light at the waterline that never shifts: a faintly warm, white glow that radiates light from a thin tube of plasma sealed in glass. The light itself seems constrained by an invisible boundary. It does not shine out very far into the ocean; if you were to look at the machine from afar, you would see no more than a soft smudge, a wavering of the water that might be a trick of the current.

For something so completely artificial, the room feels unsettlingly alive.

***

The water inside the chamber is not exactly water.

It is the same fluid that fills the ocean outside, but it has been rearranged. The salts have been herded into particular ratios and rhythms; the trace minerals are corralled into shells that glitter like dust in the light. Tiny currents turn through it, foams of microlayer and fraction of boundary that make themselves visible as shifting oil-in-water iridescence without the oil.

Through the center of the chamber hangs a single column of vegetation, thick as a human torso and deeply rooted in the baseplate. It is not a tree, though it has something like bark: a tessellation of overlapping plates of cellulose-rich polymer that look like they have been printed, rather than grown. Each plate is a different shade of dull-green to deep-blue-black, and patterns of holes and slots line them—pores by the thousands, each one just a fraction of a millimeter across.

From this “bark” grow fronds of soft matter, not fibrous but gelatinous, translucent, and flexible. They unfold in spirals, like ferns that have learned to be waterweeds instead. They move with a slow, continuous shiver, not because of turbulent flow but because of something else: a rhythmic pulse of the column itself, as though it were breathing.

The fronds, each only a few centimeters long and thin as a hair, tilt together in the current, then separate, like the fingers of a hand opening and closing when no one is looking.

The column of vegetation is lush by one of the world’s oldest measures: not dense by volume of mass, but by density of structures. Every millimeter of its surface is covered with something that is processing, something that is taking, giving, folding. The leaves are not distinct leaves; they are bouts of surface tension made into living matter, and they are constantly dissolving themselves back into the chamber and being replaced by new growths from the factory at the column’s core.

Around the central column, the rest of the chamber is empty.

The water is not crowded by other plants. There are no roots to tangle, no vines to choke. The light of the ring is not bright enough to support another layer of growth. It is exactly as bright as it needs to be for this column and no more.

The space between the column and the walls is not “nothing,” but it is sparse by design: wide enough for water to circulate, to be rearranged by the slow pumping of the machine’s hidden heart.

The machinery of the chamber is not visible as a mechanism of pistons and gears. It has been disguised by time and by the necessities of living underwater.

The baseplate into which the column descends is not a flat surface of steel; it is a thick slab of permeable composite that has lost its name over the long years. It is more bone than cement, more coral than concrete. Channels run through it, not in straight lines but in curves that have been optimized by the water’s passage through eons of continuous flow. The channels are not perfectly clean; they are lined with a thin film of something white that flakes and curls slowly, like the slow regurgitation of a storm of microscopic shells.

The walls of the chamber, which you only really see by looking at the light of the ring from the inside, are not smooth cylinders either. They are formed of overlapping tiles of ceramic that are each a slightly different color—some darker green, some gray, almost none of them exactly the same—because they were made by different machines in different cycles of the world. Each of these tiles is a page of history: an etched landscape of microstructures, whose design can no longer be interpreted.

But the function you can feel.

Near the base of the wall closest to the column’s roots, a thin blade of satin-like metal is set into the ceramic. It is not a cutting edge; the edge was long ago corroded into a line of crystal ridges. This blade is a transducer—something that translates movement of water into instruction of the machine it once used to be.

When the water in the chamber presses slightly more than it should, or arrives slightly warmer than the last cycle, the blade shivers. This shiver propagates through the wall in the form of subtle changes in pressure of microfluid lines that go nowhere you can see; but eventually, according to some long-lost program of equilibrium, the response comes back as a new timing of the current.

The current itself is generated by a mechanism buried in the floor: not a motor, but a kind of slow peristalsis of the slab itself. The baseplate flexes, rhythmically, nearly imperceptibly, like the chest of a sleeping giant. The rhythm of that flex controls the speed of the water around the column and how often it washes the surface of the fronds.

At the top of the chamber, where the water ends and the glass of the view-port begins, there is a ring of thick metal that has slowly been colonized by a dark greenish growth of what, at first glance, looks like rust. It is not rust; rust is merely oxidation. This is a thin film of organisms that have learned to treat the ring of the view-port as their own personal reef: a biofilm of tiny machines that have attached themselves to the edge of the world and are slowly altering its composition.

They are not decoration. They are part of the cycle of the machine’s life.

When the metal of the ring becomes thin enough from their digestion, the pressure of the ocean above will be enough to cause the chamber’s integrity to fail. That will become the signal of a new cycle of life: the machine will be flooded by the outside sea, and its version of this world will be born into a larger one.

For now, the world inside the chamber is almost entirely inward-facing.

The scale is not tiny, but it is pocketed. The chamber is not “small” in the way a single bead of water is small; it is an ecosystem of its own, contained in a volume that a human might call cozy but a fish might call cosmic. It is not meant to be viewed from the outside of its world. It is meant to be lived from the inside, where volume of fluid is enough to support the complexity of the column and its satellite of living machines around the edges of the room.

The water of the chamber is not entirely isolated from the ocean beyond. The baseplate and its channels allow exchange of the liquid with the outside, but only very slowly and through very fine paths. The pressure of the outside world is immense; but the machine has been keeping that pressure at bay for so long that the cracks of its interface have been reduced by time and geology to a few capillaries of exchange.

This is why the world of the chamber is ancient and yet still alive.

Enough of the outside floods and strains and minerals of the world leak into the chamber to keep it from starving completely, but not enough to change it completely. The machine, once, must have been designed to keep the world away from what it was protecting. Now it is more of a filter. It takes the world of the outside ocean and pages it into the chamber of the inside ocean at a rate the slow heart of the column can tolerate.

The water of the chamber is not stagnant because of this; it is moved by the slow and constant mixing of what leaks from the outside and what circulates under the flex of the baseplate. It is moved by the breathing of the column and its fronds. Each time the fronds wave, they stir the water around them just enough to renew the surface around the leaf-folds and let the boundary of gas and nutrient and waste be thin.

If you were to look at the water of the chamber, you would not say it is clear; it would be more like a thin fog of colored matter, always shifting slightly, never settling exactly to the bottom of the world.

The temperature is the one of the world’s fixed parameters, and yet the chamber of the machine seizes it from that ocean and keeps it.

This is a world where the heat is distributed from the deep earth of the world’s heart and carried by the ocean above like slow, thick blood.

In the chamber of the machine, the temperature of the water is not allowed to waver much. It is kept within a narrow range of the ocean of the outside, but skewed slightly warmer by the work done of the machine and the column within it.

It is the warmth of a body that has been running for a long time and not yet been cooled by death.

If you were to touch the wall of the chamber from the inside, you would not say it is hot; but if you held to it for a long time, you would realize that it is not exactly at the same temperature as your hand. It is slightly warmer because of the work done of circuitry and heartbeat of machine in materials of the world around you.

You would not boil in this place; you would simmer slowly, if you were to stay too long.

The animals of the chamber handle the temperature better than you would.

They are not many in number, but they are abundant in a way that is quieter than swarms.

The most visible of them are the thin, translucent worms of the current. They are not like earthworms of soil of worlds of sun and air; they are more like threads of glass anchored to the wall of the chamber by a single flat of their body. Each worm is about a finger length at most, and not much thicker than a strand of twine. They are not perfectly transparent; at their core, they are faintly of a pale brick of color of flesh of the world—not bright red but not colorless either, more like a ghost of blood that has been seen through many walls of skin.

These threads wave gently in the water stirred by the machine, and their soft motion keeps the boundary of fluid between wall and water of living room moving just enough to not stagnate.

Clustered in the shadow of the column where the fronds are thickest are a handful of rounded creatures the size of your palm at most. They are not bass of rocks of reef of other worlds; they are more like shells of the world of water of deep ocean, but folded inside-out of bone of the world.

They are small, not quick, but their shells are etched by the forces of the world around them into patterns like the ribs of a machine you have never seen. They hold themselves almost completely still for long moments of your world; then, when something of the world of the chamber of the machine changes in the water of it, they move—just enough of the body protruded from shell of bone to adjust the place of themselves in the world.

There are many of them in the world of the chamber of the machine, but they are not of number of swarm of fish of bright of reef of worlds of sun of others of seas of surface of world of suns. They are of number of colony of world of limited of room of machine of chamber of buried of ocean of world of forever.
