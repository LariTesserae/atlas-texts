The sky is a low, gray tongue of cloud that barely clears the crowns of the trees. No sun ever shows directly here; instead, the world glows at the edges of things, as if the air itself is slightly phosphorescent, or the light is being squeezed through the pores of the canopy and leaking down in thin, pale threads.

You are standing on a threshold between two very different kinds of space, but both of them are completely made of plants.

This is a small, high basin: not a true mountain peak, but a plateau lifted above the surrounding world, cupped in a bowl of stone and ice. The ground beneath you is not earth but something like a living carpet, dense and layered and faintly breathing. The first thing you notice is that there is no soil, no rock, no dirt—only vegetation.

The floor is a dense matrix of mosses, lichens, and fungal weaves, packed so tightly there is no room for anything else. Each step is slow and deliberate, because your foot sinks not into mud but into a yielding lattice of fibers and rhizomes that feels like a fabric of roots. You are walking on the bodies of plants, and the ground beneath you is alive and faintly warm.

This is a cold place, though the boundary of your body keeps the chill at bay for now. The air is not biting but patient, a constant temperature just above freezing, so that every breath feels like drinking clear glass. The coldness has not killed the plants; rather, it has slowed them into a different kind of life. They exude warmth as they grow, gather in clusters, and create miniature pockets where temperature rises slightly, enough to melt the frost that edges their surfaces.

The most obvious thing is the suspension of the world.

The entire plateau is covered in a cathedral of growth, but it is not a random forest. It is a city of trees.

By city, you understand at once that these are not trees like the ones you know. They share the same general shape and trunk form, but they are clearly arranged: not planted in rows, but organized in a way that suggests intention and adaptation over an unknown length of time. Their crowns are so tightly packed that the sky is visible only as a thin, broken net of gray light.

Each trunk is thick, columnar, and smooth, except where a spiral of narrow, woody steps has been grown into its surface—not carved, but grown: bulges of tissue that emerge exactly where you'd expect treads, simplifying the geometry of ascent to a subtle curve. You realize that these are stairs not for you, but for the former inhabitants of this place.

Not that there is anyone here now. The city is empty but not silent.

The absence of animals is unsettling at first, until you adjust. The more you look, the fewer you see. No insects dart in the air. No birds flit from crown to crown. No small mammals scuffle at the base of the trunks. Instead, the movement of animals is rare and half-secret: you might, if you are very patient and still, notice a faint shadow slipping between root vaults, or a single swift, dark shape that crosses the high canopy of leaves and is never seen again. This is not a world without animals, but one where they are carefully hidden, almost incidental. The dominant life is vegetable.

The soundscape is mostly quiet, but not empty. 

You hear the drip of water.

This is the wetness of the world: not roaring floods or rivers, but a constant, subtle, living moisture. Everything is slightly damp to the touch. The air itself condenses on the surfaces of the leaves and flows into thin, clear rivulets that gather along the bark of the trunks and then thread down, following the grooves of the staircases. You see the streams of waterlike sap filling microscopic channels in the wood of the steps, so that each stairway becomes a gentle aqueduct.

The water comes from above and below. From above, because the clouds lie low on the trees and small droplets of fog rain perpetually, like mist suspended just before falling. The droplets form on the edges of leaves, then slide down, dot to dot to dot, until they reach the ground and sink into the mossy substrate.

From below, because the ground is not truly ground: it is a thick, organic mattress layering over centuries of rot and new growth. Somewhere underneath that mattress, there is stone and ice and possibly groundwater, but the surface world is buoyed on a layer of older trees that have not so much died as partially transformed into a new substrate. Their wood is soft and damp, eternally moistened from the slow capillary action of their roots reaching into the colder depths below. You can feel the faint coolness of the water through the soles of your shoes, if you are wearing shoes; if not, then through the soles of your feet, which become slightly numb but not deeply chilled.

The erosion here is young, fresh, and still ongoing. The plateau itself is not ancient; the stone of the basin's edge is sharp and not yet smoothed down by ages of wind and rain. The edges of the rock where they surround the basin are still defined by sudden angles, not long curves. The basin itself seems to have been scoured out of the rock rather than slowly eroded; it is as if someone or something long ago took a huge brush of ice and rock and swept away the softer stone until only this small bowl remained.

Inside the bowl, the erosion is now more subtle and more complex than the simple grinding of wind and water. The roots of the city-tree forest are very slowly chewing the stone of the basin's floor and walls, breaking down minerals into substances that keep the trees alive and hydrating the world with their slow decay. The ground slopes in very gentle gradients, but there are incipient ravines, where channels of water have just begun to define themselves, not yet deep enough to be called streams but distinct enough to be motion paths for the falling moisture.

The scale of this place is small but deceptive.

It would not take more than a few minutes to walk from one edge of the plateau to the other, if you walked in a straight line and there were no obstructions. But such a direct walk is impossible. The density of the trunks, the roots, and the low, understory layer of growth makes the space feel like a single giant room completely filled with objects. There is no open space at the level of your body; every place you move to, you are moving through something. The trunks of the trees are columns in a hall whose ceiling is the canopy; the floor is a mat of other plants; the air between is filled with thin, hanging growths: rootlets that have dropped from above to dangle like organ pipes of an older cathedral than human ones ever were.

There is a layer above the forest floor, but below the high canopy: a sort of middle zone of woven branches and thick, leaf-veiled structures. You cannot see up clearly through the tangle of wood and leaf and hanging vine to tell exactly what is there, but you get the sense of platforms, chambers, and hidden rooms. The branches of neighboring trees have grown together and then flattened, forming broad surfaces where you could imagine people sitting or sleeping, if there were people to sit there.

The built quality of this place is not human, but that is part of the weirdness you feel. It is not a city of stone and metal and concrete; it is a city of tree. Yet the way the trunks are positioned, the way the branches have been guided to cross each other at just the right height to form platforms, the way the staircases spiral up in exactly the same style and with the same spacing even though each tree is distinct, suggests that this place was not made recently by one conscious mind, but shaped over time by many, or by one very long-lived one, or by some intelligence that worked through the plants themselves.

There are no machines. No devices of metal or plastic or glass. No tracks of wheels on the ground, no engines humming in the distance, no flicker of screens or lights of electrical origin. The technology level of this world is not pre-tool; there is no obvious hint of agriculture or even of fire, except possibly very old and faint traces of dark soot on some of the inner trunk faces where bark might be peeled back to reveal inner wood. The tools, if any tools exist at all, are not the ones you know: no sharpened edges of stone or metal, no carved containers, no visible storage of crafted objects.

The tools of this world, if they are tools at all, might be living things. The trees themselves might be tools, and the users of those tools no longer exist or have been absorbed into the system.

The light remains low throughout the days of this world. You know the concept of a day mostly because your body marks time, but here the sky does not change to a great degree. The cloud cover is constant and overcast; there is no dawn that you would see as clearly as you know from other places, and no sunset that throws long shadows. Instead, the dim light deepens slightly around the edges of the world, and then slowly strengthens again, as if the air above were being gently compressed and then released.

Your eyes adjust to the low light level. The trunks of the trees range from dark gray to brown to black, but each of them has subtle lines and patterns of a slightly brighter color—like the faint luminescence of a fungi that has not yet spread so fully it kills the host tree. The patterns of light on the bark are not random but organized: they are long, vertical lines of lighter, greenish-white that seem to follow the flow of water down from the canopy. You touch the surface of one trunk and your fingers come away slightly shining, absorbent of a faint glow that stays with your skin for a few heartbeats before fading away.

The cold of this world is not an absolute freezing cold, but something else: a low-temperature that keeps the processes of life slow but very steady. The plants are not in a rush. They grow slowly, layer upon layer, year upon year, without obvious seasons other than a very slight change in the condensation rate and the rate of growth of certain new shoots that you may notice if you stay here long enough.

The activity of the world is not violent but very continuous. There are no explosions of noise, no sudden storms of wind or rain. Instead, there is a steady dripping of water from the canopy to the floor, and from there into the living substrate. The substrate itself is not completely passive but shifts slightly under you as you walk, not because it is soft but because it is composed of many tiny layers of fibers that are turning underfoot, releasing and reabsorbing moisture. You feel the faint pressure of the world below as you put weight on it, and you imagine you could feel a response, if you were very sensitive and the world were slightly more responsive than it is.

If you listen to the soundscape for long enough, you begin to discern patterns. The drips of water fall not randomly but with a slight tendency to organize into rhythms of staccato notes: the water of one leaf meets the water of another leaf and then drips down into the same shallow root channel where it collects with a tiny sound. You hear the series of drops: pa-tap ... tap ... pa-pa-tap ... tap ... as if the world is trying to speak in a language of falling moisture.

The way of the world is not entirely static, but the change is slow, geological. The tree-city is not finished being built; it is still being woven by the growth patterns of the trees themselves. You can see where new branches have been turned slightly from their natural direction and guided into another position, where they have then grown and thickened and become part of the structure of the next level of the city. You realize that this is a world of scaffolds and frames and living structures that are continuously modified, not by conscious design but by the habits of life that have been shaped over time to form this order.

As you look at the edges of the plateau, the world of stone and space drops away below the cloud line. The ground seems to end at the very lip of the basin, where you can see, if you are careful and step close to the edge—not too close, because the ground is not firm but living and may not feel the same way you feel about edges—a steep drop of rock and ice and a few more trees that are clambering down the sides of the basin, trying to extend the city out of itself but not yet fully succeeding.

Wind occasionally reaches the edge of the plateau, but does not penetrate deeply into the basin of the city. The wind is something else that you hear from a distance, rather than directly—a sound of low moans or soft ringing of branches that never moves the air where you stand.

The longer you stay, the more you feel the borders of this place. It is not a large world; it is a small, high, chilly basin where a vast tree-city has grown and taken on almost all of the available space. If you were to walk from one end to the other, you would cross not many different kinds of terrain but a single layer of textures and microsystems of moss and root and trunk and water and warmth.

You might get lost, though not in the way of losing track of direction, but in the way of becoming lost within the interstices of the living structure itself. You could, if you wanted, climb the stairs in one of the trees and reach the first layer of platforms where the branches of several trees come together to form small floors of interwoven wood. From there, you would see the world spread not so much as a ground, but as a three-dimensional labyrinth of verticals and sideways layers and smaller rooms of growth that branch off from each other with no clear edges of self.

The world is not dead but very quiet. It is not chaotic but very dense and ordered. It is cold but not sterile. The quiet of the place wraps itself around you, and the longer you stay, the more you feel the strange, unusual nature of it: a world that has chosen to be almost entirely vegetable, except for the occasional hidden animal that slips from shadow to shadow and the few breathing humans or others like you who may happen to step into its basin and become part of its slow, active process of change and growth and dripping water.

There are no rules written here that you can see, except for the implicit rules of the world itself: how to grow without turning the place into an overgrown mess of chaos; how to have water everywhere without turning the place into a swamp; how to be cold without being frozen; how to be dense without being suffocated; and how to be a city of trees without any of the usual human markers of urban life.

The longer you look, the more you understand that this is a place of subtle edges and transformations—where walking on the floor of the world feels like walking on bodies of plants, where the walls of the world are columns of trees that have learned to be stairs and aqueducts and platforms, and where the sky of the world is not high but low, a gray cover of clouds that never quite touches the ceiling of leaves but seems to breathe slowly above the city of living wood.
