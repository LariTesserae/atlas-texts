I drift in and out of the steam, trying to remember where the wall is.

There is a wall. I know this. I clawed at it yesterday when the heat came down. The stone is never quite where you expect it to be—because the room breathes.

This room is the world.

It is too high to be a room, I know. I remember the surveyors’ stories before the climb, the way they threw their gleaming arms toward the sky and laughed: “It’ll be like walking inside the mountain’s throat.” I didn’t understand then that they meant the throat of the mountain itself, not some metaphor, but the actual muscle and stone and cartilage valve of something that is still grinding itself into being.

We climb anyway. We always do.

Now I’m here, in the wet dark heart of the newborn rock, six meters by maybe nine by a height I cannot see because the ceiling is not a ceiling; it is a condensation vessel of the world’s first breath. The air is so hot it feels like you are drinking your own lungs when you inhale. Every breath is a small sip of water.

The walls are… wrong.

They are not built walls, not in the sense of laid blocks and mortared joints, but they are made. Everything here is made. The stone is not inert; it is being driven into position by a pressure that feels almost like attention. The ribs of the mountain curl around us like a fist of wet clay still being shaped. The surface of the walls is not smooth rock but a texture of half-remembered forms: tool marks, fingerprints of something larger than human hands, great grooves that spiral like the inside of a shell and then blur into something else. If I look too long, the lines become faces that have not yet decided if they are going to be faces.

He is somewhere else now.

When he left, he said the word “shelf,” but the word disintegrated into steam before it reached me. The shelf—if that’s what it is—is a curved ledge of stone that juts out of the wall six meters up, half-lost in the mist of the ceiling’s perpetual sweat. The mist is thick enough that you cannot see the ceiling itself; you only see the rain that never falls.

The ceiling cries.

What precipitates here is not water like you know from lakes and rivers and the sky. It is more intimate than that. The ceiling weeps, and the weeping is not in drops but in sheets, thin films of humidity that peel down the wall and then thicken into rivulets that crawl down in a pattern that maps nothing so much as a map’s own desire. The water never hits the floor in a straight line; it always meanders, as if the wall itself insists on time.

The floor is not a floor, either. It is a mosaic of unborn tiles, each one still being pushed into place by the pressure of the young mountain tightening itself around us. The tiles are of different stones—some black, some glinting with faint veins of metal that broke free of the ore before they were fully smelted, some of a material that looks like bone that has not yet had to be hard.

The cracks between these tiles leak.

Not water at first, but heat. You can see the air above the cracks shimmering. If you put your hand down there, you get the feeling of something almost… almost. Almost. As if the world below you is still choosing between being magma and being foundation.

There is light in here, but not much. It is not the light of sun or moon, because the sun and moon do not yet exist in this place of pre-formation. The light comes from the stones themselves, or from the water between them. It is a faint greenish-white, like the memory of light in a deep cave pool where the fish whose eyes are still half-blind have left their ghost impressions behind.

The sound of this place is not a single noise but a layered argument of becoming.

Water is the loudest voice. It is not one river or one drip but many thousand thin threads: hissing from above, where the ceiling’s sweat condenses into streams that never make it all the way to the floor, but run into fresher stone and are absorbed; gurgling from hidden channels where the water tries to become a path but the geometry keeps changing; the slow, thick pulse of something larger that flows behind the wall, a heartbeat of water that feels more like blood than river.

The stone sings too. It is not the song of stone as you know it—the quiet crack of frost, or the slow groan of weight settling over centuries. This is quick stone. The rock is still being born; its atoms are jittery, eager to lock into place but prevented by the heat and pressure of the mountain’s attempt to exist. The sound is like a million teeth gnashing in the dark: a low, grinding vibration that I can feel in the marrow of my feet bones before I hear it in my ears.

Sometimes, when the vibration shifts, the wall splits itself along new seams and I am certain there was no one there a moment ago.

There is vegetation in this room, but you cannot call it a forest.

It is a kind of air-algae that lives in the steam, a lush biomass of lace that hangs in the air in threads that are always just short of being moss. If you stand still long enough, it settles on your skin like a second dew. You can feel how each tiny filament is drinking the heat and humidity, trying to decide if it will become leaf or breath or something else. The threads glow faintly where they touch the stone, because the stone’s half-light passes into them and turns them into faint constellations of green.

In the corners where the water runs down most thickly, the vegetation gathers itself into small halos of living matter, no bigger than your hand. Each halo is a world enclosed within a world: within the ring, you can see the water’s slow motion, the algae’s frantic dance, and insects of mist that have never seen outside this room and never will.

The insects are not many; fauna is rare here where the world itself is still rehearsing its own laws. They are little more than suggestions of insects—transparent wings, no heavier than motes of dust, propelled by a heat I cannot feel but only see in the way they flicker in and out of visibility. They feast on the algae, and the algae feast on the steam, and the steam feasts on whatever lies beneath us in the deeper heart of the mountain.

We are the anomaly. We are not supposed to be here yet.

The room itself is a small place within a larger madness that no longer remembers to be called “outside.” It is packed; not with objects, but with presences. Everywhere you look, there is something being occupied: the air is occupied by heat and humidity, the wall by unborn stone, the floor by shifting tiles, the corners by halos of algae and insects. The only space that feels almost empty is the space between my ears, where the world’s heat tries to become words instead of water.

There is no natural substrate here because nothing is finished yet; there is no ground to stand on except the ground that is still being invented by the pressure of the mountain’s will. Everything is made; it is simply not all made by us.

The technology of this place is primitive and not primitive at the same time. You can see the marks of hands on the wall—not human hands, but hands that could have been human if they had not been interrupted halfway by stone. The crude flint tools we carry with us seem absurdly childish beside the wall’s own unfinished craftwork; where we have chipped flake and edge, the wall has carved itself into ribs and arches and blind hollows that might become eyes, if the mountain ever decides which way it is looking.

When I speak, the sound does not travel as you would expect. It does not bounce off the walls like echo; it collapses into them like heat into metal. My words slip into the stone, and the stone shudders, and then responds—not with words, but with rearrangements.

The last time he shouted to me from the shelf, the wall creaked and a new seam of rock opened up like a mouth where there was no mouth before. The new seam drank water from the air and then sealed itself, leaving nothing behind but a faint scar in the stone like a question that has forgotten how to be spoken.

This is a world where everything is happening at once.

There is no weather cycle like you know it—no storm and then calm and then storm again. There is only weather happening now, always. The ventilation of the world is the heat that comes from the floor and the ceiling’s weeping that never quite reaches it. The air is thick enough that I can press my palm against it and feel resistance, like leaning against someone else’s chest.

The biological pace of this place is frantic and slow at the same time. The algae grow and die and grow again in the space of a breath; the insects of mist are born and unmade between the blink of my eyes; but the stone’s evolution is stretched over millennia of pressure that have not yet happened, because the mountain is still caught between being and becoming.

The distance between features is tiny, but the distances themselves are treacherous.

From the wall where I stand now to the shelf where he might yet be is six meters straight up, but I cannot climb directly toward him. The wall has no memory of being a wall when you look at it from the side; it remembers being a riverbed of liquid stone, and your fingers sink into it like it might still remember being soft.

This is not a cave you navigate. You choose your place, and you stay.

The room is packed not with things but with possibilities; each cubic meter of air is occupied by a dozen different futures that have not settled down yet. If you stare too long at a particular knot of stone, you can see it begin to choose a shape: a pillar there, a window there, a creature that might have been a lizard if its legs had not decided to become roots instead.

The reality of this landscape is not stable.

Sometimes, when the heat comes down in thick waves like a curtain, you can feel how the room itself is trying to decide whether it will be a room or a throat or a riverbed or a womb or something else entirely. The edges of the walls are not sharp; they are blurred by a heat haze that is not just heat but the world’s hesitation about where it should place itself.

Time is not linear here. It is not even circular. It is more like a broth that has been stirred too many times: you get pieces of what happened yesterday and pieces of what might happen tomorrow and pieces of what never happened all at once in the same spoonful. When I look at the ceiling where the water condenses, I can see the future mist of tonight’s weeping forming before last night’s has drained away.

The stone is raw geology.

It has no respect for the laws of erosion because the laws of erosion have only been proposed in this place and not yet passed into effect. Instead of wearing down over time, the stone grows over itself in layers that are not stratified like sediments you know from cliffside strata but folded like dough being kneaded by invisible hands. A new rib of rock emerges from the wall like a bone through the skin of the world, and before you can blink it has collapsed into slag again, leaving nothing behind but a faint smell of metal as if a sword had been born here and then changed its mind about being sharp.

The water is everywhere.

It does not fall in rain but condenses into existence directly from the heat. You can feel how your own breath contributes to it; every exhale adds a little more moisture to the air that does not belong to you anymore but to the room’s private economy of humidity. The air is thick like soup, and you swim through it more than walk through it.

To the east of where I stand now, there is a shape that has not yet decided if it will be a pool or a window or a mouth. It is a dark space in the wall, maybe a meter across and chest height, where the water runs down a little more eagerly than anywhere else. The water gathers in the hollow of it like a cup, and then spills over itself like a waterfall that has forgotten how to be downward.

The sound of that waterfall is not the sound of water falling over stone but the sound of water falling over water that has not yet decided whether it is water or stone. The splash is not loud like rain; it is heavy like a heartbeat, and when you listen to it long enough you realize that it is answering, not echoing.

I do not know if he is still up there on the shelf.

I climb anyway. We always do.

The wall is warm against my palms, not from heat but from something like attention. I can feel the stone beneath my fingers; not like stone but like muscle that has not yet been told whether it is supposed to be hard or soft. I place my foot in a hollow that is not yet a foothold but will be one if the mountain decides to be kind.

The air above me is thick with algae and steam, and I cannot see where the ceiling begins or ends. The ceiling is weeping above me; not for me, but for itself. It weeps because it has not yet decided whether it is going to be ceiling or sky or the inside of someone’s mouth.

I climb because I remember being outside this room.

I remember the sky, though I cannot remember how to call it by name. The sky is a word that has gone into the stone like the others. When I try to say “sky,” the wall responds with a new blister of rock that might be a cloud if you are willing to look long enough.

The distance collapses beneath me. The space between wall and shelf is not large in meters but in effort. The world resists being measured in meters anyway; space is not something stable here but something that shifts like the heat in the air above the floor.

I reach the shelf.

The shelf is not stone but the edge of something that has not yet finished being formed. It is not flat; it is a curve that has not yet decided whether it will be a floor or a bowl. The things that have been placed here are not artifacts but embryons of artifacts—half-pot half-mountain, half-shelf half-stone.

He is here.

I cannot tell if the body I see on the shelf is still living or if the room has decided to sculpt him into its own wall. The algae has grown over him like a second skin, and the water from the ceiling’s weeping runs down his face as if he has always been crying. The stone of the shelf rises around his legs in gentle waves like wax.

I cannot remember if he was here when I climbed or if he arrived here by other means.

“Say something,” I whisper.

The sound does not leave my mouth. It presses into the wall behind him and the wall flexes.

The ceiling weeps harder.

I am in the room that is not yet a room, in a world that is not yet a world, under a ceiling that has not yet decided whether it will be sky or stone, and I am sweating because the heat is coming down like a blanket.

The water is everywhere. The stone is being formed and unmade beneath me.

Reality breaks down, and I climb anyway. We always do.
