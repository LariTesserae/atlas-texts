The bowl of this valley is not a bowl at all but a shallow cup of porous stone set into the shoulder of a high mountain. It is not quite high enough to drink from the clouds, but they are often near, and the air that fills this place is as heavy as water. It smells like crushed moss and rain-shadow and something faintly metallic, like iron ore being ground between stone teeth.

The rock itself is the main feature. The valley is no longer than a large cathedral, and perhaps half that wide, but its walls rise sheer and close, forming a narrow oval lopped off at the southern end by a dark, damp cliff where water never stops falling. It is not a waterfall so much as a burst weeping wound: a thin curtain of pale silver threads that spills from the rock face and smokes into shimmer before it ever strikes the ground. It has been doing this for long enough that the stone below has eroded into long, blade-like ribs of black limestone, each one shining like a polished knife blade.

Above the great cliff, the high karst plateau is hidden behind cloud and time. No one knows whether there is a lake above, or a glacier, or just a permanent fog of ice that weeps into the rock and disappears down the fault-line until it emerges here as a lacy bandage falling over stone. The air is thick, but not cold. It hangs at a temperature that feels almost like blood in the lungs. It is not hot enough to sweat, but warm enough that moss never stops growing.

That moss is the color of a deep forest bruise. It covers everything in a low, velvet mass: the walls, the ribs of stone beneath the waterfall, the rounded boulders that have long since fallen from the ledges above and settled into the floor of the valley like sleeping bodies. The moss grows thickest where the spray falls, but even the shaded flanks of the bowl are upholstered in it. If you turn your head slowly, you can see color shifting in the green: not motion but a subtle change from emerald to viridian to a deep olive-black where the light never reaches. The moss feels like fur between your fingers.

The floor of the valley is half stone, half water. The water sheet does not fall cleanly into a pool; instead, it trickles down the ribs and into a shallow depression of travertine that has built itself, over centuries, into a series of crescent-shaped terraces. Each terrace is a shallow, discrete pool of water, each one the size of a small room, each one a slightly different color. The water closest to the cliff is as clear as glass, but the next terrace down is tinted faintly orange from iron-rich minerals, and the one below that is pale blue-green, like faded copper. Wherever the water flows over the edge of one terrace to the next, it sings a little thin ringing note, and where many flows meet together, the sound becomes a low, continuous chord.

Around the edges of this terrace system, the ground rises slightly, forming high ground that stays dry even when the waterfalls are heavy after storms. Here, large solitary stones lie scattered in sparse constellations across the moss floor. They are not very many, but they are huge: each one is taller than a person and perhaps twice as long. They are not jagged. They have been rounded by time and condensation until they seem less like stones than like the backs of half-submerged giants. The space between them feels deliberate—not enough to be open air, but enough to give each stone its own breath. Each stone sits in its own circle of damp, mossy ground, and on each stone grows a single, ancient tree.

These trees are not tall like forest giants, but they are not small either. They are twisted and gnarled, stunted by the altitude and the constant dampness, but filled with a quiet strength that comes from doing the same thing for hundreds of years. Their trunks are thick and low-slung, sinewy with age. They weave into parasitic shapes: spirals, half-circles, straight trunks bent suddenly sideways like the backs of old men carrying burdens. The branches radiate outward from them in sparse, elegant layers, each one draped with a thin, pale lichen that hangs like strange lace.

The leaves are small and dark, almost black in the low, saturated light. They are arranged in a dense tapestry of tiny overlapping shapes, like the feathers of a deep-sea bird. When the wind passes—and it does not do so often—the leaves tremble and release faint, metallic chimes, as if each leaf contains a tiny bell of crystallized water. The air smells like musk and mineral and wet, deep green.

This is a lush world, but not an exuberant one. There is no riot of flowers or chaotic jungle. There are only a few plant species, and they have had long enough to simplify themselves into a strict arrangement. Each great stone bears its one tree, and that tree owns its stone entirely. No other tree grows on that rock; no shrub dares compete. Underneath each stone, at the base where the moss meets wet rock, there are small colonies of white ferns that unfold like the fingers of a hundred hands reaching out from the stone’s shadow. They are far enough apart that their fronds never touch. They grow in silence, at a patient pace.

Sometimes there are rainstorms here—not violent thunderclaps, but long, slow, drenching rains that seem to come from nowhere and end nowhere. They happen less often than you would expect in such a wet place, but they happen when the clouds decide to lean lower and closer. The sky darkens slowly over days, not hours. The air grows heavier still. The waterfall becomes a broader curtain of blurred water, and the terrace pools begin to overflow their edges one by one until the entire valley floor seems to simmer with a thin layer of silver water.

Then, for a few days or a week, the whole valley is underwater an inch deep everywhere, except for the high stones and their trees. The moss puffs up like a soaked sponge and becomes luminous, almost phosphorescent green beneath the surface of the water. The lichens hanging from the trees glow very faintly blue. When this happens, the place feels like the inside of a submerged lung, breathing slowly.

Fauna are rare here. Not absent, but few. This is not a landscape of herds or flocks or swarms. It is a landscape of individualities.

On the broadest stone near the center of the valley sits the oldest and most arresting tree. Its trunk is nearly hollow, and in that hollow lives a single great owl. The owl does not look like any bird you know from lower worlds. Its feathers are a deep, mossy green-gray, and they are dappled with patterns like running water and stone. It has eyes that are entirely gold, without whites or pupils, and they glow faintly in the shade. When it blinks, the world around it seems to flicker slightly.

The owl is not here often. It might appear once every two weeks. It arrives silently after nightfall and leaves silently before dawn. It does not hunt in the valley—or if it does, it does not hunt visibly. There is no obvious prey to catch. No mice scurry through the sparse moss, no insects swarm in noisy clouds. The valley hums with life, but not animal life.

Instead there are small, slow creatures that are rarely seen. In the pools of the terraces, you can sometimes find the translucent ghosts of salamanders the length of your thumb, each one pale orange with delicate ribs showing faintly beneath their skin. They do not swim like fish; they crawl along the bottom, holding very still for long periods before jerking forward a few centimeters at a time. They have been known to spend months inside the same pool, never leaving it. They are not capable of going anywhere quickly.

There are also snails. The moss grows tiny snails by the hundreds, each one smaller than your smallest fingernail. They are black with spiral shells that look like miniature versions of the valley’s terrace stones. They move slowly along the moss stems, leaving glistening trails behind them that shimmer like faint, silver exhalations. When the rain falls heavily, they crawl upward—toward the edges of the stones, toward the trunks of the trees—and they hang there in clusters like tiny, dark fruit until the water recedes again.

There are no mammals larger than a woman’s thumb. There are no reptiles larger than the salamanders. There are no insects larger than gnats. But there are sounds everywhere, and they are loud enough to fill the space between the stones.

The waterfall dominates the soundscape: a constant, low white noise that varies between rain and surf and wind depending on how hard the water is falling that day. When it is light, it sounds like pages turning endlessly. When it is heavy after a storm, it sounds like a low drumbeat beneath everything else, like the heart of the mountain.

But around that central sound, there are other layers.

There is the sound of water dripping from stone to stone, each drip a small, clear note. There is the music of the terraces—each pool has its own pitch depending on the depth of the water and the shape of the stone basin, and as they overflow, they create a slowly changing chord that never exactly repeats. There is the sound of wind in the leaves, though that wind is rare: it sweeps over the top of the bowl like the hand of a god who refuses to enter.

And there is the space between.

The silence here is not empty; it is thick. It is the silence of long habit and long existence. It is the silence of stone that has found its shape and does not need to be anywhere else. The moss creeps, but not noisily. The snails move, but not noticeably. The rare small animals do not disturb the air. They step into the world like shadows stepping into other shadows.

This is a place that has been settled for a very long time. The erosion rate is slow enough that you would not see change from year to year, or even decade to decade. But if you were to sit here for centuries—which some stones have, and which the tree on the broad stone nearly has—you would notice small changes. The terrace of pale blue-green water is slowly, patiently, building outward into the next terrace down. The moss that grows on a particular stone near the western wall has been slowly consuming its surface, leaving behind faint indentations that look like ancient carvings. If there were writing within them, no one has yet learned to read it.

There are no human structures here. No walls, no roofs, no tools abandoned on the stones. But if you look closely at the base of the oldest tree, you will find the faint, circular dimple of a hollow that has been worn by something sitting there repeatedly, over a long period of time. The rock itself has been shaped, not by carving, but by pressure and weight and moisture over time. It is not a seat; it is only the ghost of one.

Perhaps someone did come here once. Not recently; not in the last few centuries. But if someone did, they would have come to this place as a shrine to water and stone and long waiting. They would have come to listen to the waterfall and to the moss growing and to the slow folding of the fern fronds. They would have brought no more than what they could carry: a staff of wood from lower altitudes, perhaps; or a simple pot of baked clay, or a stone knife dug from the earth below the clouds.

If they were still here, they would have left no mark more permanent than the moss left as it crept. The lichen would have grown over any wooden structures they left; the rain would have washed away any footprints; the stones would have refused to remember.

There is no technology here more advanced than the rhythm of water dripping on stone and the slow logic of moss growing on a rock that will never move again. There is no engine more complex than the tree’s roots pulling water from the air and stone. There is no shelter more sophisticated than the hollow beneath the twisted trunk of the oldest tree.

And yet something strange hovers here at the edges of perception. It is not a visible thing; it is not a creature you can point to and say “that is strange.” It is an atmosphere.

When you sit in the hollow at the base of the oldest tree and face the waterfall, you might notice that the falling water does not fall exactly straight down. It falls slightly sideways, as if following a current that should not exist in air. It is not enough to be obvious at first glance, but if you watch it long enough—and you will have the time—you will see how the thin threads of water trace out invisible curves in space, like they are following the contours of something you cannot see.

Sometimes, in the clear light of afternoon, when the sun manages to angle over the lip of the bowl and into the valley for a few hours, the whole terrace of pools gleams like a single instrument. The surface of each pool becomes a mirror, and the mirrors become an array. Each pool reflects a slightly different angle of sky. Each pool reflects a slightly different angle of stone. Each pool reflects the moss and the trees and the waterfall and the sky in overlapping circles that would not align anywhere else.

If you stare at this arrangement long enough, you might notice that the reflections do not always remain exactly where they should be. The image of the waterfall in one pool might not match the angle of the waterfall itself. The reflection of a stone might appear slightly higher or lower than its physical position. If there were a person sitting in the hollow of the oldest tree, their reflection might appear in two pools at once, or in none at all.

It is as if the water has not yet decided whether it is in this time or a slightly different one.

At night, when the moon is high and full, the valley becomes silver and liquid. The waterfall glows like a hanging curtain of light. The terrace pools glow like plates of mercury.
