At the center of the world is a room the size of a cathedral, and the city grows around it like a shell.

You walk first through the petrified forest that the city calls its outskirts. The trees are not stone in the way of fossils; you can see the faint grain of wood where lichen has been scraped away by pilgrims’ fingers. But they do not bend or grow. They are stable, inert, carved like columns rather than grown. Their branches are a low ceiling of interlacing fingers, sharp-edged where lightning or frost once sheared them. The ground is brittle—tiny shards of something like glass that crunch under your boots without giving. The sound of your footsteps is the first sound you have heard in days.

Above the branch-canopy, the light is dim and constant. There is no sun here, only the slow, pale diffusion of a sky that never quite brightens or darkens. You have seen no dawn, no dusk. Time has to be measured in other ways—in the settling of dust on your shoulders, the narrowing of your eyes, the slight ache in your thighs from descending into the hollow of the world.

The air is cool but not cold. You do not shiver; you do not sweat. Your breath does not plume in front of you. There is something unnerving about the stability of it all—the way you never feel the small relief of a breeze at the back of your neck, or the sting of heat on your skin. The climate feels deliberate.

The ground begins to slope down as the forest thickens. Or rather, the trees do not become more numerous but more tightly organized. Their trunks are spaced evenly, as if planted along the lines of invisible geometry. You realize the path is not wandering between obstacles; the trees are arranged to *leave* a path through them. The floor under the shards is a single, smooth curve of some hard material that makes no sound when your boot soles fall on it, and the trees are positioned exactly on the boundaries of that curve.

This is the first hint you have that the city is not built in the usual sense.

There are no animals. You have not seen a bird or an insect or any creature of fur or feather. The lichen lives in the nooks of the forest bark, but nothing grazes on it. On the first day, you thought you saw a shadow flit between the trunks, but it was only your own movement, disjointed in the angular light. The world feels empty but not dead; there is a motion of *something* happening beneath the surface, like the slow, unseen turning of a clockwork behind a wall.

You emerge from the forest into the bowl of the city proper.

The ground falls away in a smooth bowl, perhaps two hundred paces across. The air is even clearer here—there is no dust. You cannot see any floating motes. You could swear the dust itself has been removed from a space this sacred.

The city is sculpted into the walls of the bowl.

There are dwellings, pathways, terraces—all carved seamlessly from the same material, the same color as the tree trunks outside. The walls slope, not in straight lines but in gentle curves that seem to avoid right angles, as if the architect of this place disdained sharp corners. Doors and windows are oval openings, like widened eyes. The surfaces of the walls are not smooth; they are covered in faint, raised patterns like the veins in a leaf or the lines on a palm. You cannot read the script, if script it is.

No streetlamps burn. No torches flicker. No chimneys smoke. The light is even, as if the air itself emits a faint glow. Yet you can see the shadows the city casts on itself—the darker recesses of window spaces and the slight undercuts beneath each terrace. The entire city is built along the inner surface of the bowl, but you see no lamps, no fireplaces, no lanterns anywhere.

The city’s layout is simple and strange. The bowl descends in three broad rings, each a terrace of dwellings and plazas and what might be workshops or scriptoriums or market stalls. You cannot see any goods in the ones that are open toward the center; they are empty chambers, their floors carved with low benches that seem to invite sitting rather than trading. There is no obvious signage, no banners. No color, except for the occasional wash of pale blue that lichen spills across the wall, like a breath frozen on glass.

In the middle of the bowl, at the lowest point of the depression, is the room the city is arranged around.

It is not exactly a room. It is more a raised platform of the same material as the walls and floor, but here the stone—or whatever it is—is transparent. You realize you can see through the floor of this platform into the dim, hollow space beneath. The light is even clearer down there, and somehow you know that if you go beneath the bowl, you will find yourself beneath this platform.

The ceiling above the platform is open; the bowl of the city does not close over the center. A ring of sky—or whatever sky is in this place—looks down on the platform like an eye without an iris. The sky is the same flat, pale color as it always is, but you get the sense that you are closer to it, as if the city were a funnel to the source of the light.

The air is quiet. You hear the faint sound of your own breath. The city makes no noise of its own—no creaking of wood, no grind of gears, no wind whistling through eaves. There is no scent of charcoal smoke, no murmur of voices from the empty chambers. You have the almost unbearable suspicion that whatever lives here, if anything does, is not making noise because you are not yet listening in the right way.

The vegetation is sparse but not absent. Thin lichen grows along the terrace edges, like white lace. The faint blue veins in the wall patterns are moss of some kind, or at least something that stains the stone with color. In the center of the platform, there is a single, low mass of something like coral—white, brittle, and branching in a dense, spherical cloud above the transparent floor. It is not like any plant you know: the “branches” are too slender and numerous, and they seem to grow inward toward each other rather than outward toward the light.

The city’s activity is not biological but architectural.

While you watch, a terrace wall slowly changes. The surface of it—smooth stone a moment before—begins to rise in small ridges, like riverbeds forming in the desert. The pattern spreads like frost spreading across glass. You can hear nothing of it, but you can *see* the texture of the wall becoming more intricate, the sculpted lines more precise. The process lasts perhaps ten breaths and then ceases abruptly, leaving the wall inscribed with a new, intricate filigree of raised markings you have not seen before.

In another place, a low doorway grows slightly taller. It is not carved by tools or hands. You simply see the stone shifting its own boundary, as if water were freezing in the shape of an opening rather than in ice. The long oval of the doorframe eases its curve, becoming more circular than it was. The change happens slowly enough that you could blink and miss it, but not so slow that you cannot notice it if you pay attention.

You realize that the city is not finished being built.

This is not a ruin. It is not abandoned. It is newly emerged from whatever stone it was born of, and it is still growing itself. The walls are young, sharp-featured. The corners are crisp where the terrace edges meet each other. The reliefs have not yet worn with the attrition of millennia. There is no dust of being half-buried, no slumped shapes of collapsed structures. The city feels fresh and precise, like a newly minted coin.

You walk down the first ring of the bowl.

The steps are not blocks laid by hands; they are the same continuous surface as the floor itself, raised like the ribs of a skeleton beneath smooth skin. The handrails along the edge are not attached bars; they are the same material as the floor, grown upward into slender curves that you do not think to lean on but somehow feel you should.

As you descend, the air does not grow warmer or colder. It remains that cool, neutral temperature that never bothers you but never quite lets you forget it. The silence of the place is thick, but there is a subtle sense of motion underneath it all, like a faint vibration beneath your feet. You have the impression that something beneath the floor is listening—or processing, or thinking.

At the second ring, you see the first sign of what might be a creature.

You almost call it a stone statue at first. It stands on the edge of the terrace overlooking the center platform, facing the open sky. The figure is slender, vaguely humanoid, but with arms that are too long and fingers that are too many. The face is smooth—no eyes carved out, no nose. You feel the absence of its eyes more than any other feature of this place; the rest of the city keeps its gaze away from you, but this creature’s lack of eyes feels like a direct, staring blindness.

The statue is not stone of a different color from the walls; it is the same material as the terrace itself, grown up from the floor as though someone had placed a seed here and the building grew itself a person. You can see the faint trace of lines where the figure’s feet are not exactly separate from the floor beneath. It is not standing on the terrace so much as rooted in it.

The statue does not move when you approach. You step carefully around it, hearing the tiny sound of your boots on the floor—soft and sharp at the same time, like the distant breaking of glass. The hair on the back of your neck rises. You feel the urge to whisper, but you know that if you speak, your voice will be the only human voice this place has ever heard.

You are now close enough to the central platform that you can see the faint webbing in the transparent floor. It’s not exactly glass; it’s more like the hardened surface of clear ice, but warmer to the touch than you would expect. You hesitate to touch anything with your bare skin, so you crouch and extend your hand instead, hovering just above the floor. The cool air thickens a little, as if the space around your hand is becoming more dense.

Below you, through the floor, you can see the underside of the platform where the white, coral-like creature or growth or sculpture hangs suspended from the ceiling of the lower chamber. The lower chamber is empty of everything except that one massive growth. It stretches like a cloud of frozen lightning, crossing the center without touching the floor of the chamber beneath. Its filaments are thin and brittle and white, and the light that seems to come from nowhere makes the faintest shadows between them.

You realize that the central platform is not for the city to stand around. It is for the city to look into.

You sit down on the edge of the terrace. The stone is not cold beneath you; the temperature of the floor never quite changes. The lichen on the terrace edge is a thin white dust that does not smear when you brush your fingers over it. It feels like fine chalk.

From here, you can see the whole bowl of the city in one sweep of the eyes. The dwellings are arranged along the inner surface of the bowl like cells in a honeycomb or chambers in a shell. The doorway rings the terrace in a continual loop of ovals and circles. The processions and the paths of daily life would not have been along streets but in spirals down the bowl, toward the center that you now occupy.

You are beginning to understand that the city is not urban in the sense of streets crowded with people, but urban in the sense of being designed and organized and dense with construction. The layers of terraces are stacked, not like floors of a building but more like the strata of some rock formation that has been sculpted rather than deposited. Everywhere you look, there are subtle variations of the same material—the same pale stone, or bone-like substance, or whatever it is—that has been reshaped rather than replaced.

The absence of water becomes clearer. You guess, from the dryness of the air and the lack of any puddles or condensation, that there is no rain here. The floors of the dwellings have no drains for stormwater. There is no trace of mineral stains from the movement of fluids over stone. The lack of water is absolute. The presence of living beings—if the lichen and moss and the white coral below you are alive—is not based on water at all.

Instead of water, the air has something in it that the light reveals.

You sit for long enough that your eyes begin to catch the faintness of the air itself shifting. In the dim light, you can see the occasional movement of invisible particles or dust that you could not see before. The air around the central platform and the white coral below seems slightly thicker than the air elsewhere, as if there is a slow circulation of something that is not wind but might as well be.

You realize the air itself is the medium of exchange here.

The faint vibration of the floor is not random; you suspect it is related to the movement of this air. The city is breathing, not with lungs but with stone and pore and hollow chamber. The cool air flows through passages carved invisibly in the material, and as you imagine the flow of it through the walls, you see the patterns of raised lines in the stone walls more like veins or channels than decoration.

The silence of the place begins to feel like a pressure. You are not oppressed by noise, but you are oppressed by the lack of it. The city’s “activity” is not loud. It is subtle. You must listen to it by not listening at all—by watching the walls slowly adjust themselves, the doorways slowly change their shape, the terraces slowly grow a little more intricate.

You are alone, but the city was not built for you.

You are starting to suspect that the creatures who used this place—if there were any—were not people like you. Perhaps the slender figure that you came across earlier is not a statue but a body left behind in the act of becoming stone. The city’s architecture might be the species, rather than the shelter of it. The slow growth of walls and the reshaping of doorways might be the expression of a mind distributed over the whole bowl of the settlement.

You are a visitor to the middle of a mind that has not yet finished thinking itself into existence. The city feels young because, in a sense, it has only just begun to be.

When night finally—slowly—comes, it is not night as you know it. The dim light does not dim further in a sunset of color and flame. Instead, the air above the center of the platform darkens more than the surrounding sky, creating a circle of darkness in the middle of the bowl, like an eye closed at the center of everything. The city does not go dark; the faint glow from the air itself remains unchanged, but the contrast makes the platform under your feet feel like the edge of a pit into which you could fall.

You sit there until you are too stiff to sit comfortably, and then you rise carefully, not wanting to disturb the silence of this place. The sound of your clothing brushing against itself is the loudest sound you have heard in days.

As you climb back up the rings of the bowl, the city feels both smaller and larger than when you came down. It is small in the sense of being enclosed, like a room scaled to the size of a small village. The whole settlement could fit inside a single modest cathedral in your own world. But it is vast in another way—the vastness of the time that has gone into the shaping of each line on the walls, the subtlety of the air flow through the stone, and the quiet depth of the chamber below the platform where the white coral growth stretches like a frozen storm of nerves.

The vegetation—the white lichen and faint blue mosses—are scattered across the surfaces like occasional notes of music in a long piece of silence. They do not rustle. There is no wind to move them, and no water to make them grow lush. They are thin, precise, and very sparse, like punctuation marks around the architecture of the city.

You are leaving the center of this world. You have not seen any explosion of activity or violent catastrophe of motion. The activity of the place is slow and continuous and almost invisible. It is not the noise of a market or the rush of traffic but the slow restructuring of a stone brain that is still rewiring itself. You can feel, under your feet, the faint tremor of the city reshaping itself around you.

The silence of the place follows you as you climb back into the forest of petrified trees. The glass-like ground crunches once under your boots, and for a moment, you feel the weight of the noise like you have broken something sacred. You glance back.

The city is still there below, the circular platform of the center catching the faint light of the unmoving sky, and the white coral beneath the floor of the world hanging like the heart of the city in the quiet chamber of its mind.

You are walking away from the only place that makes noise within the silent city, and the sound of your own breath is the only sound you know.
