Deep in the Cut, there is no stillness.

The air flinches and regathers itself. Curtains of steam unravel and knit themselves back into gauze. Papery flakes of rust—somewhere between leaf and metal—tumble out of the fog, skittering over the wet ground and then lifting, borne on invisible little rips in the atmosphere.

The ground never dries.

Not truly. It’s a low, basin-like floor, maybe the size of a village or a small town, if you were to map it by walking. Smooth, curved depressions, like the bowl a thumb would make in soft clay, run shallow but everywhere. They’re filled with a thin layer of water that never quite evaporates and never quite spills over. Droplets coalesce on the rims of old pipes that rise out of the ground like brass straws, breathing their humidity in the cool.

The temperature sits at a cool hush, enough that your breath is faintly visible but not enough to sting. The air is thick with condensation. It gives you the feeling of being underwater, but without buoyancy, only weight.

Above, the light is neutral—no particular sun, no direction indicated. Just a uniform, pearly gray that refuses to name itself dawn or dusk or overcast. No shadows cast clear, only edges. Faces of the machinery, where the plating is still intact, are coated with a thin film of moisture that can be mistaken, at a glance, for sweat.

This is the heart of the Basin Engine.

The terrain is not natural earth. It’s all of it, underneath, something else. Wherever the water has been pried away by a momentary gust of air or a shift in the steam, you see glints of metal plating, hammered smooth by long erosion and then covered again by the same weight of time. There’s a repeating, low hexagonal pattern in the underside of things—a honeycomb of turned, discolored iron and copper alloy. It ghosts through the surface of the ground, like sinews under flesh.

The water isn’t a river. It isn’t a lake. It’s a continuous film of sheet, lacquer, thin glaze. In shallow hollows you see its faint skin of iridescence, where a thin silk of oil or rust residue or some other metallic effluent has been drawn out in the most delicate of slicks. Pebbles lie partially submerged in it, black and glassy—former slag, maybe, or long-fired refuse of some industry that’s now become indistinguishable from geology.

In the quieter, deeper basins, where the water is a finger’s width deep and no more, the surface reflects a sky of pipes and haze and dripping condensation. The reflection itself is not still. The water’s surface is always being stitched by tiny droplets falling from unseen plants above or the underbellies of machinery, or leaking from some interior of the world beyond.

Vegetation is sparse, but not absent.

Closest to the ground, thin filaments of algae and lichen—so dark you’d think them ink stains—coil in the seams of the metal. They’re not green. They’re a color halfway between the iron of the rust and the black of the oil. They’re nearly invisible, until you watch long enough to see them act slowly, like a very old time-lapse: coils of them swell fractionally with the moisture and then tighten down again as the air stirs.

Higher, there are a few kinds of plants. They’re not named trees, or shrubs, or anything that fits clean in a category. You’d call them structures.

One is a narrow, upright stalk of pale metal or something like metal, as thick as a wrist, rising from a shallow depression of water. From its upright spine, slender rods of copper or copper alloy fan out in thin parallel ribs, like a ladder or the hull of a very intricate airship. Each rib supports a handful of translucent leaves that are not exactly leaves but elongated membranes, stretched between tiny wires. They sag with collected moisture; then, when too heavy, they twist slowly, releasing a thin curtain of drops that fall into the basin below.

Others are more complex. They look like someone’s attempt to combine a pipe rack with a fern. Their main trunk is a segmented steel spine, each segment fitted with a ring of valves and nozzles that still leak a fine spray of vapor. Lacy growths of metal foam and flaked oxide spread out from each ring, like moss that’s been rendered in metal and rust. Here, the condensation that feeds on their surfaces runs faster and more visibly, forming small runnels that trickle down in thin lines.

One of the most unsettling of these plants has a central trunk that’s not entirely metal. It’s a fibrous bundle of cables and what might be vines of a synthetic nature, encased in a sleeve of something like glass or hard translucent resin. Inside, there are rhythms of turbulence—microcurrents of water and air that are never calm, swirling in a restless loop as if the entire plant is trying to breathe under glass.

These are not plants in the sense of growing from soil. They grow from the seams of the world’s own machinery, using its humidity, condensation, and the faint chemical warmth that still leaks from its depths. They feed the water cycle, as much as they are fed by it.

Sounds are not loud.

You’ll hear soft dripping, from many places at once. The slight metallic tink of a drop falling into a wet basin. The nearly inaudible sigh of a flexible valve relaxing somewhere far above. A faint, unstable hum of an electromagnet or a pump coil cycling in a distant gallery of the machine.

Sometimes, a low chug of something mechanical—like a heartbeat engine—begins to sound from deep under the floor, then fades away without reaching any discernible conclusion. The air around the noise shivers slightly.

The closest thing to a voice is the intermittent hiss of steam venting between a plant’s “segments” and the metal of the world. It’s like a quiet whisper, but it doesn’t say words.

Silence, in the Cut, is not a lack of sound. It’s a sound that’s been taken by other things: the constant dripping, the faint breathing of the valves, the heavy, humid click of droplets colliding with the shallow film of water over the ground.

There is life, but not the kind you expect.

What fauna there is exists at the edges of what you’d call animal. It’s rare, shy, and mostly made of moving parts rather than blood.

Here are the “rust-things” that clamber in the under-beams of overlapping pipes, where the condensation is thick and the rust has been softened into something like loam. They are not insects or rats or birds. They’re compact little machines, no larger than your hand, but made of parts of others—scavenged from the engines or whatever ran this basin before the engines drowned.

One of these rust-things might be a metal shell with six thin legs of corroded spring segments, each one slightly different length. The body of it is a scavenged valve casing or pistol case of some old, forgotten industrial model. Inside, there’s a whirling, restless energy source of uncertain—let’s not be polite—safety.

They’re not beautiful. They don’t have eyes. Just sensors. Maybe it’s a pressure gauge, cracked halfway but still functioning, or a thermal diode whose needle is erratic enough to give a semblance of curiosity. They move along the seams where the metal plating has been worn by long erosion into smooth, shallow gullies. They keep their depth, hugging the low floor of the Basin, where the humidity is highest and the thin film of water is least disturbed by violent air.

They’re “rare” because you never see more than one or two at once, and then not always. When you do see them, they react to your presence with a reflexive pause and then a sudden, scuttling retreat into the nearest crack of the world. The rust-things know the rules of this place.

Beyond the rust-things, there are other forms of motion—things that are not faunal so much as atmospheric.

Occasionally, a swirl of mist will gather above a basin of calmer water. The droplets of condensed moisture cluster not on a solid surface but around each other, suspended by a faint electrical charge or something like the tension of soap-film. For a moment or two, they form a translucent cloud with a vague structure: a suggestion of a sphere, a rotating ring of vapor, something like a very tentative cell of thought. It hangs there, then collapses, its droplets dropping into the water with a faint sound like rain on a metal roof, but much softer.

These are not “creatures” in the sense you would keep them or name them. They’re expressions of the turbulence built into the air, the moisture, the static of the pipes.

The built space of the Basin Engine is not exactly a city, or a factory, or a ruin in the ordinary sense. It’s something like all of them at once.

There are no soaring buildings, but there are engines of moderate scale rising from the wet floor, like cathedrals of pressure and metal, but on a more compact, “cathedral” room scale—the size of a nave or a large nave chamber, rather than a whole city block.

These are not single structures so much as clusters. The walls of the engines are not walls of masonry or wood. They’re made of overlapping plates of dense metal, bolted or forged together into rounded surfaces, smooth, polished, bulging like the muscles of a great engine.

Pipes and conduits are not laid neatly; they are the architecture. They curve in tight arcs across the face of these engines, forming arches and partial vaults. Many of them are coated with a thin layer of rust or oxidation that has been worn by the dripping, wet environment into smooth, shallow gullies. The rust’s color is mostly iron-red and coppery green, like a patina of old bronze.

The density of it all is high. There is no clear space that is not occupied by something else: pipes, vents, machinery, low, curving hard walls of the engines themselves, or the shallow, water-filled basins that cluster between all of the above.

The “rooms” of the Basin Engine are not “rooms” in the sense of having roofs and floors and walls separating “inside” from “outside.” They are more like open bays of an industrial plant whose roof has long been stripped away by time or weather or whatever force hollows things out. The perimeter of this area is defined by the engines, and the overlapping curves of pipes and plating create a sense of boundaries—womb-like interiors of machinery, where the air feels slightly more pressure, and where humidity hangs visibly in thicker veils.

There are openings—like doors or hatches, but larger and less defined by a “frame” than a “threshold.” You step from one wet basin of the world to another through these gaps where the plating of an engine wall has met and not sealed fully. The edges of these openings are smooth and worn by time, like the edges of a river stone.

Remnants of human or humanlike presence are present, but faint.

Structures of medium scale rise from the wet floor of the Basin Engine—builds on the scale of small houses or workshops, rather than skyscrapers or vast factories. They’re all metal, or partially metal, partially something else the world has grown over it.

Here is a “sentry box” of a kind of cabinetry made of grids—metal lattice with the glass of its windows long broken or dissolved—upside from the floor in a stream of “water” that flows from a nozzle in the side of one engine. The face of the box has been covered by a thin coat of rust that, in certain lights, looks like the patina of a mirror. You can see shadows of shapes in the rust, but not clear ones.

There is scaffolding of a kind in a corner of the Basin: thin metal poles that were once clearly part of some industrial system—now bent, chipped, and partially woven into the growth of the engine, like ligaments of a larger body.

Near one of the engines, there is a small platform: a rectangle of grating and metal, perhaps intended for a worker to stand on while reaching valves. The platform has been weathered by moisture and time. Its edges are curled into the shallow water; in the troughs between the grating’s beams, small puddles form and then drain into the basin below.

On some of the surfaces—deep inside one of the engine bays, where the humidity pools rather than circulates—there are faint marks etched or painted into the metal. They could be instructions: indicators of temperature, pressure ranges, or emergency symbols. None of them are legible in the proper language sense. They’ve been warped by rust and erosion into abstract patterns, like ideograms of a system that rebuilt itself several times over.

The tech of the Basin Engine is industrial, but not exactly like the factories of our world.

It is not sleek. It is not digital. It speaks a language of pressure and temperature and humidity, rather than silicon and electricity, although electricity plays a role in the hum.

The engines themselves are not machines in the singular sense of a single device with a clear function. They are machines of the plural, of overlapping, “scaled” production of atmosphere, rather than goods. The world of the Basin Engine seems built to do one thing above all else: make the air behave.

You can see it in the way the air around the engines is not still. It’s full of eddies and involutions and swirls of mist, where the moisture is being lifted from the shallow basins of water on the floor and then swung into the air once again. The engines exhale humidity outward.

Complex valves and nozzles dotted along the surfaces of the machinery cycle continuously. They rotate open in a slow, involuntary way—then snap shut with a soft hiss of vapor, or a low clunk of metal on metal. The steam that’s released is not scalding hot; it’s cool on the skin, but it feels upsetting, like a pulse of the world’s lungs.

Electrical coils and transformers of some kind—older, blocky designs—crackle with the faint charge of humming electricity. They’re covered in protective layers of rust and metal shells that the world has built over them, like a host of lichen encasing their circuitry.

There are a few, very odd “devices” that have not stayed fixed.

The Basin Engine supports “scattered” small artifacts of a more industrial kind—pieces of machinery that are not integrated into its engines yet still function, or at least move.

One example is a box of metal—maybe the size of a small trunk—standing on the wet floor. It’s not rusted through entirely. Its surfaces are pitted with wear, but the edges of its panels are still sharp and angular. It has a glass-covered window of a sort of meter or gauge, where the needle of the indicator moves not by a steady hand but in erratic little twitches, responding to a turbulence not in the machinery of the box itself but in the air of the Basin.

When you watch it long enough, the needle’s randomness gains a pattern—not predictable, but not unstructured either. It’s as if the world of the Basin Engine is using the meter of the box to display the turbulence of its own atmosphere.

Another example is a “fan” or a “scoop” of a sort of industrial air-movement device—a metal scoop of curved blades that are partially rusted but still partially functional. When the air is particularly turbulent in a particular canal of the Basin Engine, the scoop of blades begins to spin slowly, not with a steady wind but with jerks and halts. It moves air, but only because the air would move anyway.

The tech here is not “magic.” It’s highly industrial, but it’s become so thoroughly integrated into the world that it functions like a climate system of the world’s mind.

The atmosphere bends and bends.

There is an unusual quality to the Basin Engine that sets it apart from the familiar.

First, the water cycle seems not to be bound by gravity in the clean way we expect. It does not simply flow down and rest in the lowest basins. It lifts up in thin threads of mist, like heat from wet pavement seen on a hot day, but here it is constant and cool, not seasonal or warm.

The mist hovers in long, horizontal layers of cloud, at various heights above the floor of the Basin Engine. Sometimes, where you’d expect air to be clear and the “sky” visible, you instead see a ceiling of low cloud hovering not far above your head, like the roof of a world’s room.

Second, there are “rules” of matter that seem… not quite broken, but bent.

Metals rust differently than steel rusts in our world. They don’t just turn into iron oxide or something like it; they rust into a form of “material” that’s not exactly solid but not exactly liquid either. Some of the rust on the pipes of the Basin Engine is of a texture that, when you touch it carefully, you can feel give slightly under your finger, like a very dense foam of rust and air.

When you step away from the Basin Engine, through one of its thresholds, into the “outside” world of whatever this is—you remember the way your footprints were filled in almost immediately by the water’s thin film, not by running water but by condensation from the air.

That’s the unusual quality of the Basin Engine: it’s a machine of atmosphere that’s managed to remember its motions, and to keep them.

The dynamics of the Basin Engine are not quite violent, but they are relentlessly turbulent.

There is no “calm” here. As soon as any air in a part of the Basin begins to settle, the environment of the Engine becomes—uncharacteristically—still for just a moment… and then the air around that still point begins to swirl faster.

It’s like watching a whirlpool in a very slow film: you never see it clearly spin, but you know by the way that mist and droplets of moisture are drawn along it that it would.

Sometimes, a “storm” passes through the Basin Engine.

Not a storm of rain or thunder, but a storm of pressure and air.

The valves of the engines open suddenly with a sharp chorus of hissing and low metal clanging. The mist of the Basin flicks past like a film played in a hurry. The layers of cloud in the air above the floor shake violently for a moment or two, becoming torn and uneven, like fabric being jerked by unseen hands.

Then, as quickly as the storm began, it’s gone.

The air presses back against itself like a memory. The droplets that have been shaken loose from the leaves of the metal ferns and the “pipe” plants of the Basin Engine fall in a sudden thin drizzle of metallic water onto the wet floor, where they’re absorbed immediately.

Another kind of storm is more of a “bolus” of air and water that’s not a storm in the ordinary sense, but a “mouth” of the Basin Engine.

It happens often when the air above a particularly deep basin of water begins to circulate faster than elsewhere, forming a whirl of air and moisture that doesn’t quite have a solid center. It’s like a hollow of the world where air rushes into and then out of.

Wherever that “mouth” is, the environment around it becomes more dense than usual. The humidity’s veil thickens, and the droplets that are suspended in the air get more numerous—not just one or two here and there, but a small swarm of mist droplets dancing around the center of the whirlwind.

“Chaotic” change doesn’t mean “destroy” in the dramatic sense of a violent explosion of buildings. Here, it’s more subtle.

During the storms of pressure and moisture of the Basin Engine, some of the rust-things may be knocked from their perches on the pipes. They scuttle away from the turbulence, seeking cracks of each engine’s plating that they know better than you do.

When the storm subsides, you may find that one of the “sentry” boxes of latticework and metal that has been partially eaten by the rust has been disrupted further. Its supports of the metal have been nudged, or its shape of its structure has been warped slightly, by the pressure of the air.

In the most disruptive of the storms, some of the “plants” of the Basin Engine—the metal ferns and the pipe-trees—will be whipped by the air into a temporary frenzy of motion. Their membranes of water-bearing leaves and their wires will be bent violently by the wind of the engine, then relax when the storm passes.

The “destruction” the dynamic of the Basin Engine creates is not the violent demolition of a city, but the constant brushing of hands against a very old cloth of the world’s own atmosphere.

One of the most unsettling of the “storms” is not of air but of “growing” of the Engine.

Sometimes, during a particularly turbulent period of the air, when the mist has been drawn into thick, swirling layers of cloud and the droplets in the air are numerous enough to be almost like dust, a new structure of the Engine forces itself into being.

It begins like a pressure point of the air itself—an area of high turbulence of mist and droplets of moisture and a faint hum of electricity of some kind. The air around it begins to swirl faster than elsewhere.

The droplets in the air of that point begin to gather onto a finer, more filament of mist of the air. They join until they form a thin thread of moisture of almost metal-looking color.

Then, from the deeps of the Basin Engine’s floor of metal plating—wherever the rust has been disturbed by the pressure—the surrounding of the rust around the thread of moisture of the air begins to build upon itself, forming a thin line of solid of metal.

It’s as if the world of the Basin Engine has been influenced by the pressure of the air to sprout a new pipe of itself.

Afterward, when the storm of pressure and air has passed, the new of the structure, of the “sprout” of the Engine, remains. It’s not a well of roots of a tree or the trunk of a metal tree of the kind we’ve seen. It’s something like a very tentative of branch of the world’s own machine, of a “cathedral” of engine, but on a smaller scale—of a “room” of the Engine.
