The world of the Wellspring Grove lives at the upward edge of erosion.

Here, on a high shoulder of stone where the rest of the mountain has been patiently worn away, water does not fall so much as it persists. The air is warm and thick, carrying a humidity that feels like a hand held just above your skin; everything it touches glistens. Yet, this is not a jungle, nor a swamp, nor any known valley. It is a small, enclosed bowl of sky and stone, suspended in the long, calm after of some greater catastrophe.

---

**Terrain and rock**

At the core of the Grove is a shallow basin, perhaps the size of a village square, held in a rough oval of dark basalt. The basin almost wants to be called a crater, but there is no impact scar—no violence remains visible. The walls are curved, not ragged, and the stone has been worn by time and water until its edges are softened, like bone polished by constant hands.

The rim is not tall enough to be called a cliff, but it is too high to be a mere step—more the height of a cathedral choir than a garden wall. It rises on all sides, enclosing the Grove so completely that, to anyone standing within, it feels like a room of the sky.

The floor of the basin is not one flat surface, but a subtle layering of forms. The lowest point is a shallow hollow of bare stone, veined with a faint tracery of channels and dimples, like a palm print. Around that, the ground swells almost imperceptibly into low ridges—no higher than a knee, but clearly distinct from the flat center—and upon these ridges the only living things in the world grow.

The stone itself is the color of aged slate, darkened with moisture and stained with a faint mauve algae. It is not glossy smooth; millions of tiny pores and fractures and half-filled hollows give it texture, like a lung cast in rock. Water does not flow freely over it. Instead, it seeps from the rock itself, as though the stone is quietly exhaling.

---

**Water**

In the Wellspring Grove, there is no rain—but there is water everywhere.

The air carries water: not fog, exactly, but a condensation that hangs like a breath held in mid-thought. When you stand still long enough, you can feel tiny droplets gathering on your skin without any visible fall. The stone walls are damp to the touch, though not running with wet. It is as if the humidity has been persuaded to settle here rather than disperse.

The water emerges from the rock in three ways:

First, from the weeping walls. Certain patches of the basin’s rimstone are darker than the rest, and in these areas you can see thin films of moisture moving over the surface slowly—so slowly that only by watching for long minutes might you spot the change. A drop, the size of a nail’s head, might appear on the stone and remain for an hour before it detaches, trailing a thin thread of wetness as it vanishes.

Second, from the pool. The lowest hollow of the basin is not deep enough to be a lake, nor even a pond; it is more of a shallow platter of mirror-silver water. Its surface is never entirely calm and yet never shows anything you would call waves. Tiny cells of water tension well up from somewhere below, like bubbles of glass, rising and vanishing and rising again, leaving the faintest of rings that spread and disappear. The pool is not clear, but carries reflections of the sky and the rim in wavering, imperfect colors.

Third, from the channels. A faint network of grooves and furrows—some carved by time, some by the motion of water—radiates from the center pool. Many of them are dry to the eye, but if you drag your fingers over the stone after a long sitting, you will feel the faint suggestion of a damp line beneath the dust. A few of these channels, scarcely more than a hand’s width, always contain water: a thin, transparent film of it, no more than a few millimeters deep, that moves slowly outward from the center and then seems to sink into the rock again.

There is no sound of rushing or dripping. Occasionally, there is the faintest of “plinks” when the accumulated water on one overhanging edge of stone finally releases a drop, but it is a muffled, quiet noise—as if the water is reluctant to disturb the silence.

**Vegetation**

The vegetation of the Wellspring Grove is lush only by the standards of a mountain highscape: rich compared to the world above it, sparse by the measure of a rainforest.

No plants grow in the center basin—there is no soil for them. Life clings to the ridges that ring the pool, like a handful of guests seated around a hearth.

The tallest plants are the **stone-glass reeds**. They grow from narrow crevices in the ridges, where stone and a faint gray dust of weathered rock meet the constant moisture. Each reed is a single shaft, no thicker than a finger, but reaching as high as a person’s shoulder. They are hollow, and their translucent walls are veined with faint lines of milky crystal. They do not bend in the wind; there is almost no wind. They grow upright, straight as a rule, and each cluster is so precise in its proportion to those around it that the whole ring looks composed by a careful hand.

There are perhaps a dozen such clusters scattered around the basin’s rim—small colonies of a dozen or so reeds each. No single cluster dominates. Between them, the ridges of basalt remain bare, except for the films of mauve and bronze algae that creep across the stone in fractal patterns.

Around the bases of the reeds, **velvet moss** grows in low, dense cushions. It is a deep, almost black green, so thick that it appears to trap the light. Each cushion is no larger than the palm of a hand, and they are not evenly spread; each moss mound sits where exactly enough water gathers from the condensation to keep its filaments alive but not drown them. They feel like cool fur against the skin.

The most unusual growth are the **glass lanterns**—not true flowers, but complex structures that emerge when a reed is nearing the end of its long life. The outer sheath of the reed loses its color, becoming clearer and thinner, while the internal crystalline threads thicken and curl into a spherical hollow at the top. The air within this hollow glows faintly, a pale blue-white, without heat or flicker. The lanterns are not numerous; there are perhaps three or four in the whole Grove, and each one lasts for years before its structure finally fails and the glass crumbles into a dust no coarser than salt.

From any one vantage point, only a handful of plants are visible at once. The vegetation is **sparse**, but **lush** in its own terms: carefully appointed, well-fed by the water and warmth, each colony succeeding by occupying exactly what it needs.

**Atmosphere and air**

The air of the Grove has weight.

It is not oppressive, but it is **warm** and **dense** enough that you feel you could push your hands through it. It carries the scent of wet stone and something faintly metallic, like crushed copper ore held a safe distance from fire. There is very little fragrance—nothing floral, nothing spicy, just stone and water and the faint hint of ozone that might come from water passing over certain minerals beneath the surface.

Every so often, a **thermal** rises gently from the central basin. It’s not enough to be a wind, but you can feel it: a slow column of warm, humid air lifting to the sky, tugging almost silently at your clothes and hair. These thermals are slow and **gradual**, rhythmic as a breath. Their rising is balanced by the cooler air that rolls down from the shaded stone of the rim, allowing this **small** place to maintain its own **patient** climate.

The **light** in the Grove is **ordinary** in the sense of being sunlight, but not ordinary in its effect. Because the walls are curved and dark, some of the light is deflected inward, and a portion of the sky is always overhead, yet slightly **bounded**; it feels like you are looking up from the bottom of a well, save that the well’s shaft is open and full of clouds. The stone around you holds heat from the day, so that even when the sun is low at the horizons, the Grove remains **warm**—like a body whose heart has just been excited.

**Sound and silence**

The **sound** in the Grove borders on **silence**, but never truly crosses into total absence.

- A **faint** hum comes from somewhere beneath the basin—deeper, more **quiet** than a beehive, a monotone resonance that you might mistake for the blood in your own ears. It does not change much in pitch or volume; its **dynamic** is **slow** and **unchanging**.

- The **occasional** drop of water striking the surface of the pool or an exposed rock makes a **soft** “plink” or “tap,” but these sounds are **sparse**, **scattered**. You could go minutes without hearing one if you are not attentive.

- The **stone-glass reeds** do not sway, but as they lengthen over their **long** lives, tiny **cracks** creep within their crystalline structure. Each **fracture** is **too** minor to hear individually, but together they produce a **subtle** background **whispering**, like the **weak** sound of distant ice under pressure.

- Around **midday** or **midnight**, when **temperature** gradients **shift** most **slowly**, the **central** pool **rhythms** in **gentle** oscillations—minute **rises** and **falls** in **level** so **gradual** that **only** the **edge** of **silence** is **emphasized**.

- The **air** itself **carries** little **wind**; **no** branches **rustle**, **no** insects **whir**, **no** animals **call**. **Sound** here **is** the **emptiness** between **events**, **balanced-with** the **presence** of **water** and **stone** and **time**.

If **you** sit **still** long **enough**, you **might** notice **your** own **heart** measuring **out** the **pulse** that **nothing** else **provides**.

The **ecology** of **Wellspring Grove** is **strange** and **simple** by **design**—or **by** its **own** **long** **propensities**.

- The **velvet** **moss** **rel**ies **on** the **constant** **but** **gent**le **condensation** **from** the **air** and **d**ri **ps** that **run** **along** **the** ridges.** **It** **does** **not** **sp**read **like** **ordinary** **m**oss; **it** **grows** as **a** **compact** **mound** until **its** **center** **star**t **s** **to** **dec**ay **from** **lack** **of** **air** **within**— **and** **then** **a** **new** **mound** **may** **form** **beside** **it** **where** **conditions** **are** **better**.** **Each** **mound** **is** **an** **isolated** **community**, **never** **joining** **into** **a** **c**arpet.

**Rules of the world**

This **small** ecosystem **obe**ys **certain** laws **that** may **or** may **not** be **like** our **own** natural **laws**:

- **Water** **does** **not** **flow** in **visible** **streams** unless **it** is **actually** **ex**ceed **ingly** **thin**— **a** layer **of** **molecules** rather **than** a **cur**rent **you** can **s**ee **by** **eye**. **It** favors **cap**illary **action** and **films** over **fall**ing **torrents**.

- The **we**ird **quality** of **the** place **lies** in **the** **relationship** **between** **scale** and **detail**.** **From** **a** **distance**, **it** would **look** like **a** **bare** **basalt** bowl **with** **a** **few** **pat**ches **of** **green** and **a** **h**and **ful** of **t**hin **r**eeds.** **Up** **close**, **every** facet **of** stone **and** water **sh**ows **ind**ividual **text**ure **and** **history**, **and** the **glass** **lanterns** **give** **the** **ill**usion **of** **h**arb **oring** **stars** **inside** **them**.

- **No** **built** **structures** **exist** here **by** **nature**— **but** **someone** **or** **something** of **a** **more** **primitive** **technology** could **use** **this** **place**. **The** **stone** **is** **hard** **enough** to **re**quire **stone** to **chip** **it**; **the** **glass** of **the** **reeds** could **be** **slow**ly **shape**d **with** **careful** **heat** and **pressure** **from** **simple** **tools**; **the** **water** **could** **be** **channeled** in **min**ute **ways**.** **Yet** **there** **are** **no** **car**vings **or** **alt**ers **or** **fires** **here**— **just** **the** **raw** **potential** for **such** **prim**itive **craft**.

**As you look again**
