Light enters first as a bruise-colored smear, then as a faint afterthought of brightness seeping through cracks in the ceiling a hundred meters above the floor. The air is cool, hollow with the chill of deep earth, and tastes of metal and old root.

The city beneath the sky is not of stone, but of bone and resin and slow-growing heartwood.

It spreads in a low, uneven sprawl along the basin floor, where an ancient riverbed once carved its path through the planet’s crust. The river is long gone, leaving only a smooth trough of polished stone that now forms the main thoroughfare of this buried city. The buildings are neither tall nor tightly packed; they are scattered like the vertebrae of some vast, extinct thing, half-sunk into the silt and time-layered dust. Between them stretch wide, empty plazas, where nothing grows but wind and shadow.

The walls are not straight, and neither are the streets. The city is not planned—it is remembered. The foundations follow the old channels of water and the contours of buried rock, so that the whole settlement feels less like a construction than a crystallization, a pattern that has settled into its final shape and forgotten how to move. The streets are shallow grooves worn into the stone floor over millennia by the drag of countless passing feet—feet that have long since become dust themselves.

The buildings are carved from the trunks of long-dead hyperforests that once reached for the sun above the ceiling. The wood is black, polished from age, and intricately veined with patterns of fossilized resin that glow faintly, like a starless dusk sky. The structures are squat and wide, their roofs low and rounded to echo the curve of the ceiling far above. The space is open, but not sky-open; it is the open of a great hall whose far end is always in shadow. The scale of the chamber is regional, a gap between the planet’s plates that has been stable for eons; the city itself sprawls across its length, but it is the only city in this world-age below the world’s skin.

Density is sparse: there is space between each building and its neighbor, enough for a person to walk, turn, and breathe without touching. There are no alleys or second stories; there is only one level, and the emptiness of the ceilings between structures makes the occupied spaces feel like scattered islands in a deep, dark sea. Lamps of phosphorized fungus hang in niches of wood and bone, but they are few and their light is weak and patient. The glow of one lamp does not reach the next.

The vegetation is lush but strange, because there is no water as you understand the word.

There is no rain here, no river, no standing pool, not even the damp of soil after a storm. The caverns have been sealed for ages beyond the arithmetic of metal; the last true water bled away when the stars above were still young. The cool of the air is not wetness; it is the memory of water’s teeth in the stone.

Yet the plants are everywhere.

They are low and sprawling, most of them, clothed in thick, succulent flesh that glows faintly from within, like ingots of moonlight trapped in leaf and stem. They are not water-stored but time-stored; their chemistry is slow, a cold fire of rearranging bonds that has not needed replenishment in millennia. They live in the absence of water by using it as a symbol rather than a substance, their metabolism harvested from the faint warmth of the rock and the distant pressure of the world above.

The plants are lush not because they grow quickly but because they have never stopped growing. They are networks of long-lived, low-urgency life, each species a quiet empire spanning the length of the cavern floor. Their leaves are thick and waxy, overlapping like shingles on a roof, and their patterns form fractal tapestries across the walls and bones of the city. The buildings are not choked by them; rather, they are embraced by them, the wood and bone having been softened over eons into the same porous texture as the plants themselves.

You begin to notice the sound.

The initial silence of the deep earth is only an absence of human noise. When you listen, you realize that the chamber is not quiet at all. The sound comes from everywhere and nowhere at once, a pressure that has weight in your ears.

The fauna are abundant in number and presence, but not in variety.

There is only one species, and you have not yet seen it clearly. You have heard it, though. The air is thick with the sound of its breathing—deep, resonant inhalations and exhalations that never pause, rising like waves from the floor and the walls and the ceiling itself. It is a sound like low thunder folded over and over itself, or the hum of an immense machine that has run for so long that no one remembers a time before it.

The creatures are not small.

They move through the caverns slowly, on great limbs that are both flexible and stolid, like a tree’s roots that have learned to walk. The bodies are not segmented; they are continuous, like hills of exposed flesh and bone that have grown up around the city’s edges and between its districts. Their eyes, if that is what they are, are as wide and shallow as pools of polished stone, set without expression in the surfaces of their bodies.

They are the city. The buildings are embedded into their sides, fused into their skin over time until the boundary between flesh and architecture is blurred. The bone walls are not carved from fallen trees but from the creatures’ own skeletons, exposed like ribs around the hollow spaces where they allow life to shelter. The glow of fungal plants is not a decoration; it is woven into the creatures’ hide at regular intervals, providing the faint, steady illumination that keeps the city from true darkness.

The creatures are immensely old, each one a geological formation that has not changed in any important way for longer than the rock has had memory. They do not eat in the way you know eating; they are fixed to the floor, their roots anchored deep in the stone, and they draw on slow, chemical processes that have been running for ages. The dry, cool air of the caverns is a result of their breathing—a constant exchange of gases born not of water, but of ancient reactions that keep the world below self-contained.

When you look at the city again, you see that it is alive not in the way of streets bustling with people, but in the way of a forest’s undergrowth. The inhabitants—if they can be called that—are not builders of the city. They are more like tenants who have lived in the same house for so long that they have become part of the wall.

There are no humans. There are no tools. There is no technology.

The creatures that walk the caverns are not making use of anything but their environment and their own bodies. The “streets” are the paths worn by regular movement between their bodies as the inhabitants shift from the shadow of one rib to the next. The “architecture” is not shaped with chisels but by biological growth and personal familiarity over generations of bodies that have not changed in their basic design.
