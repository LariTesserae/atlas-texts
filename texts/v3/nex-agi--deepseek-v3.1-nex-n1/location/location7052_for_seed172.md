High above the ordinary world, clamped to the flank of a mountain like a metal barnacle, the Aeration Station sits.

This is not a mountain as you know it—shale, or granite, or ice-scoured—but a construction of another scale and era. The mountain itself is a standing wave of perforated steel, a kilometer-high latticework of ribs and braces, smooth and cold and utterly seamless. It reaches into bitter sky, thin with altitude, pinned to an orbit of factories that gnaw at the margins of the atmosphere. The Aeration Station is a pocket of attention within that mountain; a single room the size of a small cathedral, embedded in the steel anatomy like a seed lodged in amber.

The air inside is dry.

Not dry like a desert’s breath—deserts have ghosts of rivers, rumors of groundwater—but dry as a perfect vacuum might be if it could still be called air: nitrogen and oxygen rattled free of water vapor by decades of industrial refrigeration. The Station’s atmosphere is held to a cold so precise and steady it seems to cleave time from matter. Zero point zero zero something Celsius, the instruments insist—you have to be close to the displays to make the numbers out through the haze of condensation.

You breathe dust and the memory of dust.

Instruments line the walls—T-hinged racks of them, bolted to the floor and ceiling, a thicket of glass and polished metal you can walk around and between. The sensors are buttoned, calibrated, drilled; they are mute aside from the rhythmic click of their relays, the insectile motion of their needles. The ambient is loud enough to be almost physical: a standing pressure of noise. The hum of the induction grid—magnetic fields the width of a city, quietly levitating custom-fitted alloy plates—cuts the air into laminates. You feel the hum in your teeth as much as you hear it.

Light: the cold-white of industrial discharge lamps.

There is no sun outside; you know the mountain exists outside of doors, but you do not think of it as existing outdoors. The mountain is pure infrastructure: a platform of holding space, an array of scaffolding. You’ve never glimpsed sky through the Station’s walls—no portholes, no windows. The lights are always on, and always as bright as they are now. The rhythm of the shift never changes.

Heat: not.

Aeration does not warm the space. Air moves through towering, ducted columns—great shafts rising from a floor that you do not see beneath generations of fine-grained steel dust. You understand the system: these are not lungs, but the lungs of a lung machine. The room is being breathed—inhaled, exhaled—through a diaphragm of baffles and vanes. The air is drawn in, pushed, sorted, filtered, milled for particulates and then released again, unchanged in composition but changed in navigation.

The dust: the only loose texture.

From the floor to a height of about knee level, a thin blade of dust accumulates as you watch. It is not exactly falling; it is slowing, from the velocity of industrial blasting to the velocity of a stone’s long pause. The dust is a bloom of milled metal—micrometer-scale shavings and condensates from the mountain’s endless fabrication and refabrication. You have to be careful of the sensors. If you disturb the dust near their intakes, you can see the tiny needles of their instruments twitch at the shift of particulate concentration.

The dust is not consistent.

Some regions on the floor—where the air does not stir—are thicker with gray-white. Elsewhere, near the main ducts, the floor is bare polished metal, scoured by the wind of the machine’s respiration. The geometry of the patterns is fixed. You remember, from years before, that the arrangements of dust and bare floor have been exactly like this since training first took you into the Station. They’ve never been disturbed.

Water: nothing.

There are no condensation waterlines running through the walls—not in the way you might imagine, plumbing and rust-pockets. There’s no condensation at all. The humidity is so low that any moisture you would exhale from your own body would be absorbed by desiccation modules before you’d see it. You don’t see ice-clusters on the ceiling; you don’t see frost on the floor. You have, though, seen a single shard of ice once—very thin, translucent, a wafer of strange frost—hovered for a heartbeat between the intake of a filter and the edge of a sensor’s casing.

It was gone before you could confirm it.

(You’ve asked no one about it. There’s no one to ask.)

The machinery is not ornate.

The internals of the Aeration Station are all—or almost all—well-made. Gears of human scale, designed for human maintenance—handles, levers, inspection panels. You recognize the language of bolts: “you may tighten me,” they say. “You may loosen me.” You can open a hatched panel in the wall of one duct and find an etched diagram of the airflow printed on metal. You know, from the diagram, that the room you are in is a very small node of a much larger machine: the mountain of steel, a factory of air.

The air Factory.

Factories—plural—exist outside the mountain’s shell. The engines and the induction grids are too loud to be heard as themselves; the sound that you hear, the deafening pressure, is the sum of all the engines and the induction grids and the ducts and the compressors—the composite of a machine that you cannot look at directly but which can be heard along the metal of the floor plates.

You are inside of the machine.

You are not a “person” to the machine—at least, you have not exactly been rewarded for thinking of yourself that way—you are a moving leak of heat and moisture and a contaminant and a calibration of all systems. You’re standardized—the same jacket you’ve worn for years, the same boots—and you keep your body temperature low enough to avoid setting off alarms. You measure yourself as you measure the air.

There is no vegetation in the room.

There is no vegetation in the mountain of steel—none, not a moss or a biofilm or a stray sprouting endurance—and yet there is one area of the wall where you think, if you were a botanist, you might call vegetation sparse. You wouldn’t call it that—you’re a technician—and yet you do.

Near the front of the room, where the air is not so violently stirred, there is a thin seam of a darker powder—perhaps alloy—through the metal. You’ve seen it develop—if you can call the development of something that has been the same for years a development—over your time on duty. It is not growing upward; it is not growing outward. It is present—as though someone once, somewhere, in a wall of the factory, or in the bedrock of a planet that you do not remember, scraped away a layer of corrosion and left behind this shadow.

You’ve asked yourself if it’s a residue of floral—of contacts—lost when you wipe your goggles on your sleeve. You’ve watched your sleeve. You’ve watched your gloves.
