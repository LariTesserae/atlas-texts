They call it the New Cavern, though no one remembers who named it.

The stone is still too young to have a proper name.

---

You come down into it through a long, slanting tunnel cut from the rock—not by axes or picks but by water and time, before those tools were ever imagined. The stone here is raw and unfinished, a great gray-green throat of stone that still feels like it’s swallowing the world it’s made of. The walls have a damp, unfinished look; they’re not smooth yet, not shaped by long ages of dripping water. They’re still all rough planes and sharp edges, as though the cavern might decide at any moment to grow another wall or seal another passageway.

The air is cool, but not cold. It sits like a breath held against your skin. The light is strange—steady enough to see by, but not bright enough to feel like the work of suns or moons. It simply is, coming faintly from the stone itself and from the thin, pale formations of something that isn’t yet crystal but wants to be.

Along the walls are shallow pockets, hollows carved by water that hasn’t happened yet. The occasional drip of water falls into them from unseen cracks above, each drop a small event in the silence of the cavern. The pools are tiny—mere cups worth of water—but they’re everywhere, studding the rock like stars in an early-night sky. The stone is so damp that it glows in faint streaks where streams of unseen water are hiding beneath the surface, waiting to be born.

You hear your own footsteps first.

The cavern amplifies them; the stone seems to listen. The rhythm of your footfalls is the only definite sound. Then, as you listen under the sound of your breath, you begin to catch something else: faint, distant, like water whispering through stones too small to see.

---

You emerge into the open.

The cavern is not a tunnel anymore but a chamber so wide you can’t see where it ends in the dimness. The ceiling is low—just low enough to press you into a sense of being under something rather than inside it—with the rock still in its raw, jagged youth. The light is normal enough, but filtered by the stone and the distance, so that it seems to hang in the air like dust caught in mid-fall.

The floor is dominated by water.

Not a river, not yet. Just a suggestion of one: a wide, shallow depression of rock that holds water in thin, uneven sheets like cracked glass. The water flows, but slowly—so slowly that if you weren’t watching carefully, you’d think it was still. The current is more like a thought forming than a river running.

This is the Spine, as people call it.

It’s not really a river yet. It’s a river’s idea, a river-in-training, a hesitant line between drier ribs of stone that rise up from the floor like the bones of a half-skeleton. The Spine is the water, and the water is the first thing that ever decided to stay in this place.

---

The world is still deciding.

Where the Spine isn’t, there is stone. And where stone isn’t, there is the city.

The settlement isn’t fancy—just low, round houses of stone layered one atop another in a tight weave of walls and roofs and ladders. The place is jammed, packed, dense: every space that isn’t water or standing stone has been claimed by building or pathway or shared space. There are no open plazas, only narrow gaps between walls where people must squeeze. The city feels like it grew up between the constraints of water and rock like a plant in a crack.

The houses are built from the same raw stone as the cavern itself—rough-hewn blocks cut by hand tools and stacked by hand. Wood is scarce; there is no forest above or below. Instead, they use thin sheets of lamellar stone, carefully split from the walls, that ring like soft bells when struck.

The people are few enough to be known by name, but not so few that they’re lonely. They live simply here, in this place that hasn’t finished being born yet.

---

Vegetation is sparse, but not absent.

The plants grow only where the water is willing to gather—where the Spine sends its tentative fingers into shallow channels carved experimentally into the stone. The plants are not like trees above, not like anything with leaves meant to turn sunward. They’re dark things with thick fibrous stems that cling to the stone like hands clutching a ledge.

They grow in neat little patches along the edges of the Spine, arranged like careful stitches of moss along the edge of a wound. Some of them are only faintly green, more stone than leaf in color and texture. Their roots push into the damp stone like the first questions of a world asking to be.

There are no true flowers yet. Only ghostly blooms of pale stoneflower—a growth that seems like a cross between mineral and plant, its surfaces faintly luminescent like the light on the walls themselves. They give off no scent, only a faint coolness in the air around them.

---

The animals are rare.

You hear them before you see them—soft clicks from tiny claws scraping stone, or the faintest whisper of wings brushing against each other in the darkness above. The insects are the first to claim the air; tiny, pale-shelled things that drift like tiny dust motes through the cool light.

There’s nothing that hunts them, not yet. They’re the only predators of this small world, feeding on whatever is weaker than they are—which isn’t much. The biology of this place is still forming its first rules. There are no seasons yet, only the slow shift of time between slightly warmer and slightly cooler breaths of air sliding down from the unseen cracks that lead upward toward the world above.

The ecosystem is still drawing itself on paper that hasn’t decided yet whether it wants to be stone.

---

Around the Spine and the city of rock and canals, there is a dense network of paths and bridges and low walls, all occupying the same space that nature might have claimed. The settlement feels urban because of how thoroughly everything is used: every scrap of stone is something, whether support or stair or storage shelf built into the wall. There is no wasted space. The city feels packed because every surface is in service to something.

The people who live here are not primitive in the sense of being ignorant, only in the sense of tools. They have no great machines, no mechanisms of steel or electric engines. They have stone knives and chipped stone blades and simple stone hammers; they work with stone because stone is what the world has given them.

They build their own homes out of the same stone that holds them up, and in doing so they have become part of the cavern’s formation.

---

The technology of the place is simple and precise.

They borrow water from the Spine carefully, using raised stone channels that barely skim the surface of the river-to-be. They don’t dam it; they don’t try to bend it to their will violently. They know that the water is still deciding what path to take, and they live alongside that decision.

They light their houses with pale stone lanterns that glow with a faint inner light, like the stoneflower but more controlled. These are made by drilling carefully into the glowing stone growths that grow along the cavern walls, then sealing them with thin sheets of clear stone to keep their light steady.

Their tools are stone and bone and wood scavenged from where it falls, rarely, from above. They fashion crude handles and simple levers. They understand how to move weight without moving the world beneath it.

There’s no metal yet—not enough to matter. There’s no need for it yet either.

---

The rules of this world are still writing themselves.

The stone doesn’t erode exactly; it reforms. When water touches it, instead of wearing it away quickly like it would in older stones, it seems to soften the stone and allow it to be slowly rearranged. The people of the cavern know that if they leave water in one place long enough, the stone beneath will be gently shaped into a new curve or a new channel.

Weather doesn’t exist here in the usual sense. There are no storms above ground, no thunder below ground. Instead, there are subtle shifts of coolness wrapped around faint changes in moisture. The air feels a little more damp in one time than the next, and that’s what passes for weather.

The ecosystem itself is still active—but quietly so. Plants grow in slow, careful motions; insects move in short flights between stoneflower and stone; water flows slowly and steadily in its pattern of becoming. The cycles are subtle: the water level rises just slightly, then falls just slightly, and the insects follow the water level.

The city is part of that cycle too, though it doesn’t realize yet that it is.

---

The people of the New Cavern do not think of themselves as builders of cities yet. They think of themselves as people who live near the water. They think of themselves as people who built their houses where the stone allowed.

Over time, though, their homes have become part of the stone itself. The stone walls they have raised have guided water away from certain areas and into others. The stone steps they carved into the rock as an aid to climbing have become places where water gathers and begins its own small experiments in shaping the world.

The city itself is becoming geology.

---

The word landscape doesn’t feel quite right here.

This isn’t a fixed place, like something above ground that stands still for centuries to be seen by passers-by. It’s a place in formation, caught halfway between being and becoming. The stone is still young; the water is still learning its path; the plants are still deciding what a plant is supposed to be in this kind of world.

The city is still learning what a city is supposed to be in this kind of world.

And yet, despite all that, there is a sense of openness—a sense that you could walk in any direction and the world would continue, though it might deepen or narrow or change its character. The mere fact that you cannot see the far edge of the cavern doesn’t mean that it doesn’t continue; it simply hasn’t finished arriving yet.

---

In the quiet light, under that low, unfinished ceiling of stone, the water of the Spine flows slowly toward whatever future it has already chosen but hasn’t reached yet.

The city grows quietly around it.

The stone contemplates itself.

Somewhere beyond the next curve of rock wall, beyond the next knot of stoneflower and stone lanterns, there might be more caverns opening like lungs for the first time, or more water making its tentative decisions about how it wants to shape the world.

In this place beneath the surface, where everything is still emerging, everything is still arriving. The world hasn’t decided yet what it will be when it finishes growing. But it already knows what it wants first:

To keep its water slow.

To keep its stone close.

And to leave room, between the packed houses and the quiet streams of stone and water, for whatever comes next.
