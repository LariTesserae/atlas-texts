The floor of reality is breathing.

The first thing you feel is not air, but foliage. The second thing is not space, but time, and it is tearing itself open. You stand in the low, warm bowl of a plant-world so absolute that there is no ground distinct from the life that grows upon it—only a living mattress of chlorophyll, a root-mat thirty meters deep, turning itself inside out every few minutes to show you a new color.

Material is in short supply. Life is not.

Everywhere, plant architecture is rearranging itself. The dampness hangs in the air as a constant pressure, not a rain but a condensation of the world itself onto the surfaces of things. Water drops are not separate from the leaves; they detach from the sky-membrane that stretches between the canopies and then *reabsorb* into the nearest leaf edge before they can fall to the floor. The river here is not a line of rock but a line of behavior: the plants along a certain winding path bend themselves into a low, cupped trough of overlapping leaves, and a brief, clear seam of liquid water flows along it for a few moments before the trough itself withers and new shoots stitch it closed. The watercourse’s next position is not yet decided; you are watching the world’s first draft of a river.

Light is ordinary but wrong. The sun is a sun. There is no trick of brightness or color. But the shadows it throws are not consistent from one heartbeat to the next. The massive tree-like column that dominates the middle distance—its trunk is a braided spiral of bioforms that might be trees, or fern, or coral, or all of them—sends a shadow behind you; then the tree flares itself open into a new shape of limbs and parasols, and the shadow is not behind you, but in front. You move to avoid it, and the tree somehow *moves backward* up the time of the day, so the shadow has already fallen where you are stepping to. You get used to stepping into the past of the shade.

Everything is young here. New edges. Not new in the sense of “unformed dirt,” but new in the sense of never being finished. The terrain feels like the first season of a world, where every rock is still being extruded from the root-mat by the pressure of something below, and the plantmass is still debating whether to keep the stone or swallow it back. You are not sure the world has decided yet to remain solid.

Most of the surfaces are not stone at all, but wood-and-plant hybrids that have not yet chosen their function. The ground is a mat of overlapping stems, each so thick and leathery that they are more like sleeping animals than branches. As you walk, they groan under your weight. The sound of it is lost in the general noise, which is not loud so much as total.

There is no animal here. No birds, no insects, no worms you can see. There is only vegetation and topology and moisture and light and the sound of all of it arguing at the same time.

The sound is not a soundscape you can break into pieces. It is a wall of vibration.

The canopy above you breathes in a slow, deep sigh of sap and fiber and air; the stems of the floor creak; the water-troughs hiss as they are formed and then as they are sealed. The tree-column shrieks as it unbraids its trunk and then braids it again in a new geometry, and the sound is not like wood breaking but like the timbers of a ship the size of a continent being tested under storm. The high leaves moan as the wind picks them up and then they *reweave* themselves into a new pattern of holes that channels the wind into a different scream. The air is full of rasping, snapping, cracking, punctuating the constant drone of the root-mat flexing underground.

You are beginning to understand that the world grows not only toward the sun, but also toward time.

The settlement is not built of stone or metal. There is no technology here, none at all—no tools, no fire, no devices. There is not even a cleared space of bare ground. The built is simply the place where the plant-world has chosen to fold itself into *rooms*.

The town is a pattern of co-opted biology.

Where you expect streets, there are instead narrow valleys of compressed stem, where the plantmass has compacted itself into a fibrous floor, and layered the canopy above into a tunnel of overlapping leaves. The corridors are low and humid, the air close and warm, the passage of your body making the walls of foliage around you ripple and adjust. The stems of the floor are not flat; they have been forced into a shallow, trough-like shape that might be invited by the pressure of countless feet before you, but then you notice that the troughs are too regular to be accidental, and that they occasionally *smooth themselves* when you are not looking.

Passages branch off from one another like a textbook of vein patterns, like the intagli of a leaf writ large into three dimensions. The intersections are clearer where the plant-matter thins out into a circular pool of liquid water, no bigger than a well, but perfectly circular, and the rim of the pool is formed of knuckles of root that have braided into a low rim, then hardened into something like pale wood. If you were to try to break it with your hand, you would not be able to. The world is not fragile here; it is only *temporary*.

In the “buildings” of this town—which is not a town of humans, but perhaps of whatever memory is woven into the world itself—the walls and roofs and floors are all of plant matter, but shaped differently enough from the surrounding wild forest to be recognized as structures.

The first house you see is not a house at all, but a tree that has hollowed itself in a neat cylinder of trunk and then *grown itself outward into a room* around the hollow. The room is not square but hemispherical, the floor of interwoven mat that is not fungus, not moss, not grass, but all of them and none of them. The ceiling is a dome of woody cortex that forms the roof of the tree-house, then rises into the sky as a tower of still-living tree above. Narrow slits in the dome wall serve as windows and as light pipes; the sun falls in long bars across the floor of the plant-carpet, which shifts itself from time to time to show you a new pattern of color, like a code you are invited to read but cannot learn.

The next structure is more of a bowl than a building: a low, circular depression where the root-mat has been coaxed into a series of terraces, each one no more than a few steps wide, but stepping down toward the center of a circle that is a clear pool of water. There is a pattern of fine translucent stems rising from the water like the fronds of an underwater fennel, and they are busy turning themselves into and out of a new configuration of leaves and tiny nodules of what might be proto-fruit or proto-seed. The world is trying to produce a new kind of thing here, and the pool of water is agitated, not by wind but by the agitation of the plant-life itself, which thrums and bulks and then collapses back into its waterline.

The third and strangest structure is the one that makes you certain the world is not only living but also remembering.

There is a mound of plant matter at the center of the settlement that is not shaped like any natural tree. It is a long, low building of sorts, built like a barrow or a longhouse of leaves and stems and biased trunks that have grown themselves into straight walls and then bent into a roof. The walls are not of logs but of *entire trees* that have been bent to approximate horizontal lines and verticals. The symmetry of it is startling enough—but as you watch, the front of the structure is not finished the way you found it. The canopy of the roof peels back like a skin of book being opened to the next chapter, then the structure of the wall pillars itself into new positions, and a new pattern of openings and chambers and corridors appears within. The “floor plan” of this house is not stable in time; it is stable only in its constant willingness to be rearranged.

The settlement is not populated by people or animals. There are no bodies to move here. There are only spaces shaped for them.

The air is densely warm, not oppressive but not cool either; it clings to you like a damp cloth. The dampness expresses itself not as mist but as condensation on the leaves of everything around you, and then as the water itself being recycled into the plant tissue before it can fall to the floor. The root-mat below you often unties itself from its own weave to show flashes of black soil—then closes the gap again as if embarrassed by the revelation of ground.

Occasionally the water in the river trough will break its pattern of leaf and root and become a true river of liquid water for a few minutes, cutting a shallow temporary channel into the surface of the world; then the plant-border on either side rolls over into the flow and *dams it* with a new wall of flesh, and the watercourse is forced to choose a new route to keep moving.

The world is not destroyed by these changes. It is not exactly built by them either. It is being *written*.

You are walking in a place where the present of a landscape is not determined by its past, but negotiated by its present. The hill to the north of the settlement doesn’t remember if it was supposed to be high or low; you watch it rise itself a few meters taller, then shrink itself lower, as if trying different ideas of what hillness should feel like. The path of a river is not carved by erosion but by *argument* between the plant matrix and the water’s insistence on being a liquid line.

The sound of this is not mechanical. It is not metallic or electronic. There is no tool here, no machine. The sound of the world is like the sound of a forest under storm, but without the storm of weather: only the storm of being.

The canopy overhead is dense, not in the sense of a single solid roof of leaves but in the sense of constant layer upon layer of foliage and stems and parasols and globules and hanging fronds and bulbous growths that are not yet fruits or flowers or tumors but some of all of them. The space is not vertical open sky but a vertical city of plant matter, where the lower layers of the world are not fully dark because the higher layers occasionally *lift themselves aside* to show a new window of sun above, then slide the pattern of their leaves back into a new crib.

If you stand still, you feel the place of your body contested by the world.

The ground does not move under you in the sense of sliding or shifting; instead, the stems in the mat immediately around you slowly *remember* that they are stems, and start to curl themselves upward around your ankles and then along your calves, as if they are not sure whether you are a path to be made smoother or a tree to be integrated into their structure. The first time this happens, you tear yourself free; later, you learn that the world is not trying to trap you but to learn you, and the stems unwrap themselves when you shift your weight with a small, desultory flick of their leaf-ends, as if they are not offended but curious.

You are standing in the middle of a reality that has not yet decided fully to be a location or to be a process.

The boundaries of the town are not fixed by walls or fences or gates of stone or wood. They are fixed by the *pitch* of the world.

The forest outside the settlement is not a different forest of species; it is the same overfull density of plants, but with fewer of the shapes that are meant to be rooms. The canopies are wider and less structured into corridors and roofs and defined floors; the stems do not cushion themselves into the same trough-like paths they do in the town. The world outside is still full of rearrangement and self-bending, but the pattern is less coherent on the level of a human scale.

There is a sense of a region, rather than a single spot: the valley in which the town sits is low in elevation, but it is not a flat plain. It is a bowl of low hills and undulations of plant matter, where the ground itself is not a stable surface but a topology of living root-mat that swells and compresses itself like breathing lungs. The horizons of this world are not formed by mountains or oceans but by the limits of how far the plant-world can maintain itself in one configuration of colors before drifting into something else.

The river of the world makes its way through this valley in a complicated geometry of trial and error. The map of its water is not engraved by time but written by daily negotiation. The river’s banks are not of earth but of *border* tissue where the stems at the edge of the water arrange themselves in a particular pattern that is part-root and part-leaf and part-membrane. The temperature of the place is not tropical heat but the warmth of a world that has not yet decided to be colder or hotter than this. What is loud is not sound but the *absence* of sound in a new way.
