At the center of the city’s heart, in the highest tier of the high mesa, there is a garden that no one planted.

I call it a garden, but you might not, at first glance. You might call it a chamber. A bowl. A vault. A mouth.

The city hangs itself from the lip of the world like a chandelier of polished stone. The mesa is all knifecut edges and sheer drop-offs, a plateau of vesicular glass-basalt that rose fast and cooled faster, waterless from the beginning. Steam and time have never had the chance to soften it. No streams run on the mesa-top; what rain falls vanishes at the rim of the sky into a white-hot haze long before it reaches the ground. No river ever carved a valley here. The ground is still new enough to have opinions.

The city, called Qesh, has been built around this absence of water: on the high mesa, where the air is thin and clear and the faint, constant arc of the world’s edge is just visible on the far horizon. No aqueducts; no wells. No fountains or cisterns. Instead, there is a cultivated abundance of other kinds of flow: heat, wax, resin, mycelium, honey.

At the very center of the mesa, a hole descends.

The city circles around this vertical slit in the stone. A jagged oval, maybe sixty meters along the long axis and half as wide across the narrow; it is not exactly a chasm or a shaft, but something like a chasmal shaft, or a shaft that has been split longitudinally and had one side taken away. The stronger side of the city, the inner side, is made of stacked terraces and spiral balconies that climb down into the opening itself, so the buildings hang over the dark like pale toothed ribs of limestone.

If you see the city from afar—and there are vantage points on the lower treeline of ancestral mountains where you can, just barely—you see a white ring poured around a black center. A pupil of shadow.

Come closer.

The air around the mesa is warm and steady, but the heat of Qesh is not the oppressive wet of flood plains you will never find here. It is a high, thin warmth: sunlit stone and warm air allowing you to forget, for hours at a time, that your body ever needed cold. The days are long and quiet and saturated with sunlight. The sky is a heavy, glassy blue, thick enough to feel.

As you ascend the mesa (there are multiple paths, but normally the shallow one that is the least like a climb and the most like a processional), the city begins to accumulate around you.

First, you notice the sound of no sound.

The city is not silent. Every city makes noise, even this one. But it is…quiet. The basalt and limestone and porous glass of the mesa absorb the way sound would bounce off lower canyon walls. The air is thin. The streets are narrow and overgrown, and vines grow thickly between the paving stones, and the people move slowly.

What you hear instead is a subtle, weightless ringing of living things.

You hear:

— the click of resin falling.

— the soft, intermittent puff of vines breathing out.

— the high, thin, just-beyond-threshold vibration of thousands of pollinators hovering at the edge of hearing.

— the faint whisper of air moving across curtain walls woven of waxen fiber.

— the dull tap of fruit falling.

There is no water’s roar, no rush of river, no hum of ducts or clang of machinery. No hooves or wheels or bells. Those are technologies of different earths. Here the tools are all extensions of something else.

The city is built from the skins of other things. The outer walls are made of a pale, ridged stone that is part stone and part coral: a calcium carbonate that grows in a thin, shelly layer over structural scaffolds of living vine. The vines are heavy-trunked and soft, wider than a person’s chest at the base, and they grow in dense, overlapping circles around clusters of buildings. Their trunks bond together at the ground level, forming a single, uneven floor that rises and falls in gentle rolling waves as you move deeper into the city.

This architecture is not “built” in the sense you probably know, but it is intensely engineered. The people of Qesh understand plant intelligence and directed growth with the same precision as other peoples might understand hydraulics or metallurgy. They cultivate the vine lineages—pale-trunked, felt-textured, with deep purple bark like pressed velvet—and persuade them into low walls, high ceilings, open chambers, and hollows.

Doors are not carved but pruned. When a new passage is required between houses or terraces, the gardeners of the city’s heart make small cuts and then guide the trunks’ growth around the gap with tension and weight and the careful balance of nutrient. The cuts are healed with resin and wax and then claimed by hungry, quick-growing mycelial lattices that reinforce the newly opened edges.

When you pass through a doorway in Qesh, you are walking through a living frame that is still aware of you.

As you move deeper, the vegetation becomes thicker than any “forest” or “jungle” you have known. Huge, translucent banyan-like structures hang down from the upper terraces, their rootlike tendrils reaching nearly to the ground, forming grove-cathedral spaces of sun-dapple green. The temperature is steady but not the air; the air is dense with leaf and living and the slow, invisible swirling of fungal spores.

There is no water here.

Watch the way your eyes adjust to that absence.

In the arch of a sky where you would expect clouds, there is fabric. Curtains of waxen fiber hanging in high loops between tall towers. These curtains are host to whatever is theirs: the fibers have a faint, translucent sheen and thin, constant motion of tiny, almost-seen somethings moving across them.

Where you would expect rain, there is dust.

Pale particulate falls from the sky, not constantly, but at intervals that you can learn if you stay here long enough. It drifts down from the high edges of the mesa and sometimes from the ceilings of the grove-cathedrals, and it settles on the vines and the leaves and the broad, flat plates of fungus that have grown over the old paving stones. The dust is rich and complex and full of enzymes that your body will not be able to digest, but which everything else here recognizes.

Where you would expect puddles or seep or wet, there is only wax and honey.

You see:

Resin pools on the cobbles—thick, translucent gold, like hardened amber. It has seeped from the trunks of the giant vines. When it falls, it does not splash; it falls thickly and flows slowly, like glass.

You see rubs of wax, translucent and a faint olive-green color, spun around certain corners where the wind is shared and reused. Waxen thresholds are smoothed by bodies passing over and under them again and again. The city is built out of wax and resin and stone and vine and mycelium and honey and paper and tin.

Tin—?

Yes. Tin. There are small, thin sheets of it, hammered impossibly thin and then half-grown-over by stone, hung in places between vine trunks. The people of the city have enough to shape a few tools, but not enough to make engines of them. A metal that is ornamental and sacred and rarely used, because it is brittle and heavy and must be brought from far away.

Instead, they make use of what grows.

The “fauna” here is abundant and wonderfully strange.

It is mostly insects, but you will find no word for “insect” in Qesh. The distinction between pollinator and architect and messenger and priest is…flexible.

You see:

- Small, beetle-shaped things that are part of the wall. When you pass, they unfurl from their dark shells from thin, translucent wings that are not wings for flying but for catching light. They angle their wings toward the door and cast light down into it. A doorway is illuminated by a living chandelier of amber.

- Fat white grubs that hatch in the inner walls of the vine trunks and walk out in the dark when the moon is high. They come down from the residence vines and into the streets of the city, where they are met by handlers who lean down to them as if to rivers. The handlers are coated with a thin layer of wax and resin and oils, and they touch the grubs with their hands, and the grubs move along their arms and over their shoulders. The grubs are not eaten but harvested: they produce a kind of honey that is not sweet but intoxicating and information-dense, like a book of images.

- Silent hummingbirds that hover in the air and never land. Their wings are transparent and larger than their bodies, and the edges are fringed with trailing hair-like cilia. They hover, and you see them hovering everywhere, but they never land. You can watch them hanging in the air like a constellation of suspended eyes.

- Globular, many-legged creatures that ride the heat currents that rise from the lower fissure. They are slow to move, slow to respond, and when they land on the waxen balconies they do not scuttle but simply sit, absorbing what sun and dust and light they can manage until something else happens.
