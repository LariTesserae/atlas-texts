This is not a place where anything was built with tools or plans.

Under the dim, iron-gray sky of the high plateau, there is no stone that has not been swallowed, no ground that has not been chewed and spat back into a new shape. The air itself seems thick, not just with moisture but with the constant pressure of sound and the restless pressure of bodies. The world is breathing, and its breath is made of wings and claws and teeth and chitin.

Greatest among the living things that shape the plateau are the mound-reefs. They are the city, the architecture, the landscape itself.

From a distance, they might be mistaken for a cluster of low hills or giant weathered boulders scattered across the plateau. But there are no unworked stones here. The mound-reefs are the accretion of decades and generations of shell-building insects, mucus-spinners, wax-secretors, and a hundred other kinds of tiny makers that cement together soil and gravel and their own bodies into vast structures. Each reef rises thirty or forty meters into the air, its sides steep and overgrown with layers of brush and sapling and creeper vine, all interwoven through the living, breathing mass of the mound. The outermost skin of the reef is a mosaic of chalk-colored secretions and glossy patches of hardened oils, veined with cracks and chimneys where the countless insects of the builder caste come and go in ceaseless rivers.

The plateau itself is a high tableland of moderate elevation, its edges worn down into rounded, vegetated shoulders that drop away into a haze of perpetual mist and shadow beyond. The soil is thin over the ancient bedrock, but it is rich enough where the rains and the insects’ constant, churning work have brought it up and mixed it. The climate is warm but not harsh—a low, steady heat, like a body lying under heavy blankets.

The forests are not trees in the way you might expect them. There is no profile of tall trunks and clear sky beneath the canopy. Instead, the plateau is covered with a dense, low-tangle woodland of interlocking growths: thick-trunked shrubs with heavy, glossy leaves; spiny, bushy palms; and a thick underlayer of ferny things and mosses and the creeping mats of resin-secreting plants. The air is always humid, but the rain comes rarely, in slow, heavy, drenching storms that leave the foliage beaded and dripping and the ground in shallow, temporary ponds where the insects gather to drink and the larvae to hatch, before the water vanishes again into pumice and fibrous root.

The mound-reefs sit in clusters of three or four, separated by narrow belts of this woodland, but the forest does not really stop where the reef begins; the vegetation grows right into the reef’s sides and tops, sinking its roots into the living matrix of the insects’ home. The distinction between mound and non-mound is blurred, a gradual change from normal root and soil to the hard-packed galleries and passages of the reef, where the earth itself has been eaten and replaced with the architectural memory of generations.

And yet this is not a broken world. It is an established world, deeply worn into its own rhythms.

The erosion is gentle but thorough. The few rains carve the bare earth into shallow runnels, but those channels soon fill with roots, and the roots are soon reinforced by small colonies of burrowing insects that build their own walls and baffles in the runoff’s path. Over the years, the land has been carved into low, rounded ridges and shallow swales that now balance the slow water and the plants’ hunger against each other. The edges of the plateau are deeply incised, but the plateau itself has arrived at a kind of long-term compromise between the forces of weathering and the forces of life. The dense vegetation and the structural mound-reefs hold the soil and the slopes together, so that nothing is very steep or very unstable—nothing is very anything, except what is has been for a long time.

The air itself is thick with sound.

The parameter of “sound: 2.6/3 — deafening” does not mean the shriek of metal or the roar of engines. It means the ceaseless, collective noise of overwhelming fauna: the thrumming of wings and legs and the rasping of mandibles, the endless clicking and scraping and chitinous rubbing against chitinous. Above the mound-reefs, the sky is hazed by a constant low-flying cloud of insects, each adding its drone to the chorus. The forest itself rustles and rattles with smaller bodies working through the leaves and the thick stems.

The sound of a single creature is slight—a faint, high-pitched, almost metallic tick of two legs or two wings brushing together. But there are millions of them, layered over one another into a continuous roar. The crow calls and the buzz of the megalophonic insects that are the size of small birds sound as clear, sharp tones within this ocean of noise, but they are soon swallowed back into the general roar of uncountable smaller bodies in the upper foliage and the open air. The result is not loud in the way of a thunderclap or an explosion, but loud in the way of a powerful river: always there, always present, always moving against your ears, until your ears adjust to accommodate the constant pressure.

The darkness of the world is subtle.

The sun does not rise or set in any dramatic way. Instead, the sky above this plateau is a permanent gray overcast, thin and translucent but without breaks or visible edges. The light comes evenly filtered through this veil of mist and cloud and foggy exhalation from the forest and the mound-reefs themselves. The landscape never reaches harsh brightness or sharp contrast; even the shadows of the mound-reefs are softened, blurred around the edges by the moisture-laden air.

Daylight, when it comes, is a pale, diffuse glow that makes the colors of the vegetation stand out clearly but without sharpness. Greens and dark greens, the dull browns of wet bark and root, the chalk-white patches of insect secretions on the mounds—all are visible, but nothing is dazzling. The dark of the night is not absolute black, but a deep, heavy dimness, like the world breathing deeply and holding the breath. The air remains warm and humid even then, and the insects’ chorus remains loud, though it shifts texture and rhythm as the different species take over the night-time shift.

The fauna of the plateau is overwhelming not because any single species dominates the scene, but because there are so many species, and so many individuals of each species present everywhere at once.

The insects themselves are the primary inhabitants. They sprawl over the forest in a dense web of relationships and habits and tiny architectures of their own.

The mound-builders are the largest grouping—the builders of the reefs themselves. They are not small by insect standards; many of them are the size of fingers or thumbs, and their bodies are robust and heavily armored, their heads bearing strong mandibles and extended antennae that they constantly stroke against one another in the dim light. The bulk of the mound-reef is their work and the work of their ancestors before them.

Then there are the sweepers and gleaners—smaller insects that swarm through the upper foliage of the forest and the mound-reefs’ vegetation mats, cleaning the leaves of fungi and graze the parasitic growths that might otherwise engulf the green leaves. The gleaners are always moving, always brushing the leaves with their legs, leaving thin trails of secretions that discourage competing fungi from taking hold.

At the forest edge and the open ground around the mound-reefs, there are the pollen-hunters: large, slow-flying insects with wide, translucent wings and bodies covered in fine, dense hairs that collect dust and pollen and the spores of certain plants as they move through the air. They cluster around the few flowering plants that grow here and there in pockets of the mound-reefs’ vegetation mats, where the warmth and the moisture of the mound’s interior help to maintain the flowers. The pollen-hunters are not individually aggressive, but where they gather in large numbers, the air around them becomes thick with their wings and the fine dust they shed.

The sifters are the insects of the soil and leaf litter. They are everywhere that the forest floor meets the reef, and even where the reef’s internal galleries open into the forest’s root systems. They move through the soil and leaf litter in dense columns, turning it over so many times that nothing remains undisturbed for long. The soil they produce is soft and friable, full of tiny pellets of masticated material and bits of dead insect casing and old plant matter.

Then there are the water-hunters—small predators that go out from the edges of the mound into the open areas after rain to collect the moisture and the newly hatched larvae that gather in the pools formed in the dips and hollows of the earth’s surface. Their bodies are long and thin, their legs long and thin also, and they move over the surface of the water with a light, precise pattern of steps and pauses.

The largest of the insect species are the foragers, who go out into the forest and the mound-reefs’ vegetation mats to harvest the leaves and stems for the mound-builders’ internal food stores and for the fungi that grow in the lower, darker recesses of the mound-reefs’ internal galleries. The foragers are the size of your hand or longer, their bodies heavily built and their legs strong and thick with muscle and chitin. They move in long lines between the forest and the mound in slow, deliberate paths that they maintain clear of obstacles, so that over time the ground around the mound-reefs becomes worn into faint tracks that the water-hunters and the sifters follow when they are not attending their own tasks.

The few non-insect animals present are not large, but they are deeply integrated into the world of the mound-reefs and the forest. The tree-crawlers are small, warm-blooded climbers—no larger than your hand—with long bodies and short legs and long tails that they use as counterbalances as they move through the dense foliage of the forest and the mound-reefs’ vegetation mats. Their fur is short and dense and colored to match the bark and leaves and the occasional patches of pale insect secretion so that they disappear into the background of the plateau both when they are still and when they move slowly.

The vuggers are the small birds that nest within the living mound-reefs themselves, making hollows in the sides of the mounds by using their sharp beaks to chisel into the softer layers of the mound’s surface and the interior of the vegetation mats. The mound-builders tolerate them because the vuggers keep their openings clear and the chambers around them clear of the fungi that would otherwise overgrow the mound-builders’ legs and bodies and sometimes their eggs.

The vuggers are not the only flying creatures. The night-singers are large, slow-flying, heavily furred insects that make their way through the upper air of the plateau in the dim hours after the day’s light has faded but before the night’s deep darkness has fully settled. They are not loud individually, but their chorus adds a low pulsing to the general roar of the plateau, and their calls are answered from the mound-reefs and the surrounding forest by the countless smaller insects that cannot travel such distance but whose combined calls create the illusion of immense night singers moving between the mound-reefs on the currents of air.

The world is not stable in the sense of being frozen; it is slow in the sense that its large changes unfold over long timescales.

The mound-reefs grow slowly, adding a layer of insect secretions and plant matter to their sides and tops and the lower galleries where the sifters work their slow transformation of the soil and the debris of the forest. The forest itself moves slowly too, the edges of the dense vegetation belts around each mound slowly creeping into the open spaces between the mound-reefs and then slowly retreating again as the mound-builders expand outward or new smaller colonies establish themselves.

The rains are rare but heavy, and when they come they test the balance of the plateau. The forest floor becomes saturated with moisture and the shallow, temporary ponds that form in the hollows and swales of the plateau’s surface become full of the larvae of the water-hunters and a few other species that require the water for their early life stages. The vegetation of the mound-reefs’ surface mats grows thick and lush and then slowly thins again as the dry periods between the rains lengthen and the moisture within the mound itself becomes more and more precious.

The life of the plateau is not organized by technology or by any obvious tool use. There is no fire here, no metal, no complex mechanical devices, no lamps, no roads of stone or brick or any human-made surface. Everything is worked instead by the bodies of living things—the mouth of the insect, the beak of the bird, the root of the plant, the claw of the tree-crawler—and the work of living things over the bodies of dead things.

The mound-builders themselves shape the reefs by using their bodies as tools in the same way a beaver uses its teeth or its tail to build its dam, but at a larger scale and with more complexity of structure than the beaver’s work. The galleries and chambers and the internal layers of the mound-reefs are not random but are shaped by the behavior of the insects and the needs of the colony and the natural pressures of the environment and the other animals that inhabit the mound-reefs.

The vuggers and the tree-crawlers and the foragers and the gleaners and all the other species live within and upon and around the mound-builders’ structures and food stores and the fungi that the mound-builders cultivate in the lower galleries of the mound-reefs. The life of the plateau is not like a machine of separate parts so much as it is like a single, large organism whose organs are the mound-reefs and the forest belts and the swales of the open ground between them and the animals that inhabit them.

The plate of “tech: 0.1/3 — none” and “built: 2.6/3 — total” therefore holds not because there is any human-made artifact here, but because the entire plateau has been gradually worked over by the insects and the plants and the other animals into a form that is not natural in the sense of being untouched or unworked, but natural in the sense that its working and its shaping have been carried out by the bodies of living things over the quiet long-run of the plateau’s history.

If you stand here in the late afternoon, when the light is still pale but beginning to thicken into the deep gray of the plateau’s long night, you will see the mound-reefs as dark humps against the sky, their vegetation mats hanging over them like heavy cloaks. The paths of the foragers are faint marks in the ground beneath the outer edge mats of the mound-reefs’ vegetation, and the air around you is thick with the sound of wings and the faint scraping and churning of the insects in the mound and in the forest around you.

You will not see the edge of the plateau from here, but you know that it is there beyond the immediate mound-reefs and the forest, because the air to the west and the south carries a faint chill of altitude and distance, and because the vegetation of the mound-reefs’ surfaces mats grows thin and sparse and then finally stops altogether when you follow the foragers’ paths and the swales of the plateau’s surface toward the edge.

The world is not dark or light in any clear sense; it is suffused with a permanent gray twilight that never becomes bright and never becomes black. The heat of the plateau is not strong or sharp but slow and steady and persistent, like the warmth of a hearth bulk or the warmth of a deep-rooted tree at the end of a long day of slow rain.

The sound of the plateau is not the noise of a single great event but the accumulation of uncountable insignificant events, each itself small and almost meaningless, but together adding up to a roar that fills the air from the ground of the forest to the top of the mound-reefs’ vegetation mats and then beyond into the layered air above the plateau itself.

The life of the plateau is not chaotic or bizarre or strange in any obvious way; it follows rules and patterns and balances and limits that have been established over time and that are maintained by the slow, patient, and ceaseless work of the animals themselves. The mound-builders build; the foragers and the sweepers and the gleaners gather; the sifters and the water-hunters reshape the soil and the moisture; the vuggers and the tree-crawlers and the night-singers dwell within and around and above the mounds’ and the forest’s structures.

This is the world of the towering plateau of the mound-reefs, where everything has been made and nothing is untouched by life, and yet nothing is made by human hands or thought. The ground beneath your feet is not rock but the hardened secretion of generations of multitudinous bodies; the air around you is not empty but full of wings and bodies and living noise; the light above you is not bright but present and constant and dim enough to hide the edges of the world but not the faces of the animals themselves as they go about their slow and ceaseless work of shaping the plateau into their home and their world and their monument.
