High above the world’s warmer plains, in a thin band of air that tastes always of evening, the city of the cistern roofs sits on the crown of a stone hill.

It is a small place, by any measure—barely a handful of streets—and yet the way the stone roofs are turned into walkways, the way houses are built in layers up the slopes of the hill, the way the water runs in visible channels through all of it, gives it the feel of something larger than its size. The architect is gravity; the designer is time.

---

### The Hill and the Stone

The hill itself is not dramatic. It rises more like a swelling of the earth’s bone than a peak, a gently curved dome that has been here so long that the stone feels almost asleep. It makes a small, high world of its own, surrounded on most sides by lower valleys where mists curl and fade but rarely climb this high. The rock of the hill is a pale grey, faintly bluish where moisture gathers, and smooth in places, as if the wind had spent centuries trying to polish it.

Erosion has been at work here long enough to have reached a kind of truce with the stone. There are no raw scars, no fresh cuts or landslides. The slopes are gradual, the angles of the rock rounded. The few gullies that do slice into the sides of the hill are shallow and worn, their edges softened by time and occasional flows of water. Small terraces and shelves of stone have emerged naturally from the weathering of the rock, and these have been adapted into foundations, steps, and landing places for the city.

---

### The Water of the Roofs

This is the first thing you notice when you approach from the lower valleys: the roofs of the city are not mere shelter, but the surface of a living system.

The city itself is built almost entirely of stone—the same stone as the hill itself, worked by hand with simple tools and centuries of patience. The walls are mortared with a pale clay that dries to the color of bone, and the roofs are made of thick, flat slabs of stone fitted tightly together. These roofs are not level; they slope gently toward channels carved into their edges, and the channels on all the houses join together in a network of small runnels that snake down the streets and the curving sides of the hill.

When the mist drifts up from the valleys, or when the thin, cool rains come, the stone roofs darken with moisture. Droplets form at the edges and run along the carved channels like quiet rumors of rivers. The water is collected in stone basins, in cisterns that are themselves chambers cut into the rock beneath the houses, in low-walled tanks that sit at the corners of streets. The hill itself is a reservoir, and the city drinks its water slowly.

In the drier days, when the clouds pull away and the sky above is clear and cold, the stone roofs become dry and pale again. The moisture vanishes from the surface, but beneath the streets, inside the hill, the cisterns keep their cool, dark mirror of water.

---

### The City of the Hill

The city is not large. It is not crowded with buildings or people. It spreads around the crown of the hill in a loose ring, like a worn stone crown.

The streets are narrow and uneven, made of the same stone as the hill and worn by countless feet over the centuries. They move in slow curves rather than sharp lines, following the contours of the dome and the watercourses that run beneath the surface. There are no grand squares, no main plaza or central marketplace. Instead, there are small widenings, where the stone underfoot has been polished almost to a shine by human use and where a few low steps and ledges provide seating.

The buildings are squeezed tightly together where the slope allows, but the space between them is not oppressive. There are no tall structures here; only two or three levels at most, and even those feel low and grounded. The houses are small, each one carefully fitted into the slope so that the roof of one level becomes a terrace or walkway for the next. The stone roofs are not exclusive; they are public ways as much as they are private shelter.
