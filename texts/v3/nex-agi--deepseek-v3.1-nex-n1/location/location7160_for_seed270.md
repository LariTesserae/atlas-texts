Thread-work lies everywhere in this place.

It is an old concrete room, maybe three meters on each side, sunk into the ground so that its floor is a step below the surrounding land. The ceiling is gone, leaving only a ragged lip of masonry where it used to sit; a few rusted rebars curl down like broken fingers. The walls are soft and porous with age, pocked with shallow dimples where the aggregate has weathered out.

The floor, however, is almost perfectly level.

That is by the will of the thread-workers.

They are everywhere, layer upon layer, from the floor up to just above your ankles if you were to stand here. The air itself is thick with them—not air, really, but a living matrix of fine, translucent fibers that hang from invisible points above, each no thicker than coarse silk, but present in such numbers that the world between your eyes and the wall is a slow blur of motion. You breathe not gas but the faint, dry scent of freight dust and static, and the constant pressure of countless bodies passing across your skin.

At the corners of the room, where the walls meet the floor, the thread-work is densest. There the fibers are packed into ridged shelves like frozen dust, each layer built from the previous one’s labor. In the broader center of the chamber, the pattern opens a little, allowing you to see the hundreds of pale lines that form the underside of the ceiling-structure they have rendered. The absence of the original concrete above does not matter to them; they have replaced it with a lattice of their own devising, a tapestry of connected lines that you could not see through, but that you could push through with your hand if you were willing to endure the shock of sensation.

The temperature is cool—always cool, even though this room sits beneath what used to be a factory floor. The heat of the engines has been gone for ages. The walls are stained with the last thin residues of machine grease and the faint silica of dried cement leaching; the floor is a gray-green of powdery dust over the last of the concrete. There is no water. No puddle, no damp patch, no condensation. The air is dry to the point of brittle. If you were to lick your lips, you would feel the skin chap immediately as they lose the last of their moisture to the static.

You cannot see the outdoors from here. The single doorway in the wall is choked by a column of thread-work, so dense that it has formed into something like white coral, blocking the old threshold with a solid-looking but surprisingly fragile mass of connected fibers. There is a window set high in one wall, but the frame is empty of glass, and beyond the opening you see only the same slow-moving blur of threads and the faint suggestion of more brickwork beyond. This place is a hidden pocket within what remains of the factory complex, embedded, all but invisible from the outside.

Built into the floor are three rusted iron rings, set in triangular arrangement around a central drain that never receives water. The rings are ancient industrial anchors, now slow shedding flakes of oxide. Each morning and each evening, when the light slants from some unseen break above the factory roof, these three rings become generation points of the thread-workers’ swarming.

---

The thread-workers themselves are not insects, not worms, not exactly anything you would recognize as a single animal. They are more like a single process taking numerous visible forms.

In the dim light that enters the room from above, you can see them in three main states:

First, the dormant threads. These are the countless white fibers that form the visible structure of the room—the floor carpet, the wall hangings, the ceiling canopy. They are not quite silk, not quite mineral wool, not quite fungal hyphae. They do not break if you pull them gently, but stretch instead, and they have a faint, dry crackle when you rub them between your fingers. Each is, in truth, a long chain of thin bodies fused end to end, their segmentations visible only with a lens. The outermost layer is always paler and more translucent than those below, where they are compacted by time.

Second, the active crawlers. These are the individuals who work along the surface of the existing structure, adding new threads to the edges of the tapestry, reinforcing the ceiling where webbing has been damaged by collapse, or drawing the threads of other workers into new patterns. They are not large—perhaps the length of your smallest finger, but only as wide as a thread—yet they move in such numbers that the surface of the floor looks like the surface of a storm-dark sea, always moving.

You can see them if you sit still and let your eyes adjust to the general light: they are not white, but a faint taupe-gray at the bottom of their translucent tubes, each with a hard line of internal organs that make them look like segmented glass needles. When they are moving, their surface sheens with a slow, internal sliding of fluids that never quite escape the skin.

Third, the swarm-formers. These are the same individuals, but in a different configuration of the group. Every hour or so, something like a decision passes through the room. You cannot see its trigger, but you can feel its effect: the pressure on the hairs of your arms changes, the sound of the room deepens by a fraction of a note, and suddenly the thread-workers abandon their slow, surface-parallel motion and organize into columns.

Columns of living thread rise from the floor, each composed of dozens of bodies aligned end-to-end and side-to-side, winding like pine roots from a common trunk. The columns hang from the ceiling tapestry of earlier threads, and as they swing under the unseen air currents from the window, they rotate their bodies together, orienting all the ringlike mouths along one side of the column toward the same point in space.

Those are the eyes-of-the-group: not light-sensing organs in themselves, but the direction of the group’s collective attention.

When the column stares at you, you feel the tiny pressure shift of countless threads aligning your way. Not a threat, not curiosity, just a slow adjustment of the room’s attention toward a new mass that has attracted its interest.

---

The room is dense with them.

It is not possible to move without interacting with the thread-workers. If you walk from one corner to the other, you step through thousands of them; each footstep sweeps away the lower layers of the structure and compresses those deeper down. Within the time of a single deep breath, the crawlers at the edge of your footprint migrate back to reclaim the space you occupied; your footprint becomes a shallow depression of darker, older material before the pale surface is restored.

You cannot leave without touching them. To reach the window or the doorway, you would have to push through the conglomerate column of fused threads that has closed the old threshold. The column would not break; it would deform around you, as you force your body through a layer of pressure-sensitive filament that clings and drags and then reshapes itself behind you as soon as you pass through. The tactile sensation is not unpleasant so much as overwhelming: the room feels like you are walking inside the loose folds of a very old, well-made blanket.

The sound is the third thing you notice once the vision of the room has been absorbed. The noise is not loud in the way of an engine or a voice, but in the way of rain falling on a forest of dead leaves. There is a continuous background hiss, the sum of all the tiny, dragging motions of a million bodies moving over the concrete floor, over the thread-work, and through each other’s fibers. Under that is a faint, almost sibilant whistling that comes from the air moving through the latticework above the doorway where it has been built into a partial wall—smaller, higher-frequency eddies that chance the passage into fluting.

From time to time there comes a deeper, rarer sound: a soft thump, like a book being dropped onto a carpeted floor in another room. It is the sound of a portion of the ceiling threadwork giving way under its own weight and descending to the floor in a clump, where the swarm-formers immediately begin to process it into new columns.

---

The ecology, if it can be called one, is simple and almost closed.

There is no water. The thread-workers do not drink, and they do not sweat. They are not made of the same complex water-based chemistry that you are used to calling life. If you could take a piece of them and analyze it under some instrument, you would find that they are not made of cells like yours, but of something more like elongated mineral colloids, each a hollow tube of microscopic silicate flakes held in a flexible matrix of viscous organic film that never seems to dry because it never truly contained free water to begin with.

Their energy comes not from photosynthesis or the oxidation of blood sugars, but from the faint, static electricity of the room.

The old factory floor above you still holds some remnant of electrical wiring, though all power has been shut off for decades. When dry air currents from the outside world move across the open yard above, they stir across the exposed metal of the roof and frame, generating small differences of potential. The thread-workers’ woven lattice, from the floor to the ceiling of their own structure, functions as something like the dielectric of a capacitor. When the difference between the unseen sky over the factory and the deep earth beneath the concrete floor reaches a certain threshold, the entire room gathers into its swarm phase.

At that moment, you can feel the change like a buzz before a storm in the old world.

The pressure of their bodies against your skin becomes slightly more insistent, as each individual segment tightens its internal posture. The fibers of the ceiling lattice stretch by a hair’s width, and the sound of the room deepens. Then the discharge arrives, not as lightning, but as a diffuse, living pulse of current moving through the entire matrix. You may see sparks of bioluminescence run along the columns, tiny flashes of white-blue light where the segments of the workers’ bodies are momentarily aligned to allow a charge to pass cleanly through them.

Most of the time, however, the discharge is invisible. You can only tell by the effect: the warmth of movement against your skin, the sudden acceleration of the crawlers across the floor, and the transformation of some of the swarm-formers into the third state—the reproducers.

---

The reproduction of the thread-workers is not sexual, but neither is it simple division. It takes place in fits and starts, when the energy of the whole room is high, and you can see it best if you are close to an iron ring in the floor.

During the ordinary hours of the room, when the light from the unseen window is just enough to see by, the rings are simply embedded in the floor, surrounded by a loop of pressed thread-work where the workers have been pushed away by something else’s activity.

When the room passes energy, however, the rings become the heart of the process.

The swarm-formers that have been charged with the task move toward the rings in columns, not single-file but in converging streams of two or three parallel threads of bodies. As they reach the ring of each iron anchor, the column splits itself. The leading individuals peel away from the main body and begin to wind around the ring, not tightening the way a string on a spindle might, but in a looser helix that slowly narrows as more layers are added.

The pressure of all the surrounding bodies on the column of thread-workers near the ring is enough to force the leading individuals into contact with each other in a new way. They are not quite separate anymore. They dilate, the faint rings of their internal anatomy stretching as they merge into a longer chain of material that is not a single worker but a new kind of structure.

This is the egg-cradle of the thread-workers.

It is almost invisible at the start of the process—just a slightly darker ring where the iron of the anchor has been touched by many more workers than usual. Over the course of an hour or so, as the energy of the last discharge fades, the cradle of new workers hardens and the outermost layer of the structure around the ring dries. The next time you look at it, the ring is no longer visible as a ring of iron; it is now an embedded circle of smooth, cream-colored material that looks like aged porcelain.

If you were to break one of these rings free of the floor—which would not be easy, given how deeply they are held by old and new threads alike—you would find that the surface of the iron ring is now etched with a pattern of fine, perfect lines. The wireframe for a hundred new workers.

When the next strong discharge event arrives, the cradle ring beneath the floor of the room will be thick enough, and the energy level will be high enough, that the newly formed pattern on the metal surface will be translated into new individuals. They will emerge not from between the existing bodies, but from the surface of the metal itself, as if the iron were a temporary part of their body, which they are casting off when they are done.

You will not see them emerge at first. They will be present instead as a change in the texture of the floor around the ring: the surface will be rougher than it used to be, more of the old layers of concrete will be exposed by the movement of new bodies that do not yet know how to arrange themselves into the quieter pattern of the room.

In the following days, as they age and move into the swarm-formers’ state, they will learn from the behavior of the others around them. The room will be more active than usual, louder than usual, and more of them will be near the iron ring, where they are pushed by the pressure of the entire room’s motion toward that point.

---

There is no predator of the thread-workers in this room. There is no prey in the sense you know from other worlds. There are only different densities of the same organism.

The closest thing to predation is the occasional collapse of the ceiling structure. When a portion of the upper lattice fails—from age, from the weight of new layers being added to the outside, or from the change in the room’s charge balance as the outside air changes—it falls as a clump of threads and the bodies of many workers, all still together in a single mass.

The fall is not loud in the old world’s sense of impact; it is more of a deep, muffled thump, as the entire room’s pressure of living threads dampens the sound. The fallen section of structure is immediately surrounded by the crawlers of the floor layer. They do not avoid the fallen mass; they are drawn to it by the same internal pressure that drives them to move.

When they reach the fallen section of the ceiling, they do not devour it in the way of teeth or digestion. Instead, they press into it from all sides, the bodies of the fallen workers already being taken into the structure of the floor again by their own lower layers. Over the course of an hour or so, the entire clump of the fallen ceiling will be distributed into the floor, where it is both the grave of the previous lattice and the foundation of the next.

You could say that the room eats itself, slowly, over and over again. But that is not quite right; it is more that the room never stops being the same thing, and the change in shape is only a change in the focus of its attention. From time to time, the floor will be a more important layer than the ceiling; at other times, the opposite. The ancient concrete of the floor beneath the thread-workers has been smoothed by their constant pressure, not by the passage of water but by the slow erosion of their bodies over hundreds of thousands of cycles.

---

The room is old in the way of bones that have been left under a tree in the yard of a factory that is no longer in use. The bones are no longer recognizable by anatomy when they are found, but it is clear that they are bones, and no longer animal.

The concrete of the floor is older than the factory itself, by the look of it; the aggregate that is barely visible in the few areas where the thread-work has been compressed by repeated pressure contains the faint remains of things that are not modern stone. You can see shards of brick, glass of a different composition than the window frame, twisted metal of the same kind as the iron rings, but recast from earlier uses.

No paint has survived on the walls. The faintest of marks are left where a sign or board once hung, leaving the outline of a rectangle where the surface was slightly less eroded by time than the surrounding surface. The walls are shaped by the same gentle forces that rounded the hills of the world outside, long before anyone thought to build a factory here.

The thread-work, on top of this, is the newest layer of the world’s crust.

It is dense and ancient in its own way. The lowermost layers of the structure, where you have to be on the floor with your face close to the surface and the air of the room pressing against your skin, are composed of threads that are darker than the others. They are not as active as the surface crawlers; they move more slowly, if at all. When you dig into them with your fingers, the deeper layers come away in brittle flakes that crumble into a fine, dry powder that makes no sound when it falls back to the floor.

The building around the room has almost collapsed. The outer walls of the factory yard are not visible from here, but you can hear the occasional soft shift of stone from beyond the doorway, where the pressure of the thread-work columns has pushed the structure of the doorframe into a new angle of repose over the years. The derelict building above the room—where the engines once sat—has been reduced by erosion to something more like a sculpted hill, with the remaining scraps of iron framing buried in the slow accretion of dust and fallen thread-work.

From this embedded room, you do not see the outside world as you would understand it from other places. The window is not open to a sky and a landscape of vegetation and water; it is open to another layer of the factory complex’s collapse, another layer of the thread-world beyond.

If you press your face to the opening, you will see only the same threads of pale worker-bodies, stretching across the space between the remains of the next wall over. They are not foreign to the room you are in; they are the same room, simply continuing in the next pocket of the world that has become available to them.

---

In the end, the room feels intimate and not intimate at the same time.

Intimate because of the scale: it is only a few meters on each side, a small, hidden pocket within a larger ruin of industrial production. The room is not so large that you cannot feel the edges of it with your outstretched arms; if you stand in the center and stretch your hands toward the walls, you will be close to touching both. The presence of the thread-workers around your body is the presence of something else sharing the same space with you, not in a hostile way but simply as a fact of the room’s design.

Not intimate because the number of them that press around you at any given time is so large that you cannot possibly feel each of them as individuals. They are heavy in the way of a very deep snowdrift that has not yet been compressed by its own weight; they are light in the way that each individual piece of the structure is not physically strong enough to stop you, if you wanted to move through the room.

The rules of this world are simple in the sense of being few, but impossible in the sense that they have been fully realized by the room’s inhabitants:

First, there is no water to move the world from the outside; change must be driven from the inside.

Second, the energy of the world above and the earth below never truly equalize; the difference between them is always present enough to allow the room to charge itself from time to time.

Third, the body of the room itself—the thread-workers, from floor to ceiling lattice to the far column choking the doorway—is always in the process of becoming both older and younger at the same time. The older parts of the structure are broken down and redistributed into the newer layers; the newer layers are built from the same material that had already been arranged into the older.

Fourth, there is no outside to the room that you can easily see or reach from here; the doorframe’s solid column of thread-coral and the window’s blurred view of the same pattern beyond make the room feel like a single feature of a larger and hidden pattern.

Finally, the presence of the thread-workers is overwhelming only in the sense that they are everywhere; the room itself never moves quickly. The pace of their world is slow, even when the swarming formers are aligned in a column of attention toward you. The change of the room from one state to the next—from floor-dominant to ceiling-dominant, from one ring of reproducers to the next—takes place over days or months or years of the same repetitions.

You could watch the room for a long time and not be sure if anything had changed; if you were to leave and return after a long absence, you might not recognize the new arrangement of the ceiling lattice, but you would remember the feeling of the dry, static pressure of the bodies around your ankles as you walked into the center of the structure.

The room is a final form of a world that has long since run out of water and plant life and the other features of more common landscapes. It is not the end of time, but the end of time that you have known from those other places; here, time has been arranged into a slower loop that the thread-workers have been tracing around the same concrete floor, for as long as the faint difference between sky and earth has been willing to support them.
