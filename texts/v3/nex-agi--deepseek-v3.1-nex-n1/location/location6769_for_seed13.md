The Spine of the World lies just under the clouds, and on the day of its birth, the city at the peak of it learned what silence meant.

Below you, the plateau of Karak-Eshul is still a wound in the rock of the world. A mountain of pale grey stone and black glass rose here from the earth not a lifetime ago, when the ground floor of the world split and something ancient slid up through the fault. The peak of it is a flat table of stone that the elders of Karak-Eshul have carved into terraces and streets, though the edges of the plateau still drop away sheer and raw, as if the mountain’s spine was still pushing to break the sky.

In elevation, this place is high—high enough that the clouds are a thin grey floor just below your feet if the world is in a dry mood. The air is thin, but the breathable sort of thin; cool enough to make your lungs feel sharp and clear, somewhere between a winter morning in a green valley and a summer night on the sea cliffs. It is not cold, but it is never warm. Temperature is constant in Karak-Eshul: it always feels the same way at the same time of day, each day, exactly the same.

Days here are carved into stone blocks.

The sun climbs the same arc of sky, every day, without fail or variance. Sunrise begins the Watch. The light of high sun—normal light, strong enough to cast ordinary shadows—is the Work of Stone. Late afternoon is the Walk of Birds. The long amber of evening is the Silence of Teeth. This is the law of Karak-Eshul, and the city itself is built to obey it.

From the northern edge of the plateau, where the mountain’s first flanks of raw stone slope down toward the memory of forests, the city unfolds in neat rings. The Spine of the World is the name of both the mountain and the main avenue of the city—a street of polished stone and glassy black volcanic flow that cuts the plateau in half. The Spine of the World is the central axis of an urban grid that is the only thing in this world that does not change.

The structures of Karak-Eshul are simple but precise. They are built almost entirely of the stone of the new mountain itself: pale grey blocks, quarried at the plateau’s edge and laid by hand with dense, low clay mortar in the joints of the walls. The roofs of the lower rings of the city are flat, packed earth and pebble surfaces that absorb the rare rain and then let it evaporate slowly in the never-varying temperature of the air. The upper levels, closer to the peak of the Spine, have roofs made of sheets of black volcanic glass that angled the alien light into the sparse homes of the priests and the astronomers.

It is a primitive city in many ways. No metal tools adorn the hands of its builders; they use the living stone itself, shaped by harder and softer rocks, and the hands of countless men and women whose work never progresses to real cities. The level of technology is the stone of its own laws: simple tools, simple homes, careful observation of the sky but no understanding of the engine that drives the terrible, changeless clock of its world. The city of Karak-Eshul is frozen urban design, worked by minds of stone age thinking.

No wheel creaks in the streets of Karak-Eshul. No engine turns. The only sounds of the city are the quiet of things at the bottom of a deep well of silence.

The air of the plateau carries sound poorly today. It is so quiet that you can hear the creatures of Karak-Eshul more clearly than the city’s own people. The low of a herd of wide-horned mountain goats on the terraces echoes strangely thin, as if the sound itself has been flattened by the pressure of the never-changing sky. In the outskirts of the city, where the buildings edge the mountain’s raw cliff face, the steel-blue birds of the plateau call out to each other in the Walk of Birds—sharp cries of two and three and four notes that are always the same two and three and four notes.

Humans of Karak-Eshul are not the only creatures in the city of stone. Far above the carved terraces, the faces of the unworked mountain are speckled with the pendulous nests of the karals, creatures of fur and feathers and flat stone teeth. They are the size of large wolves, but cautious of the city, and they only descend during late evening when the law called the Silence of Teeth holds sway. They eat the coarse grasses of the plateau, and only the dead of Karak-Eshul’s stone.

The vegetation of the plateau is the most unusual of its kind. The rocks of the young mountain are still new and sharp, but the life here is adapted to the cold of high altitude and the faint fringes of the world’s life. The lowlands of the world do not know such plants.

In pockets of soil and gathered dust along the edges of the city of the Spine, strange growths sprout in the shadow of the walls. The zarun are low plants with thick, dark fronds that are almost black; the edges of each leaflet are swollen with water-storage sacs the color of slate. They live on dew and the rare condensation of the air if the world of this world shifts in some way, and their leaves can open and close slightly yet the growth of the plant itself never really changes over the day of Karak-Eshul.

The drushka are tall, thin, spindly plants that grow like pale grey reeds around the edges of the city’s collection pools and shallow wells. They share the grounds of the city with the people of Karak-Eshul, but they are not domesticated, and the city was built around them with the care of creatures who know that they will not change of the plants of the world. The drushka are lushly weedy in the way that only cold, high altitude plants can be—they are thin of leaf and thick of fiber, and they bow the same way in the same wind of the same afternoon of every day.
