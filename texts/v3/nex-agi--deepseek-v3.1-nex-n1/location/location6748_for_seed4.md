Cool air clings to the stone. You can feel it before you open your eyes—a weight in the chest, a slow, steady pressure against the skin, as though the world itself is breathing just slightly out of sync with you.

The room is not large; three paces long, perhaps, and half as wide. The walls are sheer, polished stone that rises into blackness above, where the ceiling should be. There is no ceiling, only a height you can’t see. The air tastes thick and wet, like the underside of a river rock, or the inside of a well at midnight.

Water beads everywhere. It slides down the walls in slow, transparent streams, gathering in thin lines that eventually find their way into the shallow channels carved at the floor’s edge. These are the first signs of work here—not architecture of conquest, but something simpler and more careful. The channels are shallow, irregular, not so much engineered as guided by the stone’s own memory of where water wants to run. They hold a low, steady murmur, a faint counterpoint to the drip…slip…drop of water from the unseen heights.

From floor to ceiling, the chamber is alive with plants.

They are everywhere. Not as decorative creepers, but as the room itself. The walls are not merely covered; they are grown over with a thick, matted skin of vegetation, a layered tapestry of deep greens and blackish-purples. The plants are mostly low, soft things—cushions and mosses and thick, overlapping leaves that seem more sponge than foliage. They drink the constant water from the stone and glow faintly as they do.

Yes, glow.

Not in bright colors, but in subtle, misty light. The mosses shine with a bluish halo—tiny, luminous spores that catch some invisible frequency of the world and answer in pale light. The larger leaves have a different color: a faint, greenish-white phosphorescence along their veins and edges, like the ghost of dead lightning. Together, it’s just enough to outline the shape of everything without truly revealing it.

You are standing inside a plant lantern.

The floor beneath you is not stone, but root. It looks like dark wood, but it is not wood; it is a thick, matted surface of roots grown over the years into a springy floor. The roots are not uniform; they are uneven, like a frozen river of muscle fibers, all braided together. They yield underfoot, but do not creak or complain. They hold your weight as if they have been expecting it.

All around this chamber, the vegetation is the architecture. The entryway—if you can call it that—is framed by a looping arch of jointed stems that have grown into one another over time, forming a thick, living lintel and jambs. The roots of that arch spill down the walls, forming natural shelves, where small objects have been placed carefully: rounded stones, polished little shells, a few thin pieces of carved bone or wood. It’s clear that someone has guided this growth rather than forced it; the stems and roots set the boundaries, and the people have simply followed.

The city—if you can call it that—begins outside and goes on.

Beyond the doorway of roots and stems, the space opens out into a narrow tunnel, not unlike a cave system but not entirely natural. The tunnel walls, too, are all living plants: thick, tightly woven root-felt on the lower surfaces, giving way to overlapping leaves and mosses higher up. You glimpse the suggestion of passages branching off into other chambers like this one, barely more than alcoves in the thick vegetative matter. There is no metalwork, no obvious stone brickwork—just the perpetual weaving of plants and the slow, patient guidance of human hands.

Lights of a different sort drift out there in the wider passages.

They are not lamps. They are creatures.

They look like faint, irregular stars moving through a slow constellation, each a point of soft, warm color suspended in the air. They do not buzz like insects or chirp like birds; their only sound is the faint, whispery slide of their bodies through the air, and sometimes the soft brush of a wing against a leaf.

In the darkness of this world, they are the streetlights.

Somewhere further out, you sense the thing that this small room is embedded within: the whole city is a plant, or more accurately, a plant community. It is a lowland grown thick with a single organism—or perhaps a few organisms that have learned to cooperate. The building you are standing inside is not so much constructed as it is trained, like an espaliered tree on a monumental scale. The city has been encouraged to grow into chambers, corridors, promenades, and stairways of root and stem and leaf.

The temperature is cold, but the cold has a texture.

It is not the frozen bite of ice and snow, but the deep, underground chill of a place that never sees sunlight. The air is dense enough that you can feel it on your skin as a slight resistance when you move your hand through it. There is a subtle humidity—the moisture of a constantly damp surface—that clings without ever forming into rain.

You feel the ecosystem as much as you see it.

The air shivers with life. Not in the usual way of a bustling marketplace, but in a continuous slow rustle of surfaces. You notice the floor of roots under you is not perfectly still; it shifts ever so faintly with the movement of thousands of tiny, unseen occupants. You don’t see them at first, because they are not clustered together like insects or swarms. They are scattered—one here, three over there, a cluster of a dozen beneath the edge of that leaf, but then long stretches of open surface in between.

This is the fauna of the place, and it is overwhelming.

They are small things, mostly. Many-limbed and many-jointed creatures that move like liquid ideas rather than solid animals. They flow over the surfaces—plants, root floor, stone walls where they’ve been forced back by the plant skin—with a slow, deliberate grace. They bear a faint luminosity of their own, not enough to brighten anything, but enough to outline their shapes as they go: segmented curves, trailing filaments, faint, shell-like patterns of color.

They are not rare. They are everywhere. But there is space between them. The space of the room itself is not crowded; they move around you rather than over you, respecting the sudden, large intrusion of your body without fear or aggression. It is simply that you feel the weight of their presence—the sensation of being in a place that belongs fully to them and only partially, on temporary loan, to you.

This is the horror of the place, and its gift: the world is not empty of life.

You are never the only creature present.

Even if you were alone here, by human standards, you would not be alone. The plants are not merely backdrop; they are actively alive, slowly breathing, growing, adjusting. You can feel them when you place a hand on the wall of moss and leaf: the faintest rhythmic pulse, like a distant heartbeat or the slow swelling of a tide, as the city-plant draws water from below and exhales moisture into the air above.

The silence of the place is not absolute; it is only mostly quiet.

Listen.

You hear the low, constant murmur of the water channels in the floor—the ebb and flow of a hidden current. Somewhere deeper in the structure, there may be larger channels of water, but here you only feel the presence of that water as the faint, damp pressure of its nearness. The air condenses on your skin and on the walls, leaving glistening streaks of silver in the faint glow of the moss and leaves.

You hear the soft pat of drops from above: not rain falling from the sky, but water gathering from some hidden spring in the rock above, then letting loose at its own unhurried rhythm. Drip…a long pause…slip…another pause…drip. The pattern is not random but follows some invisible liquid logic of the stone.

You hear the rustle of the fauna at the edge of awareness—not a loud chorus of chirps or calls, but an endless, subtle movement of small limbs over surfaces. The whisper of a creature crawling across root, the faint brush of something against a leaf.

And then, sometimes, a sound of deeper work.

From somewhere outside this chamber, along one of the root-lined corridors, you catch the soft, rhythmic impact of tool against stone or root. It is not the sharp clang of metal; it is the duller thud of a simple tool—perhaps a hardened wood or bone chisel driven into a root surface, or maybe stone being shaped to guide the water better.

The people of this place are not primitive in the sense of being helpless. They are primitive in the sense of being watchful. They work not with the flash of science, but with the slow certainty of those who have been learning the same plant for generations. They know the hum of root under tension, the way the leaves curl when a certain moisture threshold is passed, the small patterns of the glowing creatures and what they might indicate about the health of the whole.

Life is dense here not in the way of crowds, but in the way of soil: full of hidden layers and constant processes.

You begin to notice the patterns of motion in the room, each at its own pace.

The water drips slowly, from somewhere you cannot see to somewhere you can only hear. The mist of condensation rises slowly, pushing itself off the walls and into the air, where it eventually rejoins the moisture cycle of the whole structure. The plants grow slowly, invisibly—except in the places where someone has placed a small mark of bone or wood to indicate the boundary of new growth over the years.

The creatures of the place move at their own timescale. Some move slowly, carefully, across a surface, taking several minutes to traverse a distance you could cross in two steps. They are not in a hurry; they are simply here, being animals of stone and root and leaf and water.

And then there are the faster movements, when predator meets prey.

You see it several times in your time here. One glowing creature—perhaps the size of your palm, if you could see its outline fully—drifts across a patch of light-leaf. The creature is not wholly insect-like and not wholly jellyfish; its body is a soft shape of translucent membrane, trailing long tendrils. It glows with a faint yellow that pulses, as if its light is a rhythmic heartbeat.

It hovers just a little too long over a particular patch of root that looks like all other root patches.

But something else under that surface has been waiting for exactly that pattern of light.

The root floor there is not merely smooth; there is a slight, uneven texture of small nodules embedded in the root surface like stars in a dark sky. They look like decorative features until you notice the smallest one flicker slightly, as if something within it has shifted.

The glowing creature settles above that nodule.

What happens next is startling but silent.

The nodule splits open, not violently, but with a soft, biological unfolding. It releases not a jaw or a claw, but a mass of dark threads that move upward in the blink of an eye—not quite fast enough to exceed the world’s rule of quietness, but fast enough to feel like sudden punctuation in the slow sentence of the room. The threads attach themselves to the hovering creature’s underside, and then contract.

The creature is pulled down into the nodule, which closes around it like a mouth in the root’s skin.

There is a faint muffled sound, then nothing.

The surface of the root floor settles back into its old pattern. The plant has not changed its appearance as much as its experience; it has now eaten something.

This is the world’s system of active processes: prey and predator, root and creature and leaf and glow and water and darkness, all looping around one another without the need of steel or electricity or bright sun.

The city of roots and stone and water feels like a place that has only recently found itself.

This is not an ancient labyrinth of worn steps and deeply polished walls. The stone surfaces that are visible between the plant layers are still sharp-edged where work has been done to clear them, or to guide the water channels. There are places where the roots of the plant-city have not yet fully invaded the rock, and in those places you can see the tool marks of the people: shallow, irregular lines where someone has worked with slow tools to help the water find its path, or to encourage the root to follow.

Yet the plant itself seems older than the city-concept of this place. It feels as though the people have recently come to understand that they are not building on top of the world, but inside of it.

The world is strange, but it is not chaotic.

It operates by rules that are not the same as yours, but they are consistent. The glowing creatures are not random fireflies but inhabitants of a particular niche of moisture and light and plant surface. They are not swarming; you can see their individual patterns of movement through the space, the way they sometimes drift into groups of three or four near a particularly bright patch of leaf, then scatter again into the wider darkness of the corridor beyond.

You are not a god of technology in this place; you are a visitor with hands.

You notice the few tools present in this chamber: a simple chisel of stone or hardened bone by the doorframe, worn smooth from long use; a shallow bowl of what looks like baked root or shell, set into the root floor next to a water channel; a thin bone tool that looks almost like a needle or stylus, resting on one of the shelves of the plant wall.

These are tools of the primitive you were given, but they are not tools of ignorance. They are tools of patience.
