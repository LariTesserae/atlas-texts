High in the folded mountains of a world that remembers your breath before you exhale it, there is a small stone hall that felt the rain and never quite forgot.

The hall is not large—maybe five paces from end to end, if paces are things you still trust to measure distance by. The walls are dark basalt, glassy in places where something once ran molten through them, but the stone is young enough to remember its own making. The edges are still sharp where the mountain’s bones split the sky, and if you run your fingers along the stone you’ll find fresh chips of crystal where wind-driven grit hit them an hour ago, or last night, or tomorrow.

You are in the clouds.

This high up, the air is almost always wet, but for the last three days and nights a storm has been exhaling over the ridges. It is not violent, but it is persistent: a warm, heavy, breath-taking heat that does not break. The stone is hot to the touch even though the water on it is cold. The rock smokes where the sun breaks through and then steams where the clouds close back in.

Across the ceiling of the hall, perfect spheres of water hang and shiver.

They are not exactly rain, and not exactly dew, and not exactly anything else you have a name for. Each bead is exactly identical: half a thumb-width wide, clear, with no dust or speck, each with a faintly bluish core where the light of the world’s incrementally hotter blue-white sun threads through them. They are not only water; the people of the valley whisper that these are the memory of water.

The spheres emerge, very slowly, from the stone itself.

The heat of the day surges up the south-facing slope of the mountain and into the floor of the hall. The night, which is still almost warm enough to bathe in, drags that heat back down the north-facing wall into the valley. The rock remembers these temperature-pressure changes as if they were emotions. The deeper, recently-born stone of the mountain’s core still churns with faint self-motions from its last great eruption; it is far from being calm and stable, and those subtle flexions in the basalt lattice are like soft muscles kneading themselves. The water of last storm that sank into pores and microfissures is being slowly squeezed out—not in the random drip of limestone cave water, but in this precise, engineered fashion.

The spheres of water extrude from tiny, perfectly circular pores in the ceiling stone, as if they have been pressed by some invisible hand. They bulge outward like glassy, upside-down buds from the same invisible tree, and they hang there, quivering, gathering not only more water but also faint fields of something else: the faint, magnetic afterimage of light passing through, the braided harmonic of distant storms in the valley below, the faint charge of your own breath when you speak inside.

When they have gathered enough weight to become separate from the stone, they fall.

They fall without sound.

This is part of the strange order of the place: the spheres of water are released by their own gravities from the ceiling in exactly spaced intervals, but as they descend through the air something happens to their sound. The motion of the air they push through, and the faint hum of whatever they hold from the world around them, are sucked into a kind of stillness that travels with them like a halo. You might feel the breeze of a falling bead of water brush your cheek, but if you are on the other side of the hall, you will not hear the impact. The only sound that is permitted to cross from one to the other is the low, almost-subsonic tremor in the floor when a water-sphere strikes the stone basin.

The basin.

The floor of the hall is not entirely flat. It lifts gently from the edges toward the center, where the stone of the mountain seems to have been lazy to extrude, and in that shallow bowl someone has carved a basin of sorts—or something else has carved it for them.

It is not exactly a work of human tools. The surface of the basin is not obviously polished, but the stone of it is different from the rest of the hall: a smoother, darker material that might once have been basalt but has swollen to its peculiar form under tremendous heat and then cooled to a glass-like smoothness. It is of the mountain, but not entirely like the mountain. The edges of the basin, where they rise from the floor, are still thin and sharp; if you were to run your finger along them, you would cut yourself.

The basin is not perfectly circular. It is not any perfect shape you can name. It is as if the stone of the floor conspired with a disorder of patterns from another world to make it.

At the center of the basin, there is a single shallow depression of darker material than the rest, and in that depression is inscribed a pattern that is only legible from above.
