The Abyssal Plains of Sighisvar

You are walking—no, that is not right. You are not walking at all. You are moving through the world at the level where your feet are not supposed to be.

The ceiling is low, a dark curve of worn stone dappled with faint, wavering reflections of what you take to be light above but cannot see clearly. The water is not knee deep; it is a world-deep ocean above you, pressing down, but you are in the narrow layer where air still exists, trapped beneath the weight of an ocean like a final held breath.

This is the Undershelf. The abyssal plains of sighisvar.

---

### Terrain and Materials

Everything is shaped by water, but here you are beneath it.

The floor is uneven but not jagged. It has been smoothed by ages of slow pressure and tiny currents into a low relief of soft domes and shallow depressions. The rock is dark, almost black, but under the faint glow that filters down through the miles of water above, it carries a greenish-blue sheen, like the memory of algae and long-extinct reefs.

Basins are everywhere—small, rounded bowls that seem to have been hollowed by the slow turning of water. Each basin holds a shallow layer of silt, and every now and then, as you move, your passage stirs these fine particles into lazy clouds that rise slowly and then hang suspended in the water, like smoke.

Between the basins are low ridges of the same smoothed stone. They form faint patterns—like the ribs of a vast buried skeleton, or the lines left by vanished rivers when seen from a high place. In places, these ridges are crusted with thin, brittle coatings of pale minerals that crack when touched, leaving glittering dust on your fingertips.

Sections of the ceiling are pierced by narrow fissures that climb upward into darkness. You cannot see the sky—if there is a sky—but from these openings a faint pulsing glow descends, pale blue-green, like the light of a lantern seen from the bottom of a deep well. It never fully brightens the Undershelf; it is only enough to reveal that shapes once mistaken for solid stone are in fact endlessly moving.

---

### Water: Submerged World Without Seafloor

Water is not just present; it is the medium.

Above you is a liquid ceiling, a heavy blanket of ocean that stretches out of sight into three directions. The line of this surface is not perfectly flat—it swells in slow, low hills, like the skin of a sleeping animal breathing. Where cracks and shafts in the ceiling open upward, the water bulges slightly into them, rising into rough columns that are neither wall nor wave but both.

The water beneath the air layer is dense, cold, and clear. You can reach into it and feel your hand disappear into a colder world than the air around your body. The currents there are complex: layered flows that move like slow traffic, each layer at a slightly different angle, carrying with them tiny, translucent forms of life you can only see as shifting points of light.

Occasionally a larger swell travels through the ocean ceiling, causing water to lap over the edges of the Undershelf floor. It spills in thin, silver sheets across the stone, then retreats, leaving behind darker, wet patches that smell faintly of salt and ancient stone.

---

### Temperature and Atmosphere

It is cold here—not the freezing bite of ice, but a deep, steady chill that sinks into your bones and tells you that this place has never been truly warm.

The air is thick with moisture. It carries a constant, fine mist of spray from the waves above, forming droplets that condense on the stone ridges and trickle into tiny, invisible channels between the basins. Breathing is not difficult but it feels heavy, as if the air itself were half water.

The mist above is colder than the air near your body, and when it drifts low you see it as a faint layer of fog hovering knee-high above the floor, tracing the contours of the basins like ghostly water.

---

### Light and Darkness

The light is almost never enough.

The ceiling’s glow is faint, uneven, and far off. Somewhere above the ocean ceiling, there may be a sun or a moon or some other source of light, but its presence is refracted, distorted, and filtered by miles of cold water and swirling clouds of tiny creatures.

The result is a dim twilight that ebbs and flows with the slow rhythms of distant swells. At times the ceiling brightens by a fraction, causing the faint reflections on the stone floor to shift, like a faint aurora moving along the rock.

Wherever the water pours over a low edge or spills into a deep basin, it carries faint moving light with it—tiny phosphorescent dots, like the stars of a miniature galaxy emptying into the stone.

Far to the—what feels like “north”—there is a brighter glow, a patch of the ceiling that seems almost translucent. It is not bright enough to illuminate more than a faint halo of blue-green around itself, but it suggests an open space above, perhaps where the ocean ceiling rises higher, or thins.

---

### Sound and Quietness

This place is loud in its quietness.

The air is filled with a soft murmur of water—distant, dense, like listening to a crowd from a great distance away. There is no single crash or wave, but a constant low roar of pressure moving through the ocean ceiling.

The water spills down into crevices in thin, steady trickles. Each trickle has its own subtle rhythm and pitch. When you listen, you realize that they are not all random: some repeat a short series of notes—phrases of dripping water falling over and over again.

Closer to you, the sound of your own movement through the shallow air layer is amplified—the soft drag of wet clothing, the faint ripple of disturbed water around your legs, the occasional scrape of foot on gritty stone. These sounds are loud enough to be unnerving, because nothing else in the Undershelf speaks at that volume.

There is no wind. There is no bird. There is no shout of a human voice.

---

### Erosion and Time

The story of this place is written in its smoothness.

Everything that could be sharpened or broken has been worn away. The ridges of stone are low and rounded, like old teeth. The floor of the Undershelf is not flat but it is gentle—slopes rise and fall without a sudden break anywhere to be seen.

The edges of the basins are feathered with concentric lines, each layer a slightly different color—from dark green-black at the bottom to pale grey at the top. These are perhaps deposits of silt from ages long gone, laid down in slow, annual or even longer cycles of settling and drying.

Here and there, portions of the ceiling have collapsed. You can see where blocks of stone once larger have broken away and tumbled down to rest now as low, rounded mounds on the floor. They, too, are smooth. Their corners are gone, replaced by curves that suggest years of water skirting around them, tracing invisible paths.

There is no sense of urgency in the erosion of this place. It is happening, but it is happening so slowly that you might stand here for years and only notice the change in the layer of silt on the floor.

---

### Vegetation and Growth

What is alive here clings.

Vegetation is sparse, but it is present in thin, fragile forms that have learned to survive on the barest margins of light and air.

Moss-like clusters of dark green cling to low ridges around the bases of the shafts where light is stronger. They grow not upward but outward, slow expanding circles that are eaten away at the center as fast as they can colonize the edge.

Faint lichen patterns spider the ceiling in some places, a thin tracery of white and blue-green lines that radiate from faint cracks in the stone. These patterns look like ancient maps of forgotten rivers or constellations, but they are alive and slowly growing, following the faint glow of light above.

In the water columns that hang from the ceiling, you can see pale fronds of thin, translucent algae that are not rooted but simply float, suspended, moving in lazy loops with the currents. They are faint enough to be translucent clouds of life rather than distinct plants.

The floor itself has almost no growth. It is too dark, too cold, and too disturbed by the slow spill of water from the ceiling above. Only the smallest organisms, invisible as individual forms but visible as slight shimmering colors when the water mixes over them, seem to thrive in the thin layer that coats the stone basins.

---

### Fauna and Rarity of Life

This world is not rich with animals.

Most life you sense rather than see clearly. Small, translucent things swim in the water columns, like motes of dust suspended in light. They drift with the currents, turning occasionally, catching the faint glow from above and reflecting it in sharp points of color.

Occasionally a larger form passes across the glowing ceiling like a shadow—slow, undulating, moving without urgency in the great ocean overhead. You cannot tell if it is a fish or something more like a cloud of intertwined organisms. It comes rarely and stays for only a short time before vanishing into the dim blue.

On the floor itself, creatures are rare but striking when you encounter them. Pale, segmented shapes move like slow centipedes in the wet silt close to the edges of the basins. They seem to follow the path of the water that spills over the rim, tracing its faint warmth. You have seen only three or four such creatures in all the time you have been here.

The air layer is largely empty. There are no flying insects, no birds, no bats that might be expected to nest in such caves. Instead, the space above your head is occupied only by drifting mist and the occasional faint gleam where water droplets catch stray light.

---

### Built Structures and Absence

There are no buildings.

No walls have been raised here by hand or tool. No pillars carved to support the ceiling. No docks built into the water columns. This is one of the few places where the world feels completely untouched by human craft or artifact of any kind.

The basins are not shaped by hand; their rounded symmetry is the result of slow, circular grinding of grains and water over an age of time. The ridges that look like paths are not roads but the worn-down remains of ancient rock layers that have been revealed by water erosion.

If you look closely, you may find isolated stones that are suspiciously shaped like tools or fragments of tools—naturally occurring edges that might, if picked up, be employed to cut or scrape. But there is no sign of them having been used, no deliberate pattern of chipping marks, no accumulation of them by any hearth or fire.

The absence of built space is striking because the world itself feels so full. Everything is packed—with water, with sound, with slow movement—and yet none of that packed fullness is the result of human or organized planning.

---

### Technology and Its Shadows

This world does not demand technology to be felt, but it hints at its possibility.

The stone is dense enough to be heavy work to cut or shape. If someone were to try, it would take large effort and simple, strong tools—stone, hardened bone, perhaps the earliest form of metalwork—to produce any kind of mark beyond the shallowest scratches.

The water’s currents are strong enough to be harnessed, but they are too complex and layered in this submerged shelf to be used easily. If someone were to try, they might be able to set small floats into the water columns to drive some primitive wheel or paddle, but there is no sign that such a thing has been done.

There are no visible machines, no gears, no wires, no lights of industry. Only the faint machinery of the world itself—water pressure, erosion cycles, slow growth of mosses, and drifting of cloudlike lifeforms.

The technology here is not the work of human hands but the work of time: the slow accumulation of sediment layers, the patient grinding of stone against stone, the subtle rearranging of particles by the hidden motions of currents.

---

### Dynamics and Turbulence

The surface of the Undershelf is calm, but the world it is part of is not.

Above you, the ocean ceiling is constantly in motion. Large swells travel through it like the slow breaths of some giant creature sleeping far above. You can see this reflected in the shifting of light and the way pressure seems to fall and then build against your ears.

The water columns themselves are never still. Their internal currents are complex and layered, moving at different speeds and directions at different depths. Sometimes a whirl will appear in them, a momentary spiral that tightens and then untwists itself, leaving tiny suspended forms of life caught in its dance.

Small collapses of the ceiling happen periodically—not enough to bring the whole ocean room down, but enough to send loose sediment into the water column where it becomes a cloud of slowly swirling particles. These events are rare but loud when they occur: a low rush of falling material and a sudden denser murk in the water.

The basins themselves are dynamic in their own way. Each spillover of water redistributes silt along the floor of the basin and then reshapes the tiny channel that water follows next time. Over time, basins deepen and migrate—slowly, but in ways that can be seen if you return to the same place after a long absence.

This place is turbulent not in the sense of constant storm, but in the sense of ceaseless change hidden under the cover of apparent stillness. Nothing is ever exactly the same as it was an instant before.

---

### Rules and Logic of the World

This world seems to obey consistent laws, though some of them are strange to human eyes.

Light flows from above but does not reach the floor strongly because it must pass through thick, living ocean and layers of suspended material. The brighter the region of the ceiling above, the less distance there is between the Undershelf and the open surface of the ocean.

Water pressure is stronger where the ocean is deepest above and weaker where the ceiling is higher. This makes some regions of the Undershelf safer for creatures accustomed to lower pressure and others more dangerous.

The floor rises and falls in ways that allow pockets of air to exist trapped beneath the ocean. This balance is fragile, but it has remained stable for long ages of the world’s life.

Time moves differently here than on the surface above. The swells of the ocean ceiling are slow; the growth of moss and lichen is slower; the erosion of stone is slower still. Living creatures that are adapted to this pace can move much faster than their environment, but they cannot match the frantic energy of environments above.

Everything that lives in the Undershelf has learned to use layers of water rather than air alone as a medium for movement. Some creatures float, some drift, and a few crawl along the floor using the thin layer of water as a cushion and support.

The human body is not meant to be here, but it can survive for short periods of time if it stays in the trapped layer of air and avoids the deeper water columns.

---

### As You Move Through This Place

As you walk—or, rather, as you move—through the Undershelf, you become aware of several things:

The floor is not level but gently slopes in one main direction—toward the brighter region of the ocean ceiling. There, more shafts of narrow light pierce down, and more open water seems to be visible above.

The basins become more frequent and slightly deeper that direction, as if water has been working longer to hollow out the stone. You see more of the pale centipede-like creatures tracing their slow paths in the silt near the edges.

Farther from that light, the Undershelf grows darker and more confined. The ceiling dips lower; the layer of trapped air grows thinner. You feel the pressure of the ocean overhead more strongly there, as if the world above is weighing down on your shoulders.

Everywhere, the sound of water is the same persistent murmur. It never becomes louder than a low roar but it never completely disappears. It is the background sound of this world, the noise of its ocean ceiling breathing.

If you kneel down, you can see small patterns in the wet silt left by recent spillovers of water. These patterns are temporary rivers of grains, branching lines like tiny networks of veins and capillaries, all leading to a lower point where they disappear into a darker pool of water.

Above you, the shadows of large drifting things pass occasionally across the glowing ceiling, like storms traveling at high altitude in another world.

---

All of this together makes the Abyssal Plains of Sighisvar not just a place beneath the ocean but a world in its own right—one that is submerged, cold, sparse in life yet dense with motion, ancient in its erosion but turbulent in its slow dynamics, almost empty of built structures but full of patterns shaped by time and water alone.

It is a world of quiet sound and dim light, where nothing is hurried but nothing is truly still.
