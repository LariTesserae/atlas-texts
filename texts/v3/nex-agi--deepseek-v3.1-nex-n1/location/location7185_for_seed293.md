The Sanfish Market is a bowl of dreams and regrets that the world has mostly forgotten.

It sits low where two continents once tried to kiss each other and left instead a shallow wound of a basin. The mountains are gone now, or so they say; the ground here is flattened to a patient, near-horizontal slowness, where the horizon is a suggestion and not a border. The air is warm and dense and always smells of the next hour’s rain, whether it is coming or not.

### Roof and walls

This is not a city in stone. The city is a plant, and the plants are the city.

Over the basin, a single sacred organism has been teased into architecture and never left. It is called the Quillwood by those who feel it has a name, though its true shape is as fractured as an old mirror’s. The Quillwood is a tree, and not a tree; a colony being pulled from the soil that is itself also soil, and the trees are merely its most recent skin.

Anywhere else you might see skyline, here you see the ceiling of the world.

Massive gill‑shields—vast leaf‑sails of mottled copper and deep green—hang from the ribs of the Quillwood’s raised boughs to form angular vaults and canopies. The wood is not just brown but mineral, glinting with mica‑like sheens and metallic threads where engineered phloem loans have alternated with the original tree’s growth rings for millennia. The impression is not forest but a cathedral abandoned by its forgotten god and then recolonized by a more ambitious, living one.
