The first thing you notice is the noise.

The second is the smell.

It comes at you from the forty-meter dome of living stone that forms the world’s sky, and from the black forest that clings to every curve of it, and from the carpet of blue moss that makes the floor of the world glow like a banked ember. The smell is thick with resin, spice, and rot, and the noise is a choir of clicks and scrapes, constant as a waterfall, but more broken, more argumentative.

There is sea water on this world—far below the root-networks, the sacs of the life tree, and the kilometers of sponge rock that hang under the floor—but you will never taste it. The Great Tree Above the Sea drinks it all and distributes it through living veins that pulse in ceilings and walls and
