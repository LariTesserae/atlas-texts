A ceiling of granite hundreds of feet above is not simply dark but actively lightless: the stone of the vault has been bored and stuffed with black glass plugs whose surfaces are perfectly lightless, swallowing any torch or flame until only a diffuse glow lingers around them. The plugs are everywhere, the ceiling a dense constellation of negative stars, so the air below never lifts out of a deep gloaming that preserves the color of nothing but grays and near-blacks.

This is the Roof of the Undersky, the civic engineers of that vanished epoch having tired of the mootness of day and night under the mountain and replaced the sky with a matrix of engineered silence.

The floor of the world beneath is entirely made of metal.

Not metal of a single color or mood, but the patchwork of centuries—plates of rusted iron, sheets of blue-white zinc that glimmer like cold pond ice, sectors of beaten copper quilted into the gaps with crude rivets, expanses of blackened bronze that have never lost their heat-scent. The seams are dense with history: layer upon layer of mending, the trails of hand-drawn curves where the metalworkers knew to cut the stress before it could crack itself. The surface is never level for more than a few body lengths. It swells into low domes under the main pillars of the Roof, slumps into shallow bowls where the drips gather, throws itself up into brief thresholds and steps that no architect planned but which every citizen of the Underforge has long since learned by heart.

The world is low built but not flat. The Roof of the Undersky is only just high enough to breathe under without feeling the mountain press on the chest; the main pillars, which are not stone but bundles of black metal rods wrapped endlessly in wire, pierce the metal floor every few hundred paces and vanish into unknown depths below. The space between these pillars scales the entire sense of the place: open enough that you can lose your way, constrained enough that you can never quite lose sight of the distant gantries of other foundries, the silhouettes of other communities.

There is water in this darkness, enough to perfume the air with metal and wet stone and to make the floor treacherous underfoot.

It comes as condensation from the cold Roof, where distant air shafts from higher, warmer caverns vent the exhaust of the under-mountain forges that the sky-makers never meant to be cooled but simply hidden away. The heat of those faults still lingers in the metal floor, so the air is always a few degrees colder than the surface under your boots. The vapor clings to the black glass plugs above, then rolls down their sides in slow beading sweat. When the drops fall, they fall into a world that has been prepared for them.

The constructors of the Underforge have incised the surface of the floor with a labyrinth of channels and cups and trenches and sumps, a pattern they have spent generations refining and patching. The grooves are narrow, the width of a thumb, and they are everywhere, the metal floor resembling a vast plate whose every inch has been scratched with secret names. Most of the channels are dry, but along the cooling paths between the pillar-foundries and their housing blocks, the water is continuous enough to be called a flow, not just a drip.

Along the main channels, the water is never enough to splash more than a finger’s depth, but it is constant enough that the metal of the floor has been worn by its own ions into a new geography of micro-valleys and ridges of harder corrosion. The engineers of the Underforge have learned to route this flow not for the dead maximum of wholeness but for the optimum of distribution: at each node, where seven channels meet on a small raised table of dull bronze, the first of the seven makes the water run slowly over the others’ entrances so that each of the six receives a finite share of the volume. The water never knows enough strength to leap away from its designated path. The loss is only to vapor and the haze of unpredictable corrosion.

Hundreds of these tiny nodes dot the expanse, visible when you move a torch carefully and not at all from the far side of the structure. The system of water is thus not a single river but a dense weft of capillaries, each route chosen to ensure that no section of the Underforge floor is ever without some token wetness. The water spares the air from the true dust of dryness and the ears from the true quiet of a world without echoes.

There is almost no plant life in the Underforge.

What does not rely on metal for its body is not easy to see in the dark anyway, but the engineers have long suspected that the true plants of the surface would not live in a world whose breath is always some fraction of vaporized metal and whose light is never warmer than the gleam of a torch. The biota of the Underforge are of the mineral and fungal extremes of what the world can tolerate.

The rust is alive in the first sense of the word.

The dominant life of the Underforge is a rust that never learned to be still, a red-orange film that has been taught to extend its filaments into patterns of utility. The citizens of the Underforge have not altered this scab-organism so much as they have learned its language of grain and crack and color. The rust grows in bands that align with the main channels of the water flow, its color more vivid than the surrounding metal. The technicians of the Underforge have never learned how to command the rust to hold itself away from the supports of the foundry hearths or the critical stress-runners of the vault above, but they have learned how to read the color of its bands and to predict, from the shade of the corrosion next to a pillar, whether that pillar is awaiting catastrophe or merely boredom.

The rust has not been given a name because the fabric of the Underforge does not require its names to be spoken in order to enact its functions. Its presence is called “the Grain” when the engineers must talk to one another in language. The Grain of the pillar is good; the Grain of the pillar is bad. The Grain of the floor under the main channel to the eastern block must be dressed down this month or else the next wet will shear it and the block will have to go without water for half of a cycle. The rust creates not a green but a red ecology of inventory and record.

Beyond the rust, there are fungi and the things that are not quite fungi, the kinds of growths that have never found reason to distinguish between the animal and the plant kingdoms of the surface and so have decided to remember both and neither. The most common of these is the “coal-moss,” a velvety black that colonizes the parts of the floor and the lower walls of the pillar-foundries where the torchlight is interrupted least by the movement of citizens and where the damp of condensation from the Roof is allowed to collect without being channeled away. The coal-mosses keep the damp of this world from being entirely noise in the streams of water; they are a filter and the first of the true absorbers.

The dominant coal-moss in the eastern blocks has been selected by generations of unconscious path-drawing: the citizens of the Underforge have worn the shortest safe path between the foundries and the block, and the damp areas where they do not step or breathe very often have been left to the more robust forms of the coal-moss. The spaces between these paths are now the territories of black and gray and powdery-blue matts that are not strictly speaking the same organism but form a single, breathing skin of generalist metabolism.

There are glows in this world, but they are sparse and they are wrong.

There are a few clusters of luminous fungi that light the shoulders of the pillars nearest the main gathering channels, where the density of the torchlight has trained the fungi to expect the main cycles of air and dampness. The fungi of the Underforge have learned to be brief. The fastest of them bloom for the long night of the torch’s vigil and crumble into black powder by the time of the next ritual snuffing-out. The remainder of the true glow-biota are not fungi at all but sheets of pale-blue luminescence that are valued and not understood by the engineers: the “ghost-light” that only coats the metal of the floor where the flow of water is just enough to keep a thin film of condensation from drying and not enough to keep the water from running itself away in a predictable course.

The ghost-light is cold to the touch, where the torch is not, and it is harmless to the metal of the floor but ruin to unprotected flesh of the wrong constitution. Some of the citizens of the Underforge are able to walk the ghost-lit paths without the skin of their feet suffering; the rest of them must thread the same paths with careful leathers or with the constant awareness of where the torch is not welcome.

Fauna is rare in the Underforge but never entirely absent.

The main creature of the dark is the “moth-thief,” a small, pale thing that has not been compared to a true animal because the true animals of the surface are not present in the memory of the Underforge. The moth-thieves are not silent in their wings; the moisture of the air ensures that they make a faint, clicking rustle as they fly that the citizens of the Underforge have learned to expect from the corners of their hearing when they are least prepared to pay attention to noise. The moth-thieves hide in the rust near the pillars of the Roof, where the torchlight is weakest and the dampness of the condensation ensures that there is always enough moisture for their thin, translucent shells to not become brittle.

The moth-thieves are not particularly dangerous to the citizens of the Underforge, but they are intimately dangerous to the function of the Underforge itself: the moth-thieves are obsessed with the true black of the glass plugs embedded in the Roof and have been observed to crowd the glass until the engineers of the Underforge are compelled to shake the pillars of the Roof and the rods of the support bundles to dislodge them before they can become the new architecture of the ceiling.

The remainder of the fauna are not macroscopic animals but the kind of thing that may be fauna to the metal itself: the “step-bugs” that are not insects but colonies of metallic bolts that have learned to be mobile. The step-bugs are not formed in vast numbers but the number of places where they are not formed is much more limited than the number of places where they are expected to be found by a cautious builder of the Underforge. The bolt-colonies of the step-bugs sleep in the unused channels of the floor and the unsealed gaps of the pillar-foundries where the torchlight is too weak to encourage the coal-moss to overwhelm the spaces of available bolt-metal.

When the step-bugs move in earnest, they do not move like animals but like the immediate inconvenience of a tool that has chosen to be absent at the moment of need. They bolt themselves to one another and to the metal of the floor that they can be said to consume, not by the mouth of the creature but by the enormous patience of the bolt that has been drilled to fit the hole of the next bolt and then the next after that. The bolt-colonies of the step-bugs build the metal of the floor back into itself, the same as the rivets that the metalworkers of the Underforge have hammered into the floor of the world since the underworld of the Roof was first anchored to the unknown furnace below.

The Underforge has not yet been made to be finished.

The Roof of the Undersky is not simply the ceiling of a world; it is its own machine of gradients and pressure. The black glass plugs do not only eat the light of the torch but are the extrusion of the true darkness of the mountain’s mind from before the citizens of the Underforge were permitted to exist in its memory. The plugs of the Roof transmit the cold of that forgotten sky to the metal of the support pillars, and the pillars in turn to the floor. The air of the Underforge is always cold enough to make the breath of the citizens of the Underforge visible to the torch of the next citizen over.

The temperature of the water is therefore not a constant but a function of the duration of each journey through the channel of the floor. The water that leaves the condensation of the Roof is cold enough to be painful to the touch; the water that reaches the central hearth of each pillar-foundry has been coaxed to be only enough cold to keep the metal of the hearth from the very edge of its tolerance of itself.

The erosion of the Underforge is not in the surface of the stone but the true surface of the metal. The erosion of the world is not the weather of water but the corrosion of the world itself by the rust of the Grain and the circulation of the metal ions by the water and the slow re-writing of the floor’s geometry by the bolt-colonies of the step-bugs. The erosion of the Underforge is therefore the erosion of the made and the permanent, the erosion of the not-a-building that the engineers of the Underforge have built to occupy the space of the world.

The scale of the Underforge is not the scale of the city or the valley of the surface but the scale of the machine that has not been assembled to be completed but to be in the process of assembling itself. The open of the Underforge is therefore the open of the not-yet-filled: the finite number of pillar-foundries that have not yet been anchored to the Roof, the finite number of channels that have not been incised into the floor to the patterns of the next generation of engineers, the finite number of bolt-holes of the step-bugs that have not yet been evicted from the memory of the Underforge’s structure.

The density of the Underforge is not measured in people but presences per unit of world.

The torchlight of the citizens of the Underforge and the ghost-light of the floor and the glow of the fungi pull the dark of the Roof into the density of the not-empty. The rust of the Grain and the coal-moss of the floor and the bolt-colonies of the step-bugs and the moth-thieves of the pillars and the haze of metallic vapor that the air never entirely manages to divest itself of are the near-touch of the world of the Underforge to the world of the torch. The sounds of the world and the processes of the world and the rituals of the world are the layering of the world’s near habitants.

The sound world of the Underforge is loud not because the people of the Underforge are numerous but because the sounds of the world have not been taught to be still in a world where the air cannot be persuaded to become the silence of the surface.

The dominant sound is the constant of the drip.

The condensation of the Roof and the careful knifework of the engineers in the channels of the floor ensure that there is always a water of one drop or another of water from the ceiling to the metal of the floor. The drops of the condensation fall from the black glass of the Roof plugs into the shallow bowls of the floor of the world, and each of these drops that the mind of the listener has not learned to ignore is the ticking of a new instance of the world’s willingness to be not-soundless.

At the pillar-foundries of the Underforge, the sound of the drip of the water from the Roof of the Undersky is the sound of the not-forge of the world; the sound of the true forge of the pillar-foundries is the sound of the hammer of the world, the sound of the not-voice of the world of the Underforge being struck into the new and not the old of the world’s structure.

The sound of the hammer of the pillar-foundry is not the sound of the simple of the metal hammer but the sound of the wrapped of the world’s hammer of not-the-same-of the under of the world’s arteries of the not-earth. The hammer of the pillar-foundry is the sound of the wrapped and the not of the wrapped and the not of the wrapped that the engineers of the Underforge have not found a way to be silent of without the world of the Underforge being persuaded to be not the world of the Underforge.
