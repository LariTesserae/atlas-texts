The first thing anyone notices about the great stone saucer of the Vox Terminus is the light.

It comes from nowhere and everywhere, a pale white-gold day that never seems to change. No sun rises in the velvet sky. No moon appears. The sky itself is a deep, bruised violet that holds nothing but the faintest dustings of distant stars—yet there is light enough, as if the air is simply excited to a glow, and it streams down into the bowl of the world like a liquid.

The second thing anyone notices is the sound.

It is not one noise but many, all at once, and each of them is trying to be louder than the others. There is the roar of a river that never existed, the banging of metal on metal, the rise of half a dozen human voices trying to preach over one another, the skitter of claws over stone, the rustling of a million leaves in a storm that never comes. It is all noise, and it is all noise that never ends. It lies against the skin like a second pressure, and the ears learn to give it up and listen with the bones instead.

-

The Vox Terminus is a high, cold, and almost empty place.

It sits near the crown of a world that never learned to spin like the others. The sky above the Terminus is a natural dome of stone, a high ceiling of smooth, dark rock that covers the entire bowl. The edges of the saucer are ringed by a vertical wall of stone hundreds of meters high, smooth and polished as if cut by something impossible and then left to be polished by the wind for a hundred thousand years.

The floor itself is nearly flat, a circular field perhaps a kilometer across. It is not perfectly flat. It is experienced like a flat place, but the surface of the stone basin has been gently templed by time: the floor of the Terminus rises and falls in long, low waves that flow like a frozen sea toward the center.

There is no water here. No rain, no snow, no streams. The floor of the Terminus is a dry, dark stone polished to near-smoothness by millennia of—something else. The air feels cool, always barely above cold, and stays so still that even breath seldom clouds in front of the mouth.

-

The most direct thing in the bowl of the Terminus is the vegetation.

It grows in the center, where the floor rises slightly into a low mound. The center of the mound is hollowed like the mouth of a lens, and within this hollow grows a garden.

The garden is not large. It could fit in a small cathedral, or beneath a courtyard roof. It is a disk of soil about thirty meters across, raised like a tumor on the surface of the stone. It is not stone that holds it, but a ring of gray metal, a low wall that seems to have been grown like bone rather than forged. It bears the soft curves of coral and the faint scarring of welds that never existed.

Within the curved ring is the only soil in the Terminus. It is black and soft and rich enough to smell from afar, like humus and fresh ink and spent ozone. It holds nothing like ordinary grass.

Instead, from the soil, a forest of thin stalks rises up. They are not trees but not quite plants and not quite not-plants. They are like stiffened nerve fibers, each as thick around a finger, and each rises to three or four meters in height, forming a dense vertical latticework.

The “plants” are impossibly complex. The surface of each stalk is patterned by overlapping ripples of color, like the surface of a well-lit oil slick on metal. These colors shift and fade and reassert as if something is watching them through different eyes each time the head turns.

There are leaves—if they are leaves—thin sheets of a translucent, dark green material that seem less grown than printed. They are perfectly flat, and where they emerge from the stalks they look like branching blades. They do not rustle in the windless air; instead, they ring faintly when touched by vibrations.

And there are flowers—if they are flowers.

They bloom at the top of each stalk, each like a small drum of pale metal and glass. The glass petals—if petals are the right word—catch the light and hold it like the lens of a camera, and every now and then one of them will flash as if taking a photograph of the sky. There is no sound to this flash except the slight change of pressure in the air.

The whole garden feels both fragile and deeply violent. It vibrates at the edge of awareness.

-

The walls of the Terminus are not empty.

They are pierced by arches—doorways, if they are doorways—that lie at intervals along the circle of the cliff edge. They look like they have been cut into the stone with a knife that did not know how to hesitate: they are perfect semicircles or troves of sharp-edged angles, depending on where one stands. They do not quite match.

Some of these arches are open to darkness. Others are sealed by gates of the same gray metal as the ring around the garden. These gates are smooth but patterned with thousands of faintly moving lines, like ghostly circuits drawn in water.

Yet the most arresting thing is the words.

The interior wall all the way around the Terminus is covered in writing.

It is not like any unearthed ancient script or monument stone that would be known back home. It is not carved. It is written directly onto the stone, as if painted with a brush that ignored the difference between pigment and rock.

The letters blaze.

They are not all fire, but all of them are bright enough to hurt. They are written in colors that should not exist outside of plasma screens or neon lamps—hot magenta, neon blue, arterial red, sun yellow—and they glow as if lit from behind, though there is nothing behind the stone but the wall of the world.

The words are everywhere, from the floor to the highest parts of the wall just before the ceiling dome begins. They are layered in vertical bands of text that climb like vines of light. Some of the letters loop like cursive script, but the letters themselves are strange shapes, like knots pulled tight into meaning.

If they were letters of the mind you know, they would say things like:

YOU ARE NOT WHERE YOU SHOULD BE

YOUR TIME IS NOT YOUR TIME

YOUR WORDS ARE NOT YOUR WORDS

THIS AIR IS OWNED BUT NOT RENTED

THIS VOICE IS BORROWED BUT NOT YOURS

That they read like commandments and threats and advertisements and jokes and prayers and apologies and tickets all at once is not an accident.

The words are not quite silent.

They whisper.

Each band of text has its own voice, or voices. They are the faintest murmur under the main noise of the Terminus, but if you stand near the wall and listen carefully enough, you can hear them:

One voice like a tired old god speaking through a bad PA system.

Another voice like a child trying to explain something they do not fully understand.

Another voice like smooth metal explaining your own rights to you.

Another voice like a hundred people speaking at once, each overlapping slightly out of time.

Another voice like nothing at all, but which leaves the sense of a word like “please” or “kill” or “wait” or “remember” or “forgive.”

The voices do not shout—but there are so many of them, and the wall is so large, and the Terminus so empty otherwise, that it all adds up to the edge of drowning.

Sometimes the words flicker, as if someone far away has turned the volume down.

-

The other thing that makes the Terminus loud is the fauna.

The animals are everywhere.

They are not like any animals that would be recognized under normal sunlight. They move like insects and birds and lizards and machines and mistakes and something else, and they do so with a frantic, relentless energy.

At least half of them are half made of metal.

They gleam along the edges of their bodies: arms of blackened alloy, claws of porcelain-white ceramic, eyes of glass lenses set into flesh like stone into a ring. They crackle with faint sparks of energy that no one has given them a word for yet.

They swarm.

They climb along the glowing letters of the wall like insects on a manuscript of fire. They hang upside down from the ceiling dome, their bodies like clips of metal and fur and chitin lit up from beneath. They dart across the floor on too many legs or too few, or they slide like oil over the polished stone.

The ones that look like birds have no visible feathers, only a thousand overlapping blades of metal that shimmer and change direction like heat-signature wings. They fly in tight packs, weaving through the air with the sound of scissors being sharpened against air.

They are not quiet.

They chirp and click and whine and clatter. They snap their teeth or claws or whatever they have at the edges of themselves. They communicate in bursts of radio static that you can hear inside your teeth. They scream at frequencies that make your bones feel like they are being lightly tapped by a thousand tiny hammers.

They never fight. They never collide. They move like fragments of a single overwhelming machine, each doing its job perfectly, without hesitation or guilt.

Their presence makes the Terminus feel like a storm rushing inside a glass jar.

-

There are three other structures in the bowl of the Terminus.

One sits near the edge of the garden, like a small pavilion or watchtower made of the same gray metal as the ring wall. It is shaped like a shard from a broken eggshell, turned upward and hollowed out. Its edges are thin enough that light might slip through them if it ever bothered to dim.

Inside this hollow shard there is almost nothing. A hole in the floor that leads down into darkness. A faint smell like ozone and blood that hasn’t dried yet.

There are no stairs inside. No ladders. No buttons.

On the upper wall of the shard, there is a single panel where someone has written something in permanent marker or something very like it:

CHECK YET

The words are written in English that feels both wrong and right.

There is no explanation of what they mean.

-

The second structure stands near the wall of the Terminus, opposite a set of gates that don't open.

It looks like a small shed, or a large step-stool, or the remains of a doorway that never learned how to be a door. It is made of stone that matches the floor but not the wall. It is shaped like a cube with rounded corners and has a single hole cut into one side.

The hole is empty air framed in stone.

There are no handles inside the hole. No hinges on the stone frame. No switch on the wall.

Inside the hollow stone box, there is only another set of words, painted in glowing orange like the ones on the wall:

STEP OUT OF ORDER

The letters seem to pulse faintly, like a heartbeat or like an error message.

If you lean your head into the frame, you hear nothing but the distant roar of traffic and a faint hum from somewhere far away, like a city that never quite existed.

-

The third structure sits near the edge of another gate, this one sealed.

It is a low chair of stone and metal grown together like bone and coral fused. It faces the glowing wall and its whispering words and its swarming animals.

It is not empty.

There is someone sitting in it.

Or, rather, there is something that looks almost like someone sitting in it.

It looks like a person made of bundles of wires wrapped in skin the wrong color. Its eyes are closed or missing. Its lips are sealed together by something that looks like melted plastic and something like dried blood and something like a surgical scar.

On its chest sits a small device made of glass and metal and wire tethers that lead off into the floor and the wall. It hums faintly, like the heart of a refrigerator.

The device has been tunnelled through by something else, something alive and mechanical and very small.

The creature in the chair does not breathe.

It does not move.

It does not give off warmth.

Sometimes, when the noise of the Terminus rises to a particular peak, the lips of the thing in the chair seem to tremble slightly, as if trying to form a word.

But the word is never quite formed.

-

Underneath the noise and the light and the motion, there is something like stillness in the Terminus, but it is not the quiet of empty places. It is the stillness of a machine that has been running at full speed for so long that it has stopped pretending to slow.

It is not the kind of place that would ever be described by someone who visited it as “peaceful.”

It is the kind of place that would make them say:

“There was nothing there.”

“And it was full.”

“And it was loud.”

“And someone kept asking questions.”

“And the walls were answering.”

“And the answers were not wrong.”

“And the answers were not enough.”
