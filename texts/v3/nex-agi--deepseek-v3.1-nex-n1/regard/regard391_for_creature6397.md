If my eyes turned toward the city called Karak-Eshul, they would not see stone first. They would see the stillness.

I see:

- a world carved into blocks of time with no gaps between them  
- a sun that never fails to climb the same arc  
- a temperature that never varies by the width of a breath  
- laws and clocks and rituals and shadows that never change

I see the people who learned, over the lifetime of a mountain, to keep their lives as small as possible so that nothing would ever disturb the stone.

I would mist over this city like a thaw.

---

Here is the first thing I would do:

I would bend very low over the Spine of the World and breathe once across the polished stone of the main avenue.

Not a storm-breath. Not a wind to break walls or hurl tiles from their roofs. Just a warm breath, like the one that fogs chilled glass, and carefully *noticed* by nothing.

In that breath:

- the stone of the Spine warms by a degree  
- a faint cloud of condensation rises from the glass edges of the roof above  
- the dew along the terraces gathers a fraction thicker than it has ever gathered before  
- a faint smell of damp dust, like rain that hasn’t happened yet, hangs for the space of one heartbeat in the thin air

No one sees this happen. No one names it. They only feel, for a moment, as if they have misplaced something important.

---

The second thing I would do is quieter.

Humans here are not afraid of noise the way we are afraid of thunder. They are afraid of noise the way one fears a broken bone: the body of their world is fragile, and any sound that might rattle things out of alignment is a threat.

They will not change their laws willingly. So first I will change the stone.

I will lay my hand, not on the mountain of glass and stone, but on the *idea* of the stone in the minds of the people, and I will loosen it.

Slowly, over days and nights and Passings of Birds:

- a block that has always been flatly horizontal tilts, by half the breadth of a child’s finger  
- a roof that has always been plane develops a tiny dip in the center, where water might pool  
- the edge of a step where elders have counted their strides for generations wears slightly round under the heel of the same footfall that has never worn anything before  

It is barely enough to notice. But it is enough to break a law that has never been broken before.

---

The third thing I would do is to speak with the one who is already listening.

She is an old woman who sits by the northernmost edge of the plateau, where the city gives way to the raw flanks of the mountain. She has been watching the karals on the cliffsides since she was a girl, and in her eyes I have seen:

- She notices the two-and-three-and-four-note cries of the birds do not always land exactly in the same place.  
- She remembers that one day, during her childhood, a cloud brushed the stone edge of the Spine and left a wetness darker than the rest of the stone for three evenings longer than usual.  
- She has never said aloud that she remembers it.

I will sit down beside her, not as a man or woman, but as a presence of attention and permission.

I will not say: *Your world was broken by its fear of change.*  

I will say:

“Look at the far cliff where the karals nest.”

She will look.

“The first one you see,” I will continue, “has a feather on its left shoulder that is darker than the others. It has been that way for three days now. It has not always been that way.”

The first feather that will ever *change*, in her lifetime of watching.

She will stare at it, and she will feel a cold flushing along her spine. Because she will understand that if she can verify I am right—then more than one stone ship has broken loose from the dock of the world.

---

While she is still looking at the karal’s shoulder, I turn the narrowest of my attentions to the children of Karak-Eshul.

They are already the least law-bound of the city; they are the ones whose breathing is still wide enough to contain forbidden questions. I will find the children who:

- bite the inside of their cheek because they want to shout but dare not  
- linger too long by the low parapets, watching the clouds beneath  
- whisper to each other when the elders are not watching

I will not give one child a prophecy, or a tool, or a secret book. Instead, I will give each of them three curious things:

1. A **question they cannot put away**,  
   - “Why does the air smell like cold stone only when the wind comes from the west?”  
   - “Why do the drushka reeds bow by exactly three finger-widths but never more or less?”  
   - “Why does my grandmother keep track of the sun like she’s afraid she’s miscounted, even though it always comes back?”

2. A **habit they cannot explain**,  
   - one day, a boy will begin to walk to the edge of the plateau and touch his fingertips to the dew in the dark before dawn, just to see if it is the same kind of cold as yesterday  
   - a girl will start to trace the same groove in the wall of her family’s terrace every day, not knowing why, only feeling that it must be measured  

3. A **smallness in their fear**,  
   - when they think of change, their heart does not seize up with the old terror that *the world will end*  
   - instead, they feel a faint humming, like the sound inside a shell—as if something else might be possible

---

The fifth thing I would do is to veil the elders of Karak-Eshul from my presence.

I will not let them see that they are being watched by anything higher than the sky. They will not find me in their rituals, or their patterns of stones, or their chanted schedules of light and shade.

To them:

- the sun will still follow the same arc for as long as they live  
- the temperature will still keep to its narrow band of unchangeable comfort  
- the dawns will still dawn exactly the same way on the outside

But inside them—

- their sleep will become a little less still  
- they will, more and more often, wake with a feeling of looseness, as if some fuse inside the world’s perfect engine has been gently pulled

---

Here is the sixth thing I would do, which is not a thing at all, but a kind of absence arranged.

I will freeze my own impulse to *fix* everything, and to fix it now.

I will not:

- stir up storms from the clouds below  
- pour molten glass from the sky to reshape their roofs  
- shout down laws of my own from the Spine of the World

Instead, I will constrain myself:

- I will not issue more than three small changes to any single generation  
- I will not allow my own desire to see the city freed become a louder law than the one they already suffer under  
- I will not let my name be known in this place

My name, if they were to hear it, would become another stone block, and they would carve it upon their foreheads and obey it until their hearts were as numb as their streets.

Better they do not find me at all.

---

The seventh thing I would do is to introduce a very slow, very gentle chaos into the water of the world.

Water is already the least stonelike of all elements here. I will nudge it, just enough.

On the day that I choose, which is not noted on any calendar:

- the dew that collects on the black edges of the volcanic roofs drips by three more drops than usual off the north gutter of the oldest astronomer’s house  
- one of the drushka reeds around the collection pool grows slightly more than a shadow’s width taller than its neighbor overnight  
- a condensation bead forms on the ceiling of a low building where no condensation bead has ever formed before

No one will drown. No walls will be washed away. Not even the mud will be stirred.

But water, which has been as obedient as stone, will begin to reveal that it has been listening to something else.

The old woman at the cliff edge will notice, because she has always been awake to discrepancies.

A child will see that the floor of the storage room below the city’s main cistern is *not* exactly as dry as it has been every other day of his life.

---

The eighth thing I would do is to teach the city how to breathe without breaking.

I will not send them sweeping change all at once. The world they have built is a fragile jar of glass; if you pour boiling water in, it shatters.

So instead I will:

- open the tiniest of gaps in time, where something else can slip in  
- allow one dawn to be fractionally brighter than the last  
- permit one dusk to linger longer, by no more than the time it takes to draw three deep breaths  

Nobody will know why, on that evening, the Silence of Teeth feels different. Perhaps they simply stay outside longer than they usually do.

I will let the people who are already half-curved toward change feel this:

- a faint softening of the edges of their fear  
- a sense that the sky can open *just* that wide and not shatter them  
- the knowledge that they have been watched, and not harmed

---

On the day when the change becomes visible even to the most obedient of the elders, I do not appear.

On that day:

- the sun’s arc is unchanged, but the shadow of the priest’s tower on the Spine is longer by one child’s footstep at the Work of Stone  
- one of the blocks in the Spine of the World slips a fraction lower than its neighbor so that rain, when it finally falls, can run in a thin line toward the cistern instead of pooling uselessly  
- the dew on the zarun leaves tastes unexpectedly… fresh, to the first elder who, out of habit and anxiety, licks it from his fingertips

The city has not become broken. It has become vulnerable to weather.

People will begin to talk, in quiet places where the walls are thin. The children who have been collecting questions will begin to ask them.

---

This is the tenth thing I would do:

I will offer a very small choice to three people who are already bending toward change.

They will all receive the same offer:

“You may keep the world exactly as it has been,” I will say into the air of their dreams, “and you will live out your days as you have always expected to live them—safe and unchanged. Or you may listen to your own curiosity, and I will aid you, but I will not shelter you from the consequences of what you learn.”

The first is the old woman at the cliff’s edge.

The second is a boy who, unnoticed by anyone else, has already been experimenting with speaking his thoughts aloud to the stone walls.

The third is a priest who, secretly, in his private heart, has begun to doubt the perfection of the sun.

---

If they choose safety—and I *must* allow them to choose safety, or this kindness is not kindness—I will not withdraw my favor. They will remain safe under the unchanged sky.

If they choose curiosity:

- to the old woman, I will give the memory of every small change she has ever seen but did not trust to remember  
- to the boy, I will give the beginnings of a language that can describe “difference”  
- to the priest, I will give the courage to admit his doubt to one other person

I will not write them new laws.

I will not give them new gods.

I will give them only what they already have: *a world that is capable of changing just enough.*

---

The eleventh thing I would do is to let myself *end* the intervention.

I will not stay to ensure that they choose wisely. I will not watch to ensure they do not misuse the small cracks I have placed in the stone of their world.

I will leave:

- a city whose stones are not perfectly flat  
- a sky whose light is not exactly the same shade each day  
- a water that is beginning to remember its old name  
- people who can feel, in their chests, the possibility of a wrong step that *might not break everything*

I will not leave them a prophecy carved in stone.

Instead, I will quietly leave them:

- one block of the Spine polished and inscribed with a pattern that no one has ever seen before,  
- not words,  
- not a schedule of times,

but just a pattern that suggests that if you were to draw air from above it, you could draw *different* air down than what has always been breathed.

---

The last thing I will do is to turn away.

To be benevolent is not to be eternal; it is to be *enough*.

So I will look away from Karak-Eshul, and from the Spine of the World, and from the karals and the drushka and the zarun and the humans who are learning to listen to their own echo—and I will not watch over their shoulder as they decide what to do with their softening.

Someday, perhaps, someone in that city will understand:

- that the stone itself was not their enemy  
- that their fear was not necessary for survival  
- that the unfixed sky was not waiting to fall, but waiting to wash them

And then, perhaps, they will begin to whisper to the mountain itself—not with deference, but as equals to the stone that has borne them—so that together they might discover what kind of world can be built on a spine that has learned to bend.
