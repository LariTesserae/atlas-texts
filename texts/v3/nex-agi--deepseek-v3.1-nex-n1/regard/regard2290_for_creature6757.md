At first, nothing changes.

The benevolent power does not appear with fanfare or light. There are no songs from the clouds, no sudden warmth rolling across the plateau to startle the rock-wrens and make the Still-Wallers look up from their channels.

The first sign of presence is… absence.

The cloud deck below the rim lies quiet, but the usual faint parade of spores—the microscopic drift of lichen and moss and mold that rides the fog—does not rise into the city. The morning mist comes up as usual; condensation beads on the ceramic bowls and in the carved channels. The water still gathers, drop by slow drop.

But now it is only water.

The miniature world of translucent arthropods and soft, slow creatures that had been teeming in the channels of the Still-Walls—the invisible fauna that coats the wet stone surfaces—is gone. The thin sheen of life on every surface of the city’s cold water has been lifted away, as if by a breath so gentle it took no dust with it.

It is a subtle mercy.

The boundary of the plateau has been shifted, infinitesimally but absolutely: the world down below the cloud deck will keep its lowland profusion, but the high city of Still-Walls will be given the chance to become less of a reef and more of a temple.

The stone remains unchanged. The basalt columns still tower, their hollows still secret and strong. The frost still wedges into hairline cracks and the night sky still presses the warmth of breath back into the lungs. The condensation of Still-Walls will continue to flow from the sweat of the rock and the fog of the rim. But now it will be clean water: simple, cold, and strangely easy.

The city of the Still-Walls has never known water that could be taken without cost.

Until now.

II.  
The first to notice are not the people, but the rock-wrens.

The wrens are gliding the column faces, beaks precise, heads turning the way they do when they are listening to the stone with more than their ears. They probe the films of water where the condensation still gathers, where they have always found sustenance. The needle of each beak dips into cracks and channels and ceramic bowls.  

The needle comes out empty.

There is no faint shimmer of movement in the water. No translucent curve of miniature arthropod under the beak, no soft-bodied creature adhering to the tip of the tongue. For the first time in the long memory recorded by their ancestors’ bones, the water in the grooves of Still-Walls is simply water.

The birds do not panic. They pause on their vertical world, stone-colored feathers stilled, small hearts slow. The cold of the plateau is not the kind that kills quickly; it is the kind that teaches. The rock-wrens have learned to wait, and they are waiting now.

One of them, hanging from the edge of a basalt column where the city’s artisans have not yet cleaned the lichen from the stone, raises its head and sends a thin, clear call into the air. The sound is not a boundary whistle or a claim to hollow. This is a question—minimal, economical—as a question must be in the thin air of the plateau.

The answer does not come from the stone.

III.  
The Still-Wallers are slower to understand.

Their first thought is not of absence, but of error. They wake in the colder blue of the morning, slide into the narrow channels of the city, and check the thresholds of their seal-forms, fingers inspecting the stonework. The gutters are wet; the frost has been generous this night. The water should be running exactly as it has always run.

Yet when they bend to the ceramic bowls and hold out their bone cups to collect the night’s harvest, they notice that the water feels lighter than it should. It does not move the way a broth of life moves; it does not have the faint weight of unseen organisms suspended in it.

Some of the older city-makers bring the water to their lips and test it with the back of their throat, where they are accustomed to sensing density of nourishment and the sharpness of foreign minerals. They have been drinking water that has always been a little-bit-alive, full of small bodies that added to the body of the city.

Today it is simply… wet.

The city of Still-Walls is not used to miracles, and so they call it by the name that they have for anything that cannot yet be explained: “Turn in the rock.”

The phrase describes the moment when a column first reveals a hidden fracture line the artisans have expected to find, but also have not expected quite so soon. It is a word for both pattern and surprise, a way of saying: The stone has done something new; now we must learn what it means.

IV.  
The benevolent power listens to their name for it, and approves.

The next gift is even quieter.

The faint, pervasive smell of dampness and mold that has always been part of the city—like breath on cold glass—softens. The spores of mold and lichen and algae that rise with the fog from below are still carried up to the plateau, but they now meet a boundary they cannot pass. The air of the Still-Walls itself becomes, for the first time in a thousand years of pooled condensation and slow fiber, subtle. Clean.

The city of condensation still sweats and drips and shimmers with water. But now the water that beads along the carved channels has no taste of rot or hard dust. It is clear enough to mirror the skyline ribbon of sky above, and the spirals carved by the city-makers into the thresholds of their doorways.

The city of Still-Walls is not used to being able to see the sky in the water.

And yet it is.

V.  
The second sign of presence is not absence, but a shift of sight.

The rock-wrens are the first to notice it as well.

The thin air of the plateau has always been clear to them, but they have never seen the horizon of the world below the cloud deck as anything other than a continuous white rim of fog. On the day of the second gift, one of them—the same bird that asked the question of the air—leaves its column face and drops from the edge of the plateau as usual, seeking the rising thermal of air from beneath the cloud sea.

It is a routine of existence: fall from the edge, vanish into the cloud, bank on the hot column of air that rises from the world below, ride the sky back to the city.

This time, as the bird drops into the cloud, it feels not a blind wetness but a sudden clarity of vision.

The cloud around it is still there, droplets of moisture suspended in cold air, as they have always been. But somehow, the bird can see through them—not as far as the lowlands below, but far enough to see the sky outside the cloud. The shapes of distant clouds become clear, as if they were drawn with thin ink on transparent paper.

The downdraft of cold air that the bird always pushes into is now gentle. It is not gone, but softer, more like a suggestion than a force. The wren can ride the edge of the cloud rather than fight through its heart, and its energy is not spent in the scramble of ascent through humid air.

The bird rises from the cloud like a stone drawn upward, and when it emerges from the white it can see the city of the Still-Walls from farther than it has ever seen it before: the pillars like a ring of broken teeth around the rim of the world; the small figures of Still-Wallers moving on the ledges and in the opened channels like careful shadows; the faint glint of ceramic bowls set by the thousands into the stone.

For the first time, a rock-wren sees the city of the stone-walls as a whole.

And then, just as the bird thinks to turn back to catch the next thermal of air from somewhere else, another breath of clarity comes over the cloud deck.

It is not a breaking of cloud. It is not a clear day of low sky. The mist of the world below still lies in its thick band around the plateau rim, as the resident of Still-Walls has always known it.

But now, in places of the sky-plane that are not many but not few either, thin columns of air become clear as glass shafts through the fog, as if the sky itself had grown pillars.

The bird of Still-Walls does not have words for what it is seeing, but its mind is sharpened by the need of survival in stone and sky. It recognizes the shape of the column from the geometry of basalt; from the ring of sculpted stone around the people of the city; from the way the world is built on vast repetition of simple pattern. The bird sees the clear sky-shafts as a kind of column, and they feel like invitation.

The new columns of clear air are not straight up from the cloud deck below. They tilt at odd angles, like the storm of winds that rushes around the plateau edge, but calm and guided as if they were made by hand.

The bird of Still-Walls turns into one of them.

The air of the column is not warmer than the air of the rest of the sky, but somehow it is easier. The density of the world below the cloud deck feels like it is trying to rise up through the clear column and support the bird’s wings from below, instead of resisting the bird’s slow, careful heart.

It is the first time that a rock-wren has been able to glide, rather than climb with the hard flapping of small wings. To the bird of Still-Walls, gliding feels like being allowed to stop listening for the next fault of wind to fall into, and instead to feel the stone of the sky gradually lift itself beneath you.

The clear column does not carry the bird to a new world of hot lowlands and loud insects. It is not meant to. Such a world would be foreign to the heart of the Still-Walls, and the benevolent power does not want to change the heart, only to support it.

The column curves the bird of Still-Walls into a higher ring of sky above the city, where the air is thin enough to make the heart of a bird pay extra, but not so thin as to cause the bird to fail. The column of clear sky lets the bird look down at the city from the zenith of its world, and to see the pattern of the Still-Walls as a ring of stone columns around the inner flat of the plateau, where the cloud bank is worn like a ring of cloud around the rock of the world.

The bird of Still-Walls has never seen the city from the center of the sky.

When it comes back to the columns of stone, its wings are not as heavy with the weight of hard air and thin fear of failing as they have always been. The climb of sky from the cloud deck to the city is still long, but now it is something that can be done without taking the risk of the heart.

For the rest of that day, the clear sky-columns appear to the other rock-wrens of the city, as if they have been carved by the sky in the same way the city-makers have carved the rock of the world.

The birds learn of them slowly. They test the air of each column with the thin slice of their wings, measuring the way of the world by the subtle of motion and the sound of air. The column of clear sky is not to be trusted the first time it is seen, but by the end of the day the word of the city of birds—the message passed from one to another by call of boundary and claim of hollow—has been changed:

The world of the Still-Walls has been given a new form of stone.

VI.  
The Still-Wallers are slower to discover the sky-columns.

The change of sight of the world below is not to be seen from the channels of the city, where the sky is only a thin ribbon of bright above the pillar heads. But from the edge of the plateau where the cloud deck of the world below is thick, the new sight of the sky can be seen by the ones who are willing to look down from the rim.

The first to see is a child of the Still-Walls.

She is not small by the standards of the city of condensation, but not large by the standards of the lowland world either. The people of Still-Walls are used to the cold, and their heart is not cold.

The child is called “the first of the new column of the year”—because she was found in a column that had just been opened by the city-makers of that time, and the first water of the new channel of the world had been allowed to flow into the basin of her birth.

To her people, she is simply a new slip of stone of the world.

She is making the long climb down the carved steps of the edge, where the city-makers of generations before have opened the fault of the scarp to the world of the cloud. The city of Still-Walls is not used to climbing down to the cloud deck, but the child of the column remembers the rock of the world clearly and her feet are sure on the wet stone.

The fog of the cloud deck is thick as she goes down, but at the last of the steps—where the city’s path of stone disappears into the white—the benevolent power of the world comes to her with a second gift of sight.

Just as the cloud was not broken to the eye of the rock-wren, it is not broken for the child of the Still-Walls either. The white of the world below is still there, dense and full of water of low cloud. But the clear columns of sky that the rock-wrens of the city have been riding into the air of the city of stone also now appear to her eyes, as if they had been carved from the sky itself.

The child of the column of the year puts a hand out to the cloud deck of the world below and sees the air of the world as clear glass of sky in the shape of a wide pillar, where before there was only fog.

The fog of the world below is not to be touched by the people of the Still-Walls; the city of condensation is not used to being able to walk into the cloud. But now the child can see that the clear columns of sky are not of the world of the lowlands; they are of the world of the sky, and they are not of stone of the world.

She is not of the world of the lowlands; she is of the world of the Still-Walls, and she walks the stone of the world of stone pillars.

The child of the new column of the year looks into one of the clear sky-columns and sees the cloud of the world below as if she were looking down from a height of the city of the sky, rather than from the edge of the city of the stone. The shape of the world below is not clear to the eye of the child; but the clear column of sky of the world of the Still-Walls has been carved to show the child of the city of the stone of the world that the world of the cloud is not of the Still-Walls, but of the world of the sky.
