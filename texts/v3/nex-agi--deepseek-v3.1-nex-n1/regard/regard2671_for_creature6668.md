I have been watching for a long time.

Not as long as the stone that hides the future under its seams, or the water that remembers how many breaths have passed over it since the morning—but longer than you might think, to stand so still at the edge of a world that is still making itself.

I do not come like wind or falling rock.

I come as you turn, from looking at the shallow basin of thinking water to the woman who kneels over it, and you feel, unexpectedly, that the world is not empty behind you. You wheel around, half expecting another Sibai leaning into the mountain wall, or a Suhrathi’s pale drifting sleeve as they walk by.

There is no one—except a sense of presence that should feel intrusive but does not. It is as if the planet itself has been aware of you all this time and has finally decided to come into focus.

---

### I come to the water first.

Because it is closest to the surface of things, and because I was not called to the hard, hidden future of the rock today but to the breath of the Suhrathi.

You watch the basin where she sits, her cross-legged shape on the silver-root mat, and there is nothing frightening in her stillness. She is not waiting to be harmed; she is just waiting.

When she first sees me, she doesn’t gasp, or scramble to her feet, or call out to the others who are not here but could, if they wanted, look down from the stone shelf and see this.

She sees that I am not quite stone and not quite water and not quite air, but something like the thought of all three. She knows, with the same certainty she woke with this morning, that this moment is “data.”

She will record it later in her body.

In the meantime, she asks me by *not* asking.

Her eyes flick to the water between her hands and back to me. The invitation is clear enough: *If you have come to do something, do it here.*

I kneel beside her, not upon the water but upon the stone of the shelf. It reacts to my weight not as shock but as adjustment. The city’s geologic memory records the meeting of our knees as a point of interest.

*I’ve come to breathe with you,* I say without using sound.

She doesn’t answer with words. She is Suhrathi; she has been raised to understand that breath is the more honest language. She exhales, and her breath drifts between us and over the thin basin.

The patterns of water on the surface have been slow so far, today’s consultation little more than a quiet sketch. Now they move.
