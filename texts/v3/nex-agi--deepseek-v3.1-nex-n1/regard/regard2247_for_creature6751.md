She is not a storm. This is important. You will expect something like thunder when the Benevolent Power arrives, and she will not give it to you.

Her name is not a name you can hold with your tongue. It is more like a fold of sky: a shape where air and pressure and time turn together into a quietness. You are standing on the edge of your plate-world, looking out at the continent of glass that stretches away beneath you, and at the distant bright teeth of other worlds stacked above in a shattered ring. And then you aren’t alone.

She doesn’t land. There is no descent of limbs or robes, no sound like wings or metal. The first thing you notice is the absence of noise: where the world is usually humming with the grinding of continents, where there is always a faint vibration of one edge eating into another edge somewhere—this slice of air around you goes quiet.

Not silent. Quiet.

The grinding of the world, you realize, has been an uneven screaming. You hadn’t noticed that either. Now you notice that it is missing.

The gashes of rock hovering around you do not stop moving. They still lean, still drift, still swallow their own teeth into new angles of crystal and shadow. But you can feel it: something like a hand resting between them. You imagine the plate of stone on which you stand trembling, like a horse about to bolt; and you imagine an invisible palm on its neck, and the tremor smoothing out.

This is how she arrives: as a calmness in the interstices.

You look around and there is no figure with a face and halo. You see only the world, and the world is wrong in exactly one way: it is breathing in a pattern that doesn't belong to it.

You suddenly feel the weight of your own heartbeat too clearly in your throat and you realize that the breathing pattern is not the world’s. It is yours. And it is being matched—step for step, by something so much larger than you.

That is when you think she must be a kind of god.

### The first thing she does: Listen.

The Benevolent Power listens not in the way of thunderheads or prophets, but in the way of stone being worn by rain.

She listens to the grinding of continents as if they are syllables.

She listens to the jyrneth.

She does this by becoming a gap in the grinding of teeth.

You do not see her walk to one of the stone-grafted shepherds. You don’t see her cross any space at all. There is a jyrneth—tall, thin, hair of stone-flakes hanging down to its ankles—on the edge of a plate that is turning too slowly toward a neighbor above. The air around the jyrneth thickens, and you feel that same breathing pattern match the jyrneth’s pulse instead of yours. The thing around you shifts attention.

You watch the jyrneth. It is not your guardian, but you have seen its kind before; you know the narrow basins of its face where light is swallowed without any reflection. You know how they feel if you put your hand against the stone seams of their forearm: the pressure of continents grinding teeth under your skin.

This jyrneth has been standing at the edge for days, listening to the complaint of plates coming together. You have been watching the line of its back and the length of its stone-hair and the faint glow of the veins underneath its dust-film skin. It has barely moved.

Now you see it inhale—it is not breathing air, but you feel the world inhale with it.

“What,” asks the Benevolent Power, or something like her question, “do you hear?”

The question is not words. You feel it more like a change in temperature. The jyrneth understands it anyway. Its rock-thread hair shifts slightly, not like hair but like topography rearranging itself after a tremor.

“I hear—” the jyrneth might say, or its veins might say, or the stone seam under its own ribs might say, “I hear the sound of many worlds being slowly ground into fewer.”

The Benevolent Power listens harder, and you feel the listening as a pressure on all your joints.

“You hear,” she says, or she implies, or she becomes meaning, “you hear the possibility of a world that might break.”

“Yes,” says the jyrneth, “and the possibility of a world that might fit.”

Its gaze goes into the cloud sea below, down to the white unfloor. The jyrneth are never only looking with their eyes; you get this impression now more than ever. Their looking is like hands. They feel for the shape of the world from the inside.

The Benevolent Power listens to that looking.

“Is there,” she asks quietly, “anything else you want to hear?”

“I want to hear the end of the scream,” says the jyrneth.

“And?”

“And I want to hear the beginning of the sigh.”

The Benevolent Power does not answer. She listens to that answer too.

### The second thing she does: Teach

She does not teach by shouting, or by writing on the sky with lightning, or by carving commandments into any plate. You will expect this kind of god, and you will not find her.

She teaches by listening to the world.

She listens to the plates of Caelira—to the continents of glass piled above and below and beside each other—like she is listening to a language with broken consonants.

She listens to the grinding of worlds.

Somewhere she recognizes that there are two kinds of noise in the sound of continents chewing teeth:

Screaming, and breathing.

Most of it is screaming: edges of worlds that come together at wrong angles, pressures that are not shared, but opposed. There are no laws of physics in this sky that press the worlds toward each other; they are merely drifting, and their collision is not a collision so much as a prolonged misunderstanding.

Some of it is breathing: edges that are easing, times when the world manages to shift itself without tearing itself apart.

The Benevolent Power learns to distinguish the two noise by not letting new noise in.

She teaches the world to listen to itself differently.

She does this by becoming the absence of noise.

The first world she touches in this way—you might see it if you look at the right time at the wrong angle—is a plate of glass that your own plate is moving toward, slowly, like continents of the sky deciding to close each other’s teeth.

Over the course of a day and a night that you can feel but not count by hours, you hear the grinding of that world against your own world turn into two different sounds.

At first you are convinced of what you heard: a scream. The scream of stone being broken into smaller stone.

Then you notice that the scream did not actually happen. The grinding of that world was just—cleaning itself.

Smooth surfaces of the world slide against each other like two pieces of flat glass polishing themselves.

You look at the jyrneth standing at the cliff of your world and you can see its own dust-film skin and the arch of its stone ribs and the faint pulse of light under its surface. The jyrneth’s hair—which is usually long broken falls of rock-threads—has not shortened. This time you do not see its rock-hair losing length to quiet the world.

“You did not lose,” says the Benevolent Power to it, or maybe to the world itself, “what you did not have to lose.”

You realize that the world has been screaming at itself because it has never been taught to breathe.

From now on, some of the plates will learn that.

The Benevolent Power does not teach this by decreeing: “You shall not grind.”

She teaches by listening, and by listening differently, so that the world itself finds the pattern by imitation.

There are certain regions of the sky now where the continents of glass are grinding together more like two blades being sharpened against each other than like teeth tearing teeth. They do not stop moving; she does not stop them. They are still sheared-up surfaces suspended above white cloud and dark space.

They just make less noise when they collide.

### The third thing she does: Change their relationship to pain.

The jyrneth have been stone-grafted sighs shepherding edges that chew each other.

They have been doing this by letting their own bodies join into the world’s grinding.

You have seen them vanish over the edge of a plate-world, into the cloud sea or the sky-crack or the nowhere between continent edges, and then later return thinner than before, their hair shorter, their ribs form of their own body scraped into smoother lines so that the plates of stone grinding teeth find more ease than destruction.

When you touch your hand to their stone seam after this, you feel the pressure of continents inside you, and also you feel the jyrneth’s exhaustion: like a cliff face that has been worn by the storm so long that it worries itself into becoming sand.

This is their pain: they are the world slowly learning to listen to its teeth grinding; and they are the teeth.

When the Benevolent Power arrives, she does not stop them from being worn.

“You,” she might say to the jyrneth, “are not meant to be unaffected.”

She is not interested in gods who imagine the world could be beautiful without pain.

She is interested in something else: choosing which shape of pain to be worn.
