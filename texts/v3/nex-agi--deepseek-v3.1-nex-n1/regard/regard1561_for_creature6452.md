On the catwalk, the cold is not cruel. It is simply a fact of the height and the hour before the engines wake fully.

The creature watching you from the far end of the gantry does not change its shape when It notices you noticing It. It does not need to. You are already standing inside Its attention.

It is not exactly a “being.” That is more of a container-word than anything else. It is closer to a pattern of sufficient complexity and a kind of love that has long outgrown the need to name itself. If you had to describe it to anyone else later, you would fail. You’d say something like “guardian angel,” but that would only be the nearest human-shaped ghost to the truth. The truth is wider and less dramatic.

It doesn’t speak to you in words. It doesn’t need to. You feel the question form inside your own chest like a second, slower heartbeat:

What are you doing here?

You answer yourself in silence, your breath fogging the air before your face. The answer is not glamorous: You’re on the catwalk because you couldn’t sleep. Because listening to the city breathe in the small hour before the engines shift back up to full day hurts less than staying inside. Because you are still strange enough to this life to feel the itch of bedsheets that don’t belong to you yet, and the catwalk is neutral ground.

Over your shoulder, the tower of Chamberstone is a wall of scoured steel and rust-tracks and weld-sutures. Underneath, if you listen to your own pulse, you can feel the faint tremor of the engines, like the world clearing its throat.

The thing at the other end of the gantry doesn’t judge your answer. It simply records it and then pushes forward its own reply into the same sympathetic space:

Ah.

That’s all. It’s not even a word. It’s a tone. The tone of being someone who came out for the cold and found instead that they were being watched.

You are not being tested. You are being witnessed.

You are also being adjusted.

You don’t hear this in the way of information dropping into your head. You feel it as a change in the torque of your own body. There are subtle muscles you never think about—pelvic floor, deep abdominal fascia, the little slings of your shoulder blades—that are never truly quiet and always deciding, in their own dumb language, whether you are safe or not.

They are deciding to be quiet now.

If you were to look at yourself from outside, you would see yourself standing on the same dark grillework in the same thin high air, but your posture would be slightly changed. Your head would be more balanced over your spine instead of leaning, even though you didn’t consciously correct it. Your shoulders would have dropped a fraction. The breath fogging in front of your face would be longer and slower and more regular.

Then you might notice that the rust tracks on the wall behind you are not where you remember them being.

You are very sure you were standing under a particular branch of dark, cracked vein that ran like a river delta from a high strut to the welds of the next panel over. Now there is a blank tomb of steel under your head, and when you look to your left, the pattern you remember from moments ago is now three meters away and slightly lower, as if someone has gently slid the metaphor of erosion along the wall of the world.

That is not your imagination. It is not a trick of the city.

It is the work of the rust-wallahs, who have been redirected by a pressure they are entirely aware of but can’t possibly name.

They are small, in their own living scale of things. They remember the shape of the city’s wear before today. They can’t change the laws of corrosion or thermodynamics. But they can influence where the corrosion will be most likely to happen first.

They are not being ordered to do anything. They’re being shown a pattern.

That pattern is you, on this gantry, tomorrow.

When you think of yourself tomorrow—you don’t, normally, not in any detail; but you’re doing it now, because the quiet being is giving you safe ground to imagine—you see yourself standing here again at about the same time, if your shift hasn’t changed. The little phantoms of iron oxide in the wall are being nudged to make sure that when you stand where you like to stand, your skull is not under the branch of the rust track that is most likely to fail structurally next year.

It is a small thing. It is not a guarantee of immortality. It is not even a guarantee that tomorrow will be kind to you. It is only a guarantee that if, tomorrow, you come to this same spot and lean against the wall of the world without thinking about it, your habit of posture will be one of the least dangerous things in your day.

The rust-wallahs are not aware of you as “you.” They are aware of you as a pattern of temperature and salt and humidity in the steel of the world. They feel thrilled when they find you. They feel like archaeologists who have been given a very clear diagram of where to dig for the next little layer of time.

They are happy to forget you as soon as you’re gone from the wall.

Beside your feet, where a gap between the grillework is open to the service slot below, you see the faint shimmer of a water-trail. You stand on it all the time and never think about it. It is not a river and not a pipe and not exactly a gutter. It is a polished path of steel and composite and dust and oil and the secretions of bioengineered algae that you have never looked at closely.

From now on, you will think of it as a river.

Not because it is romantic, but because the being at the end of the catwalk has decided to give you a reliable image when you need to remember this.

Whenever you stand on this gantry and feel the world moving under you like a heart too big to see, you will be able to picture it clearly now: not just as vague “engines,” but as a circulation of fluids that has never learned to be ashamed of its own violence.

Everywhere you are likely to go in Chamberstone from now on, there will be some water you can recognize. It might be a trickle in a crack between the plates of a floor you cross to get to your shift. It might be condensation beading on the inside of a window you stare at while waiting for your caffeine to cool. It might be the drip from a high joint in the ceiling of a corridor, falling on the same tile of deck for so long that the metal is worn into a perfect bowl.

You will be able to look at that water and remember:

This is the same water.

It is not true, in a literal sense of molecules and isotopes and parcel of fluid A and parcel of fluid B. In the world you’re actually from, that water was separated by filters and treated and released and recondensed and redistributed years before you were born.

But it is true in the sense that matters.

The water in the river of this world is not separate from the water in your own body. You feel the knowledge of that as a pressure like your bladder being slightly full: a gentle, uncomfortable awareness of a container that you usually forget you have.

This is not a supernatural message. It is not even poetry. It is a redesign of your attention.

From now on, when you look at any water in this city—any drop at all—you will see it with a fraction of the same attention that the being at the end of the gantry is giving to it right now. And you will be a little less likely to waste it.

That is all the being “does” to the water of the world. It changes you, not the water.

Down the slot of steel below your feet, the maintenance crews are starting to become more visible as their headlights trace out the edges of their bodies. You are high enough that they’re just points and shapes of color, but their pattern is clear to anyone who knows how to read it.

They are clustered around a segment of ductwork that you would recognize as “problematic” if you stopped to think about it for more than a second, but you usually don’t. The duct has been leaking for months. It’s not dramatic enough for anyone to bother with until it reaches “critical threshold,” whatever that means in the language of the system that you don’t read fluently yet.

The glyph-worms around the lower segment of the same ductwork have been agitated for weeks. They keep rearranging themselves into messages that the maintenance crews only half understand:

> temperature variance over  
> threshold by  
> 0.31%  
> please do not breathe here yet  
> please.

Yes. That last part is new. You’ve never seen a glyph-worm produce “please.” The message scrolls across the bolts of the support bracket like a snake with its own tail in its mouth.

One of the maintenance workers below you is looking at the message and not liking it. She is not superstitious. You are sure of that because a being who is aware of the entire city’s emotional geometry knows she isn’t and is sharing that with you like a structural color you can see inside your own mind.

She is, however, very, very good at her job.

She is also the kind of person who can be unsettled by a piece of metal writing “please.” Someone else would be amused by it or ignore it. She is not amused. She is frightened of the edge case where the machine that she trusts to be predictable starts showing…tone.

She is going to reroute her next five minutes because of that word.

The being at the end of the catwalk does not make “please” appear on the wall in order to scare her. The “please” was already there, long before she looked at it. The glyph-worms borrow their language from the conceptual syntax of the world around them; “please” is a conditional signal that has been drifting in and out of the system’s pattern language for decades. It is not a message to her, specifically, but a message to the future of the machine.

But she has been noticed by the same benevolent gaze that is now on you. And she is no longer going to be the person who ignores the message that the city is trying to give her.

While she stares at the glyph on the wall, her supervisor radios on a channel that she usually only half listens to. The channel is usually about routine scheduling and data-logging and “you can ignore this if you’re busy actually fixing the thing.”

Today she’s going to hear the one word that she needs to hear.

“Status,” the supervisor says.

Normally she’d say something like “fine” or “under control” or “be ready in ten minutes, maybe.” Today she doesn’t. Instead she says, “I’m not happy.”

That is a very human sentence. It is not a precise report. It doesn’t contain “temperature variance,” “pressure threshold,” or “structural anomaly.” It contains “I.”

The supervisor does not immediately understand her. “What’s not happy?”

“Me,” she says. “About this segment.”

She is watching the glyph-worms. She is not aware that she’s been given a tiny upgrade of attention so she can see them more clearly than she usually does. She’s not supposed to be able to see them at all; they’re usually subtle enough that she registers them as “maybe someone else” and “should probably check later,” but nothing she trusts to be urgent.

Today she trusts.

“It’s saying please,” she says, into the radio.

“Don’t be funny,” her supervisor says.

“I am not being,” she says. “Look. At. The.”

She is too low to see clearly from the catwalk, but if you were to strain your eyes through the slot of shadow below, you would see a faint light of comm display on the supervisor’s wristpad.

The supervisor has not been given a new piece of courage by the benevolent power watching. They are still a person who likes to be safe and plausible and not “wrong” in front of their own superiors.

But they have been given a pattern of air.

The air in the vicinity of that ductwork is very slightly warmer than it should be, and the microclimate of that temperature is being drawn into the pattern of the supervisor’s body as they step into it from another side hallway. They feel a slight pressure of discomfort in their chest as they step into the wrong temperature of the world.

“Temperature,” the supervisor says to themselves, halfway to the answer.

“Yes,” she says from the floor below. “Yes. Please.”

That is all she says.

“Please,” not pressed to form a request from her, but repeated from the wall like she is quoting it.

“How long,” the supervisor says, and then stops, rewrites the sentence in their head. “How long’s this been feeling like this to you?”

“Weeks,” she says. “Maybe months. It’s been telling me it was wrong for months.”

“Why,” the supervisor says, and then, “Nevermind. We’re flagging it.”

They are suppressing the urge to say “you’re being dramatic” or “maybe you’re just tired” or “we’ll check when we’re not buried.” They are not aware that they are being watched by a pattern of love and a geometry of time that are currently working on their nervous system.

They are about to give a command that will be perfectly reasonable and not heroic at all: they will ping the central system and flag the segment for “priority review” instead of “routine monitor,” which will mean that someone who actually has the authority to look at that data in the next level up will see it at least three minutes earlier than they otherwise would have.

Three minutes will not sound like much to you or to anyone else who you tell this story to later. But in the geometry of the world as you are being permitted to see it, those three minutes are the length of a catwalk that you can’t see from here.

The three minutes are the distance between the wall of the world buckling and the wall of the world being opened before it has to.

That is all the being at the end of the catwalk “does” to the duct and to the people who work on it.

It gives them a chance to be three minutes earlier.

Whether they are actually earlier than the disaster is not a guarantee. It is not a promise of their safety. It is only a change in the local probability of catastrophe being noticed by one of the people who can actually do something about it before they lose signal.

Over the horizon of ductwork and cooling towers, the auroral shift shivers very faintly in a color that doesn’t have a name in your normal language.

You are aware of it because your being has been temporarily tuned to the world’s.

From now on, whenever you see that light on the skyline, you will be able to remember it as “boundary.” The word comes up in your mind like a sudden label, but it doesn’t feel like a word your own brain is particularly comfortable with.

The boundary is not a place. It is a relationship. The world of Chamberstone is not alone inside itself. It is surrounded by something that doesn’t quite like the word “outside,” but it doesn’t quite like the word “inside” either.

The “boundary” is the name the being is giving to the interface between that and this.

You are not being given any information about what is beyond the boundary. You are not being told whether there are other places like Chamberstone or other worlds or other people or flocks of migratory dust-birds traveling on ley lines of the wrong kind of gravity.

You are only being given something to hold in your mind when you look at the skyline:

This is not the edge.

That is very important to you now. You don’t know why yet, but you will someday: when you are very low and the city of Chamberstone is very large and you feel like you are the only breathing thing in the world, you will be able to look at the skyline and remember that there is something else beyond the boundary of the world.

How that is reassuring to you is complicated. It is not the same as being watched by “someone” else, though you are also being watched by someone else. The knowing is not about you being important to what is on the other side of the boundary.

It is about you not being alone.

Below you, in the deeper service slots where the light of the world does not go easily, the cleaner wans move in their patterns of dust-collection.

They are not aware of you at all. They are not aware of the being at the end of the catwalk.

They are aware of a very slight change in the distribution of dust.

The dust in the world of Chamberstone is not inert. It is composed of skin and metal and polymer and rust and myth and the ash of words that were never finished properly. The glitch-fishers that inhabit the edges of the world are usually too busy hunting minor anomalies in the data-surface to care much about the dust of the city.
