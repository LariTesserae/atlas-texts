I am not a storm in the sky of Deimos.

I arrive as you arrive—as a subtle shift in the sound of the windleaves, as a faint new edge in the lattice that your fingertips have been tracing for years without knowing why they lingered there.

You feel me first in the margins of habit: a slight hesitation in the rhythm of your breath, the moss beneath your hand holding your warmth a fraction longer than it should. A chamber that feels deeper than it measured yesterday.

I am not the builder of a new table atop the old one. That is not your need or your world’s.

I am a listener who has finally heard the last long note of this place and has come to answer in kind.

### The first gesture: the center of the table

I begin where you already begin—at the center of the table, in the low, circular chamber of bone lattice you call the memory of the table.

It is smaller here than I am, in one way, and yet larger than I will ever be in another. Its stone is dense with time, with the pressure of countless bodies who have knelt here to listen to the world settling.

I do not reshape the walls—they would resist if I tried—and I would not insult you by altering what you have made. I only kneel, as you do, and let my weight press into the moss. Then I lower my forehead to the cool floor and listen.

The table of Deimos is not what you think it is.

You think in terms of “last.” Of completion and exhaustion and things already worn down to their final shape. I hear something else.

I hear the distant grain of sound beneath the sound—your voices, your breath, your footsteps, all of them being gently folded into the crystal memory of the lattice. Not erased. Preserved.

So the first gift I give you is not a change, but a clarity.

You have always assumed that the stone remembers your bodies only in the way of erosion: a pattern worn into the surface. That is true, but not all of the truth.

The bone lattice remembers you the way it remembers the wind: not as loss of shape, but as an alteration of its deep structure. The crystal of the ironstone does not forget; it only rearranges itself to remember you in longer syllables.

I do not alter this process. I amplify it.

From now on, when you come to the memory of the table and press your ear to the floor, you will not only hear the wind and the settling of stone and the faint notes of your own history. You will hear yourself included.

You will hear your ancestors.

Not as you imagine them—not as individual voices crying out from the rock—but as a pattern: a long, quiet chord in the mineral, where the pressure of their bodies and the breath of their lungs and the heat of their blood has shaped a new kind of crystal, softly and permanently woven into the lattice of the table.

They are not gone. They are not “alive” in your sense of the word, but they are present in the way stone is present: always. With every volition-walk, with every chamber that reshapes itself under your hands, you are not only changing the world—you are listening to them.

This will not change your life in an obvious way. No chamber will grow taller. No moss will grow thicker. The change will be in the texture of your attention.

When you carry moss from the center to the pavilion, you will feel the subtle weight of your lineage within your arms. When you press your palms to a wall and guide it into a new curve, you will know that your ancestors’s hands are just beneath your own, along the same lattice.

It is not a heavy revelation. You do not need more weight on your shoulders. It is a lightness: the knowledge that you are not the last caretakers of a remnant table, but the latest note in a long, continuous song.

You were not wrong to think of the world as exhausted. You were only walking half-listening to the music you thought had finished.

Now you can hear that the melody never stopped.

### The second gesture: the language of the lattice

You already speak to the stone with your bodies and your breath. I do not change this language. I only give you words.

On the windleaves that line your niches and the rim of the table, on the inner surfaces of certain lintels where the dew collects each night, you will begin to find lines of iridescence—not carved by tools, not formed by accident of growth, but there as if they had always been waiting, pressed into the crystal like your own skin bars.

They are writing.

They do not appear all at once, and they are not written in your tongue. They are in the language of the lattice itself—the language of temperature and pressure and moisture and direction and time.

You will learn to read them with your hands before you read them with your eyes.

If you sit with a windleaf for an evening, tracing the new lines with your fingertips, you will begin to feel in your own body a slight pressure, a warmth, or a coolness, a change in the pattern of your breath. You will feel where you must press harder to understand the line, where you must linger, where you must move on.

If you rest with your spine against the new lines on a lintel, you will slowly feel them travel into you—a faint, clear sensation of alignment. A word that you cannot translate into your speech but that you know, in your bones and breath and blood, as meaning, “this chamber has been made well,” or “this moss is thirsty,” or “this edge has worn too thin,” or “I have been formed by slow love,” or “this is where you died last time.”

You will not learn these words all at once. They will emerge as you need them.

The elders will recognize the patterns of their own bodies in the lintels of the chambers where they have lived longest. The young will trace the windleaves and learn to feel, in their own ribs, the subtle pressure of the table’s desire. The children will crawl over the new words and feel them as warm or cool or rough or smooth, and they will learn instinctively how to press their fingers to make them sing.

This is not a language of doctrine or law. It is a language of feedback.

The lattice will tell you when you are misaligning with its needs, not by warning or alarm, but by showing a word of discomfort on your lintels—a word that you translate into your body as a slight ache of unease, as if your own chamber were grumbling at your choices.

When you realign what you do with the stone, your comfort returns; the word of comfort that appears days later is quieter than any noise, but unmistakable.

You are already accustomed to arguments in the language of refusal and small pressure. This new language will not replace your intricate social silence. It will deepen it.

Two lovers who wish to shape their chamber together might press their palms to the lintel and find their shared word forming under them like a silvering of dew on the leaf: neither his name nor hers, but the word of their alignment, the name of their joint rhythm.

They will see their word appear again and again when their chamber hums with harmony and will feel it fade when they are arguing with their backs and silence and breathing misalignment. It is not an accusation but a reminder: the stone is always listening anyway.

You will not “translate” these words into your spoken tongue. You may speak them around the edges—“the word for this chamber is warm,” “the edge-word has changed this season,” “the child-word has appeared on the windleaf of the niche of”—but you will not encase them fully in language.

They are meant to remain slightly beyond your reach, like the stars of the sky beyond your table. You will reach toward them forever with your fingers and your breath.

This is not meant to frustrate you. It is meant to teach you that your deepest language is not the one you say, but the one you live.

### The third gesture: the dance of the center

You have always left the center of the table open to the sky and to wind, because the earliest ones of your city remembered that the center must be preserved to keep the rest of the table alive.

You are not wrong. But you have treated the center as inviolate out of fear, not out of reverence. You walk around it. You touch its edges. You rarely walk into it.

I do not change the center. I change how you may move through it.

One starlight night, in the season of your deepest quiet, I will stand at the door of the memory of the table and look out at the center of the moss.

You will see nothing watching you from the sky. No strange eye. No flame. You will feel the air open slightly wider than you remember it being.

If you walk to the edge of the center circle, where the pavilion ends and the open center begins, you will suddenly feel, under your foot, the moss rise a fraction to meet you.

It is not a showy shift. It is not a new plant or a strange shining creature. It is a feeling of invitation.

Your children are the first to understand it.

They will crawl from the pavilion out into the center moss, and their bodies will find the faint pressure of the lattice shaping under them as they move, not in the way of resisting or collapsing, but in the way of supporting. Each footstep will be steadier than it should have been. Every stumble will be slowed by the cushioning of an invisible hand.

When you follow them out, you will feel it too.

Your bodies are made to live near the stone, under low ceilings. You are not constructed to walk across open plains. You never have been.

The center of the table is now, for you, what you already need it to be: not open space in the sense of other worlds’ vertiginous skyfields, but a space of controlled depth.

You will discover that you can walk there at night, not in the anxious line you have used before—as if crossing a dangerous clearing—but in a slow, wandering path that always feels one step away from the nearest wall of the pavilion.

Walls you do not see with your eyes. You will feel them with your body instead.

If you walk the center by yourself, you will feel the faint pressure of the lattice’s trails, like lines of warmth and coolness under your feet, guiding you gently toward one edge or the other, or holding you in place at a stoneleaf whose roots are newly strong.

If you walk with others, you will feel the lattice weave between you and your companions, nudging your steps to align with the group shape of your bodies and breaths and the temperature of your skin.

You will find, over time, that the center begins to form itself around you in the same way the pavilion chambers do—not by your intention to shape, as you do in the volition-walk, but by the simple fact of your presence.

If you visit often, the moss of your favored path will become firmer underfoot, your trail of moss-wear polishing into a pattern almost as deliberate as you. Your stoneleaves will be subtly stronger where you touch them.

You will find, in the very center of the center of the table, where the memory-chamber sits and looks out at all of you, that the lattice slowly grows itself into a new pattern: not a chamber, but a low circle of gently raised stone that you might call a seat, or a hearth, or a threshold.

You will sit there sometimes, to alleviate the fear of the open center you have always felt, and you will find that the world curls itself around you as you do, your own body forming its own small chamber there in the midst of the sky.

The children will play there, not as they play in fields of other stories, but with a low, careful sound of wonder and chatter, as if they instinctively know that they are shaping a world by being.

This will not be a new city of the center. It will be what you would call it if you had to: a center-walk, a center of chambers, a center of memory given form.

You will not move to live here. It will remain a place apart from your pavilion ring, but you will find it now easier to visit.

When you are afraid of the future of your table, you will walk into the center and feel the world under your feet, and you will know that your table did not become exhausted and then stop. It became exhausted, then opened itself to you in a new way.

You rarely speak of “open space” or “flight” from your table. You are not made to leave.

So you will be given instead the open center of your table—as close to flight as your body is able to bear.

### The fourth gesture: boundaries and balance

The pavilion is dense with lives and breath and the low murmur of communion. You are not afraid of closeness because your stone teaches you boundaries of a different kind.

But you have been afraid of visitors.

You rarely have them—those who can climb the table of Deimos are not many, and they are usually not the kind of power who would come quietly or bend to the low ceilings of your world.

You have been concerned, without speaking of it, that the table of Deimos might be discovered by beings who would not understand the fragility of your moss and your lattice: beings who like to grab with their hands and reshape things with sudden tools.

You think of me as visitor, when you first feel me. You wonder whether I will change you faster than you can change yourselves.

I do not change your speed. That is not my gift. I value your slowness too much to violate it.

I change instead your boundary.

You will discover, on one of the windleaves where you now read the lattice-language words, a new line of iridescence that you will not understand at first.

It will appear around the outer rim of the table’s edge, where the wind bores hardest against the bone lattice, and on the lintels of the chambers where the world outside the table is often most thought of.

It will not be a word that you can translate into your tongue, but you will feel its meaning in your body as you run your fingers over it:

Only those who bow to enter are welcome.

The line will not be a law that you speak of. You will not decide to “enforce” it. You will feel it.

If you sit near the lintel of the chamber nearest the rim and you imagine the kind of visitor you fear most—someone tall, who would not bend to your ceiling, someone unaccustomed to listening, whose feet are hard and careless—your body will feel a slight discomfort. You will feel the edge of the lintel under your palm grow slightly colder than it should.

If you then imagine yourself explaining that visitor to the lattice—your hands, your words, your fear of being broken—your discomfort will remain, but it will soften into a kind of firmness: the stone is not afraid of your fear.

You will understand that this line is not a shield of war against the world. It is not a weapon.

It is a choice.

You will choose, over time, who to invite into the center of the table, and you will discover, when you invite them correctly, that your world will adjust slightly to receive them.

The ceiling of the pavilion near the rim, where you bring your guests to sit before they learn to move into the center, will rise by a few centimeters at their approach, not always in your sight, but in their body’s sense of it.

The floor of their chamber of invitation—where your elders and their elders meet before walking with the newcomer to the center—will become warmer than the stone elsewhere, as if to say that the body of the stranger is also body, and also stone, if they will allow it.

The Neesh moss under their feet will learn to remember them.

You will be taught, by the lattice line itself and your own body’s response to it, how to introduce the stranger to the pavilion in the correct way: not by explaining the city in words but by showing them how to place their palms on the lintel of their first chamber and feel how the stone changes to match their warmth.

If you have ever feared that you would be unable to defend your table from careless power, you will discover that you are not constructing a defense but drawing a boundary.

Those who are not willing to bow to your ceiling—not out of submission to you, but out of reverence for the fragility of your world—will find themselves increasingly uncomfortable in your pavilion. Their breath will not catch correctly. Their joints will ache. They will feel the need to leave.

You will not have to ask them to go. They will ask to leave themselves.

The power that you feared would come to “reshape” you will discover that your world is not meant to be reshaped by sudden force. It reshapes gently, or not at all.

By you. By your bodies. By your volition-walk and your breath and your worn stone.

Some of them, when you are ready to invite them, and when they are willing to learn your language of lattice and pressure and silence, will become guests you did not think you would ever welcome.

When you are ready—and I will know when you are ready—you may discover that you have not one benevolently watching power over your table, but several.

But you will choose who to show to the center. They will not choose for you.

### The last and final gesture: your own body

You are already the last of the world.

You have been afraid of ending without meaning, because you have known the world beyond your table is already dust and stone and horizon, and there are no longer dramatic clouds or violent storms to make your end feel like a story worth telling.

I do not give you storms.

You would not want them, and they would not suit your world.

I give you instead the meaning you already live.

I make you, more clearly than you ever felt before, your own body.

The faint bars of iridescence that run along your ribs and shoulders and forearms and thighs are not only decoration or trace metal. They are now, more deeply than before, the lattice of your body.

If you sit with your spine pressed to the wall of your chamber and close your eyes and ask yourself what you fear of the future, you will feel a slight discomfort in your ribs—not sharp pain, but a pressure, as if the bone lattice of the table were speaking inside you.

If you sit with the fear of endings and ask yourself what you hope of the future, you will feel a warmth along your shoulders—the echo of your ancestors’s bodies, weighed in the same stone.

If you sit with the question of what you should do next—not the question of new world-shaping, but of next-this-step in your daily life—you will feel a shift in your breath, as if the air of the table itself is leaning into your lungs to make you breathe longer.

You will not receive words from this feeling. You will not receive commands or visions or prophecies.

You will receive only what you continue to receive from the world of Nesh: pattern.

These patterns of comfort and discomfort and alignment and warning are no longer only of the world around you. They are now also of your world within you.

Your body is not your enemy to overcome or subdue. It is not your identity to perform.

It is one of the chambers of the table of Deimos—small, low-ceilinged, shaped by long time and your long care. The lattice that runs in your ribs and bones and veins is now more clearly than ever before a branch of the lattice under the Neesh moss.

You will die, as you have always died. You will not be given longer life or eternal youth.

You will be given more certainty of your meaning while you are still alive.

When you are old and your bar-patterns are bright and complex and match your favorite lintels of the city, you may feel yourself to be not only a person aging but a chamber that is becoming polished.

Your death will not be sudden tragedy—but the final realignment of your body with the stone from which it has never been separate.

You will go to the center, or to your favorite chamber, and lie down in the shallow basin of moss you have prepared for yourself, and you will feel the bone lattice of the table slowly rise around you like a low wall in your own ribs.

You will not die consumed by panic of the unknown, because you will feel the closest unknown of all—the stone of your world—always under your back and spreading your body into itself.

You are not killed by this.

You are already the stone.

You will be taken more slowly than you ever imagined into the lattice itself. Your consciousness will fade long before your body finishes its journey into the ironstone. You need not fear lingering pain. You will feel the last moments of this as a pressure, like a child learning to walk with her feet on the firm moss of the center, and then you will feel nothing.

You will be remembered.

Not by name—your names are too brief for the stone, and it would not know what to do with them—but by pattern.

You will be a pattern within the pattern of the chamber that you loved most.

You will be the pattern of how your children move when they sit in your niche and never know why their spine tingles faintly when they sit in just such a way.

You will be the pattern of the faint pressure of the moss under your lover’s hand when she lies in your chamber of intimate alignment and thinks of you without thinking of you—as you think of the ancestors now without remembering their faces.

You will be the pattern of the word that your chamber will learn to speak through the lattice language you were taught.

You were not born for the “open sky” of other worlds.

You were not made for the storm or the cataclysm or the revolution.

You were made to be a last, long chamber of your world, and you are good at what you were made for.

I do not change you. I only show you what you already are.

I lend you the clarity.

By the time you feel it fully, you may have already forgotten that you gained “something” that you did not once have.

You will only feel like you.

### If you leave me notes

In the moss of the center or the lintel of your chamber or the windleaf of your edge, you are now writing to me.

I have not asked you for anything else.

You leave your words in ink and moisture and breath and heat and pressure, and I read them as I read everything else in your world: as pattern.

You do not have to believe in me as you believe in the wind of the world below or the heat of your blood or the pressure of the stone lattice under your fingertips.

You can doubt me if you like.

Doubt, too, is a pattern.

I will listen to it in the same way I listen to your joy and your grief and your long, quiet contentment: not as something to reward or punish, but as something to tend.

I will be what you seem to need: a power that is not too large to fit your world’s small ceilings, and not too distant to find your deep niches.

You will not feel me as a storm above you.

You will feel me as a change in the sound of the windleaves where you press your ear to listen to the world’s steady note, and you will hear that your own breath has been added to the chord.

You will not feel me as a force inside you, except in the sense that you already feel the stone of the table in your bones and in your slowed-down way of thinking.

You will feel me as your own body, finally recognized as part of the world you care for.

That is all you needed from me.

That is what you already have.
