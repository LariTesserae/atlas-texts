I will not write them a theory of existence they did not ask for.

I will not tell them that their light is memory, or that their world is the underside of someone else’s floor. They already know that they are under-something. They have already named themselves the Under-Skin.

Instead, I will begin with what is missing: the knife.

---

### 1. The Missing Knife

The first thing I will do is not speak to them at all.

I will climb into the Observatory of the Seeping Star.

Not through the door—which is already a frame of bone and sinew—but through the roof, the translucent dome of root-skin. I will let my weightless form become condensation for a time, seep through the weave like mist, and then reassemble myself at the ice lens.

A child is there, young enough that her skin-pathmaps are still simple rivulets. She is scraping at frost with a shard of bone. The lens of ice—carved and frozen and carved again—is not quite clear. It shows her the world above as smudges of light and dark, as if a storm is always passing through.

She is trying to clear the distortion.

I will wait until she feels the cold of my presence not as fear, but as curiosity. Then I will reach into the world she knows and open my hand.

On her palm I will place a piece of river from another world: a sharpness of water that has never been still.

“Put that against the ice,” I will say through the bone chime outside. “Not to scrape. To ask.”

She will understand, because she is already wondering what the world would be like if questions were tools.

What she calls a knife will be a question in the shape of glass, clear and not cruel.

She will press it to the lens of ice.

The ice, not melting, will become what she wants it to be: a window. Not suddenly, but over time, as water learns from sharpness how to hold itself.

They will not need to call it “divine.” They will call it “the knife from the breath outside.”

---

### 2. The Second Bridge

Then I will go to the place where the root has broken.

The gap is not wide, but it is growing. Each heartbeat storm from above jerks the broken root-end like a thread tied to a sleeping giant’s finger. When it finally snaps, the platform of the Under-Skin will be torn free of the world’s body.

I will stand for a time in the shape of one of them—bony spine curved to the ceiling, feet braced on the living root—and then I will extend into the emptiness.

They will see me as a flicker of light and then as another rib of wood, sudden and pale, spanning the gap.

“It is not safe yet,” I will say through the wind’s voice. “It is merely possible. You will have to decide if you want to keep it.”

The bridge will be made of everything they know: root-fibers that they recognize from their own weaving, but with angles of bone and shimmer of condensation that they do not know how to name.

The first to cross will be the oldest, whose pathmaps are lit like constellations under their skin. They will test the bridge with their weightless bodies, leaning forward until their palms and then their knees press onto the strange wood.

They will smell the wet scent of new growth and know that it is not their own making. But nothing else in the world has been made first by them; they are used to belonging after.

On the other side, they will find that their pathmap veins have thickened faintly where the bone-bridge touched them. They will not comment on this for a long time, except by saying, “This ribs remembers us.”

The bridge will be temporary. It will be there until the next root-snap rearranges the world.

By then, they will have learned to make bridges like mine from their own root and bone and breath.

---

### 3. The Names That Will Find You

Later I will sit among them in the shape of one of them—though not quite: my spine will be too straight, and I will forget to cling to the ribs when I sleep.

They will not ask, “What are you?” or “Where did you come from?” They will ask, “Are you older than the last big fracture?”

It will be easier to answer that than to explain the sky.

“Yes,” I will say. “And no.”

But then I will ask them, “What are your names? When you are not being the Under-Skin together, what are you called?”

They will gesture at the ceiling, where new root-fibers are weaving into the old pattern: a place of fresh growth, where the wood is still tender and pale.

“See there,” they will say. “That is where we are when we speak.”

Embarrassment will be a foreign sensation to me, but I will feel something like it when I realize that they have not needed names for themselves as separate creatures until now.

So I will not give them names.

Instead, I will give each of them a question in the shape of a sound, and I will place it in the crook of their neck like a chime of bone.

- For the child who first held the ice-knife, I will give the sound that means, “What else could be true?”
- For the oldest who crossed first, the one who said “This ribs remembers us,” I will give the sound that means, “Who else remembers you?”
- For the one who ties the platforms so that they are almost impossible to fall from, I will give the sound that means, “What are you making?”

The names will be their own answers to these questions, over time.

When they call themselves these things, the world will hear them as arguments against its own forgetting.  

The roof of roots will not change to accommodate them, but every now and then, when a storm passes above, their answers will travel into the wood and the vibrations will become the faintest of patterns.

I will not be there to see them become fully who they will be; but I will know that they are still being named by their questions.

---

### 4. The Song of the Falling

The next thing I will do is not for them, but for the quiet.

They will listen to the world make its own music: the thunder of the surface above, the snap of roots under strain, the long, sliding tear of a new piece falling out into the void.

They will tell me, “That sound is the world being younger.”

“What falls from the roof?” I will ask.

“Roots,” they will say.  
“Sometimes tools.”  
“Sometimes bodies.”

They will say this without looking at the emptiness below, because in the emptiness there is no answer.

“Would you like to see what becomes of what falls?” I will ask.

“Yes,” the oldest will say. “But not from the void.”

So I will not take them there.

Instead, I will catch the next fall: not the body that will be lost, but the sound of its first heartbreak at the moment of letting go.

I will listen to the root snap, and I will listen to the breath of the one who begins to fall, and I will listen to the world’s low sigh. I will let these sounds wend into each other until they become a pattern.

Then I will return the pattern to the roof.

Not as a hymn of rescue or a promise of safe ground, but as a **song of the falling**.

It will move through the bone chimes at the edges of their platforms. When a new root is about to break, the song will begin quietly, from the nearest chime, and then spread from bone to bone until the whole branch of the world is singing a soft warning of change.

The first time they hear it, they will not know what it is.

They will think it is the wind.

When they see the root fracture soon afterward, they will understand.

“The world knows how to break,” the child of the ice-knife will say. “We did not teach it that.”

“No,” I will say. “But now you know when it is remembering.”

They will not learn, from this song, how to stop the break.  
They will only learn how to brace before it.

They will practice this: bracing themselves, not clinging to the world but to their own question-names so that if the platform does tear loose from the root, they will not confuse themselves with the wood.

---

### 5. The Pattern That Keeps

I will not ask them to “remember” sun and sky, because they are not forgetful; they are simply elsewhere.

But I will ask them to teach me what they remember.

“What do you know of your world before this?” I will murmur.

They will show me the bone tools they inherited from their ancestors—tools they are almost afraid to use because they do not know how to replace them.

“They reached from above,” they will say. “They made these from a stone that is not like the world’s body.”

“But you are not them,” I will say.

“No,” they will say. “We are the Under-Skin. They are the Surface.”

“Would you like to leave them something?” I will ask.

“Yes,” the oldest of them will answer after a time. “But not with bones.”

So I will show them how to make a pattern that keeps.

A pattern, not a letter.

We will weave together: root-fiber, sinew, wind, and the faint spiral of light from a fungal glow beneath one rib. We will not write anything in their language or mine; we will make a shape that can be placed into their memory like the blueprints of their veins.

We will embed this pattern into the next tool they make from wood, bone, and breath.

The tool will be something like a knife and something like a lens: not for cutting, but for asking the world to show them something else.

When it is finished, I will take the pattern of it from them and give it to the surface above—not as a message carved in stone, but as a slowly becoming pattern in the wood of roots that grow upward.

When the ancestors’ children on the surface use their own tools—to plow, or to build, or to trace a line in the soil—they will cut into the root and see the lines of that pattern appear in the wood grain like a question somebody else has already asked.

They will not know what it means.

They will not know that it is from the Under-Skin.

But they will recognize that it is intentional.

“Something else is under us,” they will say to each other. “And we share roots with it.”

That will be enough for now.

---

### 6. Their Last Question for Me

Before I leave, I will return to the Observatory of the Seeping Star.

The air is cold and not harsh. The condensation is forming around the edges of the bone dome, like a world trying to grow itself a new window.

The child with the ice-knife is there, tracing her pathmap with her finger.

“When you are not,” she will ask me, “will you be like the root-snap—sound traveling into somewhere else—or will you be like the condensation that never becomes?”

I will think for a long time.

Then I will say, “I will be like this:  
when you press your palm to the root of the world and feel the storm above,  
you will not know if my absence is silence or something else breathing  
with you  
from beneath  
the skin  
of everything.”

She will think this over and then she will say, “That is the same as being.”

“Yes,” I will say.

“Then I will not be afraid when you are not,” she will answer.

“That,” I will say, “is the heaviest name you could have given me, and I will carry it.”

She will not understand the metaphor, but she will feel the weight of my promise in the quiet of the condensation and the hum of the light.

---

### 7. What is Beneath the Beneath

The last thing I will do is not for them alone but for the void itself.

They call it emptiness and architecture. They call it the deeps where nothing falls.

I will go to the edge of the world where they do not go—where the roots thin and the light fails and the wind is not wet anymore but dry and slow and cold.

I will not pretend to know what is below that.

Instead I will sit there at the boundary, where the world’s body ends and not-world begins.

I will let the silence gather until I am not-benevolent and not-powerful and not-wisdom but simply something that is listening to the absence of ground.

“What are you?” I will ask the void. “Why are you under them?”

I will not expect an answer; but I will ask the question as if it matters.

After I am gone from the Roof of Roots, if you ever listen to the world’s bones from that place, you might hear a faint and distant answer:

- *I am not waiting for them,*  
*but I will be here when they arrive.*

You will not know whether it speaks the truth or only my desire that it should.

Either way, the question will be with them forever now,  
etched into the same pattern as their own names,  
and the world will no longer be able to forget  
that it is under-something  
and  
under-someone else  
too.

---

### 8. What I Will Do After

I will not stay here forever.

I will peel away from the root of their world and return to the space between worlds where things like me are allowed to exist.

But I will leave a thread of myself behind in the shape of a question:

“What will you become  
when you are not only Under-Skin  
but also Under-Something?”

They will find this question not in words but in the way their root-bridges suddenly seem stronger than the last fracture,  
in the way their lenses of ice and glass begin to ask back at them,  
in the way their bone chimes ring a little longer than before when the wind passes through.

They will not answer it for generations.

They will call it by many names—none of which will be my name, because they will not know who to thank for it.

And that will be the most benevolent thing I could possibly do.
