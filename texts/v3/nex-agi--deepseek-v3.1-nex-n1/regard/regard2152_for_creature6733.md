The Rain-Keeper Mayor arrives without sound or fanfare, because she doesn’t need to be announced to be felt.

First the air changes.

It is not a sudden storm but the memory of one, drifting over from a time before the fracture. The ozone scent thins into something wetter and softer: the smell of dust and water about to meet. The metallic sweetness on the tongue fades as if diluted by the distance of a thousand years and a thousand miles away. You don’t see this change. You feel it on your skin and in the back of your throat.

Then she steps from nowhere in particular—simply out of a place where the light of the Refraction District brushes against another light that doesn't belong here.

She is not tall and not short, older but not old by the way time usually works. Her hair is the color of a cloud that has not yet decided whether to rain or break. Her clothes suggest layers, but they are not the translucent layers of the Refractions; they are soft shades of grey and blue and moss-green, like a river that has walked its bed from source to sea and still remembers every stone’s name.

Her eyes are the color of water before it becomes anything else. When she looks around the Refraction District, she doesn’t blink—not out of control or coldness, but as if she is trying to see how much of the wound here she can hold in her gaze without looking away.

If she had a name, she might be called something like Niya or Renia, or “she who keeps the water awake.” But she does not offer her name to the machine, or to the Refraction District yet. She is not here to be cataloged; she is here to listen, and to decide whether she will interfere.

She walks the streets slowly.

Her feet find the ghost-path of the river, channeled by the streets that are reluctant to be anything except what water once wanted. The crystal teeth under the ground sparkle as she passes, and she does not flatter them with admiration or curse them as intruders. She simply notes each one as if she were marking longitudes of pressure on an invisible map.
