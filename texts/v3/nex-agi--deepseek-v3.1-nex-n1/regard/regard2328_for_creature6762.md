The first thing they do is nothing.

No thunderclap of revelation, no pillar of light stabbing down through the haze to the waterworks. No fiery runes appearing on the limestone dam. The power that notices Ruispor is not one for dramatic entrances; it is old, and it knows how cities learn to fear what arrives from above.

Instead, it listens.

From the salt edge where the river leaves its valley and spreads out into the sea, to the lonely notch of the dam and the quiet spread of the Plain of Mirrors above it, the being turns the focus of its attention and simply *listens*—not only to the sound, but to the shape of it. The engineers of Ruispor are proud of how they know the valves and mains by the pitch of their whistle or the ache of their pipe-songs; this being listens to the city the way they listen to the water—patiently, as a kind of text.

It hears the grindstone hum of turbines and the thump of the compressors. It hears the drop hammer’s heartbeat crash and the faint distant *thunk* of shingle rolling under the river’s tongue. It hears the water hammers knocking in the mains, like knuckles rapping on the city’s ribs from the inside. It hears the soft, almost secret drip of weeping walls and the scissoring of the heron’s bill as it cleans itself in the reed-choked margins of some canal.

Most importantly, it listens to the *density* of the sound—where the city speaks, and where it goes silent.

It is most interested in the basements.

For the length of a human breath, its attention narrows, like a lens tightening down to the steam-obscured glass of a factory skylight or the damp map of a new leak on a ceiling.

It is not particularly impressed by Ruispor’s efficiency—the way the city draws power from river and stone and fat black coal, how it starches the linen of its citizens with the heat of its furnaces and orders the release of pressure from its valves with the precision of a clock. The being of light and softness has seen cities like that before.

What is interesting to it is the *persistence*.  

Ruispor is not exactly thriving; it is not terribly beautiful; it is not even particularly kind. But it has endured. The labor of the workers, the weary ingenuity of the engineers, the stubbornness of the children who still play games near the oily catch-basins—this is the city’s quiet answer to the hammer of noise and water that strikes the valley from all sides.

And then, under the soundscape the city knows, there is the *other* sound—the one that nobody in Ruispor has a name for except in vague terms of “pressure” and “phantoms.”  

Behind the wheeze of the mains and the drip of the weeping walls, there is a kind of slow, disgruntled murmur: the plateau’s echo of the city’s violence. The water table is not truly “erratic”; it is *disgruntled*. The limestone does not “respond” like a machine; it is *being pushed*.

To the benevolent power above the valley, this undernote of pain and strain stands out as clearly as the smell of an oil slick in a clear puddle.

By the time anyone notices anything has changed, the changes are already small and old enough to be mistaken for ordinary.

The first is not a change at all, but a *relocation* of something.

In the limestone’s last secret caverns beneath the civic terrace—the ones the engineers seldom enter, because the air inside is thick and warm and the sound of water dripping in the dark is far more insistent than anyone wants to admit—there is now a new fault.

No one feels the subtle shift when it happens; no one hears the faint whisper of stone sighing as a new seam of weakness is opened, a new line drawn from the deep, angry water of the plateau to some point closer to the surface. The change is not large enough to be a disaster, but large enough to be a *choice*.

On that first day, nothing dramatic happens to match the shift.

The fault simply joins the lower water table to the upper, and—crucially—to the *river*.

Before, the pressure phantoms were like a mob of angry hands trapped beneath a floorboard, trying to find any way out at all. Now, one of the hands has been offered a smaller door, but a *clear* one: a way for water to move from the bowels of the plateau to the river channel without going through the basements of houses or the cellars of mills.

The pressure has not been bled away—it is too much for that—but the highest of its mad edge has been taken.

On the same day, in the same silent breath of attention, something else is changed.

Not the world of stone and water, but the world of *mind*.

The children of the lower terraces come home from the riverbanks where they’ve been skipping stones across the oily shallows and find, without noticing why, that they are *listening* differently.

It is not a compulsion. There is no voice in their head telling them what to hear. It is only a quietness in the noise of the city, a kind of openness that they do not have words for.

A little boy who was usually first to throw pebbles at gulls or drop things into the catch-basin to see the rainbow oil spread out now instead sits very still on his front step, his hand halfway to the next stone in the pile, and his mother passing him has to say his name twice before he hears her.

“What are you listening to?” she asks finally, when he doesn’t answer to “are you deaf?”

He chews his lip.

“The pipes,” he says finally, surprised at his own answer.

But she hears the hum of the factory and the distant hiss of a valve from down the street, and she slaps at her arm as if midges have landed, and goes inside.

On the next day, when she sits at the table, she finds herself listening to the same sound she has been listening to her entire adult life—and for the first time she *recognizes* the pattern.

Underneath the rumble of the factory compressors and the grind of the overhead conveyor, she hears the rattling of the steam in the main that runs under the floorboards of her house. She has been hearing this rattling for years; but today she finds she can distinguish a *shape* in it, as if she could understand the words of a language she doesn’t know.

When she walks to the market, she notices the sound of water dripping somewhere under the flagstones of the square. It drips in a slightly more regular pattern than she recalls, and the sound is not quite coming from where she expected—it is closer to the center of the square than to the wall she always associated with it.

She shakes herself, and counts the coins in her palm, and says nothing to anyone else. But later that afternoon, when she is passing the lawyers’ offices and the waterworks engineers on the civic terrace, she sees one of them with a listening trumpet in his hand, not against his ear but against the wall, and he looks at her with the same half-awed, half-shamed expression she imagines she herself must have worn.

“What are you listening to?” she asks him, before she can think better of it.

He looks at her for a long time.

“The city,” he says finally, not as if he enjoys it, but as if he is admitting to a mild crime. “Its *bass*.”

### A Change in the Low Frequency

Something is happening in the bass.

The bass is the foundation of the city’s language—the deep, unchanging thrum of the turbines at the waterworks and the generators by the river, the under-rush of the river itself over the spillway of the dam, and the deeper hum of the water trapped in the limestone’s hidden chambers. The city has been built on that bass; its whole noise is shaped by it.

Now that foundation is shifting.

The engineers who are responsible for turbines and generators and mains pressure are the first to feel it.

They wake to find that the turbines in the waterworks, which for their entire careers have had to be monitored and adjusted and coaxed to maintain their frequency, now seem *calmer*.

They are not turning more or more quickly; the gauge of revolutions per minute and the volume of output water are exactly where they should be. But the *tone* of the turbine sound has changed. The note of it is less ragged. The second-order harmonics of stress and imbalance and too-quick wear of metal bearings in the humid air are not gone, but they are reduced—not as if they were repaired, but as if they were *given a longer leash to run out.*

At the same time, the deeper pressure of the plateau underfoot, which has been rising like an angry tide in Ruispor’s bones for as long as anyone can remember, has begun to … *breathe*.

It is still erratic by the day, but now the erratic pattern has a pattern.

If you were to lay down a tape of sound on a graph, from the day before the change to the day after, you would see that the pressure no longer shatters itself into a jagged edge of noise all the time. Instead, it shatters more *cleanly*—the same amount of excess, but now with more regular *ebb* and *flow*.

The engineers’ gauges show the same pressure; they do not, at first, trust what they read. They let the numbers sit on the page for three consecutive days, and then they call a meeting in the waterworks office under the civic terrace.

In the small room, whose walls are already sweating with the damp, they sit around the desk and the chart of the main lines and listen to the city’s bass. They know *this* sound; they grew up with it.

“What’s changed,” one of them says at last, “isn’t the pressure, but the *difference* between high and low.”

They are used to thinking of pressure as a single enemy, rising and rising and rising until they have to cut open more of the city’s body to let it out.

Now they daren’t say it aloud in the same room with the city manager and the factory owner, but under each of their hearts there is a new question: Is there *more* room than we thought there was?

When they walk down into the narrowest of the maintenance tunnels below the civic terrace, the ones with the algal film and the dripping sound and the unspecific dread of being *underneath* the city’s heart of stone and offices and pipes and pumps and guardrails, they notice that the air is not quite so thick.

Oh, it is still damp and still warm and the walls of the tunnel still *weep*—but not as much.

They have not yet seen the new fault in the limestone chamber just beyond the wall they never visit, because the wall is still sound and the door to it is still half-blocked and half-forbidden, but the water there *has* begun to travel along the new path of least resistance.

One of the younger engineers—she who has been on the pressure crews for only four years—says quietly, on the way back up the narrow ladder to the open world, “Do you hear the difference?”

“What difference,” says the chief engineer with the worn knuckles and stained trousers.

She doesn’t say *the bass of the city is less angry than it was*.  

She says instead, almost as if she does not mean to, “The *pattern*.”

“What pattern?” he says.

“The … the *hanging* pattern,” she says finally, hating the imprecision of the words but having no better ones to offer. “The one the drop hammers used to bounce off of, and now … don’t, as much.”

He does not tell her she is imagining it. Instead he looks at her for a long time, and then he says, “Write it down.”

### A Change in the Work of the Hands

The factory workers are not invited to the waterworks meeting, and they are not expected to understand the bass of the city.

They are only expected to show up to their shift with the muscles of their body and the habits of their mind intact and to make the factory turn.

But they, too, begin to change—without overthrowing their world, or stopping the rhythm of the shift.

A man who hammers the red-hot ends of iron bars into the shapes of bolts and braces and brackets of unknown machines to be sold far down the river, one day pushes his lever on the drop hammer and *knows*, before the sound in the factory floor changes, that something is different.

He *feels* it before the foreman’s arm goes down to indicate the next piece of hot metal to be placed in the machine.

He *feels* it in the ear of his body, which is not only the ear of bone but the ear of his skin and the bone of his back and the heel of his feet and the base of his tongue, where the bass of the machines and the bass of the city and the bass of the river under it all are usually low and constant and unchanged.

The *pattern* of the noise of the factory has not changed; the pattern in his body *has*.

When he moves his hammer to the next bolt of his day, his hand does not hesitate as he expected; instead of a slight stutter of *wrongness* in the rhythm of the machine when he releases the lever to bring the hammer head down on the hot iron, he discovers *himself* already having adjusted his body’s placement of the bar between the two halves of the tool *before* the machine tells him to.

He *didn’t* think about it; he just *moved*.

The bolt of glowing metal is not deformed by the hammer’s strike. It rings under the impact with a cleaner *thunk* than usual, and the shape that comes out of his tool when the shift ends is not sharper than he has been told to produce—but he knows, with a bone-clear certainty he cannot explain, that it is *straighter*, than he has been making.

He does not tell this to the foreman or his wife or his mother who lives in the same room deep under the noise of the factory, because he knows the words he wants to say will not hold the shape of the feeling in his body.

“The *noise* changed,” he says instead to the woman who works the next belt over on the forge line.

“No,” she says at once, surprised to find she is answering instead of fending off his remark with her usual bored shrug of *what’s that to me*.
“The way *you* keep to the noise changed,” she says. “You’re … listening to it *earlier.*”

“Listening,” he says, and laughs without humor. “You’re *hearing* listening?”

“I’m *seeing* it,” she says. “In your hands.”

And then she adds, before he can argue and before she can think better of it:

“In mine too.”

The *change* in the factory floor is not one of visible miracles.

The bolts are not all of a sudden perfectly shaped. The belts of the conveyor still stretch and shiver and sometimes need to be tightened and replaced and bunched up with wooden blocks and metal plates to keep the loads of hot parts from sliding sideways when the humidity rises and the belts get *even* stretchier. The furnaces that are the city’s heart of fire and heat and transformation of ore into metal and metal into parts for *some* unknown machine downriver still *roar* with the same voice of soot and coal and forced air and exhaustion of the body of the fireman.

But the *errors* are different.

Where before a mistake in the rhythm of the body would usually *compound* into the machine—wrong piece of metal to the wrong tool, or wrong tool to the wrong piece—now *the* people of the factory, without the faintest idea why they are doing it, begin to correct *before* the mistake lands.

It is not *minding* of the body, but a *new* kind of *body-mindedness*, which has been there all along but is now awake instead of asleep.

The engineers of the factory, who watch the numbers of the shifts and the output of the products of the factory floor and the *rate of error in the forging of the parts*, and who are silently expected by the factory owners to keep the error *below* certain unspoken thresholds of expense and *time* and *material* and *human fear*, are not known to be particularly emotional or poetic.

But when they meet the factory owner on the third week after the change, they say, without exaggeration of language or drama of voice:

“The *basement* of the factory floor is less noisy than it was.”

“Which basement?” the owner says at once, thinking of the foundations of the machines and the waterlogged *support* beams of the factory, which they *all* have been expecting to give *out* at some point.

“The *people* basement,” says one of them, who has been on the floor of the factory longer than anyone else. “The *hands* of the people who work the machines.”

This does not solve the underlying problem of capital and labor and the exploitation of human body for the sake of the *magnet of money* the factory owner is under, but it *does* make the day of that shift better than expected.

The owner of the factory does not say *this is a blessing* or *what benevolent power is responsible for* this *please* or *let* the *factory become* that *kind* of *place*; but she *does* look at the numbers on the page of the report and the longer of the two engineers of the floor and says, very quietly:

“Write that down.”

“It’s *already* written down,” she says, meaning the *numbers*.

“Write *this* down,” says the owner, pointing at the *page* but meaning the *feeling*.

### A Change in the Damp of the Basements

Underneath the houses and the factories and the civic terrace of Ruispor, in the low-ceilinged stone of the *most* abandoned of the basement vaults and the *least* visited of the maintenance tunnels of the waterworks and the *plain* forgotten of the *all* of the city’s structure of stone and wood and iron and brick and pipes and beams and walls and wires and cables and thin glass of skylights and thick of the foundations of the dam of the river and the *limestone* of the plateau itself, there are now several people who are not *bless*ed but who are *notice*ably *blesser*.

Call them basement guardians.

They are the ones who have already been listening to the pressure phantoms and the water and the stone of the city long *before* the benevolent power decided to pay attention.

They are the ones who have been whispering to the stone of the foundations of the factories and houses of the upper terraces, asking *please* come *forth* into *this* canal of the *roof* that *le*aks of the *side* or *through* *this* crack of the *upper* section of the *wall* rather than *under*neath of *this* floor where *we* store of *the* potatoes of the *family* and *w*here of *the* child’s shoe of *of* *the* baby of *of* of the *my* neighbor of *of* of *of* of *of* of.
