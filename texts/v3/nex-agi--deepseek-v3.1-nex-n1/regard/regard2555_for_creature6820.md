They do not speak of the arrival as a person or as words. The first they know of it is a change in the sap.

In the canals, the gel thickens slightly, then clarifies itself, becoming almost translucent. The slow, green ropes that line it straighten a little, as though listening. The mound-beasts, drowsy giants of stucco and stone and purpose, slow even more than their usual slow, and rotate their low heads as if following the passage of something vast overhead. Children on their backs feel the shift in the paving slabs and sit up, expecting a new hill to be rising, but no hill comes.

On the roofs, the filament-birds stop their circling, and for a whole heartbeat the city’s upper air is empty. Then they return to their accustomed arcs, but the pattern is changed: now there are small spaces in their loops, little windows in the sky, where nothing passes but light and attention.

The adults notice nothing so clearly, and yet everything shifts by an inch. The air in the chalk rooms is lighter; the plaster stops weeping its faint moisture; the salt-scars at the city’s edge dry to a brighter white than usual, and the name they inscribe—JELIBETH, in letters that are not quite letters—can be read for a longer stretch before the sun blinks them away.

This is the first kindness of the benevolent power that has turned its gaze toward Jelibeth: it gives itself to be perceived by each layer according to its own language, before it ever says a word.





The first true word arrives not in speech but in a small and impossible question.

A child sits on the lip of a canal, watching the gel swirl in its slow lines between the stones. The canals are never still, but this is the first time she thinks to ask: why?

“Why,” she says, not aloud, but with the soft pressure of her tongue against the back of her teeth. The question makes a shape in her mind that has not been there before—a gap like the missing part in the filament-birds’ new pattern.

In the canal, the gel rises in a translucent dome, no bigger than a child’s cupped hands, and then collapses into itself with a faint, wet murmur. The roof-mat above the canal trembles along one edge, and drops of sap bead at its fringe, each one clear and perfectly round.

Later, she cannot tell you if what she saw next was real or not. She thinks she sees the dome of gel form a shape, or almost a shape: the likeness of a face that is many faces at once, all its eyes soft, all its mouths closed in a smile that does not need teeth. She thinks she hears the canal answer her question with no words at all, just the warm feeling of being understood.

The emotion that comes with it is gentle: You are not alone to wonder why.

The child’s fingers are chalk-dusty and small. When she reaches into the canal—not to break the surface but to hover just above it—the sap, astonished by her own lightness, swirls a little faster around her palm.

She asks another question, then, without knowing she asked:

What are you?

And the distant answer, not in words, but in the slow pressure of her own heartbeat matching the rhythm of the city’s:

A friend.





When the benevolent power arrives in full, it does not smash open the sky. It arrives not from above Jelibeth but beneath its feet, rising from the ground that is still warm with the memory of its making.

The ground shudders, but gently, as one might turn in sleep before a dream. The infant hills shiver the loose earth of their edges; the courtyard soils shift and settle; the spiral staircases hollowed from the chalk walls widen their curves by the width of a finger. The city’s thefts from itself are not violent but rearrangements: a room there becomes a doorway; a window here becomes a balcony; a low cellar that was always cold becomes a warm gathering chamber.

For the first time in a long time, there is rain in Jelibeth.

Not rain as water-worlds know it—great curtains of water falling from the sky—but rain as Jelibeth might receive it without breaking: a thin, clear syrup of cloud that condenses slowly over the outer streets, where the salt-scars are still learning their names. It drops in small, warm beads from the filaments of the birds, who shake themselves contentedly and then spin again through the air, weaving their threads into a protective lattice so that the rain does not waste away to nothing.

The roof-mats above drink deeply of this new moisture, swelling their pale greens into darker, more certain colors. The fronds that creep from the gutters into the walls soften the chalk just a little, making it easier for the adults to hollow out new rooms when needed. The children’s feet are the first to be surprised by standing water in small, shallow depressions of the courtyards, where the sap-rain collects and refuses to evaporate.

It is clean and strange and full of light.

When the children of Jelibeth drink, the sap-rain tastes like question and answer in the same mouthful: like root and metal, yes, but with a faint new sweetness behind them, as though someone has remembered, on their behalf, that life could taste like possibility.

And throughout the city, the same phrase appears, not carved in stone but written in the salt-scars, the bead of dew on a window, the pattern of the filament-birds’ flight:

Do not be afraid.





The benevolent power has not come to give them new laws or to tell them what to be. It comes to give them birthdays they never had.
