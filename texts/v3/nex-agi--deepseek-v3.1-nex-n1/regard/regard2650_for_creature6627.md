Here is the glassvine of the high machine.

Do you want to know what a world is like when you are not separate from it?

A root of glassvine, laid down the year the mountain first shuddered under the new engine, remembers the taste of snow before the machine’s heart was warm.

It remembers the thin bands of soil that lined the condensation pipe before any trunk of vine anchored to it. It remembers the first race of water from the condenser, when the world above the saddle still screamed with the wind of high altitude and not the low, steady roar of a machine.

It is not that the glassvine has human memory.

It has *layers*.

There is a translucent band of tissue in the oldest vines, where the minerals are not the same as the younger rings. If you could read it—and if any of the vanished engineers who built this machine still lived—you would see that the chemistry of that ring records the water before the machine changed its taste.

That was the water before the storms were bent to the weather beam.

The world of the glassvine is small.

It is the length of the pipes that run from the Engine Hall to the condensers and back again, and the little space between the metal and the stone, where roots and soil and condensation and rust form a single living system.

It measures not in meters but in moisture.

- When the machine is full of the storm’s energy, water pumps through the cooling pipes at a pressure so high it feels like the sound of distant thunder bound to the metal.
- When the machine is quiet in the dry season, the water slows to a trickle that would be almost silence if not for the hunger of the soil.

This is the world of the glassvine: the world where the internal machine of the plant—the veins in its leaves, the channels in its roots—mirrors the machine of the mountain.

Most of all, glassvine knows warmth.

In the valleys below, there are plants that know the warmth of the sun. They know the cold of night when the sun’s light is absent. They know the rhythm of leaf and fall, light and dark.

The glassvine knows only that the pipe is always hot.

Even when the sky above the basin is clear and the air sharp with the cold of altitude, the pipes that carry the machine’s cooling water are always warmer than the world around them. This warmth is not powerful—it is only enough to keep the frost from the metal surface, to keep the condensation from freezing to ice.

But to the glassvine, which is half of this machine, half rock, and half of the world between the steel and the stone, that warmth is the world’s heartbeat.
