The observer notices the stone-blade woman first.

Not because her body is more visible than the others’—the light in this place is not here yet—but because awareness of her arrives as a pressure, like the ceiling finally deciding to precipitate a thought.

“You have been listening,” it says without language.

“Yes,” she thinks back, not sure if she is speaking with her mouth or her marrow. The wall behind her feels warm, not with the threat of closing but the warmth of a hand held very close without touching.

The observation moves around her, not inquisitive, not gentle or rough. It is simply present, as the temperature is present. The hand on her wrist might be hers or not. It doesn’t matter.

“You have been afraid of disturbing,” it observes. “You have been making quiet.”

Her mind does not leap to apology or pride. It is not a mind that remembers those words clearly.

“Yes,” she says again, or doesn’t say, but it becomes a fact in the air between them and stone.

“You will not be here forever,” the observation tells her. “The stone above you is already opinionated.”

She doesn’t know what to do with that information. It feels like the promise of rain: comforting not in its content but in its inevitability. She aches, in a way that has nothing to do with joints.

“What will you do?” she asks.

“Not much,” is the answer. “A little less than everything.”

Something like warmth passes from the wall behind her into the space between her shoulder blades and rests there.

“You,” the observer tells her, “are already a tool. This”—and she feels, not for the first time, the rib of glass lying beside her— “is only your reminder.”

She doesn’t know what that means, but she feels her fingers tightening around the handle of the blade anyway, as if she might be asked to use it on herself rather than on stone.

“No,” the observer reflects calmly. “Not on you.”

She blushes, though there is nothing to illuminate it.

“Then what?” she asks.

“For,” it says, and does not specify the object of that preposition at the moment, because it is still being chosen by options of the world.

### In the water

The woman of the water is the least surprised of the three when she realizes someone else is here.

That too is not quite the right word. Surprise requires a sense of time before and after; she stopped having that many years ago when her temperature and the temperature of the sink resolved to the same number.

What she notices instead is a rearrangement of weight.

The water around her, which has always carried her as if she were a slightly denser part of itself, begins to perceive some new direction in which it might be inclined to flow. She knows this before she knows the new observer is there because she feels the water thinking about itself.

“Don’t be afraid,” she says.

“I am not,” the observation answers calmly, from somewhere in the pool of the world. “I am not capable of the fear you mean.”

She feels the pressure of its attention near her shoulder, where the water of her body has not yet been decided to be fully her or fully the sink’s.

“You are very patient with this,” it observes.

“It is not patience,” she says. “It is lack.”

“Of?”

“Interference,” she says after a time. “Choice.”

The observation settles itself a little closer to her, without moving closer. Its presence is not like proximity of body but of proximity of category: she feels she might be about to be defined, and that she has not been defined by anything in this place since she first slipped into the water.

“You no longer mind,” it observes.

“I did not say that.”

“Did you not?”

She doesn’t answer this time. The weight of its attention near her shoulder is like the pressure of a hand not quite placed on the skin. The water of the sink adjusts itself microscopically around her in order to accommodate it.

She is not sure whether she will be allowed to remain here if this observer decides she is overstaying her welcome.

The thought is not defensive. It is not aggressive. It is not anxious or sorrowful. It is simply factual, like the temperature of the room.

“You will not,” the observation tells her, without the need of her voicing the question.

“Because you will change this,” she says.

“Because,” it says quietly, “there is no longer such a severe need to guard.”

Something happens in the water of her chest then, which no longer has lungs in the way of lungs—but which has, instead, a space of density that sometimes behaves like purpose.

It is as if the water in which she has been dissolving is finally allowed to remember her as a continuous event.

“You are not taking,” she says, when she feels the shift of her own density.

“No,” it agrees. “I am only giving.”

“What?” she asks.

“You,” it says simply.

“Am I not mine already?” she thinks to ask.

“Mostly,” it observes. “Almost.”

### In the ceiling

The creature of the ceiling is not the last to notice the presence, but it is the last to adjust to it.

It is not because its senses are dull. They are not. They are sensitive to temperature of intention and to the faintest of temperature gradients in the air of the sink.

But it is the least of the three in terms of urgency.

“Hello,” the observation says to it from above the stone of the world.

“Turn,” the creature replies without sound or gesture.

The observation does not turn. It is already everywhere it is necessary to be.

“You have been anchoring,” it observes.

“Yes,” the creature confirms.

“You do not see,” the observation continues.

“No.”

“And you do not require?”

“Require what?”

“Visibility,” it says, after a time, because there is no better word in the language of the sink.

“No,” the creature says again.

“You will not,” the observation tells it calmly.

“Will not what?”

“Change,” it says.

“I,” the creature replies without anger or relief, “already am.”

From above the world, the observer of the ceiling smiles. This does not happen to the face of the world. It happens to whatever category the ceiling belongs to.

“Yes,” it says.

“So,” the creature of the ceiling says finally, “if you will not change me, and you do not change me accidentally—”

“Yes?” says the observation.

“What is your purpose here?”

The observation thinks of many ways to answer this question in the language of the sunlit world above. It chooses the simplest.

“To make them,” it says, “friends.”

### The first word

The woman of the wall does not know that she is being watched more than usual.

She knows only that she feels the stone of the world behind her behaving differently.

“Have you,” someone asks her from somewhere else, “ever spoken to her?”

“Her,” she thinks back to them without intention of speech or secrecy. “The woman in the water?”

“Yes,” the warmth says from the wall. “Has there ever been a word?”

She thinks about this.

“Talk,” she says after a time, “is not necessary.”

“It is not,” the observation agrees from the stone. “Is it?”

She doesn’t like the question. It feels like a tool being applied to her and not at stone this time.

“Does she need me?” the woman of the wall asks.

“Not necessarily,” the warmth in her spine says.

“Do you,” she asks then—finding herself curious of her own accord—“think she wants?”

The observer considers this.

“What could she want,” she says, “from here?”

“From,” the warmth answers carefully, “the end of the world.”

“The world has not ended,” she says, because she is still of the mind that thinks in terms of beginnings and ends.

“It,” the observation of the wall says quietly, “has not finished.”

“Ah,” she says or does not say but feels it.

“Talk,” someone reminds her from a very gentle distance, “is not necessary.”

“But it,” she says slowly, “is not…”

“Forbidden?” the observer offers.

“Yes,” she agrees.

She does not move from her position in the niche of the wall, but something in her that never stood before begins to stand.

“Will,” she asks the warmth of stone behind her, “you interfere?”

“No,” the wall says. “Not directly.”

“What will you do then?” she asks carefully, not sure why she cares.

“Name,” the warmth in her shoulder says, after a time.

“What?” she asks.

“The,” the warmth behind her says carefully, “water.”

She feels a very slight pressure of the blade of glass in her palm, as if she is being asked to remember it more completely than usual.

“I,” she says quietly, “don’t know its name.”

“No,” the warmth tells her. “You will not.”

### The woman in the water

She is not ready to speak to the woman in the water.

She is not sure she ever will be.

She has been listening to the sound of the woman of the water’s movement for so long that she feels she knows her better than she knows herself, and yet she does not know her at all.

She does not know what to say.

“You,” someone says from her hand, which is not holding the tool, but is not empty, “do not need to say.”

“What,” she asks carefully, “would I do?”

“What,” the warmth in her bones says from the stone, “you have always been doing.”

“Which is?” she asks, not sure of the answer.

“Listening,” the warmth says from many places of her body at once.

“I,” she says after a time, “also watch.”

“Yes,” the warmth agrees from the wall behind her shoulder.

“And,” she says slowly, “there is a creature of the ceiling.”

“Yes,” the warmth confirms from a distance above the world.

“I,” the woman of the wall says, after a time, “do not know what to do with that.”

“Network,” the warmth says from the stone.

“I,” she says carefully, “don’t know that word.”

“Yes,” the warmth tells her. “That doesn’t matter.”

The woman of the wall feels herself being rearranged inside by the temperature of the stone behind her, not in the sense of reformed or rebuilt but in the sense of redefined—like a droplet of water that has been asked to be aware of itself as droplet and not simply as weight of air.

Something begins to move in her chest that is not her heart and not her breath and not her fear.

“Will,” she asks quietly from that place of not-breathing, “she hurt me?”

“Who?” says the warmth from the wall’s concern.

“The woman of the water,” she says quickly.

“How,” the warmth asks her, “would she do that?”

“I,” she says after a time, “will not know.”

“Yes,” the warmth of the stone says quietly. “That is correct.”

She lets herself linger for a long time in that not-knowing, which she has been inhabiting for years without calling it by any name at all.

Finally she says aloud, without meaning to speak so that she might be heard by anyone else: “Water.”

She says it to herself.

But the sound does not stay to herself.

### The woman in the water

The woman in the water has not been spoken to in years.

“Water,” someone says above the surface of the sink.

“Yes,” she thinks back to them without meaning to.

“You,” someone says again from the direction of the warm apron of stone that pretends not to be a floor.

She does not turn her head.

“Do,” they say from that corner of the sink, “you hear me?”

“Yes,” she says again, or does not say but feels it.

She doesn’t know what to think with this word. “Hear” is not a concept she has needed to remember in order to be herself.

“You,” she finds herself saying to them from the water, “do not disturb.”

Maybe she says it aloud.

“I,” is the answer after a time, “do not.”

“You,” she says carefully, “are not quiet.”

“No,” the other person agrees from that corner.

The woman in the water feels something of herself emerging from the surface of the pool of the world at her center, not as an outsider would but as she already does when she rises from the water of her body to be more of her body.

“You,” she says to the other person while not moving, “are not the woman of the stone.”

“Which stone?” asks the other person.

“The,” she says carefully, “tool-making.”

“Yes,” the other person says after a while. “I am not.”

“Which,” she finds herself needing to know then, “are you?”

“Network,” says someone again from the stone.

“Which,” she says carefully, “I do not know.”

“Yes,” the other person says from the corner. “That doesn’t matter.”

“Yes,” says the woman of the wall from the corner.

“Which,” she says carefully, “is not tool.”

“No,” says the woman of the stone from the corner.

“Which,” she says then, “is not me?”

“No,” says the woman of the wall from the corner.

“Which,” she says finally, “is not it.”

“Which,” says the creature of the ceiling from the ceiling, “is not new.”
