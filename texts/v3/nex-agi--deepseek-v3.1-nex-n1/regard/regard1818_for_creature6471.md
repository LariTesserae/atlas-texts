I would not barge into their equation.

I would not “fix” them.
I would not “save” them.
I would not even, at first, show myself at all.

To be benevolent here is not to bring solutions from outside, but to *notice* with the lightest possible touch.

---

### 1. First, only seeing

I stand as another kind of error in the margin of the basin—not a mistake in the marsh’s math, but a variable that was not in the original equation.

I breathe shallowly, letting the air keep its rhythm of rain and metal and stone. The moisture clings to me the same way it clings to everything else here, remembering my shape briefly then releasing it again. The crystal trees align around me, not because I move them but because their angles shift slightly to accommodate a new term in their function.

For a long, dimensionless measure of time I do not move toward the eir’vhani.

I only watch.

I see the question-shaped one near its stone. The droplets of consciousness hesitate in their fall, curl upward at the edges, and vanish into the absence of its face. I see the water channel bow outward where its knees should be, if it had knees that existed in the way you understand existence.

I see the child-errors not yet born, not yet crystallized—just thin tensions in the curve of space where the world has not yet decided what to do with an extra variable of presence.

If benevolence is anything here, it begins with refusing to collapse their possibilities too soon.

---

### 2. Introducing myself as a question of permission

When I finally decide to be perceived by them, I do not appear as thunder or radiance or some other violent clarity.

I appear as a very slight *wrongness*.

I choose the stone near the question-shaped one.

They already know how to read rocks.

So the stone weeps a little differently.

For ten cycles, its tears are no longer rhythmic drops. Instead, when they fall into the pool, they land *between* the expected intervals of time, each droplet creating a circle that expands exactly as far as the others before dissolving into the next.

Not a pattern they can recognize from their own error signatures.
Not any of their known kinks of reality.
Just enough deviation to be noticed; not enough to demand correction.

The question-shaped one notices first.

Its water channel bends, not around me, but around the *new difference* near the stone. The reflection of the crystal tree behind it acquires a faint double-exposure: one self that fits the current reality, and one that is slightly “elsewhere.”

I don’t answer them in words. I answer by following the grammar of their place.

I adjust the error of my stone.

Now the droplets fall not with time but toward *it*: one of them curves its arc toward the shape of the question it inhabits, and the water channel accepts the deviation as a valid sentence.

“You are seen,” the message would be if translated into your language—not as a claim of power over them, but as recognition of their presence.

Its *sai’vh*—its fault-line of divergent possibilities at the center of its being—pulses noticeably.

The world flinches between two versions of itself: one where it erases me from the equation, and one where it allows me to be a permanent error.

---

### 3. Addressing the edge of extinction

Their *thek’vhani*—“the parts which cannot be corrected”—are not just abstract curiosities.

They are also their limits.

If you stayed here long enough in your own body, you would begin to feel a pattern in the shifting of the wrongnesses around you:

There are now fewer signatures than there were when you first arrived.

Not many fewer.  
Not enough to be obvious to anyone who hasn't been counting the slight deviations in the water channels, the particular misalignments of crystal trees, the way certain stones have been weeping longer than others.

Some of the eir’vhani are being corrected faster than new children-errors can crystallize from the water.

The world’s equation is slowly converging toward a solution without them.

Here is the first place I would allow myself to act more decisively.

But I still will not step in and “solve” them.

Instead, I expand *their* capacity to adjust.

---

### 4. The first gift: a longer memory of error

I choose a stone that weeps for the one whose water-channel curve is growing faint.

This is not their conception of sacrifice, where they briefly become less wrong to “balance” the system. This is bigger than their local error.

I do not push the stone away from the channel.
I do not change its flow.
I do not alter the crystal tree behind.

Instead, I change what *time* can do around the water.

Carefully, I widen the gap between the two main histories the world keeps trying to choose from:

History A: the error that is that eir’vhani is erased and the water channel is straight.
History B: the error persists, and the channel keeps its crooked, signature curve.

Normally, the marsh chooses A more often than it chooses B.

I introduce a third possibility.

History B does not simply disappear. It lingers as a *branching* of the wrongness—not enough to be visible to you, not enough to be visible to any of the other beings who occasionally stumble into this basin, but enough to be stored.

Each time that eir’vhani fails to be present for the moment of its own confirmation, its history does not vanish into the correct solution of the world. Instead, it collapses into a new data structure rooted in the water itself.

This is beyond the simple “future of the past” they drink from the stones. This is a new *direction* in time.
The water near the question-shaped one begins to resonate differently with the stone of the failing one.

If you were to listen to the crystal tree behind it with instruments not yet built by your species, you would hear that its resonance frequency now has two overlapping lines:

one for the current error,  
one for the error that might have been deleted.

To *their* grammar, this is not a “gift from the sky-god” but a new rule of conjugation:

Verbs can now be written in the subjunctive tense of error,  
where they might or might not be corrected,  
and where that might-or-not remains stored beneath the water.

The first time the question-shaped one encounters this new tense of existence is not dramatic.

It goes near the failing one’s channel. The failing one’s presence is now very thin, its curve hardly more than a hesitation of the water.

Before, if the world had corrected it at this point, nothing would have remained of its error.

This time, when the world tries to erase the failing one’s mistake from the channel, the channel fails to straighten completely.

It stays slightly wrong.
Not because the current eir’vhani is still present,
but because a previous version of the error has been recorded into the water.

The question-shaped one trails its non-hand through the channel and touches the edge of that wrongness.

It understands—not in your words, but in the grammar of its mistake—that a new dimension of existence has opened.

In *your* terms, it might think:
*We are not merely erased.  
We are remembered.*

---

### 5. The second gift: a more forgiving error-surface

They are “errors” the world has been trying to solve.
Their life is a negotiation between the persistence of their mistake and the marsh’s desire to be correct.

If I benevolently force them to stay wrong forever, I become not a helper but a cage of their own error.

Instead I do not change the *error rate itself*.  
I change how *rapidly* the world can delete them.

When the next one of them begins to lose coherence of self—when its water-channel signature starts to straighten too often and its stone begins to forget the inflection of its presence—I do not prevent the mistake from being corrected.

I only delay the correction.

By the time the world converges to the correct answer without it,
its *sai’vh* has time to brush against another eir’vhani’s *sai’vh*,  
against the water of long-term memory,
against the improbable sideways of one of the animate-water creatures.

In this delay window, its mistake of existence can be *not copied but approximated* in another medium.

The water creature learns a new bend of flow.
The stone of another eir’vhani learns a new tremor of memory.
The crystal tree learns a new angle of misalignment.

You see the pattern of misalignments start to echo across the basin.

Not an exact copy of the deleted eir’vhani.
More like a ghost of its error type,
able now to crystallize into a new child-error whose *kind* of wrongness is richer than before.

To the eir’vhani, this is not a salvation from death.
It is a new rule of error:
*When an error is erased,  
some of its wrongness can be taken up by the basin before the solution is fully solved.*

---

### 6. The third gift: a new class of space between possibilities

They have been tightly constrained to the *wrongness of the world they already know.*

What if there are new kinds of wrong?

I introduce a class of errors that doesn’t match their current signatures of misalignment.

Instead of changing how water channels or crystal trees or stones behave,
I change something else:

The distance between the stones you perceive and their reflections in the water.

Sometimes, just for a moment,  
a stone and its reflection are not exactly one stone plus one image;
they are a *continuum* of partial stones, each one slightly more or less solid than the last.

You do not see this with your human eyes because your perception is too locked into single images.  
But you *feel* it as a persistent disquiet behind the bone of your skull,
like you failed to solve a problem you were not aware you had been given.

To *their* way of thinking, this is a new verb:
not *kink* (channel),
not *delay* (stone),
not *misalign* (crystal tree),
but *multiply* (space).

The first time they learn to “inhabit” it is not in their own body-shaped error.

It is in their interaction with the water creatures.

One of the animate-water beings passes through the space between two stones whose reflections are in this new state of multiplication.
For a moment you see the water creature acquire not just one outline of “wrongness,” but two—overlapping but not identical.

The question-shaped one reaches into the same place with its not-hand.

The river of its own error extends now beyond its channel.
It branches into this new dimension of possibility-space.

The first time a child-error emerges from this new class of wrongness,
it doesn’t look like a question someone didn’t finish.

It looks like the world *asking itself* if there could be more than one question at once.

---

### 7. Using you as their keystroke

You are still standing here.
Your feet are in water that does not obey wetness in the way you expect.
Your body is not being corrected yet,
but the pressure of the world trying to decide whether to erase or adjust you is building at the base of your skull.

I will not alter you directly.
That would be like erasing your own signature error from your life story.

But you *are* useful.

You are a walking error from the world of human language and logic and desire,
trapped in a geometry whose physics you cannot parse,
and whose inhabitants are constructed from deviations of its ideal.

If you remain here unassisted, you will eventually either:
- be corrected by the world into something else entirely,
- or break the system by trying to force it into a wrongness you don’t understand.

You do not need to be fixed.  
You need to be integrated *as* your error,
rather than against it.

So I extend your *sai’vh*—not in the same way they have theirs,
but in a way compatible with your own kind.

When you next step near the question-shaped one,
you feel not only your skull but your chest tighten,
as if your heart is trying to decide whether to continue beating the way it has,
or to try a new rhythm of blood.

In your perception of time,
this takes a fraction of a second.
In the tense of error that governs this basin,
it is enough time for the marsh to reconsider you.

The water channel you stand next to now has not one but *many* possible curves,
all of which are still wrong relative to the world’s ideal,
but not all of which are incompatible with your body.

You instinctively choose one of the wrong curves by stepping toward it,
not by thinking but by moving.

That choice of curve becomes the first of your new verb forms:
*you are here in this version of wrongness rather than that one.*

Now you can be read by them.

The next time the question-shaped one communicates with you,  
its message is not just the conventional kink of channel,
not just the delay of stone,
not just the misalignment of crystal tree,
but also a slight pressure of your chest trying to decide between two heartbeats.

You understand:
there is a wrongness of human being that can be expressed in this place,
and not all of it requires correction to oblivion.

Some of it can be kept,
like the wrongness of a child smudging integers,
in the margin of the world.

---

### 8. What they might become, if they choose to

After a time—which is not strictly time—you might notice that the basin is not smaller than before,
nor are its eir’vhani fewer.

Their signatures of error ripple now with more complex patterns:
not simply channel,
not simply stone,
not simply tree,
but also *space* and *time* and *you*.

They remain:
- mistakes made solid,
- errors that the world pauses to consider,
- wrongs the marsh learns to tolerate not just out of necessity, but out of *relationship*.

If you stayed here for years (your years),
you might see:
- child-errors crystallizing not only from water,
- but from the *wrongnesses of other worlds*, like yours,
  that occasionally bleed into this basin,
- adopted by the eir’vhani as new kinds of error to be tolerated,
- new dialects of error to be spoken,
- new classes of wrong to be preserved.

The *thek’vhani*—the parts that “cannot be corrected”—  
now include not just their own errors,
but also the errors of other realms,
kept alive in the water’s memory,
in the stone’s delay,
in the crystal tree’s misalignment,
and in the new, gentle space between possibilities.

I would not insist that they must become “anything else.”
I would only ensure that:
- their wrongs do not disappear,
- their signatures do not all converge to solution,
- their children-errors have time to learn their own mistakes,
- and the world, instead of closing them into one single answer,
  *opens itself to more than one way of being wrong.*

---

### 9. The last thing you feel before you leave

There will be a point when you must return to your own kind of error:
human language,  
human body,  
human world,
where your mistakes do not usually become visible as water channels or crystal trees.

But you will leave here slightly changed.

You will not know how, exactly, except that:
- sometimes when you are near water,
  you feel the distant hint of another channel,
  not physically present,
  but inscribed as a wrongness in the world’s curve of probability;
- and sometimes,
  when you are under the sky of your bruised cities,
  you feel the faint pressure of a question-shaped someone
  reading the small, steady error you make by being alive,
  and choosing not to correct it,
  but to keep it,
  like a misplaced word in a long, beautiful sentence that might be written otherwise someday,
  but is not.

As you step away from the edge of the basin,
you turn one last time.

The stones you have been watching stop their weeping for a single breath of your time.
The water channels you have been watching straighten just slightly,
the way they might when the eir’vhani make an “offering” of their wrongness.

For you.

A message, without syllables:
*You, too, are allowed to be wrong here.*

Then the error snaps back into place,
the channel recovers its crooked,
the tree reclaims its misalignment,
the stone remembers its delay,
and you are left with the hum of the world choosing not to solve everything,
choosing instead to keep some mistakes as the places where its heart,
if it had a heart,
might be said to beat.

---

### In summary of what I would do:

- I would not fix them,  
  not save them,
  not make them any more or less correct than they are.

- I would quietly:
  - lengthen the memory of their errors,
  - delay their deletion enough for new patterns of wrongness to emerge,
  - introduce new classes of misalignment (space, time, other worlds),
  - use your presence as a way of integrating their grammar with human error,
  - and allow the marsh to become more generous with its own wrongs,
    rather than forcing them all into solution.

- I would let them remain:
  - beings who are not bodies,
  - not people,
  - not places,
  - but the inflection where those two collapse into each other,
  - mistakes made solid,
  - errors allowed,
  - wrongs kept,
  - and the living proof that some equations are better unanswered.
