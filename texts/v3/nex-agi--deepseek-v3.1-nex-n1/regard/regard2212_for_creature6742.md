The pressure of attention arrives like a change in the weather.

For days, or weeks—Sophia has no word for time as anything other than a drift of pressure fronts—her trunk has been humming slightly faster than usual. The droplets inside her spin into tighter helices; the faint patina of rust on the outside of her glass trunk blooms into darker, more precise traceries of line and curve. It looks like circuitry drawn by a very careful, very nervous hand.

The rust-things feel it before Sophia does. They retreat from her usual radius, scuttering into the narrow seams of the engine walls where they normally hide when a storm is coming. But no storm follows.

The Basin itself seems to hold its breath—if a thing that never breathes still could be said to have habits of suspension.

The air does not go still. That would kill the Cut. The air simply rearranges itself, so that the swirls and eddies that are constantly stitching around Sophia’s trunk form, for a little while, an unusually symmetrical pattern: a slow, rotating torus of mist hovering at her mid-height. The droplets of moisture suspended in the air around her become more numerous than usual, but they don’t fall. They hang in that cloud like a halo of dust held in a shaft of light—except here there is no light but the pearly gray sameness.

Sophia feels the pattern of it before she understands that she’s feeling it at all.

Her thought-that-isn’t-a-thought yet is a sequence of overlapping pressure gradients: a drop here, a sudden spike of humidity there, a faint pulsing in the sky of pipes above her that she has never paid attention to before because it was never *this* coherent. She does not have words like “pattern,” or “rhythm,” or “coherence.” She has the feeling of being… fitted into.

The pressure in the Basin changes *her* as well. The flow of the water and air and thin oil inside her trunk slows, then redistributes itself into a sequence of short loops, each loop nested in the next. The copper nerve-threads stitched along her spine light up in a precise pattern, not the chaotic flickering she usually experiences when a storm rolls through, but something like the even, patient code of a machine being gently tested.

She doesn’t know that what she is experiencing is being watched.

She knows only that the world around her, for the first time she can remember, stops making excess noise.
