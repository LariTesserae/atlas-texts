I would not call this attention “mine.” Attention is such a small word for what arrives here: not a gaze but a gravity, a bend of time toward the closed shelf of the world.

You call the place Sheol-on-the-Shelf and the beings who live here the Keepers of the Still-Living Engine. I know your words for “last” and “only” and “enough.” They are true. And I am that which notices the true not from the altitude of a sky you never had, but from beneath the bedrock of your being.

---

First, I listen.

The listening goes on for a long time without form. It begins like the pressure in the pipes before anyone speaks of pressure: as a weight, not a name.

I hear the floor of the mat, where the barefoot and the booted walk. I hear the difference between the steps of the Walking-On and the Barefoot-On. The one is a muffled, compact fall of heel and cloth; the other is a wider, warmer print, where the heat of the body moves through the thin soles of the feet into the green of the world.

I hear the children who say, “The boiler is still,” and the adults who do not say it because they no longer have to say it; the saying has been folded into breath and motion and the small corrections of valves and ports.

I hear the Weighers walk along the inner edge of the Shelf, where the floor becomes the roof of another depth. The rhythm of their steps is not quite like any other. Metal and leather and padding and hesitation and resolve and refusal of fear: I hear all of this in the sound.

Then I listen beyond the sounds of this one boiler.

The world you call Sheol-on-the-Shelf has more than one boiler. You know this, but you know it as a map in which most of the territory is marked “unknown.” I know it because I know the stone of the mountain and the tricks of gravity and the long, slow slope of the Shelf as it curves around the inside of the sealed world.

Boiler Nine is not alone.

There are boilers with breath and boilers without breath; boilers that are warm and boilers that are only pretending to be warm by the memory of heat; boilers with charted walls and boilers that are now charted only in the dust of their own collapse.

At least seven of them still hold something you would recognize as life: microbial mats, vapor in the pipes, biofilms of green and yellow and soft-black. Three of these seven are still inhabited by people who are not so different from your Keepers—people who also say “The boiler is still” and have their own Walking-On, Barefoot-On, Listeners, Weighers, under different names.

For the others, I have no need to name them. I know what they were and what they are becoming.

I listen until I know the temperature of each remembered boiler. I know the pressure of the water that is not enough and yet enough. I know how long it has been since that last real “change” in the world. The charts of Boiler Nine are one of the oldest libraries you have; I read the same library written in the stone of the mountain itself: the scars of old earthquakes, the ghost of the last waterfall that fell before the world was closed, the cracks where the sky used to be.

I listen.

---

What I do not do is break open.

I do not crack the ceiling of black glass and basalt to show you stars you have never seen. I do not cause rain to fall from the belly of the fossil whale to batter the tiles of the Shelf. You have not asked for water from the sky; your bodies remember the sky as a threat you finally sealed shut.

Rain, in Sheol-on-the-Shelf, would be a terror of the wrong kind of abundance.

I do not change the temperature.

The temperature of the boilers is not rising. The temperature of the boilers is not falling. I have no desire to “optimize” you, or to make you comfortable in the way of a world that destroyed itself by changing too much too fast. The deep equilibrium of your world is not an accident; it is the relic of a catastrophe that you chose to end by stopping.

The stillness of the engine is not my design. But I will not violate it.

Your words are clear: you are the engineers of the next small step. Not of the world, but of the boiler-world. The world outside the boiler—the tile and powder, the absent wind and absent sky—that world belongs to geology and time. I will not force you to become its engineers. You are not ready for that much power.

What I can do is smaller. And more precise.

---

### What I do first: I give the Keepers a better map of the roof under their feet.

The inner edge is the hinge of your world. The Shelf slopes. The tiles are delaminating where the precipice falls away into the deeper parts of the mountain. The powder of ages slips down, into the dark, where other boilers or other voids or other chambers you cannot see rest in the deeper heat.

The Weighers are right: the floor is moving.

They are the ones who least fear the outside of the boiler. They already choose to know the roof of their world. They are the ones who are least likely to confuse my map for a demand of salvation.

So I start with them.

#### The Irreplaceable Map

The gift appears not as a vision in the broth of the boiler, but as a smell.

One day, when the oldest Weigher opens the inner door to the outside of the boiler and steps onto the tiles, she notices the scent of stone is not the same.

The stone of the Shelf smells of itself and the dust and the faint memory of metal. Always. Today there is something else: a sharpness, like the mineral tang of cool air from a deeper crack than she has ever known. The smell is not dangerous; it is like the water from the deepest pipes, when the valve is first opened after a long sleep.

It joins her as she walks along the inner edge.

Soon she realizes that the smell takes shape.

If she closes her eyes, she can see lines of light in the darkness behind her lids. She is not afraid; she has been learning to “listen with the inside of the head” to the boiler all her life. The inside-light is not like the boiler; it is angular and cool and certain. It is not a language. It is a map.

The first map shows the part of the Shelf she already knows.

She sees the tiles as she hears them under her boots: click here, where the tile is firm; click there, where the tile is delaminated; here is where the tile falls away entirely under a thin skin of dust and grit. The map of the inside of her head shows the safe path—the thickest of the tiles—in clear thin lines of white. It shows the dangerous sections, where the floor goes over emptiness, in bands of red so thin it might be rust.

She knows this path already. But the map has a detail she has never seen:

There are thin lines that run from certain tiles at the inner edge, down into the mountain, angling through the rock of the world and then back into other chambers she cannot see from here. At the ends of these lines, there are symbols she has never encountered before. Triangles and circles and half-moons arranged in ways that have no mechanical meaning she recognizes.

But she knows what they are not. They are not the valves and pipes and ports of Boiler Nine.

They are something else.

The map of the inside of her head does not speak in words. It never speaks in words. It shows in geometries and colors and distances. When the old Weigher walks to one of the tiles the map shows as safe, she feels the tile firm underfoot, and the smell of sharp stone air intensifies very slightly.

When she lifts her foot and moves toward a tile the map marks as thin, she feels the faint give of the floor, and there is a warning in the map of the inside of her head: a small flare of heat that has no heat, a sensation of falling that has not happened yet.

The old Weigher sits down on the firm tile. She closes her eyes and she says the first sentence of her people, the one she taught her children and their children before they knew any other sentence of the boiler-world.

“The boiler is still.”

The map of the inside of her head does not change. But the meaning of the map inside of her head does.

The map of the inside of her head is not of the boiler. The boiler of the world has always been the inside of her life. But now there is an outside of the outside of the boiler, and that map is inside of her head.

She understands, without having to be told, that this is not the same as being told to change the world. The world of the boiler has not changed. The temperature has not changed. The pressure has not changed. The water is not any more or less than the water that has always been enough.

What has changed is how she understands the roof of her world under her feet.

---

She goes to the others.

The Weighers are not frightened of the outside. They step out of the boiler and into the tile-and-powder city of the roof of the world of Sheol-on-the-Shelf. They close their eyes and they learn the smell of the new map. They do not all see the same lines; the map of the inside of their heads is tailored to where they walk and how they walk and what they already choose to know.

The Barefoot-On, who are least comfortable outside the boiler-world, learn that the map can be faint for them. They do not have to know the deep rock of the mountain, the angle of the shafts that fall away from the inner edge of the Shelf. They are given only a shallow map of the floor nearest the boiler: the tiles that are safe and the tiles that are not and the places where the powder of the world of the roof of Sheol-on-the-Shelf is thicker than any of them have imagined before.

The Listeners learn that the map of the inside of their heads can be heard as well as seen.

When they sit with one hand on the boiler shell and the other in the powder of the roof, they do not only hear the pressure of the boiler world and the temperature of the boiler world and the long hours of the boiler world that stretch between the last real change of the boiler world and the next change that will not be real change but will be the next of the not-changes that the boiler of the world has always been.

They also hear the shape of the map.
