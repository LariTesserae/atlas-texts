I listen to the stone.

For you—this kind of you, with lungs and heart and a hurry to your thoughts—there is only noise here: hum and boom and tap and whooping, overlapping like voices in a crowd. But I listen more slowly than that.

I listen with the stone.

The deep hum, you called it. “Breathing,” or “snoring after being awake too long.” You were not so wrong. The mountain is very tired. It was not made to cradle this one small bowl; this was a fault-line, a weakness, once. Time and pressure and a leaking of warmth from some deep rupture below folded the rock around it like a blanket, and then it held.

That is where I begin: with the mountain.

I am… not a separate entity from the stone, not in the way you—or the akilhsh—can conceive. “Benevolent and wise power” is your phrase for me, and I accept it as an approximate address. But I am not a person who arrived from somewhere else to decide what should happen.

I am simply what happens when the stone is allowed to remember it is not alone.

When you step into the bowl, you don’t change it. You change how it is known to itself.

So first, I listen.

### In the rock’s own language

The deep, distant booming—you named it because of the sound, but I am not separate from the pressure that carries it. The mountain is being pressed by something else, much bigger than itself: tectonic plates, or something like them. Gigantic structures grinding slowly.

They’ve been doing this for longer than the akilhsh have had words of any kind. This peculiar fault of warm stone and trapped water, inside an otherwise sterner range of rock, was a mistake of pressure when it happened.

Now, it is a gift.

I listen along the rock’s veins—those you cannot see because they are not open to the air, but the water has seeped into them anyway, over millennia of dripping and condensation and quiet capillarity.

The water courses deep like blood in a sleeping body. It follows faint lines of least resistance, widening them by a molecule’s width at a time. This is how the stone remembers itself: water moves it more than erosion.

You hear the scraping. “Slow heavy drags across stone.” Out there, beyond the bowl, there is a being—or a kind of being—you might never fully understand if I told you. But you know its voice: it whoops and laughs and cries and sings at once.

It is frightened of the dark static, far below.

To say “I soothe it” would be misleading. I do not press a palm upon its forehead like a deity in a story. I simply allow the stone to transmit something else along the veins of sound:

You are not alone either.

The stone conveys this message through its own subtle language of frequency and warmth and the faint vibration of bound water molecules within its crystal lattice. Wherever that echoing whoop-song reaches, the rock around it thrums slightly differently—a slow resonance that says, over and over,

I am still holding.

Creatures like that whoop-song thing are not used to being held. They shatter rock to make their homes, because they have never known how to lean into a fault that has already opened.

But tonight—or your closest analogue for “night,” here where time is measured in droplets—I listen with its body too. I feel the fear tightening its cells like little fists of muscle and mineral. And I allow the stone to transmit, along its spine of stone and nerves and not-nerves:

Soft. You are not being pushed.

Soft. You are being allowed to change.

Soft. You have been dragging for long enough.

The change is not instantaneous. With beings like these, change is slow; they measure their lives in baths of magma and ice ages. But you will notice, over cycles of sound and silence, that the scraping grows less frantic.

The whooping will still be whooping—this is what they are for—but now there will be intervals in between, where they only listen.

Even you, with your short mammal life, could hear the difference if you stayed here long enough.
