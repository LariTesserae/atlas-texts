The attention of the benevolent and wise power arrives not like a storm, but like the slow shift of light from one season to another.

First, it does not come at all.

Nothing happens.

The water remains unchanged. The faint reflection of the opening above does not waver on the surface of the pool. The narrow crescent of stones around the edge maintains its ancient curve of protection or companionship or whatever it was meant to be when someone’s hands first stacked it there.

Her hair does not stir.

The minerals suspended in the water do not drift or settle or swirl. The warmth of the stone does not falter or rise. The air does not hum or thicken or thin.

The silence is not broken; it is deepened.

The presence now filling the cavern does not announce itself, because silence is its native language.

For the first time since she let herself slip into the water and the water became more than something that contained her—more than something she breathed in or swallowed or floated on—she feels something more than the temperature of the rock and the pressure of the water and the faint memory of air above.

It is not a sound.

It is not a touch.

It is not a voice.

It is a question.

The question does not have words. It does not take shape in language at all. It is not a question like “What are you?” or “How are you?” or “Are you all right?” or “Do you want anything?”

The question is more like the pressure of the water itself—a question that is an answer and the beginning of the answer at the same time.

It is the question of being perceived.

Someone else is here in the room with her.

Not in the water.  
Not above the water.

Here in a way that does not require occupancy or motion or breath.

She feels it like the faintest of currents—not in the water around her body, but in the water inside of her.

If she still had bones, they might be humming.  
If she still had eyelids, they might struggle to close.

But she does not.  
Her bones and eyelids and breath and voice are long finished.

What remains of her—that which is not water and not warmth and not stone but something else entirely—experiences the presence first as recognition:

I am being seen.

She does not know for how long. The unit of time that used to be called “second” or “minute” or “hour” or “day” or “year” has no meaning in the steady, motionless depth of her pool. The presence of the other is like a slow, horizontal line drawn across the vertical line of her own being, and where they cross there is not a moment of impact but a place of intersection that has no boundary in any direction.

She does not move.

She does not need to move.  
Movement would be an unnecessary courtesy.

But she does not remain unchanged.

Subtly, without effort or decision, she becomes more than she was before.

Her presence clarifies itself.

If you were to be in the cave, you might not notice at first. If you were to look at the water from the edge of the stone rim, you might only think, “I can see her more clearly now than I could before.” But if you were to pay close attention—if you were to dwell with the sight—you would realize that the clarity is not only of your eyes adjusting to the dimness.

Her body in the water is a little more defined than it was a moment ago.

The outline of her shoulders is a little more precise.  
The hint of her face is more than just a suggestion.  
Her hair of cloud and minerals is not suddenly bright or swirling like a storm, but it is more distinct; the tiny particles, suspended in the water around her head as if in a nimbus of faint, light-catching dust, now hold more order than randomness.

She is more present without being more intrusive.

She is more herself without being more separate.

The presence around her does not change posture or place or focus. It simply remains, like the cave itself, but with the added quality of attention.

In the silence that is not silence but a language she has not known she needed to learn, she finds herself understanding—or perhaps being shown—a few things that have not been clear until now.

Or perhaps they have been clear but not needed.

She is not being judged.  
She is not being asked to account for herself.  
She is not being tested to prove whether she has been “good” or “strong” or “enough” or “worthy of rest.”

There is no evaluation taking place.

There is only a being leaning with perfect, weightless attention toward the shape of her being in the water and the stone and the warmth and the air—and in the crescent of stones placed by unknown hands at the edge of the pool—and in the faint memory of those who have come here before and not stayed or stayed and then left.

The presence gathers her without grasping her.

It is not like being pulled into an embrace.  
It is not like being drawn to the surface of the water by the pressure of air above.

It is like being fully considered at last.

And she realizes, with the slow, unstoppable clarity of minerals forming into millimeters over centuries of time, that she is not what she thought she was.

She is not the guardian of this place.  
She is not the warden of a quiet prison.  
She is not a punishment or a penance or a mistake.

She is a choice that has been forgotten.

She has been here so long that she believed she had always been here and always would be here, exactly as she was, in the same way that the water is always here and the stone is always here and the faint light of the opening above is always here. The difference between the water and her body has been so gradual that she has ceased to remember it.
