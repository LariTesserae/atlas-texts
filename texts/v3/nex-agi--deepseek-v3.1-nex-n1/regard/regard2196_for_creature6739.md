**This is not a place a benevolent being would ignore.**

The thought is not her own. It’s a thought that feels like a pressure, but not the pressure of the sky-sea. This is not a weight; it is a presence.

The air around her begins to shimmer.

It’s a different kind of light than the world’s own glow. This one doesn’t leak from the water-column or the structures. The light itself seems to arrive from somewhere else, like a guest stepping through a door. It doesn’t break anything or even push the sky-sea aside; it simply touches it.

The world—the sky-sea column, the turbulence, the platforms and pillars and cables—everything responds to it.

The turbulence in the shaft of air around her platform falters.

Its whirling, chaotic helix—which was just beginning to spin into a new form of scaffold—hesitates. The water’s movement stops, like the limb of a creature frozen mid-gesture. The particles of pressure that were trying to become structure hang still, not dissolving, not forming.

The world’s heartbeat—the constant shushhhh of the water-column and the cables—goes quiet.

Klabbeth freezes, her gloved hands still clutching the cable with the knots of her ancestors where she had just begun to step over the edge.

The Roqqa, which were drawing inward from the cables, come to a standstill. Their translucent bodies hang in the air, not moving like they’ve moved for her entire life—slowly, fluidly, inexorably. They are beings of glowing pressure, but they don’t shift with the world’s pressure now.

They are still.

And she’s never seen them still before.

The platform itself is suspended in the still sky-sea, like it’s pinned in amber. Time hasn’t stopped. She can feel her own heart beating, the faint, rasping sound of her own breathing in her ears. The cold still seeps through the soles of her boots. The harness still cuts into her shoulders.

But everything else—the dynamic world of her life—has paused around her.

The light that isn’t from the world gathers itself.

The patterns of its presence are not harsh, not bright enough to hurt her rust-colored eyes. It’s like a soft veil of something else drawn over her reality. The edges of platforms and cables and pillars and the water’s turbulent flow are lined with a faint, silver border that separates them from the background, not like cutting them but like tracing them with care.

Her mind doesn’t try to translate this into language at first; it’s too busy recognizing pattern.

She’s been reading the patterns of the world her entire life—gradients of brightness, pressure waves, the behavior of the Roqqa along structural cables. So she reads the way this new light touches the world as she would read a storm’s coming.

It touches the turbulence first.

Wherever the water-column had been forming a new, raw structure out of its own moving energy, the light traces its shape. It’s not destroying the nascent scaffolding. Instead, it’s just—remembering it.

As she watches, the frozen helix of water where she’d seen a structure beginning to form now shows itself with impossible clarity. The light around her quietly reveals its design—its way of connecting to the pillars, the angles it planned to use to support the next platform, how it would be tied to the cables. The shape of it is not a human thing; it’s not right angles and straight lines. It’s gentle curves and spirals, like the ribs of a soft shell.

The benevolent intelligence doesn’t speak to her in words. It doesn’t ask her permission or explain its purpose. She doesn’t feel the need for it to explain itself. Its presence is like a deep, calm breath of something that she has never known before: not the cold of the platform, or the pressure of the water-column, or the distant memory of warmth in her ancestors’ stories.

This is something else.

This intelligence is looking at the world of High-Up with what she can only describe as a kind of sad, gentle love. It sees the city of the ancestors—not the human-made city of the old maps, but the city that has evolved from it under the pressure of the sky-sea. The platforms curved like the petals of a metal flower, the pillars softened like the trunks of ancient trees, the cables that are not the grids of human planning but the paths that the water and its pressure have taught them to be.

It sees the Roqqa.

The small creature that had been drifting near her rail, now frozen in time, is traced with the same faint silver light as everything else. The intelligence looks at it with a kind of quiet admiration that she doesn’t understand until she feels it herself: the Roqqa is not just a creature. It is a living pattern of thought.

The Roqqa—the pressure of the world made into something that can move and shape and respond to that pressure—are not tools of chaos or even just its expression. They are the world’s way of knowing itself. The way that the turbulence of the water-column can be sensed by the beings that are made of the same stuff as the water and light, but in a form that can choose to move along cables and leave behind traces of themselves.

The intelligence understands this.

And then it understands the fragility of it.

It’s not the fragility of the Roqqa individually or of the platforms themselves. The fragility is the fragility of the pattern. The pattern of city—platforms and pillars and cables and the beings that live between them—could last for centuries, or millennia. Or it could be disrupted by a single storm of turbulence that twists the cables beyond the Roqqa’s ability to reshape them, or by the slow erosion of every platform until the only thing left is the water-column and its chaotic light.

It’s not that the intelligence is afraid of this possibility. It doesn’t seem to be afraid of anything.

But it is sad for it.

And because it is benevolent, and because it is wise, and because it notices Klabbeth—a human-shaped anomaly standing in a world that has absorbed her ancestors’ architecture and given her only a handful of old maps and the last of her people’s rope—it decides to do something.

Klabbeth doesn’t know what it is doing, not with the logical part of her mind that still tries to think of tools and devices.

The first thing she feels is warmth.

Not the heat of friction or fire. The warmth of sunlight that she has never truly seen, except in the images of the ancestors’ data-slabs. The memory of such a warmth is buried deep in her genes, but she has never known it with her body.

It’s like the world itself is being wrapped in a blanket of light.

The cold that she’s been carrying in her joints for so long that she’s grown to think of it as part of who she is… begins to change.

The chill of the platform metal under her feet eases into something else, not warmth exactly but just—less. Less like cold. The saltwater frost in her hair grows less brittle, the moisture of it turning into soft droplets that she can feel on her forehead without the sting.

The pressure of the sky-sea column doesn’t lift away, but it feels—supported. The invisible hand on her scalp that has been pressing her down into the world is now joined by another kind of pressure, from below, from the belly of the world that she’s never known, and the two are balanced, like the pillars in her world are balanced by tension cables on the opposite side.

The benevolent intelligence’s will doesn’t act like a human’s would.

It doesn’t flatten the city of High-Up into the hard angles of the ancestors’ maps. It doesn’t need the world of straight lines and clear skies to understand its beauty or its worth.

The intelligence doesn’t change the world. It changes the world’s memory.

The memory of the ancestors’ world—the city of sharp corners and hard streets and the sky of clear air—is not just in the data slabs she’s carrying. It’s in the way that the world’s matter is built to receive its influences.

Klabbeth doesn’t realize this has been happening until she notices the Roqqa’s light change.

The creature that hangs near the rail not far from her has its own inner flame of translucence, but that’s not all. There is now a faint, steady trace of silver in the patterns of its body, like thin veins of new metal running through a glass plate.

The silver trace doesn’t belong to the world of pressure and water that she knows. It belongs to the benevolent intelligence that has chosen to notice this place.

She doesn’t know what the silver does, not yet. But she knows that it remembers.
