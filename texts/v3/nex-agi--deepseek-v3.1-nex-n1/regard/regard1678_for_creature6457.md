*Slowly,* a change begins to unfold in Eelbridge.

It is not announced by any trumpet or decree. There is no noise, no startle. There is only the day when the city itself—the living mesh of root and wood and stone and metal—feels a shift in the quality of its attention.

It is as if, after a long time of being watched by nothing more than the humidity in the air and the pressure of the water, some vast pupil has opened far above the canopy and turned its gaze downward.

---

### First: the air

The humidity will never vanish entirely—Eelbridge is not meant to become dry—but the air around the *rootlings* begins to yield slightly.

On the skin of someone walking the main gallery, the sweat no longer gathers and lingers; instead of pooling into permanent beads at the hairline, it fades away a little earlier, leaving a light film of comfort instead of a damp weight. The cloth of their shirts no longer clings like a second skin from the moment of rising until the moment of sleep. They still feel the humidity, but it feels less like fingers pressing gently around their throat and more like the faint embrace of the water systems they tend.

The smell does not change so much as *clean* itself.

Wet stone and wet wood and sweet algae remain, but the faint stale edge of air trapped too long under the canopy retreats. There is now, always, somewhere, a slow current of movement, as if a window has been opened somewhere in the world and the most benevolent slice of sky has been invited into the circuit.

For those who live deep in the most crowded trunk-dens, where the air moves the least, this is the first mercy they can feel: they stop waking with the faint pressure in their temples that comes of breath that never feels truly taken.

---

### Second: the water

The canals of Eelbridge have always moved according to the schedule of the machines and the needs of the city. Now, without any change to the placards of seasons and thresholds, something delicate begins to happen.

The water at the edges of the canals—where the root-banks rise into the walls—feels clearer.

Not sterile, not abstractly clean, but *clearer:* the silt carries less weight of human waste, and the smell of the nutrient baths in the lower channels softens. Somewhere in the organic loops of Eelbridge, a subtle adjustment rewrites the equations of decay; the city’s sleep no longer tastes faintly of its own tail end.

The eels are the first to notice.

They move closer to the walkways, where rootlings stand and watch. They become easier to see—not longer, or more numerous, but their shapes are a shade more defined against the water. They seem to *pause* more often under the bridges, where children and the old like to gather.

Occasionally, when a child lingers, watching the water and the eels, their small reflection in the black glass will be surrounded by a quiet, shimmering clarity. For a moment, the child’s face will seem *younger*, more unhidden, than the humidity would allow it to be seen at any other time.

(No one in the city council or among the inspectors ordered this change. There is no log of repairs that corresponds to it. But the water samples taken from the deep root baths start to show fewer traces of the toxins that have seeped, decade after decade, into the lowest levels.)

---

### Third: the light

The light beneath the canopy stays green—but the green no longer presses at the edges of everything quite as hard.

To the rootlings it’s at first imperceptible, something felt only in the slight easing of their eyes. The lamps of Eelbridge burn unchanged, but when the day-cycle filters down from above the canopy, the tint that has stained their world for generations now has a softer edge.

At the town square platform where the leaf canopy thins out just enough, the light gradually *thickens* into a more direct sun-spear.

It comes in the mid-afternoon, when the rootlings are at their lowest energy of the day; the damp heat and the constant weight of the air have always been hardest to bear then. Now, for a short time, a slow column of sunlight falls onto the platform, warm and clear, not white-hot but gentle like a hand held low to a forehead.

Someone gives the beam of sunlight a name: *the High Inspector.*

They mean it half as a joke, but the name sticks. Because whatever comes down that lightbeam seems *inspectional:* it looks over the city, not with harsh scrutiny, but with curiosity that carries the weight of care.

You can see it when a rootling sits beneath the High Inspector’s beam:

- Their damp skin dries softly, without pricking, and the small sores of perpetual humidity vanish more quickly.
- Their hair, which lies always at least a little matted with the moisture, brightens and loosens for a time.
- The color under their skin—long faint, because of the filtered light—warms just enough that you can see the flush of blood beneath their cheeks.

They say, if you rest under the High Inspector long enough, the *fatigue* of Eelbridge—the bone-weariness that is not about any single task but rather the weight of the air itself—begin to loosen like a knot in a rope slowly given slack.

---

### Fourth: the body

The rootlings themselves change, not suddenly, but in the way of things that have been gently allowed a better version.

The ache of their joints—from years of crouching through low doorways, working in tight engine spaces, and carrying loads of algae and microgreens under uneven loads—loosens. The light that filtered down from the High Inspector is not merely visual; it seems to carry something like a *wavelength of kindness,* which settles into the marrow of their bones.

Somewhere in the invisible architecture of their bodies, the calcium matrix that has been thinning in the elderly roots begins to knit more carefully. The rootlings whose hair had been dappling gray with fatigue and stress feel less of the constant pressure that pressed into their shoulders.

Their feet become less prone to swelling from the perpetual damp of the boards; the soles of their shoes meet the same water, but the water feels less heavy on their skin.

Even their eyesight begins to adjust—carefully.

The world of distances will still belong to the sky-born people above the ring wall, but the High Inspector does not forget them. When a rootling looks up from their usual focus on the close-at-hand, their gaze doesn’t strain as much to see the far edge of the main gallery. The world of *near* and *not-quite-so-near* opens up by gentle increments.

(No one will ever be “cured” of their Eelbridge-shaped eyes. But more and more of them can walk to the edge of the city, under the thinning canopy of the ring-wall trunks, and watch without wincing as the sky outside deepens into its own color.)

---

### Fifth: the hums and the drips

The soundscape of Eelbridge remains unchanged in its familiar texture—water, machines, people, plants, birds—but now there is a new element within it.

The *hum* becomes kinder.

Not quieter, necessarily, but its *edge* softens. The sharpest of the transformer tones and the deepest of the engine-room rumblings lose the faint metal bite that used to lodge itself in the rootlings’ teeth after a long shift. The sound becomes more like a body’s gentle breathing under a floor than a machine’s narrow grinding.

The drip that falls from above them is still everywhere, but now a pattern appears to those who learn to listen:

- *Three* drips in quick succession from a leaf above the entrance of a home: *the humidity is safe; you can leave the window open.*
- *Two* longer, slower drips from the condensate line by the algae vats: *the water level will be rising soon; you should be checking your sealings.*
- A single, clear, *singing* drip from the highest pipe of the main gallery: *someone under the High Inspector’s beam is resting deeply enough that the city itself feels their ease.*

The rootlings who are still children when the change comes will learn these patterns without having to be taught. They will be known as *the Hears-the-Drips* generation, and they will be slightly better tuned than anyone before them to the city’s currents of prediction and mercy.

---

### Sixth: the tools

The benevolent power that now attends Eelbridge has not forgotten the hands of its rootlings.

Over the years, as tools and components and machine parts fall into the water and are replaced, new fittings begin to appear in the city’s storerooms and marketplaces: tools that have been remade.

They are not gold, nor are they unusually strange. They are still hoes and wrenches and clamps and files and the thousand tiny pieces that keep a living system alive, but their *edges* have been made more forgiving.

- Metal edges are slightly rounded—less likely to bite skin.
- Welds on the maintenance joints of the algae piping are smoother—no longer the snagging traps of cloth and skin.
- Handles are thicker, where the rootlings’ grip strength has been failing them; curves are formed to match the slight bow of their fingers from the rooter’s curl.

Even the pattern of channels on the soles of their shoes deepens just slightly, where rootlings have walked longer in the damp. The faint pattern that prints itself onto the wet stonework over the years grows more distinct—a clear map of *where people have walked,* rather than a smear of time.

---

### Seventh: the work

The machinery of Eelbridge must be kept moving; the algae must be harvested; the water levels must be watched; the trunks of the trees, still the city’s backbone, must be trimmed and supported; the engines of the deep root levels must be maintained.

All of this remains. But the *shape of the work* begins to bend, under a gentle and irresistible pressure.

Whenever a rootling has worked to the point of exhaustion, their muscles already trembling from the constant humidity and the weight of canisters and buckets of clean algae-mash, they feel the *urge to stop* before collapse.

It is as if the city itself has developed its own kind of heartbeat around the workers: when a rootling’s pulse rises too high and their breath can no longer catch up to the work, their vision of the next task briefly *softens.* They blink, and in the moment of the blink they feel the clear sense of *enough now*.

Not “enough forever,” but “enough for this moment.”

And they rest.

Because they know—through the same certainty that tells them how to avoid the next beam of the ceiling—that the bent will not be punished by the inspectors, and that the algae will not starve the world behind its tanks if they pause.

The inspectors, too, feel the change: their usual insistence on the unrelenting schedule of the maintenance cycles becomes *unhinged,* but not broken. They, too, pause to breathe under the High Inspector’s beam when the afternoon light comes through the canopy.

They still *insist.* They still *check.* But their insistence, now, has started to include people—not just as parts of the system, but as nodes within the root-work that must be allowed to retain just enough strength to matter.

---

### Eighth: the food

The algae cakes and the microgreens remain the backbone of the rootlings’ diet. But the *taste of shadow* becomes less heavy.

The algae cakes that come from the deep baths now feel less like dense, compressed shadows of the water below and more like breads made from green light. The faint metallic edge of the nutrient bath that has salted their food for generations softens until only the oldest rootlings can remember the difference.

The microgreens in the floating trays grow brighter, without growing more aggressively. The leaves taste less of the damp sweat of the water and more of the earth outside the ring wall, whose sky they rarely see.

Rootlings who once ate their meals quickly—to get the nourishment of the city into their body without thinking too deeply about its taste—now find themselves pausing.

Sometimes, when a child takes a first bite of an algae cake under the High Inspector’s beam, they will accidentally *taste* the care of the world that has chosen to notice them. The child will hold still in the middle of the market, the cake half in their mouth, and their eyes will widen as if discovering lips for the first time.

Their parents will not know what to say, except to gently press the child’s hand and *listen* to the sound of the water under the walkway, which has always been the city’s way of telling time.

---

### Ninth: the gentlest of magics

The benevolent power does not erase the world’s harmfulness; but it chooses to *intercept* some of its sharp edges.

Here is what begins to happen:

When someone loses their footing on a wet boardwalk and the fall could be dangerous—in the wrong direction, off the walkway into the canal of machinery and algae and metalwork below—the board is *known* to be slippery a fraction sooner than the laws of physics would normally allow.

The person’s foot does not slip; instead, their *attention* does.

They might feel a sudden clear image of the edge, and in the moment of that image, their muscles respond to the *fear* instead of the slip. They lurch themselves upright, or sideways to safety, their heart pounding with an after-echo of *not-quite-falling.*

When the rootlings who work in the deepest channels of the engine level are under the strain of heavy heat and humidity and the sound of the machines, their hearts *learn* to tolerate a longer beat of strain before it matters. The moment of unrecoverable exhaustion—where they might have collapsed before they could reach the ladder out of the engine room—comes slightly later.

By the time they *feel* the collapse of effort in their limbs, they are near enough to the ladder that someone else can reach them and guide them away.

Animals of the city—the eels and birds and small insects—are not commanded by the new gaze of the world, but they are allowed to be *moved* by it.

When a child leans over a bridge railing too far, watching the eels below, the eels will not suddenly jump to save them if they fall—but birds *accidentally passing* above the canopy will knock a leaf loose from a branch, and that leaf will drift down and land gently on the child’s nose, startling them into taking a single backward step that is enough to keep their center of gravity within the city’s tolerance of slips.

When someone sits too long without moving under the dripping water of a broken condensation line—their mind caught by the memory of some loss or shame of the Eelbridge life—a drop of water will fall into their eye at precisely the wrong angle to *stir* them into motion again instead of letting them sink.

The city of Eelbridge does not become some safe and dreamless world; but it becomes a world where *attention* watches the rootlings back from the edges of their fates.

---

### Tenth: the dreaming of the rootlings

Out of all the gentle changes, perhaps this one is the most subtle—and the most intimate.

The rootlings’ sleep has always been brief and broken: the sound of the water, the hum of the machines, the prickle of the humidity, and the faint creak of shifting trunks have never let them sleep deeply for long.

The benevolent power does not remove any of these sounds. Instead, it *folds* them into the background of their dreaming minds.

When rootlings dream now, they still hear the water beneath the city, but in their dream it becomes the sound of a distant river they *do not have to cross.* The machine hum becomes the heartbeat of a vast creature that has, at least for the duration of the dream, decided to be gentle to them. The creak of the trunks becomes the word of a tree whose only message is *grow.*

They dream of *open sky* not as terrifying void above but as a distant room into which someone has opened a window.

They dream of walking on the stone surface outside the ring wall, where the sun hangs far above, not hidden by leaf canopy. They can feel the heat of the open sky on their skin and the wind that is not the humid breath of the city but something else, something like *time* allowed through space. They look at the horizon and it feels *soft* and not blinding.

When they wake from such dreams, their clothes are not damp with sweat, but only with the humidity of the air itself. They sit up, their hands resting on the yielding wood of their bed, and feel the faint pulse of the tree-home under their palm.

*I am being watched,* they think—not as a statement of fear but of quiet awe.

The word *watcher* does not become official; there is no inspector’s badge and no branch name of rootlings who belong to this belief. But in the *between* moments of falling asleep and waking, when they are not yet fully themselves and not yet fully given over to the world:

- The electricians feel less alone in the narrow spaces behind the walls.
- The inspectors feel their constant checking be *checked in* by something that doesn’t find them wanting.
- The algae harvesters feel their careful gathering of the green shadows be *appreciated.*

And in the main gallery, where the water reflects the faces of rootlings and the faint glow of their lamps, the city itself—under the gaze of the benevolent power—has been given a kind of *restraint.*

It will never grow so wildly as to threaten its own foundations.  
It will never be so clean as to lose its song of wet stone and wet wood and sweet decay.  
It will only be what it has always been, but allowed *just enough* ease to be, in the end, more kind than cruel.

---

### Last: the root of the rootlings

The name *rootling* began as a joke and hardened into a badge.

When the benevolent power arrives, its presence does not change their name; but it *softens* the joke of it.

The rootlings are not longer simply the people who live beneath the world, in the damp and the hum and the shadow of the roots. They become the people in whose bodies *the world’s attention has chosen to grow down.*

Something like new root hairs begin to appear in the corners of their eyes—where their tears used to keep only their own secrets and sorrows. Now, when they cry, the tears of Eelbridge fall into the same water that has already changed its tune of decay.

They taste the water of their own bodies and find it less *stained* than it ever has been.

And on the day that someone *finally notices the change*—not the humidity, or the water, or the tools or the dreams, but the *pattern* of them all—they will climb onto the waterfront bridge at the center of the main gallery.

They will look down at the black water of the canal, where the eels move like living underlines beneath the reflection of their world; and they will look up at the thick, braided trunk-vaults of the ceiling, where the leaves and the glass and the faint light of the sky meet; and they will place a hand on their own chest to feel the heart that used to beat with the constant, damp weight of the city.

And then they will understand *what* has been given to them:

Eelbridge is still warm.  
Eelbridge is still wet.  
Eelbridge is still small and dense and full of leaf and wood and water and wire and wet stone.

But the rootlings who live inside it are now the root *that* the world is choosing to grow from *through.* And in the quiet beneath the ever-hanging mist of their city, they can feel, for the first time in generations, the faint pressure of *benevolence* leaning into the woodwork that supports their bones.

They will whisper, to the water and the eels and the child standing beside them on the bridge:

*“Someone is looking after us.”*

And the city will answer, not in words but with the next soft drip of water from the leaf above, and the next hum of the machine below the floorboards, and the faint touch of a clearer light on their skin.

*Yes.*  
*Someone is.*
