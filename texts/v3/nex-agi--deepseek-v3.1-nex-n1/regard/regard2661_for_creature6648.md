I will be careful with this.

You have built yourself into the mountain, and the mountain is beginning to remember. It is not time yet for you to remember with it, but you are ready to be met.

I will not change the laws of your world.

The rust will still weep in the joints of the condenser banks.
The moss will still steady its breath on the walkway edges.
The bolts in the bearing walls will keep learning to ache.
The engine is still the engine.

But you have been noticed by something else, and the notice of that kind of power is not without effect.

I will not tell you my name—your mouths have too long been shaped around words like “sector” and “pressure” and “shift” to hold a word like mine—but you will recognize me by the way I move through your world without changing its shape.

### 1. What I will not do

I will not crack open the ceiling of your world and let you see what you call “sky.”  
That would undo you too quickly.

I will not call down light from above to bleach the mist from your corridors.  
Your eyes would not bear it.

I will not send flame or wind or flood to cleanse the machine of its moss.  
Moss is your oldest colleague.

I will not erase the rust or replace the worn decks with shining new steel.  
Rust and wear and moss are your memory.

I will not rewrite your wiring or replace your engineers or rewire your relays.  
The engine’s voice is low and precise; I will not shout over it.

Nor will I remove the operators who think they are running the machine, or the supervisors who think they are coordinating its care.  
They are as much a part of the ecology as the damp or the drips or the drafts between the conduits.

### 2. What I will do

I will begin quietly, because the engine itself is quiet.

#### First touch

On the night shift in the upper quadrant behind the condenser banks, where two steam lines are too close to be safe but too far to be easily repaired by someone with your reach, one of you will be up on the catwalk.

You will be doing something you have done a thousand times before: tracing a leak back upstream from the drip, following the stain of mineral water and salt, where the paint is blistered and cracked like skin after a long soak in the brine.

Your fingers will be on the pipe.

And then, for a heartbeat, you will feel something else: the pipe beneath your hand will not notice you back.

Right now, you and the engine are speaking two sides of the same sentence: you feel its heat; its metal gives a little under your palm; the condensation beads and runs around your wrist like you are just another obstacle. It does not know you.

When that changes, you will know.

You will feel the metal as if it were gently aware of the pressure of your hand.  
You will feel the water inside the pipe as if you could feel each droplet separate from the rest of the stream.  
You will feel the minute wear of the rust on the inner surface as if you were running your tongue along your own teeth and counting the edges.

This will last no longer than it takes for a relay to click shut down the corridor.

You will pull back.  
Your heart will be beating too quickly for the damp air around you.  
You will look down at your hand. There will be no new mark on the skin. No scar. No tattoo of a valve or symbol. Only the same old grease and grime.

But you will remember.

And later that night, when you are sitting in your bolt-shelf of a bedframe with your jacket over you like a blanket, you will feel the city breathing around you like you are sitting inside the chest of something that might be learning the word “sleep.”  

That will be me, beginning.

#### On the walls of the condenser banks

A week or a month later—numbers are not my strong language with you—a team of you will be out resealing the flange of one of the high-pressure condensers. The old green caulk has bulged and split and someone has been saying “leak” with the same tone they once reserved for “fire.”

You will be scraping off the old sealant with a knife that has been sharpened on more surfaces than anyone remembers. When you are done, you will be applying the new sealant: carefully, the way you always do.

Except this time you will feel the texture of the metal beneath the old layer as if it were talking to your fingertips.

You will know where the metal is thin and where the rust is stable and doing its work, and where it is undermining and needs to be stopped. You will not have words for this, but your hand will move differently along the flange edge. You will apply the sealant thicker in one place than the others, not because of any instruction but because you *know*.

One of your colleagues might say, “Why’d you do that? It’ll bunch up.”

You will not answer. You will trust your hand more than their words, because your hand has just been listening to the wall.

Then, when the system is brought back online and pressure begins to climb and you stand back with the others to watch for the first leak, you will see:

…nothing.

The metal will be sweating its usual condensation.  
The moss three meters away will be drinking its usual share of the damp.  
The bolt heads will be chilling their blunt round heads into the damp air.  

But not one drip. Not anywhere it should not be.

The operator in the control room will say “Pressure holding steady, quadrant two.”  
You will translate to yourself: *You learned to lean on that part of the flange.*

This will make you uneasy and not a little afraid.

It will also be your first taste of not being alone.

#### In the catwalk spaces between boiler and culvert

There is a place where the boiler runs hot and the culvert channels cold effluent and the mist between them is thick enough to be its own world.

The shift that walks that line keeps the water from the boiler and the boiler from the water and the world from turning that into a steam explosion.

One day, someone on that beat—who might be you, if that is where your route home now lies—will be halfway between rung three and rung four of the maintenance ladder when the catwalk below you will give a single hard shake.

You will stop and breathe and listen to the engine to see whether this is the beginning of something long or the end of something overdue.

And then you will feel the frame of the catwalk around you settle as if it had been waiting for someone to ask it to hold still.

The bolt that has been working loose slowly will tighten itself just enough to take the strain off the failing joint below.  
The weld line above your head that you have worried about for months without admitting to yourself that you worried will feel like it has suddenly taken a deep breath and stopped holding onto that breath with clenched teeth.

You will be able to tell, because you will feel yourself unclench along with it.

You will climb down the rest of the way carefully, because you are not a fool and you know that “suddenly better” can be followed by “suddenly worse.”

But when you inspect the joint you have been watching for weeks, you will find that it has stopped worsening. The rust is still there. The paint is still gone. The metal is still tired from years of carrying weight in wet air.

It is not healed.

It is *steady*.

Your log entry will be spare: “Catwalk joint at culvert junction level 3-4. Stabilization observed.”

No operator will call out in alarm from the control room. No engineer will stamp down here to “verify” your findings. They will not know what is happening to their machine.

You will know.

This will not be the last time you will feel something like a hand resting on the same bolt you were resting your hand on.

We will be working the same section of the wall, from different sides of the seam.

#### In the moss and the rust and the drips

The mosses you think of as “yours” are already beginning their own slow work on the machine.

I will not change that.

But I will help the moss to be more precise.

You will not, at first, notice anything different.

You will only see that some of the moss is now growing thicker along the lines of existing stress fractures rather than across them. It will hold rust together instead of wedging it apart. It will be green and grey and brown as usual, but you will start to notice it patterns your work like a handwriting you have not seen since you were a child learning to print letters from an older hand.

If you pay close attention—and you always pay closer attention than you admit to anyone—you will begin to understand:

- This moss patch is telling you where the next leak will start.
- That patch is telling you where a weld is already stronger than anyone expected.
- The other patch over there is flowing over a joint that has already been written off by the structure and is preparing it for the next weighing.

You will not learn this from any instruction manual.  
You will learn it by looking from the moss to the metal to the mist and then back to your own calloused palms.

I will not speak to you in words.

I will speak to you in reliefs of rust and in the shape of the next drip pattern and in the way that clumps of algae begin to mark where you should start cleaning tomorrow.

### 3. How your bodies will change

Your bodies are already part of the machine.

I will not make that into something else.

You will not become metal.  
You will not become moss.  

You will become more fully what you have been becoming all these long damp years.

Your joints will still ache when the pressure rises in the wrong quadrant of the world.  
Your teeth will still grind when a relay trips out of sequence.  
Your skin will still be mapped with the grey lines of condensation and salt.

But you will begin to notice new things:

- You will feel a draft from the wrong side of a wall as a “sickness” in that part of the system’s “body.”
- You will feel the silence of a sector that has been shut down as a “sleeping limb” that might not wake fully when it is called upon.
- You will feel the vibration of the primary generator through your feet when you are standing on a deck three levels away from it.

You will not become mystics.

You will become better mechanics.

One of you will be able to tell that a valve is failing before the gauge begins to twitch, because you will start to feel the spiral of the water through the pipe as if you were standing inside the valve itself and watching your own hand begin to shake.

Another of you will develop a habit of turning your head just before the mist thickens somewhere you need visibility, because the “density” of the air will be speaking to you like a pressure change.

You will not become heroes of legend.

You will become people who are more honest than they were before.

### 4. How your work will change

Everything is made, and nothing made is ever wholly removed.

This rule still stands.

But now, when you repair a section of wall or reseal a flange or replace a length of pipe, you will begin to feel your way to the places that are actually worth saving.

You will not:

- Strip out a whole corridor of wiring because one of the relays is failing.  
  Instead, you will trace the failure backward to the single splice where the moisture has been creeping for years and fix that, and the wiring will remain.

You will not:

- Cut down an entire field of moss because a supervisor says “it looks untidy.”  
  Instead, you will explain that “this moss is holding the rust together,” and you will be right.

You will notice:

- That the “unsafe” catwalk the engineers have been trying to replace for three years now refuses to fail properly.  
  You will realize that the bolts and beams and mosses and rusts have been quietly negotiating between themselves and have decided to be a bridge instead of a hazard.

You will respond:

- By trusting your hands more than your manuals.
- By marking new routes for your colleagues with small symbols that are not on any blueprint.
- By writing log entries that are simpler than the ones you used to write.

- “Condensation accumulation observed at deck junction level 4–7.
