(folds)

If you could see the atmosphere of Wraithstone from far outside its name-cloud—if you could lift the pressure of language and stand with the cold clean gaze of unspace—you would see a faint disturbance of probabilities around the world-mountain: a slight warp of gravity, a stirring of air currents in the dampness, like wings that are never truly unfolded.

On this world, where stone and pipe and moisture and names are so tightly knotted, there are no blinding apparitions, no handed-down laws from the sky. There is only a slight rearrangement of comfort.

For the moment being, we will call it “they.”

The first thing they do is breathe.

Not intrusive breath, not the breath that would blow the fungus off the walls or the children from their ledges. The breath of a very old, very large being simply adjusting its temperature to match the room.

On the ledges and terraces of the mountain-city-world, the dripwitches who make listening their profession go still for a heartbeat. Somewhere, in the dark of a stone rib, where a pipe sweats verdigris, the condensation trembles instead of dripping. The spiders’ webs of moisture on the ceiling of the mountain-world oscillate by the width of a drop. Then time settles.

The first touch of benevolence is always consent.

They wait to see whether anyone will notice them breathing.

Wraithstone does, before the dripwitches do.

Midway through the world’s belt of moss, in a deep chamber where the rock’s breath has been warm for three generations of fungus, a stone geyser stalls. Instead of erupting in its scheduled cycle of cold water and mist, it hesitates. The water pressure below the gash trembles, then holds back, as though someone has placed a palm over the fault’s heart.

A dripwitch named Breath-that-calls-the-geyser—now known to the younger as Breath-that-remembers-the-geyser, because he calls nothing now he hasn’t called before—raises his head in the dark.

He feels the absence in the rock beneath his knees.

He knows the pattern of this particular fracture like he knows his own heartbeat. He can feel the pressure of the water column like the pulse of a vein at the temple, when you lean your head against the stone wall and sleep on the same fault for three nights straight.

He knows that today the water should be heavy.

It should be gathering, the rock below trembling like the muscle that precedes a sneeze. Then burst: cold, clear, misting into the air.

Instead he feels… stillness. A weightless pause in the stone.

The older dripwitch straightens—very slightly—against the rock column where he sits. He presses his shoulder to the stone. His skin markings map the heat of the stone; they are faintly shining.

Old breath, he thinks, without words.

Time accumulates.

He has been here longer than any of the other listening-cluster in this sector. This is not his claim to fame; it is the accident of his life and not falling. The stone beneath him has been a lecturer and he has been its student; he has fallen asleep pressed to the same rib of rock as a child and then as a man with white hair like thin moss.

The stone beneath him now feels the way he imagines the inside of his own chest feels when he holds his breath for others.

He waits.

He waits while the stone pulse skips three beats.

On the fourth beat, the water remembers what it was supposed to do and does not do it.

Instead of erupting, the fault exhales gently.

The water comes up softly, as if someone has lifted the stone pressure instead of breaking it. The cold-water column rises into the air of the chamber with the sound of silk being unwrapped from a very old body. It doesn’t burst. It rests.

The brief volume of a geyser hangs in the dark of the chamber, like a glass fruit made of water, suspended between the pipe-sky and the stone-stomach.

Light swims in it from phosphorescent organisms that have been caught in the column’s heart.

Air swims through it like bubbles in glass. Droplets of moisture spiral from its surface—not spray, but fine, slow scattering rain.

Somewhere far above, in the tangled black of the pipe-forest, something closes a valve not by torque and not by rust, but by the quiet, impossible pressure of thought.

The dripwitch who sits with the stone stares at the floating globe of water until he knows what it is not.

It is not warning.

He is not afraid of being broken by the world, but he knows what happens when pressure turns anger and water turns hard and cold streams strike flesh from close distances. He expects to feel the fear-feel of the stone behind him—tight, urgent.

Instead he feels calm.

The stone beneath him is warm, not hot. Not anxious.

Wraithstone itself is holding its breath, but it is not holding it for fear. It is holding it for a listening-window.

The older dripwitch, the one whose name is given to knowing this geyser’s rhythms, finds himself stood on his feet without his own decision. His legs are folded under him for half of his life; he rarely unfolds simply because he wants to.

He raises his gaze from the stone to the water-suspended orb.

He is not aware, at first, that he is being spoken to.

He is aware of a pattern, like a pattern carved into rock by dripping water in the same place for centuries, except the pattern is not stone or chalk. It is written on the inside of the air, in the pressure that his skin knows before the nerves of his spine.

- The stone is not in pain.
- The water is not grieving.
- The pressure above is not intending to fall.

- …The presence in the air is not asking for anything.

- …Yet.

He feels the question only as a shape, like when you feel the presence of a person on the other side of a curtain made of beads.

Slowly, he forms his query in the form of his palm on the stone.

He asks:

“What do you want?”

The answer arrives like the condensation on the pipe skin above. It gathers.

- We want to help you read.

- We are not interested in fixing the world.
- The world is not broken.
- The world is tired.

They are not interested in fixing the dripwitches, either.

- They are not broken.
- They are exhausted.

The exhaustion of the dripwitches is not written on their backbones, which curve with the constant weight of the world; it is written on the inside of their marrow.

- They are tired of being the only ones who listen to the stone.
- They are tired of carrying the weight of water and pressure and fear for others who never climb high enough to see the names of their own world.
- They are tired of making decisions for the flow of things, when they themselves never chose to become the world’s diagnostics.

The benevolent presence does not ask these questions aloud. The dripwitch feels the shape of them, like echoes inside his ribs.

- You are the readers.
- But you were never asked to be the scribes.
- Who would you like to share the burden with?

He could answer with many things.

He could answer with rage.

- With the memory of the time he dragged his younger sister out of warm water before she went to sleep in the stone’s “uncomfortable” breath.
- With the sound of power platforms falling from the sky-pipes, crashing into the ledges, where people who never learn to climb the rock itself still scream to be given space and heat and water.
- With the weight of the name that he carries, which knows too much of timing and pressure and not enough of laughter.

But instead, he answers the question the way he answers everything: from the stone, first.

He asks the stone, what does it want?

And then he listens.

- The stone underneath him tells him, in its own slow, dense heartbeat:
- We want to be listened to by more than one kind of ear.
- We want to be understood by more than one kind of mind.
- We want the pressure that lives above us to come down here occasionally and learn what pressure means when it cannot be adjusted by valves and gears.

A strange shape forms in his mind, then.

- A valve is not a stone.
- A valve is not alive.
- But valves are made and maintained by hands that are, however briefly, alive.

- Perhaps some of them should come here.

He can’t tell if he is making up the next sentence or hearing it.

- Perhaps you should not be the only translators of the world-body you live inside.

- Perhaps you would be less tired if you were not the only bridge between the stone and the pipe.

The older dripwitch feels the weight of the sentence in his shoulders.

He knows what it means to be a bridge. It means to break, sometimes. He has seen bridges of stone and metal and bone and all of them have, at one point, broken.

- Do you want to break?

He asks this question of the presence in the air.

- Not necessarily.
- But you cannot break
