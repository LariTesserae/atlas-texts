The first thing they notice is not light or thunder, but a subtle change in the rhythm of the water.

The pressure around you gathers, then releases—a long, slow in-breath followed by an equally long out-breath that you feel not with your ears but in your chest. The faint chiming of plant fibers, the almost imperceptible groan of stone settling into its own tiredness, everything for a moment arranges itself around a new pulse.

You are hanging in your usual corridor, half-asleep, suspended by a rope of hair and root, when the water around you becomes, for just an instant, warmer. Not by much, but enough to notice—like the faint exhale of another body in the same room.

When you open your eyes, nothing is visually changed. The bioluminescence of the wall plants is still the faintest suggestion of green, white, blue. Your fingers can still feel the familiar smoothness of the stone, the way the fronds of your favorite corridor species drift against your knuckles like soft fur.

But then the water speaks your name.

It doesn’t use sound, exactly. It doesn’t use your words. It places your identity inside you like a stone placed into your palm. You do not wonder whether your name is Block- Shifter or Door-Finder or whatever you have been called by your line; you simply know that *this* is you, and that you are being addressed by something you cannot see or grasp or push away.

---

For the one Driftline, you recognize the paintings of warmth in the water.

The temperature against your skin is subtle, but undeniable: a thin line of *more-than-cold* tracing from your left shoulder, down your spine, to the base of your ribs. It is not a fire’s heat; it is closer to the remembered heat of another body sleeping next to yours.

When you turn your head to follow that line of warmth, you sense something else: the water pressure around your cheeks softens, as if someone has placed gentle hands on either side of your head. You have not been touched by anything visible, but you feel *held*.

You are not afraid. This world does not make sudden deaths; you cannot fall to shatter on stone or be burned by fire or torn by predators. There is only the slow arrival of coldness, and even that is usually an event of years, not moments.

So you hang, curious, and let your fingers drift outward from the wall.

The water answers your reach with a pressure that feels like *contact*. You sense not the shape of a hand, but the shape of *attention*—where attention presses, the water becomes slightly more resistant, as if it remembers how to be a body.

You are very aware of your lungs: the slow tide of cold water entering your mouth, filtered and cycled through your plant-filter chamber, then expelled. That tide has not changed. But the *pulse* of the world around you has. The water moves like a heart that you have never before been allowed to feel.
