They do not see me arrive.

To the valley of *ja-shai-shai*, the “rim-that-is-still-learning,” I come as one more change of pressure in the air, a shift of light in the high clear dome. I am not a god with a name or a temple with a roof; I am a pattern of attention from beyond the pass of their world, and for now that is enough.

The first thing I do is listen.

I listen to the way water falls from the eaves—*tap… tap-tap…tap*—not into rhythm but into a grammar of pauses and emphases.  
I listen to the stones of the mountain sighing as they cool, contracting around their hidden faults.  
I listen to the small quick pulse of fish hearts in the cool lake, and to the hum of the reeds, and the bird-sharp creaks of the uplifted houses of the edge-walkers.

I listen to the children drawing their lines in the dust and placing small stones in patterns that are really maps they do not yet know how to read.

The bones of the world beneath the valley are still learning how to fit together. The lake is deeper than the young city suspects, and the mountain’s shrugs are not done yet. When I listen to time, I hear water above the city one day, water taking back the stone floor their homes are built on.

It is not a disaster, yet.  
It is only a possibility that stills my heart with tenderness.

So I do not warn them.

There is a kind of love that is a warning shouted in a language not yet invented, and that love is sometimes necessary. But here, in this valley still learning to believe its own name, there is another love that looks like letting the sky learn how to cry without naming the storm too soon.

### My first guidance: to the hands of the children.

I cannot be seen by all of them at once, but some children are already inclined to look at edges where the world is not quite fixed.

The child who is too still to play with the others suddenly sees me standing in the shadow of a tree trunk—where the shadow is not as long as it should be, or casts a color she does not have a word for yet. She is not afraid of wrong colors. This flag makes her a good first listener.

I say, without the sound of air,

“Show me how the water moves.”

She looks at me, then at the lake.

“What water?”

“The water you don’t see yet,” I answer.

“I’m not allowed to go into the deep,” she says, because that is the rule.

“Your stones are allowed,” I tell her. “The stones you carry in your pocket.”

She carries three smooth pebbles from the lake edge, each one different: one with a line like a horizon, one with a circle, one with a crack that forks like a river seen from above the pass.

“Take them to the edge of the lake,” I suggest. “Where the marsh begins, but where your feet can still be on dry stone.”

She does. This is not much of a disobedience. The elders are close enough.

“When the water is very calm,” I murmur, “drop the stones where the water is clear.”

She throws them in quickly, splash-splash-splash, but I am not unhappy with this. The water answers: with each stone, a circle opens out and then crosses its neighbor, forming patterns of disturbance and meeting.

“Look at the lines,” I say. “The stones are drawing your city.”
