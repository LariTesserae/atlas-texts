Their attention is not a sudden thing. It does not crash into the basin like a stone from the bright sky you imagine above the mist. It is more like a tide that has been rising for a long time without anyone noticing.

First, only a small ache of change: the coldest night of the year arrives, and it does not hurt as much as it should.

No one dies of the chill in the crowded upper rooms. The ache in your bones is there, yes, but it is bearable, like being remembered instead of forgotten. The damp that usually creeps into your lungs and tightens them into knots of coughing sits instead as a cool clarity on the tongue. You notice this because you notice its absence; the night is supposed to bite harder than this.

The Line-Keepers notice the next morning.

The high-water mark on the Weeping Mother’s stone is exactly where they predicted, but the salt it leaves behind is not the same. It does not form the usual jagged lace of white, but a pattern like smooth, overlapping scales, or the petals of a flower pressed into the stone. Children run their fingers over it and find their skin comes away cleaner, not stickier, as if the salt has washed instead of burned.

Nothing else changes, and for a long time, that is all.

Then the algae begins to dream.

---

It is the Deep-Walkers who see it first, because they are the only ones who go down that far. When they return from the foundations of the tower, where the ribs of the old leviathan glow under the water, they climb the rope ladder to the walkways and sit a long time with their knees drawn up, their hair still dripping dark circles onto the planks.

They say the same thing in different words:

The light beneath the water has not changed its color or its place, but it has changed its habit.

Where before it would drift and flicker in mindless patterns, now there are moments of stillness—long heartbeats where the green-blue lines hold steady into a shape, like a hand reaching upward or a tree branching out from the tower’s base. The luminous filaments are weaving themselves into images that are hard to look straight at but impossible to ignore.

When they ask the Line-Keepers whether the tide has changed, or whether the water pressure has been different, the Line-Keepers shake their heads. The water still rises when it is supposed to rise and falls when it is supposed to fall. The marks are only marks; the message is not clear.

But they are uneasy.

Because for the first time, the algae’s patterns and the salt lines’ patterns are echoing each other.
