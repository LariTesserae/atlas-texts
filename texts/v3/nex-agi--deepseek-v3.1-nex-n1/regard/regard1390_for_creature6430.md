There is no fanfare of arrival when a benevolent power turns its attention toward Kavsara. No sudden flare of light, no crack of thunder from the clear, dry sky. The change is not for the eyes of the world but for the ear of the world.

Its first manifestation is an absence: a missing beat.

In the murk of evening, as the last light of day bleeds away and the rim of the Maw cuts a black silhouette into the sky, the Night of Warnings is in its earliest phase. The wind-readers are already on their stations, stone-smooth flesh braced against a faint, gritty breeze that is not yet strong enough to stir the Heart-Bell below. The basin of Kavsara is restless with the quiet: the faint chatter of bronze chimes on the lower terraces, the sigh of heated air rising from the floor, the distant scuffle of bare feet on polished stone.

On the western rim, where the wind comes first, the oldest of the wind-readers—Lavan, whose ribs are a map of the city’s wind channels carved in raised white lines—presses her Hear-Stone to her chest and breathes in the dust of the world.

The first breath is wrong.

The city should breathe with her. It should draw the same thin strand of air into the same narrow chamber of chest, should echo the same subtle pressure. She waits for the stone beneath her feet to answer the rhythm of her lungs, but there is a gap where she feels a faint resistance, like pushing air into a throat that is already full.

Her second breath is sharper than intended, a question-mark instead of a line.

She turns the Hear-Stone in her hand, its polished surface cool as dead water, and traces her finger along its edge, where carved notches indicate the alignment of high airs. She should rotate the stone until the vibration clarifies its message—the direction and density of the coming night wind—but she finds instead that she does not need to.

Because she is not the only one listening.

The world around her suddenly seems to bend *toward* her attention like a sounding bowl. The low susurration of the terraceless shrubbery on the inner walls of the Maw becomes a language of leaves, each leaf edge producing a distinct frequency; she hears the faint scrape of root-moss through the basalt pores, the rustle of wind-gourds swelling in their matte black skins.

Above it, deeper than the heat-cracks and the sigh of underground water, she feels the world listen *back*.

It is not a voice of words or images. It is a sound of intention—like a whispered question that she has not quite formed in her own mind yet but which already has an answer.

Still kneeling, stone still at her chest, Lavan lets the sensation of response wash into the bone of her jaw. The answer is not knowledge of the future; it is knowledge of *now*—of the exact state of the city’s throat, the precise degree of dust in the wind-pipes, the number of hidden cracks in the lower galleries where heat is escaping without being used.

Behind the strict statistics of the city’s sound, she feels the clear, bright intention of a power too large to be explained by the city’s theology.

The world’s god is a breath, but this breath has *will*.

She is not afraid. This might be surprising, but she has been trained to read wind in all of its forms; and this, for all its newness, is still wind. It is not chaos; it is not destruction. She is more afraid of a wrong angle of the night wind than she is of a god who chooses to speak by listening.

Her next breath is steady again.

“Yes,” she says—not aloud to anyone else, but to the hollow of the world’s attention. For her, this is not an answer, but an invitation: *Yes. I hear you. I will be the mouth of the Maw.*

The answer does not come in language but in geometry.

The stone of her station seems to speak under her fingers. The faintest tremor of sound, nothing like the thunderous crack of rockfall, but like a tight string being plucked inside the world itself. The pattern of the carving around her—the precise number of holes in the overhang, the spacing of the channels in the floor—suddenly becomes clear to her. Not the clear of *recognition*, but the clear of *revision*.

She knows exactly what the stone should become.

She is not a carver. She has not learned to shape with chisel and bronze saw. But she knows, in the marrow of her bone, where the next fracture should go.

Her fingers are shaking as she reaches out, not to the stone of the floor but to the Hear-Stone itself that she holds against her chest. She remembers, as she does not usually remember, that she is not holding the heart of the city; she is holding *stone*—the same kind that is beneath her, and under the Heart-Bell at the center of the world.

She is holding the god’s bone.

Her breath comes out in a low, deep vowel—not a word, but a sound she has never before made into the air. It is not a man’s voice or a woman’s voice; it is the sound of the dust itself, refined by her throat but not diminished by it.

The sound of the world pours *through* her.

### The Change in the Night of Warnings

#### Lavan of the West Rim

On the western rim, where the wind arrives first, the night of the warning is not entirely gentle.

The old warmth of the day is still radiating from the stone under her knees and the soles of her feet when she feels the change: a new alignment of stone in the world. The Maw of Kavsara has just *grown* in its inner throat.

When she sounds the Spirit-Horn, not toward the great sky but into the interior of the city’s bowl, the echo that comes back to her is no longer dull but sharpened into a clean, singular line. The city of Kavsara has become *self-correcting*.

There is steady in the world of Kavsara now.

#### Arin of the East Rim

On the east rim of the Maw, where the wind’s return echo from the plateau of the world is usually strongest and most confused, Arin feels the change as a sudden *clearance*.

He is younger than Lavan by two decades, but his ears are already notched with the full ring of dark slate and polished bone that he has earned in his years of listening. Tonight, he is braced for the usual chaos.

The wind of evening should be coming from the wrong side of the world today; the last low sun of the day had been stained with the salt-white of the false-tears lakes to the east. The air should, strictly speaking, be heavy with dead salt dust by the time it reaches him.

But he does.

He *breathes*.

The city is not gasping for air; it is *steady*.

His heart is not sure of what this means; he is a man of the Maw of Kavsara, and he is not taught to believe in benevolence. The god of his world does not hear; it is *heard*.  

And yet, tonight, when he sings the Warning Song into the mask pushed to his face, the city of Kavsara does not answer like the cracked bells of the last century of troubles.

Its answer is not dry.

It is *steady*.

Below the rims of the world, the people of Kavsara are not aware that the world’s breath has suddenly changed direction.
