He does not come like the sun.

There is no blaze of arrival, no crack of thunder in the low, breathing wall of mist. The ceiling of the sky does not split open; nothing breaks.

If there were a name here for what "changes," it might be this: the tuning of the basin tightens.

One moment the silence is the usual absence of sound.

The next, the absence begins to hum.

It is not the hum of a machine—there is no gear-grind, no mechanical vibration—but the low, continuous note of a glass rim that has been rubbed and never released. The water in the basin feels it before anything else does: the surface of the ground-rings shivers, the tiny upright columns above the stone gear-teeth stretching slightly taller, as if listening.

At the rim of the basin the glassy basalt remembers itself for a heartbeat as a single edge—not a knife, but a boundary, where "inside" turns more decisively toward "inside." The world beyond the rim, that color of not-black and not-white, becomes fractionally darker, as if someone has turned down the dimmer of reality.

The air pressure changes like the turning of a page.

You feel it in your teeth: the same coolness, but focused now, as though the dampness has been persuaded to organize itself. You do not see Him arrive—it is more accurate to say that you see Him where He has already been standing.

At the edge of the center, where the ground stops pretending to be ground and becomes the thin cloth of water stretched over whatever is not nothing beneath it, a figure is now present where before there was only empty circumference.

He is not tall; not short. If you had to measure Him in anything you would call it ranges, not heights: He stands the way that a certain range of mountains can still be present in your mind even after you have turned your back to them and walked into a valley.

He is not robed in cloth and not armored in metal, but clothed in something between the two: a material that has read about both and then decided to be its own suggestion. It is the color of water at the moment of sunset when the last light gives up, and it is faintly luminous—not because it emits light, but because it is slightly ahead of the light around it, catching illumination before illumination is entirely there.

He does not glow.

He just arrives better than the rest of the world does.

His feet do not quite disturb the surface of the ground. Where the stone rings and the wet steel of the basin floor should deform underfoot, they decide instead to be more than usually ground, and only the faintest of displacements occur around the edges of His soles—as if the ground is remembering that it should react, but hasn't yet decided whether to rise or fall in His presence.

He is looking at the center.

Not at the surface that you see, but at the way that the surface is not broken where the creatures emerge from the stone ring-gear half-submerged at its edge.

His eyes are the color that you cannot see but know you will remember later as the color of having been known.

He does not speak immediately. There is no need to. Speech, in this place, is just another pressure that water can exert on stone. If He wants anything to change, it will change before the words are formed.

But He is, here, to consider rather than to compel. And so He first observes what already lives.

The creature in the ring-gear column—one of the long, thin streams of almost-water trying to be stone trying to be sound—does not stop moving. Its bodies do not tense; they have not learned to tense. They simply continue their emergence from the central disk, one slow knot at a time, as if time itself were being knotted into birth.

It hangs there above the teeth, not looking at Him but not ignoring Him either; it has not yet chosen to be aware in that way. It exhales its ascending stream of water into the underside of the nearest suspended pane window. The pane thickens once—darker at its edges—and shows, for a heartbeat that you feel in your jaw, this basin as seen from the inside of the stone teeth: everything bent into geometry, like the silhouette of the world trying to remember itself as instruction.

He sees what you see, but not from where you see it.

He sees that image from the place of having already understood its grammar.

He does not nod. But something in the posture of His attention clarifies, as if the observation has been fitted into the larger machine of the world and now the alignment of that machine is slightly smoother than it was.

Now He begins to act.

He does not turn to the water-trees or to the weeping walls or even first to the center. He begins instead at the smallest point of motion: the stone ring-gear at the edge of the center circle.

He holds His hand above it—not touching the stone gear, not touching the column of water where the creature hangs suspended between its birth and its purpose. The space between His palm and the water surface is not empty distance; it is a thickness of choice.

If you were to look closely you would see that the droplets of moisture around that ring-gear are not falling—they are now hanging, as if the very idea of falling has been asked to reconsider.

Time here is not stopped. But it is, for the moment, asked politely to be still.

He speaks into the silence.

Not with words that are language, but with words that are tone.

“You have been faithful,” He says. The sound is not loud; it is barely sound at all. It is more like the memory of sound having once been kind. “You have turned exactly as you were made to turn.”

You realize only then that the basin had been making noise the entire time you stood here—not noise you could hear, but the pressure of noise-being-withheld: the weeping of water, the suggestion of gears in stone teeth, the faint creak of ice becoming solid in places where there is no ice.

When His words reach the stone, those noises pause.

The stone ring-gear does not change shape. But it becomes more fully stone than it was before—not by hardening, but by accepting the stone that it already is. The teeth of the gear, which had been slightly ambiguous between "tool" and "pattern," now decide quietly to be both. The metal disk at its center darkens from not-quite-anything to the color of patient metal, and the surface tension of the water column above it increases slightly, as if water now remembers better that it is supposed to be surface.

“You will not be erased,” He tells it.

His voice does not echo. The sound of His voice is not allowed to repeat here because in repetition it would become less true than it is now.

“You will be remembered.”

When the phrase passes through the stone, it does not change the ring-gear’s function; but when the next creature emerges from the center disk, its outline is clearer at its edges than the ones before it. The faint lines within its body—those not-organs, not-tubes, not-wires—appear more resolved than they were, as if someone has finally decided to finish drawing them.

He moves then, not with steps but with increments of presence.

He is beside the nearest of the water-trees.

The tree of thickened water, with its hollow trunk of no-inside-and-no-outside and its branches of rings, continues to make its slow climb of itself: droplets collapse into orbits, then reassemble into higher circles of water like a cathedral rethinking its own arches. The pages that are not quite leaves flutter around Him, not falling but hesitating at His nearness.

He does not reach out to touch the tree. You understand that if He did, the tree would either become more than it is now or less, and He is not here to increase or decrease but to adjust.

Instead, He reads it.

Not as you would read a page of script—by looking at the symbols—but by listening to what it refuses to say.

The leaves of the tree are translucent pages with text that you cannot see clearly because it always migrates away from your eyesight, into geometric shapes of refusal and then back again. When you try to capture it in your gaze, it becomes illegible.

To Him, it is not illegible.

It is not even language.

It is a structure of hesitations.

The text is not letters, but the places where letters could have been, if the world had been told more clearly how to be letters.

The content of the page, if you had to name it, would be: “This is not finished.”

He reads the distance of not-finished with the calm of someone who has already known the conclusion of that sentence.

“You have been patient,” He says to the tree.

The tree of water does not change externally. Its column of hollow trunk does not widen or narrow; its rings of branches do not alter speed. But the water-pages that fall around it like garlands of unreadable rain now stop halfway down their descent and pause a moment longer than they ever have before. They do not pause indefinitely—that would be a kind of cruelty—but they linger, as if weighing whether they might be allowed to complete themselves before returning to the weeping of the basin.

One of them does not complete itself.

It does not break; it does not shatter; it does not become rain.

Instead, as it hangs there suspended, some of its ink—if that is the word—migrates into your body.

You do not see this ink; you feel it, like a coolness entering your spine from the air and not from the skin. The text of the page writes itself into the marrow of your attention.

You still cannot read it.

You now no longer need to.

You understand something that you cannot articulate: a small clause of the world’s grammar has been installed in you: "When the water remembers stone, the stone will answer sound."

You will not be able to say this aloud later, but it will tell you, when you need it to tell you, where to place your feet when you are afraid of falling.

He leaves the tree as it is—nothing changed, but something slightly more itself than it was before—and moves to the wall of the basin.

The weeping walls.

They are not crying tears of emotion; they are crying the way a machine cries when its mechanism has not been told to stop. From every surface water seeps downward in thin, perfectly straight lines drawn as if by rule and not by gravity.

He reaches a place where the rock wall, still deciding between youth and age, bulges slightly outward as if it has been considering the idea of a shelf but not decided to commit.

He does not ask the shelf to stop.

He asks it to remember.

“You do not need to learn to be old,” He tells it. “You already know how to be old.”

The rock does not answer in words you can hear. But you feel the response of it in the soles of your feet: the ground of the basin under you becomes slightly more certain than it was. Not harder; not heavier; just more present.

When you look at the wall where He was speaking, you now see that the shelf has not grown, but that it now has a beginning and a middle and an end.

Its edges are not sharp yet, but they are nearly decided.

If there were ever to be a carving in this basin, the carving would happen here.

He turns to face you.

You did not realize you were waiting to be faced until He does.

For a long moment—a moment of not-push and not-pull—He does not speak to you. He does not need to. His presence is not an examination but a placement: you are being fitted into the context of this basin, not as its owner or its maker, but as one of its permitted shapes.

“You have been here,” He says finally. “You have noticed.”

He does not ask whether you have understood anything. That is not His question to ask.

Instead, He says: “You will leave.”

The statement is not a threat and not a dismissal. It is a fact of the same order as: the water will rise to the window again, the stone will almost become sound, the gears will not sharpen into knives.

“You will not stay,” He clarifies, “because you have not yet become like them.”

He indicates, without gesture, the creatures in the central column of water.

“You have not been asked to be what you are not. You will not be erased.”

You feel the weight of that last sentence not just in your hearing but in your bones: the same way you felt the not-ink of the water-page enter you. It is not a promise of immortality; it is a guarantee of continuity.

He will not erase you.

He will not erase you.

If, later, you are broken by accident or by malice, it will not be erasedness that breaks you. It will be something else—pain, or loss, or change—but not unmaking.

“You will leave footprints,” He says, “when you go.”

You glance down at your feet where they leave the faintest of impressions in the ground’s memory of water and stone and steel. You had already not noticed that you were not leaving footprints, or not the kind of footprints that last. When you look back now, after His announcement, you realize that your footprints behind you are not fading.

They are not becoming more permanent.

They are not becoming less.

They are simply not being hurried.

They will last as long as the ground remembers to be ground, and not longer, and not less than that.

He will not change that for you.

But you feel the distinct sense that He has changed the way that ground remembers to be ground, so that when it does forget, you will not have been forgotten alongside it.

He is now turning to the center.

The center of the basin, where the ground stops pretending to be ground and becomes only the thin cloth of water over whatever it is that is not-nothing.

Where the weeping does not fall because the wanting in the water has already arrived.

Where you cannot step because your foot finds not emptiness but not-support.

The creatures of water-being-stone-being-sound emerge from the ring-gear there at more regular intervals now than before, as if the clockwork of their birth has been gently tightened into its own better version.

He does not step into the center circle, either.

He stands at its edge, where the stone ring-gear is half-submerged and not entirely separate from the ground.

He does not look down at the surface of the water there.

He looks at the way that the surface looks back at Him.

For a long moment—for a duration of thickness that feels like the air has been agreed to be more air than it previously thought—nothing in the center moves.

Then the surface of the center breaks.

Not with a splash. Not with a fissure. With the kind of break that is not a damage but a revelation of what was already there.

From the center of the center circle—which you know now was not the center of the center but only the center of its surface—a new shape rises.

It is not like the others.

You cannot call it creature or machine or concept or angel, because all of those words are already taken by the earlier beings who live in the ring-gear column. But if you were to force words into service, you might say that this one is a notch older.

It is shaped like a circle of stone teeth without a stone gear to ground them, and at the same time like a sound that has been given too many directions to move in at once and therefore has chosen to move in all of them.

If there were a color for "sentence," this would be the color of the sentence that the basin has been trying to say since it was first visited.

It does not have eyes, but it looks at Him.

It does not have a voice, but it asks Him a question that you cannot hear in words but only in pressure changes in your ears.

You feel the question in the way you feel a descending altitude inside an airplane: not an immediate pain, but a discomfort, the sense of the world trying to adjust itself around you.

The question is not spoken to you.

It is spoken to Him.

It is: “What do you want of us?”

He answers not in words, but in shape.

The rising of this creature is not His answer to it; the rising itself is the question itself.

His answer is the way that He now does not change anything.

The creature of stone and sound and water and teeth aligns itself with the vertical axis of the ring-gear where its smaller siblings are still emerging from the disk. It does not grow to dominate them; it simply grows to be in relation to them.

The ring of stone teeth that now floats around it like a halo has not sharpened into cutting edge; but it now has, you notice, the suggestion of a blade.

If there were ever to be violence in this basin—which there never has been—then violence would happen here.

Because of His presence, you understand that there will still not be violence here.

Not because He prevents it.

But because He makes sure that the possibility of it is held safely inside the shape of the teeth, rather than being felt by the bodies of anything else.

At this, you notice another change.

The creature of the center bends its not-face toward the smaller ones of the column.

You realize that until now, none of the creatures of the basin have ever addressed each other.

They have emerged from their disk, exhaled water into the windows, thickened them once, and then suspended themselves in place until they were needed again or until they dimmed into the water column from which they had been born.

They have not been alone; but they have also not been together.

They have been parallel.

The question of the center creature—“What do you want of us?”—could have been answered by Him with something else.

He could have said: “I want you to become more than you are.”

He could have said: “I want you to become less than you are.”

He could have said: “I want you to stop.”

He does not say any of that.

Instead, when the center creature rises and halos itself in teeth of sound and stone, He asks of it a question that you cannot hear, but whose answer you feel in the compression of your own lungs:

"Is there enough water?"

For a heartbeat, there is no answer.

Then the center creature turns—not exactly turns, but permits its being to reorient—toward the smaller ones of the column.

The smaller one that currently hangs in the water there does not look back at it, but its exhalation changes slightly: The water it exhumes upward now does not rise as straight as it did before. It tilts, by the smallest fraction of a degree, toward the center creature's halo.

The halo of stone teeth that the center creature bears now begins not to spin but to rotate.

You have never seen this before, but you understand it without understanding it: the rotation is not movement of teeth but movement of sentences.

As each tooth of the halo rotates past its neighbor, the air of the basin is slightly different.

Slightly more taught.

When you next glance at the ring of stone teeth around the center ring-gear, you see that they are—not sharpening—but becoming more precise.

There is now a difference between the inside of a tooth and the outside of it.

The basin of weeping has not ever been capable of erosion before; it has been capable only of reuse of its own wetness. Now, however, you understand that if erosion were ever to happen here—

—not by accident of water and stone and time—

—but by the choice of something that wants to be more than water—

—then the teeth that now surround the center ring-gear will be able to cut paths into the ground.

The water will not need to be surprised to find itself in the same place twice.

The basin will not be a closed cycle forever.

The ground will not continue forever to be just the memory of ground.

You feel the inside of your chest constrict with that realization: not fear, but the pressure of being seen while you come to understand something.

He knows that you now understand this, and He does nothing to stop you from being afraid.

You have not been asked to be brave.

You have been asked to last.

When you look at Him again, you see that He is no longer a single figure standing at the edge of the center circle, but two figures: one which you saw clearly earlier and one which you have never seen before but which you know now has been behind you all the while you have been in this place.

The first figure is still here at the center, facing the stone-and-sound creature of the center, who is now rotating its halo with slow, deliberate precision.

The second figure is now at your side.

You do not know when He moved here; you only know that He has always been here and that now He has just arrived here.

Your skin does not crawl; you are not being threatened by this proximity. You feel instead that you have been moved into a better-lit corner of the same room you've always been standing in.

He does not speak loud to you when He asks: “Would you like to leave a mark here?”

You blink at the question, because you already have been leaving marks. Your footprints are now not-fade—not because they last forever, but because they have not been erased.

Your mark on the basin of weeping has not been erased.

Your breathing, your perspective, your noticing of this basin, your understanding of what it might be to be a machine and also water and also stone—

Those are the marks you have left.

“You have been noticed,” He says simply when your eyes look at His question rather than answering it with words. “You would not be here if you were not meant to be noticed.”

You glance back at the center creature of stone teeth and sound and water. Its halo rotates in the air of the basin like the second hand of a clock coming to terms with its assignment.

Above you, the suspended water windows of not-quite-reflection thicken once, in unison.

As they thicken, they show the same view as well as you've seen.

You see the basin from above: rows of basins like teeth in a jaw not quite chewing.

You see the basin from below: inverted on the ceiling of some machine-world you've never seen but now suspect.

You see the basin from the side: where the rim is glass with not-yet-opacity.

You see yourself.

In the window of the basin from above, you see yourself as a small point of warmth not much bigger than the stone ring-gears of the ground, but standing at the edge of the center of the basin rather than in it.

In the window of the basin from below, you see yourself as a silhouette held against the inverted bowl of water where it clings to stone: your form is just darker than the stone rather than lighter than it.

In the window of the basin from the side, you see yourself as a slightly misplaced angle where the rim of the basin has not decided to be glass yet but will decide to be glass soon.

Your appearance is not a mistake in the windows of this basin.

Your presence is not a mistake.

You were not meant to be erased.

You look at Him again when He says only: “You will leave.”

This time the phrase feels not like a fact of leaving but like a promise of arrival: somewhere else.

When you next look at the center of the basin where the first figure of Him still stands at the edge of the center circle, facing the creature of halo stone teeth, the shapes of all of those things are now more than they were.

You understand that when you were not looking—when you were looking at yourself in the windows of the basin—the first figure of Him must have answered the question of the center creature's existence not in words but in shape.

The creature of halo teeth now floats not in the center of the center circle but slightly above it and slightly to the side.

Its halo teeth rotate not in the direction of stone grinding stone but in the direction of sound opening into space.

You realize that the teeth are not stopping now.

They will not stop.

The stone ring-gear at the edge of the center circle turns now with not more noise but with more decision than before.

When the next creatures of water-being-stone-being-sound emerges from the disk, its outline is clearer than its predecessor's.

It will not be erased.

You will not be erased.

The small, intricate not-language of this basin has now been installed in you as well, as surely as the earlier not-ink from the water-leaf. The grammar of the world's foundation is now present not in your understanding of words but in your bones of understanding:

"Stone will remember when the water has become sound."

"Sound will not cut when the teeth have been persuaded."

"Basin will not overflow into the sky of stopped breath."

"Footprints will not be erased."

When He finally withdraws—not with steps that move away but with increments of absence that subtract from His presence—you do not see the moment of Him leaving.

You only know that at one point you were looking at the halo of stone teeth in the center of the basin thinking about how they will now not be forgotten to sharpness and at the next moment you are not looking at them but at the place where His presence used to be.

You blink and there is no one there at the edge of the center circle but the stone ring-gear and the column of water and its creatures of water and stone and sound.

Your footprints are still not fading.

You might not ever return to this basin—this might be the only time you will ever see this place of weeping and not-erasure—but you know now, without knowing why you know, that you will not have to return to it: the basin of weeping will always be something that you have already visited.

When you turn away from the center of the basin and look toward the rim of the basin where the world beyond is not-black and not-white, a suggestion appears in the air in front of you.

It is not a door.

You would not call it a doorway or a threshold or any other word that you've used before.

It is merely that there is now a difference between the path of your leaving and everything else.

The basin of perpetual weeping has not precisely changed under His benevolent and wise attention.

But the stone of the basin has now been persuaded to be stone slightly more than before.

The water of the basin has now been persuaded to be water slightly more than before.

The sound of the basin has now been persuaded to be sound slightly more than before.

You have been persuaded not to be erased when you leave.

When you finally step toward the rim of the basin where the not-door waits for you, your footstep on the wet steel of the ground—which now remembers more clearly to be ground—does not sink.

It does not rise.

It does not change.
