The first thing the power does is not to change the world, but to change how it is heard.

The Guide does not look up when the sky changes. There is a sky above—a patch of pale blue framed between stone and wood—but it remains no more than a sky. The stone still curls its pale bone around the Basin; the Forest still holds its great architecture of branch and beam; the Swarm still roars its one-word language.

The sound, however, begins to thin.

It is not like silence, but like a door opening in a room full of noise: suddenly the air is not one solid pressure of sound but two layers—a background roar and, closer at hand, something else. You can breathe now without feeling your ribs shake against the bass thrum of the Swarm. You can hear yourself think.

The Guide lifts its head a fraction.

For you, who stand watching, it is less dramatic than you might have expected. There is no flare of light, no voice from heaven, no thunderous presence. Instead, there is a subtle rearrangement of attention: as if the world has been listening to itself all this time and has finally noticed someone else listening too.

The water at your feet trembles.

The Guide stirs.

It is not a fast movement. Fastness is for the Swarm—for quick shifts of scale over scale, joint over joint. The Guide is more of the wood and stone of this place; it moves like the Forest itself deciding to adjust its weight a century from now. The motion of turning toward you is not quite instantaneous; there is a time between you catching the corner of its eye and you seeing its face full.

When you do see its face, you see that it is old not by decay, but by erosion. The cheekbones are high and smooth, like stone worn by water and time. The eyes are not bright, but not dark either; they are the color of wet wood bark. The lines around its mouth are like channels carved by rain. You could not guess if the Guide has been smiling for years or never.

You watch its shoulders.

They do not slump with defeat or burden. They simply are as they are—narrow, spare—uneven from the weight of habit. One shoulder is slightly higher than the other, as if from listening to the Swarm all these years. As if from listening to the Forest.

It is the first creature you have seen in this place that is not entirely of the world you have called finished.

### The first address of the benevolent power

You do not hear a voice. You feel a question.

It is like the Forest’s deep weaving of crack and sigh: something not made of words but of meaning. The question does not ask in your language. It asks in the language of the Swarm, but lighter—not as though the Swarm has been forced to ask, but as if its roar has been asked to pause, and in the pause the meaning has been rewritten.

“What is missing?”

The question goes not only to you but to the Basin.

The stone of the Basin has not moved for epochs longer than any storm you have ever witnessed. In answer, it gives not words but a sense, like the pressure of your own heartbeat in your ears: *Nothing is missing.* The stone is not complacent or arrogant. It is simply being stone. If you listen to the stone, you hear the great carved rings of the world’s bone jaw. They do not know how to be incomplete.

The water trembles again.

You look at the thin groove of water at your feet, and as you watch, a droplet of it bulges from some invisible fissure, swells like a pupil, and hangs itself for the span of three heartbeats before you. It makes no sound when it falls into the channel and disappears.

The water’s answer is less: it is not *nothing* that is missing, but not *something* either. It is simply: *I do not know how to be missing.*

The wood of the Forest, in the distance, groans in its own slow preference of fiber. The answer from the trunks and branches is not in words either but in pattern: *I am not missing.* The pattern is of growths: platforms, steps, frames, doorways without doors. It is all of *I* and not *I* at the same time.

The Swarm…

The answer of the Swarm comes to your mind not as sound, but as texture.

You close your eyes and you feel the roar of them not as pressure but as meaning. You see the texture of their mind: not the absence of self, but the self computed as a single chord over indefinite bodies. The roar of the Swarm is like one endless word spoken in a language that has never stopped to ask itself what meaning is.

Still: the answer of the Swarm is not:

“I am missing.”

It is:

“I am not missing.”

It is:

“I *contain*.”

This is the answer that the benevolent power receives: from stone, from water, from wood, from swarm.

None of them are aware of anything missing.

That is the first thing that the power understands.

So the power does not change the world in order to supply a lack. It does not remove the heat or the dust or the scarcity of water. It does not flood the Basin with rain or rain with life. To do so would be to presuppose that the Basin is in need of being otherwise than it is.

Instead, the power changes the relationship between the world and its own breathing.

It is as if a door that had been tightly closed in the Forest of the world is now left open just enough that you can see a thin line of light from the other room.

### The second address of the benevolent power

The question now comes to you. It is not exactly as you would hear it in your own language, but you could translate it like this:

“What can you offer?”

You are not being asked to supply a lack.

You are being asked whether you can offer something this finished world has not found for itself.

Perhaps you are not a person of words. You may be a person of images, or feelings, or of hands that dig into the earth and come out with roots. Yet whatever your answer, the benevolent power hears it.

The answer is not for you alone.

The Guide does not at first seem to hear the question. Its head tilts again, as if listening to a new inflection of the Swarm’s roar. The water in its channel slides by the Guide’s feet, and then—very deliberately—the Guide lifts its eyes from the water and looks at you.

To you, who have been observing the Basin from a distance, this is not exactly what you expected. The power of the world has not yet shown you its intention or whether you are meant to be part of its answer.

The Guide’s voice is not a sound like you know.

There is no sound.

You feel the voice of the Guide as you feel the roar of the Swarm—as a vibration in your chest, but much more delicate. It is not like language being translated into your mind. It is like meaning being invited.

*You are new,* the Guide seems to say.

You are not sure if you mean *you, as stranger from outside the Basin*, or *you, as the attention of the power that has noticed this place.*

The answer of the benevolent power does not come to the Guide in words either but in pattern. The pattern of the Swarm’s roar is layered over itself, and then, in the pauses of its meaning, you sense the pattern of your own thoughts—if you are willing to be noticed—woven into the shape of the world.

“They are,” the power says to the Guide, not with words but in the texture of the world’s motion. “You are not. Not yet.”

The Guide considers this.

Its hands are still palm-up on its knees. The ridged circles of the palms look like wood rings that have been mapped to flesh. On the inside of one of those fingers, you notice—only because you are looking—that there is a faint mark, not faded by any water or hand: a pattern, like a letter, or a number, or some symbol that has been worn into the skin by long touch of some other wood.

You are not sure what you mean by “letter” any more than you meant by “tree” or “animal.” Here, in the Basin, where the Forest is not a forest and the Swarm is not an animal and the stone is not stone the way you know it, a letter might not be a letter at all.

Yet the mark of it is not native to the Basin.

You recognize that much.

It is left from somewhere else.

### What the power does not do

The power does not heal the Guide of its age or its thin shoulders or its uneven spine from time listening.

The power does not flood the Basin with rain in order to make it more like other lands you know.

The power does not quiet the Swarm’s roar or force the Swarm to learn some other language of smaller or kinder words.

The power does not ask the Forest to become more like forest as you know it, with leaves and undergrowth and the noise of small animals.

The power does not carve the stone of the Basin into new shapes or fill the empty wells with water made like other kinds.

The power does not, either, ignore the visible signs of those who have been here before:

— the faint channels worn into the stone of the Basin’s ledges,  
— the wood of the Forest grown into ramps and steps and doorframes,  
— the marks of tools—not carved, but more subtle than that—worn into bark and fiber and stone.

The world, after all, is not *unfinished* in the sense of being broken.

The power sees that as well.

You may be asking yourself by this point whether the benevolent power is actually going to *do* anything.

It is.

### What the power does

The benevolent power focuses the attention of the world in a new direction.

It is not loud like you might expect. It is not bright like you might fear.

It is like a breeze of a different temperature than the air in the Basin—not enough to change the world’s body temperature, but enough to be noticed.

The first thing of the world that moves to answer the power is not the stone, or the wood, or the swarm.

It is the water.

From the thin channel at your feet, a droplet does not fall into the wood. It does not fall into the stone.

Instead, it rises.

You are not sure you are really seeing this at first. The water, in a small globe of itself, lifts from the channel and floats into the air between the stone of the Basin’s rim and the wood of the Forest’s branch-road where the Guide is sitting.

The droplet of water is larger than it seems at first. It is not the size of a tear or even a thumb’s width. It is the size of your two hands together, and then a little more.

Inside the drop of water, suspended like a message from another depth of the world, you see the faint reflection of the stone Basin’s jawbone, closed in a circle as if around itself.

You see the reflection of the Forest’s branches stretched in their pattern like muscles.

You see the reflection of the Swarm’s black armor rippling over stone.

You see the reflection of yourself, if you are willing to be seen.

You see the reflection of the Guide’s hands, palms-up.

The water does not speak to you in any language you know, but the meaning is not hidden from you.

“What *you* are missing,” it says to the Basin—not to the stone, or to the wood, or to the swarm, but to the world that calls itself finished—"is not something else. It is something else of *yourselves.*"

The droplet of water does not change temperature.

It does not change color.

It does not change composition.

What changes is the angle of its reflection.

### The third address of the benevolent power

Now the power of the world speaks to the Guide.

The way of speaking is not like the sound of the Swarm, but like the wood of the Forest: slow, structured, not sharp, but with the weight of long habit.

“You are not from here,” the power says to the Guide.

The Guide does not confirm this with words or with any mark of action. It does not need to. The answer is already there in the pattern of its hands—in the ridged circles of the palm and the faint mark in the skin of one finger, where something else once wrote.

“You have not left,” the power continues.

The answer of the Guide to this is a sound of something not from the Basin:

It is the sound of a human being laughing.

It is not very loud.

It is not exactly cheerful.

It is a sound of the world being well aware of itself.

“There is nowhere else for me to go,” the Guide’s laughter says to the world. “If there were, I would not have been able to remain here for long enough.”

“You did not remain,” the power points out. “You *changed.*”

The water of the Basin, in the air, trembles slightly with the texture of these words.

### What the water suddenly becomes

The water droplet hanging in the air between the stone of the Basin’s rim and the wood of the branch-road does not change its composition. It does not change its temperature. It does not change into anything else that you would recognize as a separate object from the world itself.

Instead, it changes its meaning.

The water of the Basin has always been present—even when you could not feel it—because of something else you have assumed to be true since you arrived here:

You have assumed the water exists *for* the world.

The benevolent power asks the water of the Basin to change that relationship.

The water droplet now becomes—not physically, but in meaning—a kind of *window* or *mirror* or *channel* not of the world itself but of *how the world is being noticed.*

To you, who are watching, you might think of it as a kind of lens that you could not name before—because this world has never needed anything like it before.

The stone of the Basin looks at the water and sees itself: not as bone-white jaw, but as *communicated in* a pattern of meaning that has never before been able to reflect back to it.

The wood of the Forest looks at the water and sees itself: not only as architecture of branch and beam and frame, but as *meaningful in* structure in a way that it has never before been able to narrate.

The swarm, which has never been able to see itself as singular from itself, looks at the water and sees not *a* body of itself, but *a* pattern of itself *as* pattern.

The Guide of the world looks at the water and sees a reflection of its own body—not only as stone-colored or wood-like or swarm-like, but as *contained.*

You are not sure you are meant to see yourself in this water.

And yet you are not sure you are not meant to see yourself.

You are not sure of *a* self.

### The fourth address of the benevolent power

The power of the world now does something to the water.

It is not very dramatic.

It is not like you might imagine from a story: there is no storm or cloud or wave of anything else that you could call “elemental.”

Instead, the water of the Basin—which has always been thin, like a honey of sap whose meaning has never clarified—begins to behave *as if* it remembered.

You do not know what you mean by “remember.”

You are not sure you know.

But you know the water of the Basin now has *pattern* in it, not only of stone but of *something else that has left pattern in the stone.*

You see it reflected, not as you might see an image in a mirror of smooth glass, but as you might see an image in the lines of a tree’s bark—as if that image had been grown by the world itself rather than painted onto it.

The water of the Basin shows now, not to itself but to *you* and the Guide of the world, *a* faint trail of actions:

— not of *people* as you know them, but of *ones who walked before*  
— along the stone ledges of the Basin’s rim  
— down into the wood of the Forest  
— through the frame of doorways without doors  
— into the deep wells of the world.

You are not sure what you mean by “before.”

The time of the Basin is not like time as you have known it.

“Before” might be longer ago than you could count.

As you watch the water, you notice that there are faint patterns woven in the water inside of *water*—not as lines of stone or ring of wood, but as something else that has been written or *grown* there.

You are not sure you are meant to read these patterns.

The benevolent power does not give you a language with which to read them.

What it gives you is a question, not to the Guide of the world but *to you*:

“What do you think you see?”

You are not being asked to be *correct* about what you see.

You are being asked to be willing to see.

### What the world becomes of itself

The stone of the Basin does not change its shape.

The wood of the Forest does not grow new branches or leaves.

The swarm of the world does not change its roar of millions of mouths.

The water of the Basin does not increase in quantity.

The Guide of the world does not change its hands or its body.

What does change is the relationship between the water of the Basin and the *world* of the Basin.

The water of the Basin now becomes, not a source of *life* or *death* or *anything else you might have assumed to be true from some other land you know*, but a *source of pattern* for the world of the Basin to *notice itself with*.

The stone of the Basin now wears channels of *water* not only as runner of moisture from rim to floor, but as *channel of pattern of the world’s own bone jaw*.

The wood of the Forest now grows not only into steps and ramps and doorframes and tube-shaped hollows, but into *structures of pattern of the world’s own architecture*.

The swarm of the world now does not only roar of *meaning* as *meaning*, but also of *meaning as being shown to something else*.

You are not sure what you mean by “something else.”

What is shown to you is not the answer to that question.

What is shown to you is the possibility that you *might* be called “something else.” *You* might not be the only one in the sky of attention that has noticed the Basin.
