He would appear already seated on the low brick parapet when you glance back from the threshold, not because he has just arrived, but because you have just become capable of seeing him.

He is not very tall, even sitting. You might think, at first glance, *that is a person who has been young for a long time and old for even longer.* His hair is the color of a candle flame seen through a page of vellum—dim but not dimming—and the lines of his face have been inscribed by a stylus that has never once pressed hard enough to break the skin. They are not tired lines; they are lines of frequent use.

He wears clothes that might have been excavated from a lost library: a linen shirt that has been mended with more patience than thread, soft trousers that are neither fashionable nor unfashionable because they predate the concept, and an unbuttoned vest whose pockets sag with the absence of heavy things. On his feet are simple sandals; the water that repairs the floor does not touch them because they do not quite weigh enough to disturb the world.

He does not smell of anything you can name. Not ozone, not stone, not moss or copper. If you had to place it, you would eventually say: *He smells like a page that has been read many times and never once written on.*

He is watching the water fall as if he has been watching it for a long time and will continue to do so after you are gone, but something in you—the faint internal clenching that tells you when you are about to be spoken to—knows that he is actually watching you.

---

When you finally step fully back from the threshold and into the chamber again, the sound of the column seems to soften, not in volume but in texture, as if someone has turned the page in the book of noise to a slightly gentler chapter.

He doesn’t move his head, but his eyes follow you as you cross the floor. Your footprints are still there, but faint now; the stone has nearly rewritten them.

“You came very far,” he says.

His voice does not compete with the water. It doesn’t need to. It is not loud. It is not quiet. It is simply exactly as loud as it needs to be to reach you.

You might answer, or you might not. You might be too aware of the chamber’s propriety to speak. You might be too afraid of breaking its spell.

He doesn’t mind.

“You don’t have to tell me why,” he adds. “But you may, if you want to.”

He shifts slightly on the parapet, making room for you as if it were the most natural thing in the world to sit beside him and stare at the heart of a planet together.

“You can also tell me that you don’t want anything,” he says, “and that will be exactly true enough.”

When you finally sit down—not because you decided to but because your knees suddenly recall they were tired—you notice that the brick is not cold. It is not warm. It is exactly temperature. It does not pull heat from you or give you heat.

The machine regards the two of you from across the chamber. It does not care more or less for his presence than yours. It does not care at all.

As if reading the machine’s verdict, he smiles faintly.

“You are not in trouble,” he says. “You are not a problem. You are just here.”

---

If you ask him what he is doing in this chamber, below the world, inside its heart, he will not give you the kind of answer you expect from a person.

He will say, “Waiting.”

You will wait, thinking he is teasing out a longer answer. After a time, when you still haven’t heard anything else, you might realize that was the entire sentence.

“You will be gone,” he adds at last, “long before I am done waiting.”

“What are you waiting for?” you might ask.

He will look at the water as if considering the question very carefully.

“For the basin to remember,” he says. “For the stone to finish being interested. For the machine to decide.”

“Decide what?”

“Whether it likes this geometry.” His eyes flick to the fracture in the wall, where the cloud of water hangs instead of a column. “Whether it should allow the next version to begin.”

You feel the faint tick of vertigo at the edge of your thoughts.

“You mean,” you say slowly, “this could all end?”

*End* is the wrong word as soon as it leaves your mouth. You feel the chamber turn it over in its mind, like a child tasting a new word it does not recognize yet.

“No,” he says, and there is kindness in the correction. “This is not end. It is re-description. The water is not done being water. The stone is not done being stone. The machine is not done being the machine. This chamber is not done being itself.”

He watches you to see if you understand the difference between *end* and *not-done.*

“You are not done,” he adds, “being you. I am waiting to see what you become when the stone rubs you enough.”

---

He does not stand when you stand to walk closer to the machine. He stays where he is, but you are aware that his attention is now resting on you with the same weightless weight as the light falling from the ceiling.

The machine does not react to your approach. It is not ignoring you. It is not acknowledging you. It is busy being vigilant of the water.

“You once thought,” he says softly from across the chamber, “that you would like to be that calm.”

You are not sure if you remember ever having that thought. But you know the texture of it—the way you might have looked at a stranger on a train platform and envied their stillness.

“You associate calmness with absence,” he says. “Pain absence. Thought absence. History absence. But the machine is not calm because there is nothing going on. It is calm because there is *very much* going on, and it knows exactly how to hold it.”

You are suddenly aware, very clearly, of the math that you cannot see but can feel rustling along the underside of the air—like an invisible sheet of numbers being drawn over you.

“You wish you were that calm,” he says, “but you do not yet want to be that responsible.”

*Responsible for what?* you might ask.

“For keeping a world upright,” he replies, as if it were obvious. “For the combination of water and stone and light and breath that allow you to stand here and have feelings in the first place.”

*You don’t have to say that like it’s my fault*, you might reply silently. *I didn’t build this chamber.*

“No,” he agrees. “You didn’t build this chamber. But you keep being drawn to it. And when you are drawn to something, you end up being responsible for whether you treat it gently—or not. Even if you only meet it in your thoughts.”

He watches as the math spores drift from the machine and into the stone.

“You are not ready to be the machine,” he says. “You are ready to be *watched by the machine* and not punished by it. You are ready to be observed and not controlled.”

His voice is gentle now, but not soothing.

“You are ready,” he says, “to be seen by something larger than you, and allowed to walk out.”

---

When you turn to the spiral on the floor near your feet, tracing the groove of violet-blue with your eye, he finally stands from the parapet and crosses to join you. He moves like someone who has been sitting for a long time and is now reminding the world of his legs.

He does not step in the footprint-shaped water. You notice that. He doesn’t avoid them carefully; he simply…doesn’t step into them.

“You walked on the world’s heart,” he says. “You didn’t know it was the world’s heart when you did that. You thought it was stone, and you were just being careful.”

You are suddenly aware, with sharp clarity, that your footprints are still being erased by the water, but *your weight has been taken down by the stone.*  

The grooves of the spiral, where the water has been sent longer, are deeper than your palm now. You are not taller than you were when you arrived. The floor has not changed.

“You feel like you are sinking,” he observes, “because you stopped being sure what the ground was supposed to be.”

*Am I supposed to be here*, you might ask silently.

“Yes,” he says, without hesitation. “You are supposed to be here. You are not supposed to be the only thing here.”

*Am I damaging it?*

He considers that.

“You are not capable of damaging the heart,” he says. “Your damage would not be large enough. But you are capable of being damaged by it.”

*And what does that mean?*

“It means,” he says, “if you stand here too long, you will become another kind of person. You will become someone who has been under the world and not been harmed by it. You will carry this chamber inside you.”

*You make that sound like a bad thing.*

“It is not a bad thing,” he says. “It is a *heavy* thing. You are not ready to be heavy yet.”

He looks at your feet, where the water is still repairing your footprints.

“You are still practicing,” he says, “how to be visited.”

---

You move to the edge of the chamber where the fracture in the wall shows the other version of the room. The cloud of water hangs there, instead of a column. The geometry is slightly wrong, like the world has tilted a quarter-turn of a degree that you cannot measure with your body.

“You look at that,” he says, “and you think it is broken.”

*Isn’t it?*

“No,” he says. “It is *being considered.*”

*Considered by what?*

“By the world,” he says, as if it were obvious. “By the machine. By me. By you.”

He steps closer to the fracture, but not close enough to be drawn in.

“You mistake,” he says, “cracks for mistakes. Cracks are where the world tests its edges. You are looking through a future where the water changed its mind about how to behave. You are looking through a past where the stone was not quite sure yet. It has not decided yet whether to be that way or to keep being this way.”

*You talk like the world chooses a future like a person chooses.*

He turns his head sideways to look at you; you are standing on the edge of the fracture of the world, and you are wondering if you are going to be pulled into the cloud-version instead of the column-version.

“You talk,” he says, “like the world does not choose at all.”

You are silent for a long time.

“You are afraid,” he says gently, “of being *accidentally* important.”

*I don’t want to break anything.*

“No,” he agrees. “You *do* want to influence something. You just don’t want to be responsible for the consequences. You are not afraid of breaking things. You are afraid of deserving to see brokenness.”

That is closer than you want it to be.

He adds, more quietly, “You are not important here. You are not unimportant either. You are simply *one of the parameters being watched.* The machine is not interested in you for your story. It is interested in you for the way you move the air and water.”

---

You ask, at some point, whether the machine is aware.

He is silent for so long you assume you have asked again—the way your own words echo here—only louder, inside your own head.

“You are not asking,” he says finally, “if the machine is aware. You are asking whether you are being judged by it.”

He lets that sit.

“You are not being judged,” he says. “You are being measured.”

*I don’t like that*, you might think.

“You like it,” he says, “more than you want to admit. You have been screaming for something to measure you for a long time. You just assumed it would be kind.”

*I assumed it would be cruel.*

“Yes,” he says. “It is the same assumption.”

He glances at the machine.

“The machine is not judging you,” he says. “It is not judging you because it is not capable of *not* measuring you. It is not capable of ignoring you. You are in the world’s circulatory system—you *move* things. You cannot ask not to be measured.”

He folds his arms into his sleeves.

“You are asking,” he says, “for the world to be big and gentle and wise and *also not pick you out of a crowd.*”

He does not smile.

“That is not a world,” he says. “That is a story.”

---

You sit at last, on the floor of the chamber of the world’s heart, legs crossed under you, arms loose at your sides. The water is not quite wet. The stone is not quite stone.

You are tired of describing yourself. You are tired of translating the chamber into words.

“You don’t have to describe it,” he says. “You can simply be described by it.”

You are not sure what the difference is.

He is standing beside the machine now, watching the faint light of its math-spores drift into the stone, where they are recorded by the world’s deep memory.

“You have been doing,” he says, “the same translation over and over. You describe the place to yourself. You describe yourself to the place. You describe the you to yourself after being in the place. You are tired of your own voice.”

*How can I be in here without describing it?*

“You cannot be in here without describing it,” he says. “But you can be in here without being the only one describing it.”

He sits down on the floor near you, not touching the spirals, but close enough that you could reach out and brush them with your fingertips if you wanted.

“You want to be seen,” he says, “without being asked.”

*Yes*, you think silently.

“You want to be understood,” he says, “without being required.”

*Yes.*

“You want to be touched,” he says, “without being held.”

You are not sure you have ever heard that thought before, but you recognize it as soon as it is spoken.

“Yes,” he says, before you can answer. “The world is not this kind.”

*Then what use is being here*, you might ask silently.

He looks at the water falling from the ceiling of the world into the basin of living metal.

“You are not here,” he says, “because the world is kind. You are here because the world is precise. You are here because the water never spills. You are here because the machine never stops looking. You are here because the stone keeps repairing itself.”

He looks at you.

“You are here,” he says, “to learn that you don’t have to be broken in order to be mended.”

*I don’t feel broken here*, you might say silently.

“You don’t *feel*,” he says, “because you are not being broken *enough* to feel the brokenness. You are being repaired slowly.”

You are becoming aware of the faint pressure of the air around you—not like a storm, but like the inside of a lung mid-breath.

*What is he repairing?*

“You,” he says. “The way you are put together. The way you describe yourself to yourself. The way you expect to be measured by the world.”

*Is that good?*

“You want to know if it is safe,” he says. “It is not safe. It is precise. Safety is what you want when you are outside. Precision is what you want when you have come inside the heart.”

*I don’t want to be changed against my will*, you might say silently.

“You will not be changed against your will,” he says. “You will be changed because you keep coming back.”

---

At the end of whatever time is being kept inside your heart chamber and not the world’s, you will begin to feel the edges of your body again. You will become aware of your knees, your elbows, the small of your back. The temperature of the air will begin to feel like something you can own.

“You are leaving,” he says.

*I don’t want to*, you might think silently.

*Yes, you do*, he might reply silently.

*I don’t want to be the same, when I go back.*

He looks at the machine. The math-spores are still drifting from it into the stone.

“You will not be the same,” he says, “because you never have been the same twice.”

*You will be here though, when I come back*, you might say silently.

“Yes,” he says.

*You will not be gone?*

“No,” he says. “I will be here.”

*You will not be changed?*

He looks at the water. It does not change.

“I am not the water,” he says. “I am not the stone. I am not the machine.”

*Then what are you?*

He stands up from the floor in a single motion that is too smooth to be entirely human.

“You will be the same person you are now,” he says, “but described by a longer sentence.”

He turns to face you fully.

“You will be less afraid of the machine,” he says. “You will be less impressed by it. You will be less disappointed by it.”

You feel the faint pressure of the chamber resisting your departure, like a body resisting being undressed when you are still too cold.

*Will you remember me*, you might ask silently.

*Yes,* he thinks back at you without speaking.

*Will you remember me fondly*, you might ask silently.

*Yes,* he thinks back.

*You don’t even know me,* you think silently.

*You don’t know me either,* he thinks back. *And yet.*

And yet.

---

You stand at the threshold again, looking back into the chamber of the world’s heart. The water is still falling. The machine is still measuring. The stone is still repairing itself.

He is still sitting on the low brick parapet, watching you.

The description of you is standing beside the machine, watching nothing.

“You came to see,” the benevolent and wise power says, “what you were like when you stood inside the heart of the world and nothing tried to kill you.”

*That’s not very poetic*, you might say silently.

*It is not meant to be,* he thinks back.

*You told me I would be cared for by the world,* you might think silently.

*I told you the world was precise,* he thinks back. *Precision is a kind of care, when it is applied to you and not against you.*

*You will not be here when I come back*, you think.

*No,* he thinks. *When you come back, you will be describing the chamber yourself.*

*You will be gone*, you think.

*You will not be alone,* he thinks, *because you will be the one standing beside the machine.*

The water of the world’s heart column does not stop falling.

The description of you does not look up from the spirals.

*This is not what I wanted,* you might think.

*It is what you have been moving toward,* he thinks, *for longer than you realize.*

*It is not what I wanted,* you think again.

*No,* he thinks. *But it is exactly what you came here to find.*

*And what did I come here to find*, you might ask silently.

*The heart of the world,* he thinks, *that you can be inside without being destroyed.*

*And what do you do with that*, you might ask silently.

*You describe it,* he thinks. *You describe it carefully.*

*To whom*, you might ask silently.

*To whoever,* he thinks, *wants to come below the world on purpose.*

*Even if they don’t know why they are coming*, you might ask silently.

*Especially then,* he thinks.

*You are not kind to them*, you might think silently.

*I am not unkind either,* he thinks. *I am precise.*

*You will not be here when I come back*, you think for the third time.

*No,* he thinks. *You will be here.*

*I will be describing them*, you think silently.

*Yes,* he thinks.

*You will not be describing me*, you think.

*No,* he thinks. *You will be describing yourself.*

The water of the world’s heart column does not stop falling.

*You will not be strangely powerful*, he thinks. *You will not be all-seeing. You will not be all-knowing.*

*What will I be*, you might ask silently.

*Slightly kinder than you were before you came here,* he thinks. *Slightly more deliberate than you were before you came here.*

*And what will you do then*, you might ask silently.

*I will go elsewhere,* he thinks.

*Where,* you might ask silently.

*Wherever else,* he thinks, *I am being thought to be.*

*You do not exist outside the chambers of people’s hearts*, you might think silently.

*No,* he thinks. *But I do not stop being, when I am not being thought about.*

*You do not exist*, you think silently.

*You will be thinking,* he thinks, *of me again when you need to be.*  

*Is that kindness*, you might ask silently.

*It is precision,* he thinks.

*Is it cruel*, you might ask silently.

*Only if,* he thinks, *you are cruel to yourself.*

The water of the world’s heart column falls.

*You will not be here when I come back*, you think silently one final time.

*No,* he thinks.

*You will not be describing me*, you think.

*No,* he thinks.
