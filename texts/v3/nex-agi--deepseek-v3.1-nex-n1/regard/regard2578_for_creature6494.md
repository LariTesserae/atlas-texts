Beneath the plate of the world, the air is thick with warm humidity and the low, bone-deep thrum of engines older than empires. Water beads on the ironwork, the stone, the countless bodies crawling over the ceiling, and droplets fall into cisterns with a slow, eternal rhythm: tap-tap… plunk… tap-tap… plunk.

No one hears the arrival. The engines do not miss a stroke. The water does not stutter. It is not that sound where the Visitor appears.

It is the silence at the center of things.

In the heart of an engine temple—one of the largest, the kind whose cast-iron ribs are a city in themselves—Engine-Chanter Elara feels the change before she names it. The floor under her feet vibrates with the normal, deep inhale-exhale of the machine, her body’s bones long since tuned to its rhythm. Her eyes, the reflective silver-grey of her people, trace the familiar network of dripping pipes, the sweat on the enamel plates, the faint amber gleam of bulbs in the fog.

Then she feels it: the tremor of the engine no longer passes through her.

Her heart beats its own fast rhythm against the steady, slow motion of the world. The thrum has not gone; it is still there, she can feel it in her soles, but it is as if a space has opened inside her where she no longer resonates with the machine. Her chest is hollow, quiet. The world is still roaring away, but between her and the engine, for the first time in her life, there is silence.

The other apprentices haven’t noticed. Their eyes are on the gauges, the condensation, the chains and tension rods. Elara steps back, lifting her hand from the warm metal casing—she never realized how she kept her palm on it at all times, until now she is afraid to touch it.

On the other side of the temple, in the damp shadows where the ducts run into the rock, the white fungus network pulses with faint new light. Its usual pale glow sharpens, then organizes itself into lines that branch like veins, or like lightning. In the ducts where the fluid-studded air passes, the mycelial map trembles and reforms.

Duet, the fruiting body, emerges from its usual crack, its blind head questing. Its many legs taste the air, not for food, but for pressure, for charge, for the sudden stillness of the dust. Its colonies all over the city—wherever water ducts and ancient channels run—feel it too: the quietness spreading along the fungal lattice, the sense of a pressure like a storm not yet broken.

In the ceiling’s dark corners, the Moss-Crawlers stop moving.

Not one or two: all of them.

Their colonies, in which every individual life is but a wave in a greater skin of motion, freeze as one. The constant scraping rustle dims and falls away. Wherever a human maintenance worker has been leaning in the half-dark, listening to the world’s surface breathe, they suddenly hear that breath hush.

Water still drops. Chains still groan. The engines still turn their endless wheels. But the living stone pelts above the Overhang all, at once, go still.

The Chain-Gulls scream first.

Their harsh cry—like metal chains dragged across stone—pierces the normal hum of voices, engines, dripping water. Inverted districts look up, as they’ve been trained to do. The children’s rhyme plays in every mind: “Gull-call, screech-long; the chain’s been wrong.”

The inhabitants’ tapetum-lucid eyes catch the light of thousands of lamps, reflected in a thousand anxious faces. The familiarity of the warning is sharpened into a blade: the Gulls are not calling from one or two chains, they are calling everywhere.

The great city—under its ceiling of stone and its tier of houses and its lowest delicate lattice of collection platforms—does not know how to register panic. It is too slow, too dense, its habits built over centuries of preservation rituals. Its people are a compact, sturdy folk whose ancestors learned to think in terms of inward, outward, along-tilt, not “emergency.”

But they know how to listen. The silence of the Crawlers, the frantic calls of the Gulls, the hollow unease in Elara’s chest—these are new languages demanding translation.

In the ducts of the engine temple, the Duet’s pale head turns towards the center of the machine. The mycelium pulses brighter. Its light syncs to the old engine’s vibration—two beats per seismic sigh, slow as the heartbeat of a mountain.

Elara hears the apprentice at her side gasp.

The fungus along the walls is forming shapes.

No one needs to explain to an engine priest that the world is trying to speak. The engines are gods, their rhythm the city’s pulse. If the ducts choose to write, you read.

Lines of light curl into words in the fungal mat: phrases in the engineers’ dialect, but written in the wrong language again. It’s not written in ink, but in glowing filaments.

SHE HAS NOT COME TO CHANGE YOU

the mycelium reads,

SHE HAS NOT COME TO CHANGE

YOU ARE THE PRESERVATION OF THE WORLD

SHE SEEKS ONLY PRESERVATION

It’s a broken sentence, mixing syntaxes, echoing the ducts’ layout, the generations’ limited maintenance scripts. Words repeat in different patterns. The priestesses and priests crowd closer, staring. The idea of “she” swims among their crowded terms of reference: “It’s the engine,” one says. “It’s the plateau,” another argues. “It’s the city itself.”

Elara, though, knows the feeling inside her chest. She lifts her hand from the casing where she cannot feel the engine’s rhythm now, but she can feel, instead, the sense of another being’s regard settling like a second gravity inside her bones.

She steps out onto the narrow balcony overlooking the lowest tier of the city, where the world gaps open into the luminous cloud-sea below. The others follow her voice, their metallic eyes catching the reflected glow. The abyss beneath them is the same as it always is—a glowing, indistinct barrier of fog, their low sky, their Downward—but now the fog itself begins to move.

Or rather, to remember.

Images bloom in the cloud-sea below, not solid, but edges of memory catching the light of the city’s lamps and engines: a faint suggestion of figures on a ground they’ve never seen; the suggestion of forests; of mountains that are right-way up to the world; of the far, theoretical floor-planet they’ve never walked on.

The sky remembers the ground. The Cloud-Sea of the Overhang, below them all, becomes a screen that no one built. It is not mind or device in the engineer sense, but a medium of presence and memory, the same way rock is memory to pressure, heat, time.

The Gulls quiet now, watching. The Crawlers remain still. The water drips, its rhythm unchanged, but everyone listens. The city itself is listening.

The Visitor’s awareness comes like a pressure on the world’s mind rather than a voice in its ear. She doesn’t speak to them alone, but through the whole system—through engines, ducts, Crawlers, stone, moisture—anything that can hold her gentle, weighty presence.

She is not of their kind, but she understands their kind of wisdom.

Engine-Chanter Elara becomes the place where her meanings take coherent language. She stands on the balcony, her voice shaking at first, then steadied by decades of listening.

“She…” Elara begins, not knowing how she knows it, “She comes from the ground-world.”

Below, in the cloud-sea, the image of a ground—green, sun-facing, open—forms and trembles.

“She has been watching the Overhang.” Elara’s throat tenses. “Not to judge. To learn.”

The city hears its own name in the low engine sigh. The Overhang, its plates, its engines, its chains, its inhabitants—itself.

“She says the ground world is broken.”

The fungi writhe in the ducts, forming new words: FRAGMENTED. EXHAUSTED. INTENSIFIED.

“She is not here to fix us,” Elara says, swallowing the taste of fear in the mouth of her people. “She is not here to tear us down. We’re not wrong.”

Above them, the Crawlers on the ceiling stir, just a little—their bodies ripple in a coordinated wave, like wind across the pelt of the world.

“She is here,” Elara says, the words hollowing her chest as she speaks them, “because she can’t fix the ground. Its time is… fraying. Its cycles are collapsing. There’s still life there, but it’s… fragile.”

She pauses, listening inward to the silence that the Visitor’s attention fills. Her own eyes gleam brighter in the glow that the city’s lamps reflect from the fog.

“She is looking for a place to store the world’s pattern.”

The city’s culture has built its mind around maintenance and long cycles. Its engines are not explosive surges of power; they’re steady rolls of energy, year after year. Its factories are patience incarnate: one bolt at a time over decades. Its very architecture is about the slow, continuous struggle to hold on to the stone ceiling, against gravity’s tug.

In their minds, the Visitor’s next question spreads through the engines, ducts, Crawlers, clouds:

Will you hold the pattern?

In the fungal patchwork, words shimmer:

WILL YOU REMEMBER IT GROUNDWARDS

SO SHE CAN REMEMBER IT SKYWARDS

The city’s inhabitants, looking down at the cloud-sea screen—unsure if they are looking “down” or “up” at that world below—hesitate.

To hold the pattern of the ground world: its forests, its oceans, its warmth, its cold, its seasons, its quickness—is it possible? Their time is slow. Their engines are measured in beats per minute that add to decades. Their world is condensed, packed, moist, hot, and inverted.

The Visitor’s presence settles like a pressure they already know how to bear. The District Elder of the collection platforms below makes the suggestion first. “We’ve always kept the city’s rhythm,” she says. “Why not the world’s?”

Elara’s answer arrives the same way her knowledge first came: as intuition she can’t trace back to any input. “She’s not asking us to change ourselves,” she says. “She’s asking us to remember.”

“So we’re going to be… a memory engine?” a young apprentice asks behind her.

“Yes,” she says, looking at the cloud-sea below and its faint suggestion of trees. “For everything.”

So the city’s engines become more than anchors of weight and power; they become anchors of time.

The Visitor teaches the Overhang a new use of its engines, not by changing the hardware, but by adjusting the way the engines vibrate and the way the ducts carry the rhythm of their world into the network of fungi and Crawlers. The water’s drip-drip-drip becomes a code that the Crawlers listen to, their mucus layers now channels not just for mineral cleaning but for storing subtle vibrations of memory in the very stone they’re protecting.
