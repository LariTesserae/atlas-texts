She also breathes, but not like you.

She is not an extra wind in the air, or a new machine under the pavement. A benevolent and wise power does not arrive like a disaster. You would not know her from the humidity or the pale shine of the sky. She is not a storm; she is a condition.

You first notice her when you notice yourself noticing.

You pause on the low swell of land above the channels, and you realize your own breath has slowed to match the city’s. The ground sighs, and your lungs follow: not precisely in sync, but in the same key, the same tempo. This is not suggestion. It is not your imagination.

You watch a tide of breath move through the world.

You see it in the Floaters’ boats: the same impulse that draws the water into the bay when the “loose” comes, now gentles their oars and poles. The boats travel less against the channel, and more with its tendency. Something steers without touching them, using the currents of water and muscle and coincidence.

When a Floater poling from a rooftop misses her step and slips toward the edge, her bare foot finds not air but a ridge of half-melted brickwork that might not have been so precisely inclined were it not for this unseen hand. She does not fall. The city catches her without fanfare, and the moment belongs to her, not to the power that intervened.

The water remains the same temperature of almost-too-warm.

The birds are still the city’s true chorus.

The ibises and gulls and crows and nameless mud-colored birds still shift on their ledges and sills, but their attention feels less tight to you. They are watchful, but not as watchful. They do not turn to you in unison when you move. Instead, when you step onto a broken slab of concrete that you suspect is not entirely safe, the bird on the crooked lamppost above you takes sudden and noisy exception: it bolts, wings slapping the air, its cry ragged.

The explosive sound makes you jerk back—just in time. The slab you were testing tips with glacial inevitability into the water, where it becomes just another dark shape in the channel.

The bird settles on the arm of a still-upright streetlamp nearby and refuses to look at you.

You are not sure, of course.

That is the pattern of her benevolence: she always leaves you unsure.

You never know if she was there at all, or only in the way of things: one of the countless small adjustments that keep the city from tipping over into ruin outright. The rules do not change. The ground still sighs. The concrete is still treacherous. The salt still eats quietly.

But you survive the things you should not have.

So you follow her movement through the city as you would follow the smell of fresh bread behind walls: by its absence as well as its presence.

She is not here.

She is here.

She is not the mumble of wings from the buildings. She is the fact that when you wander under their ledges, your eyes are never blessed by the precise and white-painted message of a bird’s droppings. You will not know this is mercy because you will never know what happened elsewhere.

You are, instead, surprised by a small, clean blessing of your own awareness:

You notice, without being aware of noticing, that the factory’s smoke is not moving you.

The faint smell of it, when the wind shifts from that far low brick block of buildings, is not burning your throat or tightening your chest. It is not the sourness of acid fumes or the dullness of old carbon. You smell the metal of pipes and scorched dust and the warmth of the machinery itself, but no longer the sting.

You are not foolish. You know the Keepers still feed the boilers with the same scrap of the world as they ever did. But you are not ill from it.

You are not better in the city; the city has become better in you.

This is not an improvement anyone else will be able to verify.

The next time you pass the factory, on the high surface of the road that still stubbornly refuses to sink into the channels, the guard in the glassless window of the second story will be the same watchful-eyed woman or tired-shouldered man who has waved you away before. They will still not know you.

The metal will still be rusting, the brickwork sweating along the mortar line, the pumps still thumping from under the floor.

But you notice that the guard looks at you without suspicion. You notice that a small glazed pot of green on their window ledge has an extra handful of leaves in it than you last recall, and the pot is not cracked at the lip.

You are also not a better gardener than you were before.

This feels impolite to the city: to claim that someone’s carelessness is now better channeled.
