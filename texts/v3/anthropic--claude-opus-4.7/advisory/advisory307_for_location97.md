# Preparing for the Kettle Terraces

## What you are actually going to

Before anything else, understand what this place is and isn't. It isn't a destination. It's a landing between staircases. Someone built it — or something did, or it built itself under instruction — as a place where several elsewheres are made briefly viewable from one small hot shelf of new rock. You are going as a guest to a room full of windows that look onto other people's weather. Prepare accordingly: quietly, with the minimum of luggage, and with the assumption that you are the loudest and clumsiest thing that will be there.

## Clothing and body

The heat will hit you as a wall, then become a medium. Dress for a sauna you cannot leave. Thin, fast-drying layers in light colors — wool or technical synthetics, nothing cotton (it will soak and stay soaked and chill you the moment a mist rolls through). A brimmed hat to keep condensation off your face. A light shell in your pack for the cloud-forest below and the cold descent afterward; you will want it badly on the way down, when your clothes are wet through and the altitude starts taking its heat back.

Footwear: stiff-soled boots with good edges. The andesite is glass-sharp where it's fresh and slick where the moss has colonized it. A fall here is not a scrape, it's a laceration, and the nearest medicine is however many days back down the mountain you came. Gaiters help with the wet ferns.

Bring more water than you think. You are inside a humidifier that is also a furnace. You will sweat liters and not notice because it evaporates before it beads. Electrolytes. Salt tablets. A small towel for your hands and face — you'll want to touch your eyes at some point and your hands will be streaming.

Eat before you arrive. Don't eat on the shelf. Crumbs are a kind of offering and you are not there to make offerings.

## What not to bring

Leave behind anything you would be devastated to lose. Loose coins, loose jewelry, anything that dangles off a pack strap. The rule of the place — *don't put anything in the pools* — extends to don't-let-anything-fall-in. Check your pockets before you enter the basin. Tape over zipper pulls if you have to. If you wear glasses, use a strap.

Leave the drone. Leave the instruments. Leave the sample kit. The makers of this place built no instruments of their own, and the pools are not the sort of thing you measure; they are the sort of thing that measures you, quietly, and declines to report its findings. A scientific attitude will work against you here — not because curiosity is wrong but because the impulse to *verify* will push you toward the one thing you mustn't do, which is reach in.

No stones from elsewhere. No coins-for-luck. No ribbons tied to ferns. No ashes of anyone. The shelf is not a shrine and does not want to become one.

## What to bring

A notebook with a pencil (pens will run in the humidity). Record what you see through the pools — not to publish, but because you will forget, and the forgetting will feel wrong later, like having lost a name. Write small. Don't sketch the pools' interiors in detail; sketch what you felt about them.

A watch, mechanical if possible. The sun moves and the shadows cross the pools and this is the real clock of the place. Knowing the hour lets you predict which pool will shadow next and gives you time to position yourself quietly nearby.

A small piece of food for after — something you like, to eat once you are off the shelf and back in the cloud. You will want the ordinary world back.

A headlamp with a red filter, in case you stay past dusk. (Consider carefully whether you want to. The pools at night are a different proposition, and I don't know anyone who recommends it.)

## How to move on the shelf

Walk slowly. The quiet is not decorative; it is a property of the place, and loud movement is an intrusion the pools seem to register even if they don't react visibly. Keep your footfalls deliberate. Don't talk. If you are traveling with another person, agree beforehand that you will not speak on the shelf, and that you will signal by touch or by prearranged gesture if you need to move each other.

Approach the pools from the sides, not the downhill edge. Kneel to look, don't lean. Keep your center of gravity back. A pool without a bottom is a thing you can fall into in more than one sense: physically, if you slip, and attentionally, if you stare too long. Give yourself a rule — look for thirty seconds, look away for thirty seconds, reassess. Don't look into the same pool twice in a row without a break between.

Watch the shadow line. When the ridge's shadow is about to cross a pool, be already near it, already still, already not-thinking-about-yourself. The shadowed view is the clearest view and lasts only minutes. This is the closest the place comes to offering a gift; be ready to receive it by being unobtrusive.

Do not approach the fourth pool when it is shadowed. Look from a distance. The grey sea under grey sky is the one the intuition flags hardest, and intuition on the shelf is your best instrument. If a pool gives you a feeling of being looked back at, back away without turning your back to it, the way you would leave a room where something old was sleeping.

## The frog

There is a pale frog at the third pool. It has watched many people. Do not try to touch it, photograph it, or feed it. Do not stare at it directly for long. Acknowledge it the way you'd acknowledge a shrine-keeper whose language you don't share: a small nod, eyes lowered, and then you carry on with your own business. It is probably just a frog. It is certainly at least a frog.

## The cairns

The three stone cairns are positioned at viewpoints between certain pools. Someone who understood the geometry of the place put them there. Stand at them. Look from them in the directions they seem to indicate. Do not add stones to them. Do not take stones from them. Do not touch them more than you would touch someone else's grave marker.

The low half-collapsed wall is the one thing on the shelf that invites contact. You may sit on it. It has been sat on. Sitting is a legitimate thing to do here; it may be the most legitimate thing.

## Mental preparation

Spend the week before your visit reducing your input. Less news, less conversation, less music. You want to arrive with a quiet mind because a loud mind on the shelf will rattle around in the silence and you will find yourself doing something stupid just to break the tension — dropping a pebble, saying something aloud, wading in to cool off. The place's only defense against tourists is the tourists' own sense of awe, and if you have arrived full of chatter that awe will come late and find you already in trouble.

Decide, before you go, what you will do if you see something through a pool that you recognize. A place from your life. A person. A room. This happens, apparently, to some travelers — not often, but often enough that it is worth having prepared. The discipline is: look, note it in your book, do not lean closer, do not call out, do not reach. Whatever is on the other side is not reachable from here. The pool is a window, not a door, and the temptation to test this is the temptation that ends badly.

Similarly, decide what you will do if you see nothing. Some visitors report that every pool for them showed only ordinary water, ordinary stone, as if the place had declined them. This is not a failure. The shelf does not owe you a view. Sit on the wall. Watch the steam go straight up. That is also the thing.

## Leaving

Leave before you feel you need to. The heat compounds, the humidity compounds, and people have been found on the shelf confused and heat-sick because they forgot that a medium can drown you. Set yourself a time — two hours is generous, one hour is plenty — and leave at it regardless of what a pool is doing. The shelf will be there next time, or it won't; either way you are not its resident.

On the way out, turn at the lip of the drop-side and look back once. Not to say goodbye. Just to confirm to yourself that the place continues without you — the steam still rising in columns, the beetle still lifting and resettling, the frog still blinking — and that you were, for an hour, a small, loud, sweating animal permitted to sit among these things and watch them work.

Then go down into the cloud. Eat your small piece of food. Do not talk about what you saw for at least a day. Let it set, the way new rock sets, with its glass edges still on it, before you handle it with words.
