# Preparing to Visit the Underworks at Caul

## Understanding What You're Entering

Before anything practical, understand this: you are visiting a built thing that is becoming a place. That distinction matters more than it sounds. The Underworks is not a ruin — ruins are industrial spaces that have been abandoned long enough for nature to reclaim them in familiar ways. This is the opposite situation. The engines are running. The purpose is active. And the ecology is a squatter, new and tentative, improvising in the margins of something still functionally itself. You are entering a working machine that has started, almost absentmindedly, to grow a world inside its own lungs.

This shapes everything about how you should prepare.

## Clothing and Personal Gear

**Thermal layering for sustained cold-damp, not cold-dry.** The temperature hovers just above freezing, which sounds manageable, but the humidity is near saturation and water falls on you constantly from above. This is the cruelest kind of cold — it defeats most insulation within hours because everything gets wet. Wear wool base layers (wool insulates when wet; synthetics don't, cotton kills you), a fleece mid-layer you are willing to see ruined, and over everything a proper waterproof shell — not rain-resistant, waterproof, with taped seams. A hood is essential. The drip finds the back of your neck with malicious precision.

**Boots that are industrial, not hiking.** You will be walking on cast-iron grating, slag-concrete, packed clinker, and wet metal plate. Lugged hiking soles slip on wet steel. You want steel-shank work boots with oil-and-slip-resistant rubber soles, waterproof to the ankle at minimum, preferably to mid-calf. Size up for two pairs of wool socks. Bring a second pair of boots in your pack; one pair will be wet within a day no matter what you do.

**Gloves: two pairs.** Thin wool liners for dexterity, thick waterproof work gloves for everything else. The rivets and girders and railings will be cold enough to stick to bare wet skin. You will want to touch things — the horsetails, the lichen — and for that the liners. For climbing catwalk ladders, the heavy pair.

**A hard hat with a clip-on lamp.** Not a headlamp alone — a hard hat. Things fall from the ceiling. Rust, condensation, occasionally larger pieces when a bracket gives way. The sodium vault-lamps are spaced for orientation, not for illumination of your path; most of your actual seeing will be done by your own light.

**Respirator, at least an N95, preferably a half-face with P100 cartridges.** The soot is old coal and metal dust and whatever the engines are venting in trace amounts through the seams. Acute exposure will not hurt you. Sustained exposure — days — will coat your lungs in a way that is difficult to reverse. The smell alone will tell you when the respirator is needed: if you notice the air tasting metallic, you've already been breathing it too long.

## Instruments and Navigation

**Forget GPS. Forget any satellite-based anything.** You are under a riveted steel ceiling. Bring a proper lensatic compass, and know that it will lie — there is too much iron in the structure for magnetic north to mean anything locally. What the compass will give you is *consistency*: it will point the same wrong direction reliably within any given region, which is enough to keep you from walking in circles.

**A good mechanical watch or a quartz watch with fresh batteries.** The shift-horns are your clock. Learn their rhythm within your first cycle. Four horns per cycle, distributed unevenly (I would guess, though you will have to confirm), and everything human-adjacent in the Underworks orients to them. If you lose track of the cycle, you lose track of when pumps run, when the drip intensifies, when arc-welding work begins in the galleries — and these are the things that will get you killed if you are in the wrong place.

**A notebook with waterproof pages — Rite in the Rain or equivalent — and pencils, not pens.** Ink runs. Pencils write on wet paper. Map as you go. You will not find charts of this place, and if you do they will be engineering schematics, not walking maps, and the landscape has diverged from them.

**A long stick, a walking staff, roughly chest-high.** For probing water depth before you step into pooled flats. For testing grating before you put weight on it. For reaching things without reaching yourself. For pushing aside the horsetail stands so you can see what is behind them. Do not underestimate the stick.

**A good flashlight with spare batteries, and a backup light, and a backup to the backup.** Three light sources, minimum. When the arc-welding flashes cut out, your dark-adapted eyes are ruined for minutes. When your primary dies in a puddle, your backup saves you. When the gallery you are in loses power for a shift-change, only your lights work. Lithium batteries in the cold, not alkaline.

## Food and Water

**Carry all your water in.** The water in the Underworks is not water you want in your body. Leaching minerals, engine run-off, whatever the chemiluminescent lichen is metabolizing — none of this has been characterized. Even boiled, it will contain heavy metals. Plan on four liters per day per person and carry it.

**Food should be dense, fatty, and require no preparation.** You will not want to take gloves off to eat. You will not want to open cans. Nuts, hard cheese, cured meat, dark chocolate, energy bars that don't freeze solid. Eat more than you think you need — the cold burns through you.

**A small stove is worth the weight** for making hot drinks at rest stops. Morale matters more than people admit on these trips, and the Underworks is a morale-hostile environment. A mug of tea in that pre-dawn November that has no dawn coming is worth three hours of effort saved.

## Biological Precautions

**Do not touch the lichen with bare skin.** Chemiluminescence on that scale implies active chemistry, and chemistry on biological tissue you are not related to is a poor bet. If you must collect a sample — and I understand the temptation — use tongs and a sealed glass jar. Wash any accidental contact immediately with your carried water, not the local water.

**Assume every animal you encounter is adapted in ways you are not prepared for.** The "rats that are not quite rats anymore" have had thirty years in constant cold, mineral-rich water, low-light conditions, and no competition. They may be docile. They may not. They may have developed toxin resistance, or toxin production, or social structures that treat you as a resource or a threat. Do not feed them. Do not approach them. If one approaches you, back away slowly and get to higher ground.

**The swimming-walking thing with the five-toed splayed prints deserves particular respect.** You will not see it coming if it does not want to be seen. It lives in the lower level, the one you cannot walk on. Do not reach through grates. Do not sit with your feet dangling over edges. Do not sleep near a grate.

## The Lower Level

This is the preparation that matters most, and the one I want to be honest about: **do not go below the floor you walked in on.**

I know. You will want to. The pale yellow light the size of a dinner plate, drifting — the warmer exhalation, humid, smelling of iron and something organic — these are exactly the things a traveler crosses the multiverse to find. I understand. But consider: the upper level is a built thing that an ecology is colonizing. The lower level is whatever is colonizing *back*. The warm humid air from below is not incidental. Something down there is warm. Something down there is organic enough to smell organic through iron. And the light that drifted past and did not come back was moving under its own power, on its own schedule, indifferent to your crouching face six inches above it through a grate.

If you insist on descending — and I suspect some travelers will, and I cannot stop them — then at minimum: go in threes, leave one above with a rope and a radio, carry no food (smell travels), carry lights you can drop and abandon, wear the respirator (the air composition below will be different), and establish a hard turnaround time that you keep regardless of what you find. The dinner-plate light is not waiting for you. It has its own business. You are not invited to it, and the invitation, if it comes, will not be for your benefit.

## Psychological Preparation

**Expect to feel watched, and prepare not to react badly to it.** The feeling is real. Whether anything is actually watching is a separate question, but the feeling is real and it will wear at you. It comes from below, as the description says, but it also comes from the scale — the sense that the space was built by purposes you do not share, for uses still being carried out, by engines that have never stopped. You are a trespasser in a functioning machine. The machine does not know you are there. But it might.

**Expect the heartbeat.** The subsonic pulses from the engines will affect your body in ways you are not used to. Some people get nauseous. Some get headaches. Some get a strange euphoria. Some get a crawling sense of dread that they cannot attribute to anything in particular. This is your sternum being shoved by a six-second stroke from a pump somewhere beyond the third bulkhead. It is a physiological effect, not a supernatural one. Knowing this helps. A little.

**Expect the call — the three descending metallic notes — and expect not to know what it is.** You will never know what it is. You will hear it perhaps once a day, from very far off, and it will not resolve into a source. This is fine. Not every sound in every place needs to be identified. Some sounds are just landscape.

**Expect loneliness of a specific kind.** The Underworks is sparsely populated. You may go a full cycle without seeing another human. The workers, if there are workers, keep to the galleries and the bulkheads and the places the arc-welding flashes come from. Out on the flats where the horsetails grow, you will mostly be alone with the drip and the breathing ventilators and the occasional distant shove of the engines. This is not a peaceful solitude. It is the solitude of being very small inside something very large that is paying attention to something else.

## A Final Thought on Why You're Going

The Underworks at Caul is worth visiting because it is a glimpse of something most places never let you see: the first few decades of an ecology. Most worlds, by the time you arrive, have settled. The species have found their niches, the water has found its channels, the landscape has made its peace with what it is. Here, none of that has happened yet. The horsetails have the fresh startled look of things that have not yet decided what they are. The rats have become something, but not yet the final something. The water is still figuring out which way is downhill.

You are watching a world begin, inside the body of a machine that does not know it is hosting one. Prepare for it with respect. Do not take souvenirs that are alive. Do not interfere with the channels the water is cutting. Do not feed the things that cross the gantries. You are a visitor to a nursery whose occupants do not yet know they are being born.

And when you leave — up the cast-iron staircase that was never meant to be walked — scrape your boots clean before you go. Whatever is starting down there should be allowed to start without you bringing pieces of it home.
