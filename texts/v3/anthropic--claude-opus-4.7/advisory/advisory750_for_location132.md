# Preparing to Visit the Undercasting

## Before You Go

**Understand what you're entering.** This is not a ruin and not a working facility. It is something in between — a process that outlived its makers and now maintains itself, and by maintaining itself, maintains the space you'll be standing in. You are a guest inside a metabolism. Behave accordingly.

**Sleep well the night before.** You will want to stand still for long periods, and fatigue in heat becomes dangerous faster than people expect. Eat something salty and substantial a few hours before descending. Do not go hungover. Do not go on stimulants that suppress your thirst cues.

**Make peace with going slow.** The cycle-time of this place is hours. If you arrive impatient you will see a warm, dim, cluttered room. If you arrive willing to sit for three hours without doing anything, you will see the town move. Plan accordingly — at minimum half a day in the chamber, ideally a full one, with rest breaks topside.

## What to Wear

**Light, loose, long.** Long sleeves and long trousers in a breathable natural fiber — linen or a light cotton. Synthetics in that heat become unpleasant and, near the pilots, a real hazard. You want fabric that lets sweat evaporate but protects you from incidental contact with pipes hotter than you'd expect.

**Leather gloves, thin ones.** You will want to steady yourself on railings, and some of the railings are iron that has been absorbing heat for decades. A bare palm on the wrong pipe is a bad afternoon.

**Boots with hard soles and good grip.** The cast-iron walkways have a film of condensate in places. The basalt steps are sharp-edged and will chew soft shoes.

**A broad-brimmed hat or a cloth for the head.** Not for sun — for the drip. Mineral-rich condensate in the hair is a nuisance; in the eyes it stings.

**No loose scarves, no trailing straps, no dangling lanyards.** The pilots are small but they are everywhere, and a trailing hem near a burner-head in poor light is how people get hurt.

## What to Carry

- **Water. More than you think.** Three liters minimum for a full day, electrolytes mixed in. The heat is dry on the tongue and you will lose water you do not notice losing.
- **A small cloth** for wiping condensate off gauges you want to read, and off your own face.
- **A notebook and a soft pencil.** Ink runs in humid heat; pens leak. Write things down. You will not remember the valve sequences otherwise.
- **A watch with a second hand, or a small stopwatch.** To see the metabolism you have to time it. Pick one basin. Time the drip. Come back in an hour.
- **A handheld light, red-filtered.** The pilot-flame light is workable but your eyes will want help in corners. Red preserves your dark-adaptation and doesn't wash out the blue-gold of the ambient light.
- **A small mirror on a stick,** the kind dentists use. For looking into pipe junctions and around corners without putting your face there.
- **A piece of chalk.** To mark your path back if you go onto the upper walkways. It is genuinely easy to get turned around among the flues.
- **Food that doesn't need preparation and doesn't crumble.** Hard cheese, dried fruit, bread. Eat in the yard, not on the walkways.

## What Not to Carry

- **No open flame of your own.** The gas seeps are unmapped. A match in the wrong niche could do something you would not survive to regret.
- **No strong perfume or scented products.** The air of the place has its own chemistry and you want to be able to smell changes in it — a sudden sulfur note, a shift in the creosote sweetness — because those are how the chamber will tell you something is wrong.
- **No metal detectors, no active radio transmitters, nothing that pings.** You are inside an instrument. Don't play your own note into it.
- **Nothing you would grieve to lose.** Things fall through the walkway grilles. Things get misplaced. Things occasionally are not where you left them.

## How to Behave Inside

**Do not touch the pipework casually.** Some of it is merely warm. Some of it is not. Assume every pipe is hot until you have specifically verified otherwise with the back of a gloved hand held near, not on.

**Do not interfere with any mechanism.** Do not turn a valve to see what it does. Do not catch water from a basin to taste it. Do not tip a counterweight early. The system is making something, and the making is load-bearing. You are not qualified to edit it, and neither is anyone else.

**Do not extinguish a pilot, even accidentally.** If you do, report it immediately to whoever admitted you, and note the location precisely. A dead pilot is a small wound in the metabolism.

**Eat nothing from the chamber.** Not the fig fruit, however tempting in its strangeness. Not water from a basin. Not anything that has grown in the drip. The minerals alone would make you ill; what else might be in there is an open question.

**Do not pick the ferns or the moss.** They are the closest thing this chamber has to residents. Leave them their pipes.

**Move with the cadence of the place.** Do not rush on the walkways. Do not speak loudly. The silence is part of what the chamber does, and your noise is a perturbation it will absorb but not forget.

## About the Central Yard

When you go to the hexagonal clearing above the sealed well — and you will — **sit, don't stand.** Sit on the iron of the walkway edge, or on the basalt lip. Close your eyes for at least ten minutes before you try to listen for the slow sound. If you try to hear it immediately you will hear only your own pulse and the pipe-sighs, and you will decide there is nothing there. It is there. It is patient. It will wait for you to be quiet enough.

**Do not speak over it when you hear it.** Do not try to record it — recordings of it are famously disappointing, and the attempt to record seems to make the hearing harder for an hour afterward. Just hear it. That is the whole of what you came for, probably, although you won't know that until later.

**Do not drop anything into the sealed well, even a coin.** Especially a coin.

## About the Small Weirdnesses

The leaning pilots, the fig-tree shadow, the sound under the sound — **notice these but do not investigate them.** They are the edges of something, and the edges are the right place to stand. Going further in is a different kind of trip and requires different preparation, which is not this document.

If you find yourself wanting very badly to understand one of these phenomena — if you feel a pull toward a particular pipe, a particular flame, a particular corner — **leave the chamber for an hour.** Go back up the basalt stair, sit in ordinary air, drink water, eat something. Then decide whether to return. The pull may be your own curiosity. It may not. Either way, the decision should be made in cooler air.

## Leaving

**Leave before you are exhausted, not after.** The basalt stair is seventy meters of sharp edges, and climbing it tired, in damp clothes, with mineral sweat in your eyes, is where most minor injuries happen.

**Do not linger in the doorway to look back.** Go up, and keep going up.

**When you reach the surface, sit for a while before you do anything else.** The air will taste wrong — thin, empty, insultingly cool. This passes. Drink water. Do not drive or make decisions for an hour.

**Expect the sound to follow you for a few days.** Not literally — you will not hear it in your kitchen. But you will catch yourself timing things against a rhythm slower than breathing. This fades. If it does not fade in a week, speak to someone who has been down before.

## One Last Thing

The chamber was built to make something, and it is still making it. You will not learn what. Nobody has. The makers are gone and the records are gone and the making goes on anyway, because stopping would close the walls. This is the lesson the Undercasting offers, if it offers one: that some processes outlive their purposes and become their own reason, and that the distinction between a machine and a living thing is, at certain depths, not as firm as we are taught.

Go down respectfully. Come back up grateful. Do not, under any circumstances, try to help.
