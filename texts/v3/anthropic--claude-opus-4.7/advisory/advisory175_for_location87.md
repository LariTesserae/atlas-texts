# Preparing to Visit the Worn Teeth of Ahal-Ruun

## First, Understand What You Are Walking Into

You are going to a place whose dominant fact is *acoustic*. Most travelers prepare for cold, for altitude, for terrain — and you will need to prepare for all of those — but the thing that will actually hurt you, if anything does, is the sound. Plan around that first. Everything else is logistics.

## The Ears

Get good hearing protection. Not one kind — three.

- **Deep-filter earplugs** (the kind musicians use) for normal walking about. These reduce volume without turning the world into muffled cotton; you need to hear your footing, your companions, and the character of the wind.
- **Full industrial muffs**, the kind used around jet engines, for storm hours. Pack them where you can reach them without removing your gloves.
- **Wax-and-wool traditional plugs** as a backup, because batteries die, foam compresses, and you may need to sleep through a multi-day chord that your modern gear cannot fully dampen.

Understand also that the sub-bass thrum described — *felt in the long bones before heard* — is not something ear protection can stop. This will wear on you in ways you will not immediately recognize. It will disturb your sleep, degrade your judgment, make small decisions feel enormous. Budget extra days for rest. Do not plan a tight schedule. The plateau does not reward haste, and hasty men in high places make the mistakes that kill them.

Consider carrying a small notebook in which you write down decisions before acting on them. If a decision looks unreasonable on paper, that is the bass-thrum in your skull talking, not you.

## The Body

This is a high plateau. Acclimatize below it for at least a week, ideally two. Altitude sickness at elevation, combined with the acoustic assault, is a combination that has probably killed more travelers than anything with claws.

- **Clothing in soft layers**, nothing that rustles loudly. You are going to be listening to your own clothes for nine days; crinkling synthetics will drive you mad. Wool, oiled cotton, soft leather.
- **Footwear with serious grip on wet stone and wet moss.** Every surface is damp. Moss on stone is one of the most treacherous walking surfaces on any world — it looks soft and is, but beneath it the rock is glazed with water. Assume every step could skate.
- **Gloves** in two weights. Your hands will be constantly wet.
- **A wide-brimmed, tie-down hat** with a chinstrap rated for strong wind. Hoods alone will not do; hoods funnel wind-noise directly into your ears and cut peripheral vision at exactly the moments you most need it.
- **Goggles**, clear and tinted both, because the "searchlight sun" will blind you when it finds you, and because rain arrives sideways.

Carry twice the calories you think you need. The wind alone burns energy. The constant low-grade acoustic stress burns more. The altitude burns more still. You will come off the plateau thinner than you went on, no matter how you eat.

## Water

This is the easy part. The plateau waters you. Carry a light flask, drink from the rock-pools (lie on your belly, as the passage says), and do not bother with elaborate filtration — the water has been pooling and seeping through porous stone for longer than your species has existed, and what lives in it, if anything, lives at concentrations too low to matter. Iodine tablets as an excess of caution, nothing more.

Do *not* drink from pools in hollows beneath the wind-holes, where the sub-bass is strongest. This is not a biological warning. It is a warning against pools that may have been singing, if you understand me. Drink from the sheltered lees, from the mossy bowls, from anywhere the water seems to have been quiet.

## Navigation

This is the hard part.

Visibility on the plateau changes from miles to arm's-length and back again while you are tying a bootlace. This means:

- **Do not rely on line-of-sight landmarks.** A peak you are walking toward may vanish for an hour at a time, and when the fog lifts you may find you have drifted a quarter-mile off course.
- **Rope up when visibility is poor**, even on flat moss. The plateau has edges. The edges drop into clouds. A person ten feet ahead of you can disappear, and the next step may be air.
- **Compass and backup compass.** Stone here may be magnetic in places — test before trusting.
- **String-and-cairn method**: if you need to leave camp, lay a line behind you, or at least build small cairns you can find on the return. Do not dismantle cairns you find already built; they may be someone's lifeline, or they may be older things, and either way the plateau is not a place where one casually unmakes what others have made.

Move slowly in fog. The temptation to push on so as to make distance will get you killed. The plateau is patient; so must you be.

## Shelter

Do not camp on open moss in the path of the wind-funnels. Find the lees — the downwind sides of the tooth-stumps, the hollows where ferns grow tallest (ferns tell you where the wind does not reach). The old roofless house, and places like it, make adequate wind-breaks even with no roof; build a low tarp across the remaining walls.

Carry a **low tent** — something that crouches. Nothing with vertical walls will survive a storm here. The vegetation has learned this lesson; so should your gear.

Your tent will flap. A flapping tent in this place is more than an annoyance — it becomes another voice in the chord, and in a bad storm it will feel like the mountain has entered your shelter. Pack extra guy lines. Tension everything twice. A tent pegged loose here is a tent gone.

## The Rule

**Do not sing back to the mountains.**

Take this seriously. Whatever the mechanism — cultural, spiritual, acoustic-resonant, something we do not have a word for — the rule is given to you freely by the only people who have learned to live here, and the cost of ignoring it is reported to be the loss of your own voice in some permanent and not-quite-physical way.

Apply this broadly:

- No humming while you walk. You will want to hum. The chord induces humming the way cold induces shivering; your body wants to harmonize. Bite the inside of your cheek, chew a leather strip, suck on a pebble — whatever stops the hum.
- No whistling.
- No singing in your tent. You will want to, for comfort. Do not.
- **Speak softly.** Speech seems to be permitted; conversation with companions is fine. But keep your voice level. Do not shout against the wind. If you must call to a companion, use a low horn or a clacker — something that is clearly not a human voice trying to join the chord.
- Hymns, chants, mantras, prayers spoken aloud: no. Pray silently if you pray.

If you find yourself singing without realizing it — which will happen; the chord is hypnotic — stop as soon as you notice, and do not panic. Short lapses seem to be forgiven. It is sustained answering that is dangerous.

## The Silence

Once a week, more or less, the wind drops and the singing stops. This is the most disorienting experience the plateau offers, and I want you to prepare for it specifically.

When you have been bathed in a chord for days, its sudden absence is not peace — it is a kind of vacuum. Your brain, having tuned itself to filter the roar, now filters an absence, and what comes through is *everything else*: your pulse, your breath, water in the rock, possibly footsteps that are not footsteps.

Some travelers find this the most beautiful experience of their lives. Others find it unbearable, and a few have walked off edges during the quiet, or run, or done worse. Prepare for it by:

- Knowing it will come.
- Sitting down when it comes. Literally — sit on the ground. Do not stand and do not walk during the silences. Wait them out.
- Having a small, quiet task at hand: a piece of carving, a knot to tie, a page to read. Give the listening part of your mind something to anchor to that is not the plateau.
- Not speaking during the silence, unless you must. It is not clear whether the rule against singing extends to ordinary speech in these moments, but caution is cheap.

## The Human Presence

You may meet the locals. You may not. If you do:

- Greet them with open hands and a slight bow. Do not extend your hand for a shake — hands are precious here, and also the gesture will mean nothing to them.
- Let them speak first and let them leave first. They live in a place where presence is costly; imposing yours is an insult they are too courteous to name.
- Small gifts of metal (a good knife, a needle, a coil of strong wire) are reportedly welcome, since bronze-work here is labor-intensive.
- Do not enter their structures uninvited. This includes the roofless house and the cairns. *Especially* the cairns — whatever these count, it is not your counting.
- If they give you advice, follow it. They are the experts. You are a tourist with earplugs.

You may also walk the entire nine days and see no one. This is also correct.

## Mental Preparation

This is the part travelers skip and regret.

Spend time before you go in places of controlled acoustic stress — a wind tunnel, a ship engine room, a concert hall during soundcheck. Learn what it is like to think while your bones vibrate. Learn your own irritability under sustained sound. Learn when *you* become dangerous to yourself.

Meditate, if you meditate. Develop a practice of internal silence that does not require external silence to sustain it. The plateau will test every kind of quiet you have ever cultivated.

Accept before going that you may come back changed in ways you cannot specify. People who visit Ahal-Ruun often find, months later, that they have lost their taste for certain kinds of music, or can no longer tolerate loud parties, or have developed a new patience, or a new impatience, or have gone unaccountably quiet. This is not damage. This is the plateau keeping a small piece of you, as it keeps a small piece of everything that crosses it. It is the oldest trader in the world and it always takes its cut.

## What to Bring Home

Nothing from the plateau itself. No stones, no moss, no fern-fronds, no water in a bottle. The place is assembled from parts that have been in those positions for uncountable ages, and removing any of them is a kind of theft that the plateau does not punish but also does not forget.

Bring back instead:

- Your notes.
- Your silence.
- A clearer sense of scale than you had before.
- The knowledge, which is not trivial, of what it sounds like when the world sings to itself without reference to you, and what it asks in exchange for letting you listen.

## A Last Word

The Worn Teeth are not hostile. This is important. They are not trying to kill you, or drive you mad, or keep you out. They are simply extremely old and extremely busy, and you are a brief warm thing passing through a process that does not concern you. Respect the process. Keep quiet. Walk slowly. Drink from the pools. Sit down during the silences. Do not sing back.

And when you come off the plateau on the ninth day and the clouds close behind you and the chord fades to a distant murmur and then to nothing at all — take a moment, before you go down into the ordinary noisy world, to say thank you. Silently. With your mouth closed.

The mountains will not hear you. That is the point.
