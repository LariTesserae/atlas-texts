# Preparing to Visit the Cooling Room

## Understanding What You're Walking Into

Before anything else: internalize that you are not visiting a natural place, and not quite visiting a built place either. You are visiting an *organ* — the exposed, breathing edge of a system that is mostly elsewhere. The mountain grew around it. The chamber is older than its context. Whatever runs it tolerates visitors in roughly the way a cathedral tolerates pigeons: not welcomed, not driven out, registered.

This shapes everything about how to prepare. You are not going to explore. You are going to *attend*.

---

## Physical Preparation

**Altitude.** Four thousand meters is not trivial. Spend at least three days acclimatizing at two to three thousand meters before the climb. Bring acetazolamide if you have a prescription for it. If you arrive breathless, you will not be able to sit still long enough to do this place justice, and sitting still is the whole point.

**The climb itself.** Scree above the lichen line is miserable and dangerous — loose, sharp-edged, and in this case freshly minted basalt that hasn't had time to settle into stable angles of repose. Bring:

- Stiff boots with ankle support, already broken in
- Gaiters (the scree will pour into anything open)
- Trekking poles, the kind with real carbide tips
- Gloves — not insulated ones, the sharp new basalt will shred cotton and skin alike
- A helmet. Fresh scree slopes shed rocks without warning.

**Clothing for inside.** This is the counterintuitive part. The chamber is hot but has weather. You need layers you can adjust without rustling — merino base layers, a light shell you can stow silently, nothing synthetic that crinkles. Your outer climbing layers should come off before you enter; leave them folded near the entrance. Inside, a thin long-sleeved shirt and loose trousers. The heat from the walls will bake one side of you while a cold draft sweeps your ankles; you must accept this and not fuss with it.

**Water.** Bring more than you think. The dry heat pulls water out of you without you noticing, and you may sit for hours. Four liters minimum. Do not, under any circumstances, collect water from the grooves in the floor. It is going somewhere.

**Food.** Something quiet. No crinkling wrappers, no crunching. A boiled egg, a piece of bread, dried fruit in a cloth. Eat outside before you go in.

---

## Mental and Temperamental Preparation

This is more important than the gear.

**Learn to sit.** If you cannot comfortably sit still and alert for two hours without fidgeting, practice this at home for several weeks before you go. Meditation traditions are useful here, but you don't need the metaphysics — you need the endurance. The chord shifts on its own schedule. The dust patterns resolve on their own schedule. If you cannot outlast them, you will see nothing.

**Calibrate your expectations about meaning.** The room is doing something, and you will want to know what. You will not find out. Decide now that you are comfortable with that. Travelers who go in hoping to decode the hexagons or figure out what the lilac light *is* tend to come out frustrated and somehow smaller. Travelers who go in to witness a process, the way one might sit beside a river, come out larger.

**Prepare for the sense of being observed.** When you hold your hand over one of the cylinders and the chord quiets, something in you will go cold and bright at once. This is correct. You have been registered. It is not malevolent. It is not personal. It is the attention a thermostat pays to a room — except the thermostat is vast and you are the room. Breathe through it. Do not flinch away from the cylinder; do not linger either. Let the chord return.

---

## What to Bring Inside

Less than you think. The room has opinions about company, and those opinions seem to favor modesty.

- A small notebook and a pencil (not a pen — pens click)
- A water bottle with a silicone gasket, not a screw top that squeaks
- A thin foam pad to sit on; the floor is hot and the stone is unforgiving
- A headlamp with a red-light setting, for when you leave in the dark. Use it as little as possible inside. The dim lilac is sufficient once your eyes adapt, and brighter light seems — this is subjective, but consistent across accounts — to make the chord thinner.

**Leave outside:** anything electronic that beeps, pings, vibrates, or logs. Phones off, not silent. GPS watches off. Cameras are a judgment call; if you bring one, no flash, no shutter sound, and understand that photographs of this place tend to come out wrong in ways that are hard to articulate later.

**Consider bringing:** something small and ordinary to leave behind, if the room accepts it. The ceramic cup in the east wall is a precedent. Not an offering, exactly — more an acknowledgment that you were there, quiet, and that you noticed. A pebble from your home. A button. Nothing valuable, nothing that will decay, nothing that would embarrass you to find a hundred years later. If it feels wrong to leave anything, don't.

## Protocol Inside

**Entry.** Pause at the threshold. Let your eyes adjust for at least two minutes before stepping in. Listen for the chord. Note which wall the ticking is currently concentrated in — this tells you where the room is working, and you should sit away from that wall, not against it.

**Movement.** Slow. Flat-footed. Do not scuff the floor. The dust is doing something, and you should not walk through the rising columns if you can avoid it — skirt them. Treat them the way you would treat someone praying.

**The pit.** You may approach the rim. Do not reach into it. Do not drop anything in. The lilac light is not a light in the ordinary sense — it is, as the description hints, the *edge* of something, and you do not put your hand past an edge when you do not know what is on the other side. Stand at the rim for as long as feels right, then step back.

**The cylinders.** You may hold your hand over one, briefly, to confirm that you are noticed. Do this once. Do not play with it — do not try to send signals by covering and uncovering. The room is not a toy and probably not a correspondent.

**The plants.** Do not touch them. The rosemary-thing especially: snapping a leaf releases ozone, which suggests chemistry you do not want on your fingers. Admire them. They are the only other guests.

**The worn patch.** Stand before it. Do not press your palm to it unless you have sat for at least an hour first and feel that you have been invited to. This is intuition, not rule, and you must decide. Most visitors do not press it. Some do. No one reports consequences either way, but the ones who pressed it speak of it the way people speak of important decisions.

**The ceramic cup.** Do not move it. Do not take it. Do not drink from it. It is someone else's gesture and it belongs where it is.

## Duration

Plan for four to six hours inside. Less than two and you will not have heard a full cycle of the chord. More than eight and you risk dehydration, heat exhaustion, and the subtler problem of overstaying — a feeling, reported by several travelers, that after a certain point the room begins gently but unmistakably turning its attention *toward* you rather than past you, and that this is uncomfortable in a way that escalates.

Leave before you are asked to leave. You will know.

---

## Afterward

**Descent in daylight only.** Do not try to descend new scree at dusk. Plan your visit so you exit with at least three hours of light.

**Do not speak on the way down.** Not for mystical reasons — because your ears will still be tuned to the chord, and you will want to let them detune gradually. Chatter reintroduces the ordinary world too quickly and you will lose most of what you heard.

**Write within twenty-four hours.** The memory of the chord, the dust patterns, the shift of the hexagons — these degrade fast, faster than ordinary memories. Sit down with your notebook that evening and write everything, even the parts you don't understand. Especially those.

**Expect aftereffects.** For about a week after a visit, travelers report:
- Heightened sensitivity to sustained low tones (HVAC systems, distant traffic)
- A tendency to notice patterns in drifting things — smoke, snow, crowds
- Mild, not unpleasant, feeling of having been *filed*
- Dreams involving geometry that resolves into other geometry

These pass. If they do not pass within a month, consider that the room may have made a more lasting impression, and that this is neither good nor bad but is now part of you.

---

## A Final Note

The chamber does not need you. It was cooling before you arrived and will cool after you leave. Everything about visiting it is for your sake, not its. This is the correct frame for the whole trip: you are the one being changed by the encounter, and the room is simply continuing its work.

Go quietly. Sit. Listen to the chord shift. Watch the dust read the air.

Leave while you still want to stay.
