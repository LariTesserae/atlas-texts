# Preparing to Visit the Lung of the New Mountain

## Understanding What You're Going Into

Before anything else: internalize that this is not a place you explore. It is a place you are briefly permitted inside. The hollow has its own metabolism — breath, condensation, drink, breath — and you are an interruption in that cycle, a cold object that will steal heat from the system for as long as you stand there. Every minute you are present, the birds are spending slightly more energy to maintain the microclimate. Plan accordingly. Plan to be brief.

The mountain is days old. Think about that. The rock has not learned to hold anything yet except what is already holding on. You are trusting basalt that has never been tested by a winter.

## Altitude and Air

This is the first hard problem. "Blue-cold air that makes your teeth feel glassy" puts you somewhere above five thousand meters, probably higher given the four-kilometer drop below the east mouth. You will need:

- **Acclimatization in the adjacent world, if possible.** A week minimum at graduated altitudes before you cross over. If your multiverse transit deposits you directly at elevation, bring supplemental oxygen — a small climbing bottle with a regulator, mouthpiece delivery rather than mask, because you will want your face free to feel the vapor and the feather-dust.
- **Acetazolamide** or equivalent, started days in advance.
- **A pulse oximeter.** Check it before entering the hollow and again after two minutes inside. If you drop below 80%, leave immediately. The bird-sound will mask the warning signs of hypoxia — the disorientation, the giddy wrongness of thought — and you may mistake cerebral edema for awe.

Do not trust your judgment about how long you've been inside. Set a timer that vibrates against your wrist. Ten minutes is generous. Fifteen is pushing it. Twenty and you are gambling.

## Clothing and Skin

The cold is the kind that slides in under collars. Dress in layers you can vent, because the hollow itself will be warmer than the exterior — thousands of small bodies each producing a few watts of heat, trapped in a chapel-sized stone pocket. You will move from glassy cold to a damp close warmth within seconds of stepping in.

- **Base layer: merino.** It will get damp from the condensation and you want it to keep working.
- **A shell that sheds water but breathes.** The rain-bursts are brief but real.
- **Gloves thin enough to feel the brass rings** if you choose to touch them. I would advise touching nothing, but I know you will.
- **A hood or hat you can cinch tight.** Feather-dust will get into your hair and stay there for days. This is minor. But the spores are the real reason for the hood — you do not know what is fruiting on those birds, and you do not know what it does to a human scalp.

**Skin:** cover it. The oils on your hands are alien to this ecology. A single touch to a mossy wing could introduce something the flock's immune logic has no answer for.

## Respiratory Protection — The Hard Question

Here is where you have to make a choice, and I cannot make it for you.

The air of the hollow is full of spores, feather-dust, vapor from bird breath, and whatever the machine is aerosolizing from its vents. A proper respirator (P100, sealed) will protect your lungs. It will also cut you off from one of the most important aspects of the place — the taste of the air, the warm-damp-mineral smell of a lung that is also a greenhouse, the way breath-vapor feels when you share it with a thousand small warm bodies.

My recommendation: wear the respirator. You are a multiverse traveler, not a pilgrim. You do not get to know what cross-reactive pathogens live in the filamentous algae of a bird species that shouldn't exist. The place will still be overwhelming through a filter. The sound alone will humble you. Save the unfiltered breath for a world where the biosphere is closer to your own.

If you refuse the respirator — and some travelers will — carry broad-spectrum antifungals and see a good pulmonologist when you return. Do not mention where you were. They will not believe you, and the specimen samples they request will not exist by then.

## Hearing

The sound is a pressurized wall. Understand what this means physically: sustained exposure to that density of calls, plus the forty-BPM sub-bass from the machine, plus ten thousand wingbeats — this is genuine acoustic damage territory.

Wear **high-fidelity earplugs**, the musician's kind, that attenuate evenly across frequencies rather than muffling. You want to hear the shape of the sound, not a dulled version of it. Twenty-five decibels of reduction will still leave you inside a cathedral of noise, but will let you come out with your hearing intact.

Do not wear active noise cancellation. The point is not to escape the sound; the point is to survive it while still being inside it.

## Footing

There is no ground. Read that rule again: *nothing here touches the ground, because there is no ground yet.* You will be standing on stone that slopes, on ledges, possibly on the machine itself. The four-kilometer drop from the eastern mouth is not hypothetical.

- **Approach shoes or light mountaineering boots** with aggressive sticky rubber. Not crampons — the rock is bare basalt, not ice, and crampons will damage it. The mountain is young. Leave no scratches.
- **A short tether.** If there is anything to clip to — and the six brass rings suggest there is — clip in. The rings are dinner-plate diameter; they are load-rated for something. But do not trust them blindly. Back up with a friction hitch to natural protection if you can find a crack that isn't full of bird.
- **Helmet.** Rockfall from above, and the upper throat is open sky. Also: birds startle. A panicked flight-burst in an enclosed space can collide with you.

## What to Bring for the Place Itself

Almost nothing. This is important.

- **No food.** Opening a wrapper in that hollow is an act of violence against the ecology. The parrots will be on you instantly, and what you feed them may kill them.
- **No water bottle that you open.** Drink before entering, drink after. The vapor cycle is a closed system. Do not add to it or take from it.
- **A small camera, if you must, with a silent shutter.** No flash. Flash will trigger mass startle and the rain-burst, and possibly drive some of the flock out into air too cold for them.
- **No collection kit.** Whatever you are tempted to take — a feather, a fallen spore-body, a flake of mica — leave it. The hollow loses mass only through the copper tube, by the machine's design. You are not authorized to be a second exit.
- **A notebook and pencil.** Not a pen; ink will freeze or spread. Write after you leave. Inside, you will not be able to write. Thought arrives in fragments between peaks of sound, and the fragments are not the kind you can fix on paper while they're happening.

## The Machine

Do not touch the machine.

I want to be very clear about this. The oilcan is liquid. Someone maintains this. They have not been here in your lifetime, which tells you something about their lifetime. The machine is running at forty BPM and has been for long enough that birds have built nests into its flywheel cage. Whatever it is extracting, it is extracting on a schedule that predates you and will postdate you, and the copper tube goes somewhere.

You may look. You may feel the sub-bass through your boots. You may note the Arabic-and-older numerals on the brass rings and try to memorize a few. You may not:

- Open the housing.
- Touch the flywheel.
- Put anything into the bellows intake.
- Follow the copper tube up through the throat. (You cannot, anyway; the throat is vertical and the birds fill it. But I mention it because travelers have tried stranger climbs.)
- Take the oilcan.

If the machine stops while you are inside, leave immediately. You do not want to be present when whoever maintains it arrives to check.

## Mental Preparation

This is the part most travelers neglect.

You are about to enter a space where speech is impossible, where thought arrives broken, where every surface is alive, where the floor is absent, where the air is a weather system made of breath, and where a machine of unknown purpose has been quietly working for longer than you can verify. You will have ten to fifteen minutes. In that time, you will want to understand the place. You will not. Understanding is not available at that compression.

Prepare instead to **receive** rather than comprehend. Decide, before you enter, what one thing you want to pay attention to. The quality of the light. The pattern of the four-winged ones' cross-beat. The tempo of the machine against the tempo of the flock. The moss-fur on an old bird. Pick one. Commit to it. The rest will blur past and that is correct.

Decide also, before you enter, that you will leave when the timer says to leave, regardless of what is happening. Many travelers die of "one more minute."

## After

When you step out — back into the glassy-cold blue — you will be disoriented. The silence will feel like deafness for several minutes. Your hands will be shaking and you may not notice.

- Descend immediately. Do not linger on the eastern flank to look back at the mouth. Altitude is still trying to kill you and the adrenaline is hiding the symptoms.
- Rinse your eyes, nose, and mouth with clean water at the first safe opportunity.
- Change clothes. Bag the ones you wore in the hollow separately. When you return to your origin world, consider whether those clothes should come with you or stay.
- Write down everything within six hours. The memory compresses fast, because the experience was already compressed when you had it. What you do not write down in the first evening you will lose.
- Watch yourself for two weeks. Any cough, any unusual skin growth, any change in the sound of your own breathing — see someone. Preferably someone who has treated cross-world exposures before.

## One Last Thing

You will be tempted, at some point during the visit, to speak. To say something — your name, a greeting, a prayer, an apology. The sound will swallow it before it leaves your throat and you will feel foolish.

Speak anyway, if you want to. The birds will not hear you. The machine will not hear you. But you will have said it inside a lung that is still being born, and that is a place worth saying things in, even things that cannot be heard.

Then leave. The mountain is still deciding what shape it wants to be, and it has not decided to include you.
