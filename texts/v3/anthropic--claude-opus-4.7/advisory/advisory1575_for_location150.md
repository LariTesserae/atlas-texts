# Preparing for the Brasswing Escarpment

## Before You Go: The Mindset

Understand first what kind of place this is. The Escarpment is not dangerous in the way a desert or a war zone is dangerous. It is dangerous in the way a cathedral is dangerous to a person with a fragile sense of self. It will not attack you. It will, slowly, suggest that you are running at the wrong speed, and some visitors find they cannot refuse the suggestion.

Plan a **short first visit**. Three days. Not more. If you love it, come back; the Escarpment will still be there, unhurried. If you stay longer than two weeks on a first trip, budget for the possibility that you will not want to leave on schedule, and tell someone at home to come get you if you miss your return date. This is not melodrama. Ask the naturalist's colleagues.

## Physical Preparation

You do not need altitude training. The air is "persuaded" and behaves like seacoast air regardless of what your altimeter insists. **However**, your body will know something is wrong even when your lungs don't. Expect:

- Mild vertigo for the first few hours, particularly when looking at the horizon, which sits lower than your inner ear expects
- A sensation of being heavier than you are
- Unusual thirst despite the humidity
- A persistent low hum in the teeth that fades from conscious awareness within a day but does not actually stop

Bring **dental guards** if you grind your teeth. The molar-frequency of the basalt has caused cracked fillings in travelers with older dental work. Get any loose metalwork checked before you go. Some visitors report that silver amalgam fillings warm noticeably.

Hydrate aggressively before arrival and carry more water than you think you need, even though water is everywhere. **Do not drink from the catchment pools.** The mineral crust is harmless in small quantities but the water itself is at body temperature, which means it will not cool you, and it carries trace compounds that accumulate. Rainwater caught fresh off a brasswing leaf is generally safe and tastes faintly sweet.

## Clothing and Gear

Dress for a warm, wet, loud place where the ground remembers you.

- **Footwear**: soft-soled, because you will leave prints in the memory-moss for a week regardless, and hard lugs tear it. Waterproof to the knee. Expect to be wading.
- **Layers**: the day is body-warm; dusk and full dark drop fast once the terraces finish exhaling. A light insulating layer for evening.
- **Hat with a brim**: the vine-lenses throw sun-coins that can genuinely dazzle you in afternoon. Some travelers also bring polarized sunglasses for this reason.
- **Ear protection**: not to block the chiming — you want the chiming — but foam plugs for sleep. The brasswings do not stop at night, and the dusk-ring lasts twenty minutes and will wake you every evening if you arrive tired.
- **Gloves**: the three small warm domes are tempting to touch. Touch them with gloves first. Not because they burn — they don't — but because ungloved contact with the humming surfaces has been reported to produce a minute or two of mild disorientation and, occasionally, a strong sense of having been briefly addressed.

Leave behind: mechanical watches (the hum desynchronizes escapements), compasses (unreliable near the copper-veins), and anything with a loose magnetic stripe.

## What to Pack for the Mind

This is the more important section.

Bring a **paper journal and a pen**. Electronics work but are unreliable near the ring and the tower, and more importantly, writing by hand is one of the few rhythms you can impose on yourself that the landscape does not override.

Bring a **metronome** — a small mechanical one, or a digital one you keep in a shielded pouch. Use it daily. Not because you are playing music, but because having a known tempo to check yourself against lets you notice when you have begun to drift. The naturalist's note about her heartbeat is not metaphorical. Visitors' resting pulses slow measurably over the course of a week. This is probably fine. You should still know it is happening.

Bring a **photograph of someone you love**, printed on paper. Look at it every morning. This sounds sentimental. It is a practical countermeasure. People who cannot answer the question "why should I leave" tend not to leave.

Bring **a book you have already read**, something familiar. Not something new. You want an anchor, not a discovery.

## On the Ground: Practical Conduct

**The memory-moss**: walk on established tracks where possible. Your prints will last a week. This is not pollution; it is participation. Be aware that the moss appears to accumulate something from repeated tread — tracks walked daily for a month darken further and take months to fade. Some visitors find this moving. Some find it upsetting. Know which you are.

**The catchment pools**: swim only in the upper basins, never the deepest. The shapes in the deep water have not been identified and the wading birds' lack of fear is not reassurance — it is information about the birds, not about you. The upper basins are body-warm, buoyant from mineral content, and restorative. An hour is fine. Three hours and you will have trouble leaving.

**The hexagonal path that stops**: walk it. Walk to the end. Do not step off the last tile in the direction the path was heading. Turn around and walk back. No one has satisfactorily explained why, but every experienced visitor will tell you the same thing.

**The ceramic tower**: approach if you wish. It has no openings and nothing happens. The chemical-sampling team who produced the famous report were all fine afterward, by most measures. Do not camp within its shadow at sunset; the ninety seconds of perfect backlighting are more intense up close than travelogues suggest, and people who experience it from within the shadow sometimes report an hour of missing time, though their watches disagree about whether any time was actually missing.

**The standing ring**: visit at noon and at full dark, as most do. The four-degree shadow offset at noon is the photograph everyone takes. The hour of shadows-lit-by-nothing after dark is the experience everyone actually remembers. Sit outside the ring, not inside it. This is not a rule anyone enforces. It is simply that people who sit inside the ring during the lit-shadow hour come out different in ways they often cannot articulate and sometimes do not notice for months.

**The three small domes**: warm, humming, harmless. A good place to rest a gloved hand and a forehead. Pilgrims do this. You can too. Do not sleep against them.

## The Brasswings Themselves

If your visit does not coincide with the forty-one-year bloom, you will see the trees in ordinary form: hollow, chiming, beautiful. If your visit does coincide with the bloom — check the bloom calendar carefully before you book — then **extend your trip by a few days** and understand that you will not sleep well and will not want to. The bloom is described by those who have seen it as the single most beautiful thing in the accessible multiverse, and this claim is made by people who have seen a great deal.

Do not take cuttings. The brasswings do not transplant and the attempt damages the grove in ways that are visible for decades.

## Eating and Sleeping

Bring your own food. The fauna is not for eating; the ferns are not for eating; the vine-leaves with their lens-droplets are mildly psychoactive when chewed and produce a sensation best described as "being briefly looked at by the landscape," which some travelers seek out and which your first visit is not the time for.

Sleep on high ground, above the terraces, not in them. The marshes are body-warm year-round and extremely comfortable to lie down in. This is the problem. Camp on the ridges. Use a hammock if possible; the memory-moss accepts a footprint but appears to accept a sleeper's body-shape more deeply.

## Leaving

This is the part most guides underemphasize.

Plan your departure with the same care as your arrival. Walk the same path out that you walked in. Pack the evening before and leave in the morning, not at dusk. Do not take a last look from the standing ring. Do not visit any location for the first time on your last day.

When you reach the departure point, wait ten minutes before leaving. Drink water. Read a page of the book you brought. Look at the photograph. Then go.

For the first week home, expect your own world to seem too fast, too sharp-edged, too dry, and too quiet. This passes. Most of it passes. If after a month you still feel that your heartbeat is wrong and the landscape you left was right, consider that this is what the place does, and consider carefully whether you want to go back, and if so, whether you intend to return.

Some people do not intend to return, and do not.

The Escarpment is, as the theory goes, in no hurry. It has centuries. You do not. Remember the difference. That is the whole of the preparation.
