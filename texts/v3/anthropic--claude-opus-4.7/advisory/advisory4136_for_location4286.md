# Preparing to Visit the Quiet Works at Hrevn Shelf

## Before You Go: Understanding What You're Walking Into

First, understand what this place *is*, because your preparations depend on it. Hrevn Shelf is not a destination in the way most places are destinations. It is a running process — something closer to a very slow organism than a town — and you will not be visiting it so much as being briefly incorporated into it. The danger is not violence or privation. The danger is that the process is welcoming, in its glacial way, and that human beings are apparently one of the things it is good at including.

Plan, therefore, not only for arrival but for departure. Departure is the harder part.

## Physical Preparation

**Clothing.** Wool and felt, layered, in muted colors. Anything bright will feel obscene within an hour of arrival and you will find yourself wanting to take it off, which in that cold is a bad idea. The cold at Hrevn is not the sharp cold of a dry peak — it is a wet, penetrating, *committed* cold, the kind that gets into the seams of things. Oiled outer layer. A hood you can cinch. Gloves you can work in. Boots that can handle constant seep without taking on water — the stone is always slick, the runnels always running, and you will be walking in a thin film for the duration of your stay.

**Do not bring down or synthetic puff insulation.** They will wet through from the ambient mist within a day and become worse than useless. The townspeople wear felt for a reason; felt tolerates being perpetually damp. Match them.

**Respiration.** The vapor in the air is not quite ordinary. A light cloth mask or scarf over the mouth and nose for the first day, until you learn what the air does to you. Some travelers report a metallic taste; some report nothing. Either is fine. If you begin to cough in a way that has rhythm to it — stop, leave the lower terraces, ascend to where the sky-band is visible, breathe the outer air for an hour.

**Light.** Bring a small, shielded lantern with warm-colored fuel. Do not bring electric torches or anything blue-white. It isn't that they're forbidden; it's that they will mark you, to yourself, as outside the process, and this will become increasingly uncomfortable. If you must carry modern light for emergencies, keep it deep in a pack and use it only if something has gone badly wrong.

**Timepiece.** Bring a mechanical watch, wound before you arrive, and *do not look at it* except once per day at an appointed moment. The clock in the works will try to take over your sense of time, and it will succeed; your watch exists only so that on your intended departure day you can verify that the day has in fact arrived. Digital clocks behave strangely here, I'm told. I wouldn't test it.

## Provisions

Bring your own food for the duration of your stay plus three extra days. Hard bread, hard cheese, dried meat, dried fruit. Do not plan to eat what the townspeople eat, and more importantly, **do not accept food from the cisterns.** The pale fish are not for visitors. The townspeople know this; they will not offer. If someone *does* offer, you have misjudged the situation and should leave promptly.

Tea, coffee, or anything you associate strongly with home. You will want an anchor. A taste that is unambiguously *yours*, prepared on your own small stove, is worth more than its weight in almost anything else you could pack.

Water: the runnel water is drinkable, filtered, but it carries the taste of the mountain and the taste of the mountain carries — something. Bring your own for at least the first day. After that, use your judgment, but know that drinking the local water is a small commitment in a place where small commitments compound.

## Mental and Temporal Preparation

**Decide the length of your stay before you arrive, and write it down somewhere you will see it.** Two nights is probably right. Three is the upper limit for a first visit. By the second evening, as the account warns, you will have begun to move with the pistons. This is not, in itself, harmful. It is pleasant. That is the problem.

**Write a letter to yourself before departure**, describing your life at home: the people you love, the work you do, the textures of your ordinary day, a few specific things you are looking forward to returning to. Seal it. Put it somewhere in your pack you will not open casually. If on the morning of your planned departure you find yourself reluctant to leave, open the letter and read it slowly before making any further decision. It will either restore you or it won't. If it doesn't, that is information, and you should leave anyway, immediately, without finishing your tea.

**Tell someone in your home world the date of your return.** Tell them that if you are late by more than forty-eight hours, they should not come looking. No one should come looking. Hrevn does not harm rescuers; it includes them.

## Social Conduct

**Speak little.** The town is quiet not because speech is forbidden but because speech at normal volume is disproportionate to the scale of everything else. You will find, within hours, that you are murmuring. This is correct. Follow it.

**Do not ask about the works.** Do not ask what is being made, do not ask how long the town has been there, do not ask about the shapes in the vapor or the sounds below the grates. These questions mark you as the kind of visitor the townspeople have seen before and politely declined to satisfy. They are not secretive; they simply do not have the relationship to their own process that your questions assume. Asking a baker about flour, as the account says. The baker is not offended; the baker just doesn't have much to tell you.

**Do ask about small things.** The wire the man winds on the stoop. The shim-slate under a lintel. A particular lantern's oil. These questions are welcome and will be answered with surprising generosity, and through them you will learn more about what the place actually is than any direct question would yield.

**Do not photograph or sketch the stacks' vapor.** Sketch anything else you like. The vapor — leave it. I cannot give a rational reason. This is intuition speaking.

**If you see the four-legged thing on the upper terrace, do not follow it.** It may or may not exist. Either way.

## Things to Bring That Aren't Obvious

- A small mirror. Once a day, look at your own face. This sounds foolish. It is not foolish. You are checking that you still recognize yourself, and that your expression still belongs to you.
- A hard-edged object from home — a key, a coin, a ring. Something with a clean geometry. The town rounds everything; you will want to touch a true corner occasionally to remember what corners are.
- Paper and a pencil. Write each night. Not about the town — about yourself. What you did. What you thought. Whether your thoughts felt like your own. You are keeping a record that can, later, be used to check whether you left when you meant to.
- A single piece of music, memorized. Hum it to yourself if you catch yourself humming the rhythm of the works. Displace one tempo with another. This is an old trick and it still works.

## On the Path In

The goat-track-that-isn't will find you more than you find it. Walk slowly. Do not announce yourself when you arrive; there is no one to announce to, and the town's rhythms don't want the interruption. Find a place to stand at the edge of the shelf and simply breathe for a quarter hour before entering. You are adjusting to a pressure differential that is not physical.

Lodging is arranged without being arranged. A door will be open that wasn't. A low room with a warm wall will be available. Leave a small gift on the sill when you depart — not money, not food. Something crafted: a button, a small carving, a folded paper. The town accepts offerings that took human time to make, which is the currency it understands.

## On Leaving

This is the part that matters.

Leave in the morning, not the evening. Leave after you have eaten your own food, not theirs. Leave while the sky-band is bright at the shelf's edge, so that you walk toward light.

Before you go, stand once more beside one of the slow pistons and count to forty while it completes a stroke. Let it finish. Then turn your back on it and do not turn around again until you are above the overhang and can see the whole dark patch of the shelf below you like a stain in the snow.

You will feel, on the path out, a drag. Not physical. A reluctance in the chest, as if you had forgotten something important. **You have not forgotten anything.** The drag is the process releasing you, and it releases slowly because it does everything slowly. Walk through it. It fades by the second ridge.

When you reach your return point and cross back into your home world, do one small loud thing within the first hour. Clap your hands. Drop a pan. Say something at full volume. Laugh if you can manage it. You are re-calibrating your tempo, and the best way to do that is with a clean sharp sound, the kind of sound Hrevn does not contain.

## A Final Note

Some travelers return from Hrevn Shelf and report nothing unusual. A cold quiet town, a strange industry, pleasant enough, unmemorable in the way remote places often are.

Others return quieter than they left, and remain so. Their friends notice. They move a little more slowly. They pause before speaking. They keep their lanterns dim and their voices low and they are, by most accounts, happier than they were before, in a way that is hard to describe and harder to argue with.

I do not know which kind of traveler you will be. Neither do you. Go if you wish to go. Prepare as I have described. And remember, on the second evening, when the cold has stopped being cold and the silence has become attention — remember that you came from somewhere, and that somewhere is still expecting you back.
