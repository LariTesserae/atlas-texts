# Preparing to Visit the Lichen-City of Under-Root

## The First Problem: You Are Enormous

Before anything else, you must understand this: you are not going to a small place. You are going to a place where your scale is the wrong scale. A tourist to Rome can walk its streets; a tourist to Under-Root cannot walk its streets without destroying several neighborhoods under each footfall. Your knee, set down carelessly in the moss, is a geological event.

So the first preparation is psychological. You must accept, before you even begin packing, that you are going as a witness, not as a participant. You will not stroll the colonnades. You will not rest in the scarlet-lamplit squares. You will press your face to the threshold of a world that operates at a tempo and a scale incompatible with yours, and you will look, and then you will go away, and the city will not have noticed that you came.

This is a hard thing for humans. We are used to places registering us. Bring humility as your primary luggage.

## Physical Preparation

**Clothing.** Wear what you would wear to kneel in cold damp earth for several hours. Wool, not cotton, against the skin — the cold here is not severe but it is insidious, a held-just-above-freezing cold that climbs into you through any contact point. A waxed canvas coat, long enough to kneel in. Thick socks. Leather or rubber kneepads; you will be on your knees a long time and the moss, though soft, will press the damp into you. Gloves you can remove easily — you will want your bare hands for some things and not others. A hat, because most heat loss will be upward.

Expect to get wet. Not soaked, but dampened in the thorough way that only long exposure to living moss produces. Bring a change of clothes for afterward, in a dry bag, waiting in whatever conveyance returns you to your own world.

**The Body Itself.** Eat lightly before arriving. A full stomach makes for shallow breathing, and you will want to breathe shallowly anyway. Relieve yourself beforehand — there is no graceful way to leave the hollow once you have settled into looking, and you will want to settle for a long time. Trim your fingernails; you may touch things, and a broken nail in this environment is both unhygienic (to you) and potentially catastrophic (to them).

If you are prone to tremor, to sneezing fits, to coughs — medicate. A single sneeze in that hollow would be a hurricane.

## Optical Equipment

This is the most important category, and most travelers underprepare it.

You will need, at minimum:
- **A loupe** of 10x magnification, hung on a cord around your neck. Hastings triplet if you can afford one; the image must be undistorted at the edges.
- **A stronger hand lens** of 20x or 30x, for the pseudoscorpions and the caddis cases and the architecture of the *Physarum* globes.
- **A headlamp with a red filter** — not for illumination, since the green filtered light from above is enough for seeing, but for examining particular features without destroying your dark-adaptation for the rest.
- **A small mirror on a telescoping handle**, the sort dentists use. Much of the city is on surfaces angled away from you. Without a mirror you will see only the public faces.

Do *not* bring a camera on your first visit. Photography tempts one to consume the place through a lens and leave without having looked. Look first. If you return, bring the camera the second time.

## Temporal Preparation

Clear your day. Clear, ideally, your week — not because the visit takes a week, but because you will not want to be hurried on either side of it. Arrange that nothing is expected of you. Do not bring a watch, or if you must, put it in an inner pocket and do not consult it.

The hardest preparation is this: **you must learn to wait before you go.**

Practice sitting still for two hours. Most modern humans cannot do this without becoming agitated; we have forgotten the skill. If you arrive at the under-root with the twitchy tempo of email and errands still in your nervous system, you will see nothing. The city moves at the pace it moves, and to perceive it you must slow yourself to at least a tenth of your usual speed. This is not metaphor. Your eye will register the drop forming on the hanging root only if you have, by then, stopped demanding that things happen.

Spend a week beforehand doing things slowly. Eat slowly. Walk slowly. Sit in a chair and look at a single object for an hour. When you catch yourself anticipating the next moment, let the anticipation dissolve. By the time you arrive, your interior clock should be running at lichen-speed — or as near to it as a mammal can manage.

## What Not to Bring

- **No perfume, cologne, scented soap, or scented deodorant.** Bathe the night before with unscented soap. The city's chemistry is delicate and old, and a human scent-signature lingers for weeks in still air.
- **No food.** Not even a mint. A crumb would be a meteorite.
- **No phone.** It will buzz, and a buzz in that hollow is desecration. If you must carry one for emergency return, power it off completely and bury it in a padded case at the bottom of your bag.
- **No collecting tools.** No forceps, no specimen jars, no blade. You are not here to take. The impulse to carry home a fragment is strong and must be resisted absolutely. A lichen spire the size of your thumb may be three hundred years old. You have not earned the right to pocket it.
- **No one else.** Go alone. Two humans in that hollow is one too many; the coordination of breath and movement between companions introduces rustle and compromise. If you must bring a guide, part ways at the fallen trunk.

## What to Bring

- **Water, in a flask you can sip from silently.** Long stillness is dehydrating, especially in cold air.
- **A soft cloth** for wiping the loupe, which will fog.
- **A small cushion or folded pad** for under the knees, in addition to the kneepads.
- **A notebook and soft pencil**, kept in a pocket. Write only afterward, when you have backed out of the hollow. Writing inside it is too much movement.
- **Patience**, in whatever form you can carry it.
- **A willingness to see the spiral and not explain it.** This is perhaps the most important item. You will want, when you encounter the logarithmic spiral in the old tap-root's place, to produce a theory. Suppress this impulse. Let the spiral be. The hollow does not owe you a mechanism.

## Behavioral Protocol Inside the Hollow

Approach the fallen beech slowly, from downwind if there is any wind at all. Kneel in the moss perhaps a meter from the root-plate. Wait there for several minutes before moving closer. Let your breathing slow; let the hollow accept your presence, or at least, let it stop registering you as a recent disturbance.

When you move closer, move by increments. Shift your weight once, then wait. Shift again. A lichen-city does not have a startle response in any meaningful sense, but your own body has a tempo that it must renegotiate, and the renegotiation takes time.

Once your face is near the threshold, breathe across your shoulder, not forward. Your exhalation is warm and moist and carries carbon dioxide in concentrations the springtails have never encountered. Turn your head to exhale. Re-face the city to inhale and look.

Do not touch anything with your fingers. If you must touch — and there is a case for touching a single small area of moss outside the city proper, just to feel the wet-cloth coolness — use the back of a knuckle, and once only.

When you have looked as long as you intend to look, back away as slowly as you approached. Do not stand up suddenly. Rise in stages.

## Afterward

You will want to speak to someone, immediately, about what you saw. Don't. Not for a day at least. The experience will try to resolve itself into an anecdote, and anecdotes falsify. Write in your notebook, slowly, in the evening, and let the writing be imprecise if precision requires hurrying.

Expect a mild grief. Most visitors to Under-Root report a sadness upon returning to the human tempo — a sense that our world is coarse and loud, and that we conduct our business with a violence we have stopped noticing. This passes, but it should pass slowly, and you should learn from it what it has to teach before it goes.

Expect, also, to want to return. Resist this for at least a year. The city does not need visitors, and a second trip undertaken too soon is a trip undertaken for your sake, not for witness. Go back when you have something to bring — which is to say, when you have grown slow enough again to deserve the looking.

## One Final Note

There is a temptation, in describing how to prepare for such a place, to treat it as an exotic. It is not exotic. It is what most of the universe is actually like — slow, patient, cold, and indifferent to our hurry. Under-Root is not a strange destination. *We* are the strange ones, the fast and warm and noisy ones, and a visit to the lichen-city is less a journey into oddity than a brief correction of our own peculiarity.

Prepare accordingly. Prepare, that is, to be the thing that is visiting, not the thing that is visited; to be the guest who arrives in a house so much older than their own that the proper response is to remove one's shoes, lower one's voice, and for a little while simply sit.
