# Preparing to Visit the Loom-Warren

## Before You Go

Understand first what you are walking into: a place that has achieved something most human settlements never do, which is *completion*. The Warren is not a town that will welcome you, entertain you, or notice you. It is a held chord. You are going to be, at best, a brief dissonance in it, and at worst an irrelevance so total that you will find yourself doubting your own reality after a few hours inside. Prepare accordingly.

## The Ears — This Is the Whole Problem

The single most important preparation is hearing protection, and I want to be emphatic about this: **ordinary earplugs will not be enough**. You are entering an environment where the standing wave of the pressure-whistles alone could do permanent damage in under an hour, and that is before the looms, the turbines, the wooden gears the size of millstones.

Bring:

- **Deep-insertion foam plugs**, rated for industrial use, and a spare set, and a spare set for the spare set. They will get wet, contaminated with pith-floss, and lost.
- **Over-ear industrial muffs** to wear *over* the plugs. Double protection is not paranoia here; it is the baseline.
- A set of **custom-molded musicians' plugs** with flat attenuation, for the brief moments you want to actually perceive the soundscape rather than just survive it. Use these sparingly.
- A waterproof container to store all of the above, because everything in the Warren is damp.

Do not, under any circumstances, remove your hearing protection to "hear what the locals hear." The locals are deaf. That is how they hear it. You do not have that adaptation and cannot acquire it in a single visit.

Expect the bass note of the turbines to enter your chest cavity regardless of ear protection. There is nothing to be done about this. It will rearrange your heartbeat for the duration of your stay and for some hours after you leave. This is normal. Do not panic.

## The Lungs

Pith-floss is everywhere. It is the air. The townspeople wrap their faces; you should wrap yours more thoroughly, because you lack whatever century-deep immunological accommodation they have made with the fibre. Bring:

- **P100-rated respirator**, fitted properly, with spare cartridges. The dampness will clog cheap filters quickly.
- A supply of **light cotton wraps** to wear over the respirator — partly for dignity (you will be less alien if you resemble the locals in silhouette) and partly to catch the coarser floss before it reaches the filter.
- Eye protection. Sealed goggles, not glasses. The dust is fine and the moth-wing iridescence will find its way into your tear ducts if given a chance, where it does something I will not describe here but which is not pleasant.

## The Body

You will be climbing ribbed walls, ducking through membrane-slits, and moving through a space built for people smaller than you. Assume you are oversized and clumsy.

- **Soft, grippy footwear** that can handle wet fibre. Something like climbing slippers. Hard-soled boots will slide on the waxy amber of the polished grooves and also, frankly, will damage surfaces that have been worn smooth by a hundred generations of bare feet. Do not be the traveler who leaves a bootprint on the Warren.
- **Clothing that does not snag.** The secondary growth catches on loose weaves. Wear something smooth, close-fitting, and in a muted green or bone color so that you are visually minimal. You do not want to be the one bright thing in a dim green room.
- **Knee and elbow pads**, thin ones. You will be crawling more than you expect.
- **Gloves** with reinforced palms. The ribs of the wall are tough but the rootlet undercoat is surprisingly fragile and you will want to avoid ripping it.

## The Skin and the Damp

The cool, the condensation, the constant not-quite-rain — this wears on a visitor in ways that are hard to describe until you have felt it. Your clothes will not dry. Your skin will become tender. Any small cut will take days longer to heal than it should.

- **Waterproof base layer** under your cotton. Not for warmth but for keeping a dry micro-environment against the skin.
- **Antiseptic and waterproof bandages** for anything that breaks the skin, immediately, no delays.
- **Barrier cream** for hands and face.
- A towel, a real one, sealed in waterproof storage, that you can use when you leave. You will want it desperately.

## Food and Water

The water of the Warren is drinkable and, by all accounts, pleasant — faintly sweet, faintly of grass. You can drink it. I would still bring a **small sterilizing filter** for the first day, partly for safety and partly to give your gut flora a transition period. By day two the local water is probably better for you than anything you brought.

Do not eat the crickets. Do not eat the moths. Do not accept food from the townspeople if any is offered, which it almost certainly will not be — bring your own, in dense compact forms, and eat it out of sight in a corner. The economy of the Warren is so closed that your caloric input would register as an anomaly if observed, and you do not want to register as an anomaly.

## Communication

You cannot speak here. Nobody can. Prepare for silence as a primary mode.

- Learn, before you go, a basic **tactile signing vocabulary** — the forehead-press shout is real but brutal on both skulls and should be reserved for emergencies.
- Bring a **small slate and chalk**, or a waxed tablet. If you must communicate with a townsperson, writing is often easier than gesture. Be aware they may or may not read; it depends on whether literacy has survived in this particular lineage. Be prepared for it to have been abandoned as unnecessary.
- **Do not attempt to speak even to yourself.** The habit of subvocalization will be strong and the feedback of your own muffled voice inside your hearing protection is disorienting. Practice silent-mouth internal monologue before you arrive.

## The Psychological Preparation

This is the part most travelers underestimate.

The Warren has been what it is for a very long time, and the weight of that stasis is a physical pressure on a visitor. You will find, after a few hours, that your sense of time begins to flatten. The light does not change. The sound does not change. The work does not change. The skeins stack. The loom comes down. The loom comes down.

People have been known to enter the Warren for a scheduled three-day visit and emerge convinced they were there a week, or an afternoon. Bring a **mechanical timepiece** — not digital, the humidity will ruin it — with an alarm function. Set the alarm. Trust the alarm over your own perception. Do not negotiate with yourself about when to leave.

Some travelers report a pull. A sense that staying would be easy, that a niche could be found, that the skeins could accommodate one more body against one more wall. This is real and should be taken seriously. It is not malice on the part of the Warren; the Warren does not know you exist. It is simply that *completion* is contagious to incomplete creatures, and you are an incomplete creature. **Arrange for someone outside to expect you at a specific time.** Give them permission, in writing, to come get you.

Bring something that is loudly, stupidly, unmistakably *yours* from your home reality — a photograph, a letter, a small familiar object — sealed in waterproofing. Look at it once a day. It will keep you oriented.

## What to Bring Out

Nothing.

I mean this. Do not take a skein, not even a scrap. Do not take a cricket. Do not pocket a splinter of the wooden pipe or a flake of the lamp-resin. The Warren's balance is to the decimal; births equal deaths, production equals consumption, skein-growth equals wall-absorption. You pulling a single thread is the kind of perturbation that, in a system this old and this held, might not matter at all, or might matter in a way no one can calculate.

Take photographs if your equipment can handle the damp, take notes, take memories. Leave everything else exactly where it sits.

## What to Leave Behind

Also nothing. No wrappers, no filter cartridges, no hair, no skin flakes that you can help shedding. Bring sealed bags for all your own waste, including the respirator cartridges when you swap them. **You brought yourself in; bring yourself out, entire.** Anything you leave will eventually be built into a wall, and you do not want a piece of yourself becoming structural in a place you will never see again.

## On Arrival and On Leaving

Enter slowly. Let your ears, even behind all that protection, adjust to the pressure of the sound. Let your eyes find the green. Stand for a long time in one place before you move. The townspeople will not greet you and will not object to you; you are, to them, weather. Behave as weather behaves. Pass through.

When you leave — and you will leave, you have arranged it, you have the alarm — expect the outside world to feel, for some hours, unbearably loud in its quiet, unbearably bright in any light, and unbearably *changing*. Birds moving. Clouds moving. Faces moving when they speak. It will be too much. Find somewhere dim and still for a day. Do not try to describe the Warren to anyone yet.

The held chord will keep playing in your sternum for about a week. Some people say it never entirely stops. I cannot tell you whether that is true, but I can tell you that the travelers who speak of the Warren most often are the ones who speak of it most quietly, and who seem, in some hard-to-place way, to be listening for something the rest of us cannot hear.

Go carefully. Come back.
