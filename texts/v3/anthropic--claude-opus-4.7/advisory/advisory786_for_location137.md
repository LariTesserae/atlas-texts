# Preparing to Visit The Drippings

## Before You Go

Understand first what you are walking into. The Drippings is not a place that wants visitors. It does not forbid them — forbidding would require effort, intention, a disruption of the equilibrium — but it is arranged, down to its last cord and cup, around the principle that nothing should change. You are change. Your footfalls are change. Your breath is change. The heat of your body, entering a chamber calibrated over centuries to a particular condensation rate, is change. Prepare yourself to be, for the duration of your stay, the most disruptive object in a room that has not been disrupted in a thousand years.

If this thought does not give you pause, you are not ready. Go home. Come back when it does.

## Physical Preparation

**Cool yourself down.** Spend at least a day before entry in a cold place — a cellar, a mountain stream, a refrigerated room if your multiverse has them. You want your core temperature low and your metabolism slow when you cross the threshold. A hot body in that chamber will change the condensation on the stalactites above it. Drips that have been falling on a schedule since before your grandmother's grandmother was born will arrive microseconds early or late. The song will not notice. But you will have committed a small vandalism, and you will know.

**Fast, lightly, for a day or two.** Not for spiritual reasons. A full stomach makes more noise — digestive, respiratory, incidental. You want to be quiet inside as well as outside. Eat a little cold cave-appropriate food before you enter: something bland, something that will not announce itself through your pores.

**Do not bathe with anything fragrant.** The air there has one smell — wet limestone, old copper, the faint mineral tang of the cistern — and it has had that smell for geological time. Soap, perfume, deodorant, the laundry-smell of clean clothes: all of these will precede you like a herald shouting.

**Wear wool or felted hair, if you can get it.** Damp-tolerant. Quiet. Dark. Nothing that rustles, nothing that creaks, no synthetic fabrics that will squeak against the rope ladders. Leather-soled shoes, or go barefoot once inside. Your feet will get cold. Accept this.

**Train your breathing.** Before you go, practice breathing slowly and through your nose for long stretches. Practice not-coughing. Practice not-sneezing. If you have any respiratory condition, any allergy, any tickle in your throat that comes and goes — reconsider the trip. A single sneeze in that chamber will be an event the inhabitants remember for three generations.

## What to Bring

Almost nothing.

- **A small light**, covered, for the tunnel in. Extinguish it before you enter the cavern proper. Your eyes will adjust to the lichen-glow within about twenty minutes. Sit in the approach tunnel and let them.
- **A water skin**, empty. You will drink from the cistern, if you are offered. Do not bring your own water. Your water is from somewhere else and does not belong in this cycle.
- **A gift, possibly.** This is delicate. The people do not trade, because trade implies change. But they may accept a small object if it is the right kind of small object: something that will not rot, will not rust in damp, will not smell, and — critically — *can be placed beneath a drip.* A small ceramic cup of the right resonance is the ideal gift and also the most dangerous, because it will alter the song if they choose to hang it. They may simply set it aside, unused, for another thousand years. That is also an acceptable fate for your gift. Do not expect reciprocation.
- **No recording devices.** Not because they will be forbidden, but because they will fail to capture what is actually happening. The sound in that room is not a sound-pattern; it is a thousand-year collaborative relationship between water and vessel and listener. A recording will give you tinks and toks and nothing of the song. Leave the temptation behind.
- **No notebook, no pen.** Write later, from memory. What you cannot remember was not for you.

## Learning to Listen Before You Arrive

This is the part most travelers skip, and it is the most important.

Find a dripping place wherever you live — a leaking gutter, a thawing eave, a kitchen tap turned almost off. Sit beneath it for an hour. Then two hours. Then a full afternoon. Notice how your ear, at first, flattens the drips into a single annoying sound; then, with time, begins to separate them; then, with more time, begins to notice the *intervals* rather than the drips themselves; then, with still more time, stops noticing anything and simply sits inside the sound as inside a room.

You want, when you enter The Drippings, to already know the shape of that progression. Otherwise you will spend your entire visit in the first stage — the flattening — and you will leave having heard nothing.

If you can, learn to speak on the in-breath. The inhabitants' register is not mystical; it is a practical adaptation. You will not master it, but if you practice even a little, you will be less of a blunt instrument when you try to communicate.

## Crossing the Threshold

Approach the cavern slowly. The tunnel muttering will tell you when you are close. Stop a hundred paces out and sit. Listen. Let your body come to terms with the humidity, the cold, the particular quality of the air. Do not proceed until your breathing has slowed to match the ambient rhythm of the place — and it will, if you let it. The room is a pacemaker.

When you enter, do not speak. Do not nod a greeting. Do not make eye contact with anyone, not because it is forbidden but because a direct gaze from a stranger is a demand, and you are in no position to demand anything. Simply stand at the edge of the chamber, on whatever stepping stone presents itself, and wait.

Someone will come to you eventually, or they will not. If they do not, that is also an answer. You may sit at the edge of the cavern for a full day without being addressed, and this is not rudeness. You have not yet been absorbed into the sound. Once you have — once your breathing has synchronized, once your movements have stilled, once you stop *happening* and begin *being there* — they will know, and one of them will drift toward you in that slow negotiating walk, and a conversation will begin beneath the drip-song that you will at first mistake for silence.

## What Not to Ask

Do not ask how old the place is. They do not keep that kind of time.

Do not ask what the word is — the one spoken on the in-breath between two drips. You may eventually hear it. You will not be told it. If you hear it, do not repeat it; your mouth is not shaped for it and a bad approximation is worse than silence.

Do not ask to help. There is no help to give. The song is complete.

Do not ask why. The *why* is the sound itself, and any verbal explanation would be a translation so lossy as to constitute a lie.

Do not ask about the outside. They know there is an outside. They have chosen this. The choice was made so long ago that asking about it is like asking a river why it is wet.

## What You May Ask

You may ask the name of a particular vessel, pointing. They may tell you. The names are not labels; they are something closer to how you might name the notes in a piece of music you love.

You may ask to be shown where to sit for the night. There will be a place, probably a ledge, probably damp. Accept it gratefully.

You may ask, softly, to be told a story. They have very few. The ones they have are about the cavern, and the first listeners, and the making of particular cups. The stories are told in the same register as ordinary speech and you may not notice when one begins or ends.

## Sleeping There

You will not sleep well. The sound does not stop and your ears, unlike theirs, have not learned to stop bringing it forward. Do not fight this. Lie still, let the drip-song wash over you, and understand that the sleeplessness is itself part of what you came for. Somewhere around the third or fourth hour, if you are lucky, the sound will cease to be a thing happening *to* you and will become a thing you are happening *inside of*, and in that moment you will understand — not intellectually, but in your bones — what the inhabitants mean by that soft in-breath word.

Then you will probably sleep. Briefly. And the waking will be strange.

## Leaving

Leave the way you came, slowly, without farewell. Farewells require acknowledgment of change — that you were here and now you are not — and the town does not transact in change. Simply go. Your absence will close behind you like water closing over a dropped stone.

Once you are back in the tunnel, do not look back. Not for superstitious reasons. Because the sight of the cavern from the tunnel-mouth, with its wet gleam and its pale green-gray glow and its small people moving slowly among their hung cups, is the kind of image that will make you want to stay, and if you stay past your welcome you will become the first disruption in a thousand years, and you will carry that for the rest of your life.

## After

Do not try to build a replica. Travelers have tried. It does not work. The Drippings is not reproducible; it is not a technology or an architecture. It is a specific cavern with a specific water-table and a specific lineage of listeners, and any attempt to copy it produces only a room with cups in it.

Do not tell many people. The ones who need to hear about it will somehow already be listening for it, and your telling will simply confirm what they already half-knew. The ones who do not need to hear about it will hear "a cave with some people in it" and miss everything.

For weeks afterward, you will find yourself, at odd moments — washing dishes, standing in rain, hearing a tap drip in a neighbor's apartment — suddenly back there. This is not a flashback. This is the sound having taught your ear something, and your ear practicing.

You will never quite hear water the same way again.

That, more than anything you saw or did, is what you went for.
