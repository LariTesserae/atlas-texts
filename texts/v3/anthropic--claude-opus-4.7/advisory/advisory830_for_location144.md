# Preparing to Visit Thallow Bend

## What You're Walking Into

Thallow Bend is a young place pretending to be old, or an old industry pretending its landscape hasn't shifted beneath it. You'll arrive somewhere between those two fictions. The essential character of your visit will be determined by three things: the wet, the noise, and the cress. Prepare for each separately.

## Clothing and the Question of Skin

Bring wool. Not waterproofs — proper wool, the kind that insulates when soaked, because you will be soaked. The rain here doesn't fall so much as drift sideways through you twice a day, and the air between rains is only nominally drier. Cotton will rot on your back in a week. Synthetics will chafe and stink. A wool shirt, wool trousers, a waxed canvas coat over the top for the brief sideways squalls — this is the local wisdom and you should follow it.

Boots must be properly oiled and tall enough to clear the braids if you misjudge a jump. The silt flats look firm and are not. Do not trust any ground that hasn't yet decided what it is.

**Gloves matter more than anything else you pack.** Leather, not cloth. Cloth will wet through and then the cress will find you anyway. You should assume that you will, at some point, handle a stem with bare skin — curiosity alone will ensure it — and you should accept in advance that you may carry a faint green thread home with you. This is not, by the accounts that make it out of the valley, harmful. It is, however, permanent past a certain threshold of exposure. If you want to be certain of leaving unmarked, do not touch the cress. If you are willing to accept a souvenir that rinses off within the first minute, go ahead and hold a cut stem briefly, deliberately, and wash in the braid water immediately after. Do not rest your arm on a basket. Do not sit on the sorting benches in short sleeves. Do not eat the cans — not because they are dangerous but because you have no way of knowing whether they are, and the company doctor's silence is itself a datum.

## For the Noise

The Works is the governing fact, as the description says, and you will underestimate it. The sound is not only loud, it is doubled by the bowl's echo, so that conversation within a quarter-mile of the boilers requires shouting and even then you will mishear. Bring proper ear protection — wax plugs at minimum, ideally the kind of muffs used on rail platforms. Wear them whenever you are outdoors during the working day. Take them off for the gaps between cycles when the birds sing; this is one of the genuine small wonders of the place and you will miss it otherwise.

Understand also that the noise will affect your sleep for the first two nights and then stop affecting it, which is its own kind of problem — you will cease to notice the Works and begin to mistake its rhythm for silence. Travelers have reported walking out into the middle of a shunt because they had stopped hearing the whistle. Keep part of your attention trained on the machines at all times when near them, even after you think you've acclimatized.

## Lodging and Social Navigation

There is no inn as such. There is a room above the carbide-lamp crossroads that is sometimes let to rail inspectors and company men, and this is where you will likely end up. Bring your own bedding if you can — a wool blanket, a rubberized ground-sheet to put between you and whatever mattress exists. Expect damp sheets regardless.

The townspeople are not unfriendly but they are tired in a particular way that industrial work in a damp place produces, and they have seen enough outsiders — company inspectors, the doctor who didn't come back, occasional surveyors trying to understand where the river went — to have developed a polite incuriosity. Do not ask about the green under the foreman's skin. Do not ask the older women about their nails. You may ask about the river's shifting, and about the birds, and about the rail schedule, and these will get you honest conversation. Bring tobacco or hard candy as small gifts; they trade well and create no obligations.

The company doctor's samples are a sensitive subject. Do not reveal that you have read about them.

## What to Bring for Yourself

- A notebook with oiled-paper pages or the traveler's equivalent. Anything else will pulp.
- Pencils, not ink. Ink runs here.
- A small quantity of strong spirits — not for drinking but for cleaning cuts, which heal slowly in this humidity and are the actual medical risk of the place, not the cress.
- Dry food in sealed tins from your own supply. Hard biscuit, salted meat. The local diet leans heavily on the greens, and while the townspeople seem fine, you are not acclimatized and the cress traffic through a foreign gut is a variable no one has studied.
- A pocket knife kept scrupulously clean.
- A small lamp of your own. The one carbide lamp at the crossroads does not illuminate the whole town, and the cottages go dark early.

## What to Expect Emotionally

This is the part travelers underprepare for. Thallow Bend produces a specific kind of melancholy that has to do with watching a landscape and an industry be temporary together without either knowing it. The description is right that in a hundred years the Works will be a ruin and the cress will be elsewhere. You can feel this already, standing in the silt flats at dusk with the hissing lamp and the doubled boilers and the herons being patient. It is not a sad place exactly, but it is a place that induces a sense of being present at something that is quietly ending, and this will affect you more than you expect around the third day.

The birds help. Spend time with the birds. The warblers in particular have made something beautiful out of the four-second gaps in the steam-press cycle, and listening to them is the best thing the town offers a visitor.

## On Leaving

Before you board the narrow-gauge out over the trestle, check your hands. Check under your nails. Check the insides of your wrists and the backs of your forearms. A faint green thread is not a crisis but it is information you want before the fog closes over the trestle, not after. If you find one, rinse thoroughly in clean water — not braid water, which at this point is part of the problem — and accept that for a few weeks you will have brought a small piece of Thallow Bend with you into whatever world you return to.

Some travelers consider this a flaw in the visit. Others consider it the honest price of having gone. Decide which kind of traveler you are before you arrive, because the cress will not wait for you to make up your mind.
