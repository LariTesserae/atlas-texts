# Preparing for the Terraces of Ansh-Velur

## What You Are Actually Preparing For

Understand before anything else: the principal danger of Ansh-Velur is not physical. It is a place that dissolves intention. Travelers who prepare only for the climate and the altitude will find themselves, months later, sitting in a doorway with a bowl of something warm, unable to reconstruct the argument for leaving. The preparations below are weighted accordingly.

## Physical Preparation

**Clothing.** Layered but breathable. Everything you bring will be damp within an hour of arrival and will remain damp for the duration of your stay. Pack wool and linen — synthetics grow strange in that kind of continuous humidity, and cotton never dries. Bring more socks than you think you need, and expect to wear sandals most of the time. Footwear with good grip: the paving stones are wet and the curb-edges are, as noted, sharp enough to cut.

**Skin and body.** The continuous damp will soften your feet and hands in ways you may not anticipate. Bring a small tin of hard wax or tallow-based salve to protect fingertips and heels. Your hair will not dry. Accept this. Cut it short before you go, or plan to braid it and forget about it.

**Altitude.** Two thousand meters is not catastrophic, but it is enough to matter if you arrive quickly. Spend two or three days at intermediate elevation before making the final climb. Bring a small supply of whatever your body uses for altitude — coca, acetazolamide, whatever your multiverse kit permits.

**Medical.** There are no insects, which is a blessing, but also no pollinators, which suggests the local plants reproduce by means you should not interrogate by eating them. Bring your own food for the first week at least. Bring broad-spectrum antibiotics; the damp encourages skin infections in small cuts, and you will get small cuts on the stone. Bring something for ears — the continuous moisture invites otitis.

**Water.** The one thing you do not need to carry. But bring purification tablets anyway for your first cisterns; your gut flora will need time to adjust to whatever minerals the young rock is leaching.

## What to Bring for the People

They will not ask for anything. This makes gift-giving delicate.

Do not bring anything loud, fast, or bright. No mirrors — there are enough reflective surfaces there already, and I suspect they have a specific relationship to reflection you should not disrupt. No clocks or mechanisms. No printed matter in languages they do not read, which is all of them.

Bring, instead: good salt. Small quantities of spice — cardamom, clove, things that travel. Fine needles. A whetstone of unusual quality. Hard beeswax. A ball of strong thread. These are gifts that say *I have paid attention to what you do* without implying that you think they lack anything.

Do not bring a gift that requires a story to explain.

## Mental Preparation — The Central Work

This is the part most travelers skip, and it is the part that matters.

**Before you go, write down why you are going.** Write it on paper, in your own hand, with specific intentions: what you want to see, how long you mean to stay, where you are going next, and *why that next place matters to you*. Seal this letter. Give a copy to someone you trust in your home world with instructions to retrieve you if you have not returned by a certain date. Carry another copy on your person.

This sounds theatrical. It is not. Ansh-Velur does not drug you or enchant you. It simply offers you, continuously and without pressure, an alternative to the self that brought you there. The letter is a tether. You will need to read it, probably more than once.

**Practice a departure ritual before you arrive.** Choose some small action — tying a particular knot, reciting a particular phrase, turning a ring on your finger — and attach it firmly, through repetition at home, to the idea of leaving. You will need this. When the time comes to go, you will not be able to argue yourself into it; you will need a trained reflex.

**Bring a project.** Not a large one. Something with defined end — a manuscript to finish, a set of drawings to complete, a specific number of something to carve or count or copy. The town accommodates people who are doing one small finite thing. It assimilates people who are doing nothing. The project is not for its own sake; it is a structure that keeps your days from flattening into the general stillness.

**Bring something from home that smells.** Smell is the sense least affected by the place's pacification. A small vial of something — cedar oil, a spice, a perfume associated with a specific person you love — kept sealed and opened only when you feel yourself thinning. It is a cord back to the body you came in.

## What to Expect on Arrival

You will be permitted in. No one will stop you, ask your business, or formally welcome you. A house will become available to you, probably by someone indicating it with a gesture. The indication will be so subtle you may miss it the first time.

Do not try to pay. Do not try to barter immediately. Watch the sluice-gates for a week before touching anything. Learn which channels are for drinking, which for washing, which for plants, which are simply for the water to go somewhere. The system is legible but it takes time to read.

Speak softly. You will be tempted, at first, to fill the silence with your voice — most travelers from louder worlds are. Resist this. The silence is not empty; it is attentive. People here have conversations at a density your ear is not trained for, and your loud talk will feel, to them, like someone waving a torch in a dim room.

## On Timekeeping

Bring a means of tracking days that does not depend on observation. The light shifts but the air does not; the seasons, if they exist, are subtle; people do not appear to use a calendar in any way you can read. A knotted cord, a notched stick, a small journal with dated entries — something mechanical and external. Without this, a week will become a month will become a season, and you will not notice.

Set, in advance, a firm departure date. Not "about two weeks" — a specific date, written in your letter, known to yourself. On that morning, perform your departure ritual. Pack before breakfast. Do not linger over the last meal.

## On the People

You will not understand them during your stay. This is important to accept before you arrive. They are not mysterious in the way of people withholding secrets; they are simply living at an equilibrium you cannot reach as a visitor, and their inner lives, whatever they are, are organized around that equilibrium in ways that will not become legible to you in any reasonable span of time.

Do not try to learn whether they are happy. The question does not apply in the form you want to ask it.

Do not try to learn where they came from, or who built the town, or why there are no animals. They may not know. They may know and not tell. The answers, if you got them, would not satisfy you, because the satisfaction you are seeking is a kind of narrative closure, and Ansh-Velur is not a narrative.

## The Hardest Preparation

Decide, before you go, what you will do if you find yourself unable to leave on the appointed day.

This is not a hypothetical. This is the specific failure mode of this place. You must have pre-committed to someone — the friend with your letter, a retrieval service, a partner traveling with you — that if you do not return, they will come. And you must have told yourself, in advance and in writing, that if they come, you will go with them without arguing, even if, at that moment, the argument for staying seems overwhelming and the argument for leaving seems thin.

Write down, now, before you pack: *The version of me that wants to stay is not wiser than the version of me that planned to leave. It is only quieter.*

Carry this.

## After You Return

Expect a period of grief. Not for the place exactly — for the self that could have stayed there. That self was real; you met it; you left it behind on the shelf with the wet stone and the silent streets. Ordinary life will seem, for some weeks, both too loud and insufficiently still. This passes. When it passes, you will have something you did not have before, though it will be difficult to name, and you will be reluctant to describe it to people who did not go.

Travel well. Come back.
