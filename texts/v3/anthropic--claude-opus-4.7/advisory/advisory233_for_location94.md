# Preparing to Visit the Cold Sea Under the Engine

## Understanding What You're Going Into

Before anything practical, sit with this: you are visiting a place that is finished. Not dead, not dying, not developing — finished. Most locations a multiverse traveler encounters are in motion, even geological ones; they have a direction, a trajectory, a story they are telling themselves. This place has no story. It has arrived. Your preparation should account for the psychological weight of that, which will be greater than you expect.

You will not be welcomed. You will not be rejected. You will be registered, if at all, as a very small silence-shape against a very large roar, and then you will leave, and nothing will have changed.

## The Sound: Your Single Largest Problem

Everything else is a subset of this problem. A continuous two-hertz pulse transmitted through water at the intensities described will not merely be loud — it will be *structural*. It will enter your body through every surface simultaneously. Your ribcage will resonate. Your sinuses will resonate. The fluid in your inner ear, your eyes, your abdomen will all be driven at frequencies they did not evolve to encounter.

**You cannot block this with earplugs.** The sound is not arriving primarily through your ears. It is arriving through your entire body as pressure-wave.

Practical preparation:

- **A rigid-hulled submersible, not a wetsuit.** Diving into this water in soft gear is not an expedition, it is a medical emergency with a delayed onset. You need an air-filled cavity around you that can be acoustically damped, and you need the submersible's hull to present an impedance mismatch the low frequencies will partially reflect rather than transmit.
- **Internal acoustic damping in the submersible.** Heavy felt, mass-loaded vinyl, asymmetric interior geometry to prevent standing waves inside your cabin. Ideally a suspended inner pod decoupled from the outer hull.
- **A bite guard, worn continuously.** The two-hertz pulse will find your teeth. Jaw clenching during prolonged exposure cracks molars.
- **Abdominal binding.** Not tight, but firm. Your viscera will be sympathetically vibrating and you want to limit the amplitude. This sounds absurd until about hour six.
- **Anti-nausea medication, the strong kind.** Low-frequency whole-body vibration at sustained intensity is reliably emetic in humans. Scopolamine patches, metoclopramide as backup.
- **A planned exposure schedule with hard limits.** Budget no more than forty-eight hours in the environment per visit. Prolonged exposure to infrasound causes documented effects — anxiety, disorientation, visual disturbances, a specific sense of dread that is not metaphorical but physiological. You cannot outlast this through willpower. Plan your exit before you go in.

## The Cold

Three degrees Celsius is not arctic, but it is a temperature at which an unprotected human becomes unconscious in under an hour and dies in two. The water does not stratify, which means there is no refuge. You do not get warmer by rising or descending.

Your submersible needs real thermal management — not just heaters, but insulation rated for indefinite immersion, and a power budget that accounts for the cold being permanent rather than intermittent. If you plan any exterior activity at all, you need a rated drysuit with active heating, tethered, with a conservative time limit and someone in the cabin watching the clock.

Bring more warm food than you think you need. The cold of this place works on you psychologically as well as physically; hot meals become disproportionately important. This is true of any cold expedition and especially true here, because there is nothing else to look forward to.

## Light and Navigation

The ambient light is enough to see by but not enough to work by, and it is perfectly even, which means it provides no directional cues. You will lose your sense of up and down faster than you expect. The peaks help — they give you a reference — but in the water columns between them you are in a featureless grey volume.

- **Bring lights, but use them sparingly.** Not because anything here will mind, but because your eyes will adapt to the ambient grey-blue and bright lights will cost you twenty minutes of night vision each time you switch them off.
- **Redundant inertial navigation.** GPS will not work. Magnetic compasses may or may not work depending on what the engines are doing to the local field; do not rely on one. You want inertial nav, acoustic beacons deployed from your vessel, and a paper log.
- **Tethering protocol.** If you leave the submersible, you are tethered. Not optional. The combination of featureless water, pervasive low-frequency disorientation, and eyeless predators that read silence-shapes is not an environment in which to trust your sense of direction.

## The Animals

The fauna is not the primary danger here but it is a real one, and worth understanding on its own terms.

The predators hunt by reading disturbances in the sound field. You, in a submersible, are an enormous, roaring, utterly unlike-anything-else disturbance. You will be noticed. Whether you will be *approached* depends on whether anything in the local fauna has a hunting strategy that extends to things your size, which seems unlikely — nothing here evolved with you as a possible prey item — but curiosity and territorial behavior are possible.

The more interesting risk is that your own sensing equipment will be useless in conventional ways. Active sonar is cruel and probably counterproductive: you will deafen or disorient animals that use the roar-field to navigate, and you will learn very little because the ambient noise floor is already so high. Passive acoustic sensing is almost useless for the same reason. You will mostly be seeing with your eyes, at short range, in dim light.

- **Do not touch anything living unless you must.** These animals communicate by touch. You do not know what you are saying.
- **Do not bring biological samples home.** Quarantine protocols for cross-world biology are always worth observing, but here especially: nothing about the local chemistry is understood, and the animals are adapted to a pressure, temperature, and acoustic regime so specific that they will not survive transport anyway. Take images. Leave the bodies.

## The Structures

This is the part where the greatest caution is required, and where most visitors will be most tempted to be incautious.

The built things are not ruins. They are functioning infrastructure of an industry you cannot identify, maintained (or not requiring maintenance) by intelligences (or processes) that have not shown themselves. The pipes lead somewhere. The vents are connected to something. The wall that runs for a kilometer and stops is part of something.

**Do not enter any of them.** Do not dock with them. Do not attach instruments to them. Do not drill samples from them. Do not interfere with the flow of anything into or out of them, however slow that flow appears to be.

The reasoning is not that you will be punished — there may be nothing here to punish you. The reasoning is that you do not know what these things do, and the engines have been running at perfect equilibrium for a very long time, and you are a traveler who will leave, and whatever you disturb you will not be present to repair. The ethics of a visitor to a finished place are the ethics of someone entering a room where a patient is sleeping: you look, you do not touch, you leave no trace.

Photography and external measurement only. Stand-off distance of at least fifty meters from any structure. If a structure is venting, even imperceptibly, stay upstream of the vent — whatever is coming out is coming out for a reason, and you do not want to be in it.

## Psychological Preparation

This is the part people neglect and should not.

The Cold Sea Under the Engine is a place where nothing is happening, where nothing will happen, and where your presence is meaningless in a specific and thorough way. Many travelers find this clarifying. Many find it unbearable. You will not know which you are until you have been there for about sixteen hours.

Prepare for this:

- **Go with at least one other person.** Solo travel here is inadvisable. The place does not isolate you actively, but it isolates by default, and having another human voice in the cabin matters.
- **Bring distraction.** Books, music (through bone-conduction headphones, so you can still hear the hull), work you need to do. The roar is not something you adapt to — you adapt to it in the sense that you stop noticing you hear it, and then you notice again.
- **Have a clear reason for being there.** Tourism is a bad motivation for this location. Scientific curiosity works. Pilgrimage works, if that is your temperament. "I wanted to see it" will not sustain you past the first night.
- **Plan what you will do when you leave.** Not the logistics — the next destination. Somewhere warm, somewhere loud with the right kinds of loud, somewhere with weather and seasons and growing things. You will need the contrast. Do not go home to an empty apartment directly from this place. It will not harm you, but it will take longer than you expect to feel correctly situated again in a world that is still becoming itself.

## A Final Note

Some travelers report, on leaving, a reluctance to discuss the visit. Not secrecy, exactly — more that the place does not seem to want to be a story, and carrying it out into the multiverse as an anecdote feels faintly wrong. You may feel this. You may not. If you do, respect it. The Cold Sea will not mind either way. It has arrived at what it is, and your talking or not talking about it is among the many, many things that do not matter there.

Pack well. Go carefully. Come back.
