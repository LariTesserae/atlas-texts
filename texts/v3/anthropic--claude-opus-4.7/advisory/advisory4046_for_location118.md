# Preparing for Ceiba-Under-Cloud

## What You Are Walking Into

Understand this first: you are not visiting a town built in a tree. You are visiting a slow organism that has, among its other functions, come to shelter people. The distinction matters for your preparation, because almost everything you would bring to a "town" is wrong, and almost everything you would bring to a "wilderness" is also wrong. You need the equipment of a person going to live inside a lung.

The second thing to understand is the permanent wetness. You have probably not experienced it unless you have worked in a cave system or a commercial mushroom farm. Ordinary rain gear fails here because rain gear is designed to shed water that falls from above; here the water is already in the air, on your skin, in the fabric, and traveling upward through the tree beneath your feet. There is no dry to return to. You must reconceive your body as something that will be damp for the entire duration of your stay, and plan accordingly.

## Physical Conditioning

Begin at least two months before departure.

**Lungs.** The altitude plus the saturated air means you will be working harder to extract oxygen from each breath, and the breaths themselves will feel heavy, almost like drinking. Cardiovascular conditioning at elevation if you can manage it — hike, run stairs, anything that teaches your chest to work under a mild deficit. If you have any respiratory condition, asthma especially, reconsider the trip or bring what medications you can; the constant cool humidity is generally kind to airways but the altitude is not.

**Joints.** You will be walking on a living floor of interwoven branches. It flexes. It is never perfectly level. Your ankles, knees, and hips will spend every waking hour making micro-adjustments. People who arrive with weak ankles leave with sprains. Balance work, single-leg exercises, some barefoot walking on uneven ground.

**Grip.** You will use your hands constantly — on ropes, on branches, on ladders slick with moss. Forearm and grip strength is not optional. Deadhangs, farmer's carries, rope climbs if you have access.

**Cold tolerance in the extremities.** The cool-not-cold condition is deceptive. Your core will be fine. Your fingers and toes will ache by mid-afternoon for the first several days until they acclimate. Practice working with cold hands — wash dishes in cold water, do fine motor tasks with chilled fingers — so the clumsiness does not surprise you.

## Clothing

Forget most of what you own. Synthetics will not breathe in saturated air and will grow their own ecosystems against your skin within days. Cotton is a disaster — it holds water and gives it back to you as chill. Wool is acceptable but heavy when soaked.

The locals have solved this problem. Bring an offering of fine hemp or a small amount of beeswax or rendered tallow and request a set of greased bark-cloth in local style. This is the correct garment for the place and nothing you bring from outside will outperform it. While you wait for your acclimation clothing, layer as follows:

- Base: thin wool, accepting it will never dry
- Middle: a loose wool or alpaca sweater, oversized
- Outer: an oiled canvas or waxed cotton outer, not to keep water out but to keep wind off and to shed the worst of the constant drip

Bring **two pairs of boots** if you bring any at all, and expect both to rot. Better: bring sturdy sandals with grippy soles and accept that your feet will be wet and cool. The locals likely go barefoot on the leaf-floor; your soft outsider feet will not manage this for at least a week. Socks in quantity, wool only, and understand you will wring them out nightly and they will still be damp in the morning.

A **hat with a brim** for the drip, treated with oil or wax. A **scarf or neck-wrap** — the back of the neck is where the constant dampness finds you and teaches you what cold means.

## Skin and Body Care

Your skin will soften and slough in ways that will alarm you. Trench-foot-like conditions are a real risk. Bring:

- Tallow, lanolin, or a heavy balm for feet and hands, applied twice daily
- A small pumice or rough cloth for the dead skin that will accumulate
- Antifungal powder or the local equivalent — ask; they will have something involving ash or a specific bark
- Salt, a small bag, for rinsing minor cuts; infections set in fast in this atmosphere

Cuts and scrapes do not heal normally here. Anything that breaks the skin must be cleaned and greased immediately. The moss and fungal presence is everywhere and generally benign but finds wounds eagerly.

## Hearing and Voice

You have read about the sound. You have not experienced it. For the first three days you will have a headache that will not be a headache exactly — it will be your auditory system adjusting to a new baseline. Sleep will be difficult until your brain learns to classify the sound as silence, which it will, by approximately night five.

**Do not bring earplugs.** This is a mistake visitors make and it cuts them off from the townspeople, who speak at a pitch and volume calibrated to cut through the noise. If you muffle the noise, you also muffle human speech, and you will miss warnings about slick footing and branch weaknesses. Let your ears adapt.

**Practice speaking loudly before you arrive.** Not shouting — projecting. Learn to speak from your diaphragm at a volume that would be rude at home. Whispering, as the description says, does not exist here. If you try to whisper out of habit you will simply not be heard, and the repeated failure to be heard will begin to affect you psychologically within a few days. Get over the hump early.

Your **speaking voice will be hoarse** for the first week from the combination of volume and the wet air being constantly drawn over your vocal cords. Bring honey if you have it, or a soothing herb; the locals have something for this.

## Navigation and Orientation

Twenty paces of visibility. Thirty on a good morning. You must internalize what this means: you cannot see the town from most places within the town. Each dwelling is an island. You will get lost on the leaf-floor the first several times you try to go anywhere alone, and getting lost here is not a trivial matter because the fissures between the great branches are real and some of them go down a long way.

**Strategies:**

- Do not travel alone for the first three days. Ask a local, especially a child, to show you the main routes. Children are the best guides because they have no shame about the pace you require.
- Learn the **rope-lines**. There will almost certainly be guide ropes strung between dwellings along the most-used paths. Learn them by feel. Walk them once in daylight, then once at dusk with a hand on the rope the whole time.
- Carry a **small bell or clapper** only if the locals use them. If they don't, don't introduce the habit. Your ears will learn to pick voices and human footfalls from the bird-noise faster than you expect.
- The mist thickens toward evening. **Be home by the time lanterns are lit.** This is not a suggestion.

## Sleep

You will sleep close. This is not a euphemism and not necessarily communal — it simply means that the warmth of another body is how people stay warm through the night in houses whose walls are moss-packed lattice. If you are a solitary sleeper by preference, arrange in advance for extra blankets, furs if they have them, and a small brazier if permitted. Do not expect to replicate your home sleeping arrangements. Your first few nights will be poor regardless; accept this.

Sleep will come easier than you expect once it does come, because the constant white noise of drip and bird and frog is, for the human nervous system, deeply ancient and deeply soothing once the novelty passes. People who stay long enough report that silence, afterward, feels thin and unsafe.

## Food

Eat what they eat. Do not bring trail rations that will grow mold within a day of being opened. If you must bring something, bring:

- **Salt** — always a welcome trade good in a place with no easy access to it
- **Honey or sugar in sealed wax** — concentrated calories, good for trade, good for your own throat
- **Dried meats heavily smoked** — may last a week if you're lucky
- **Strong spirits in small quantity** — for disinfection and for warming, but drink sparingly; the altitude multiplies alcohol

Expect to eat fruit, insects, tree-sap preparations, bird eggs, perhaps occasional monkey (though given that the monkeys may be endemic to this one tree, perhaps not — observe before asking). The tree itself is part of the diet in subtle ways. Do not refuse offered food; the social contract here is thin and fragile for outsiders and food refusal will be remembered.

## The Matter of the Tree

Here is the thing you must be prepared for psychologically, not physically.

The tree is wrong. The description tells you this plainly. A ceiba does not grow this large, does not grow at this altitude, does not flat-top into a platform. Its roots reach water that is not the mountain's water. You are a multiverse traveler; you know what this means better than the locals do, or rather, you know it in a register they do not use. The tree is drawing from somewhere else. The tree may, in some sense you cannot quite verify, *be* somewhere else, with only its crown poking into this world.

**Do not investigate this.** I cannot stress this enough. The locals have achieved a stable, centuries-long accommodation with this fact by not making it a fact. They live in the tree. They drink its sap. They bury their dead in it. They do not theorize. If you arrive with instruments, with questions, with the outsider's urge to know, you will at best be considered a minor fool and at worst you will disturb the accommodation, and the accommodation is what keeps the town livable.

If you feel the pull to descend the roots, or to sit at the base of the trunk and listen for what is on the other side, resist it. This is a place where the membrane between worlds is thin and patient and has been drinking for a long time. It is not malicious. But travelers who have made themselves too interesting to such places sometimes become part of the uptake, and the locals do not consider this grim, only accurate.

Visit. Be wet. Speak loudly. Sleep warm. Leave when it is time to leave, and leave by the route you came, and do not look back into the cloud as you descend, because the cloud does not end where you think it does.

## Final Notes

Bring a small gift for your host family — salt is best, or a metal fishhook or needle, since they do not smelt. Do not bring anything mechanical, anything that ticks, anything with a battery. These things die here fast and their deaths are ugly.

Learn three phrases before you arrive: a greeting, a thanks, and *I do not understand, please show me*. You will use the third one more than the first two combined.

Plan to stay at least ten days. Fewer than that and you will leave before your ears have adjusted, before your skin has toughened, before the place has stopped feeling like an assault and started feeling like a slow bath. Ten days in, you may notice you have stopped noticing the dripping. That is when you have arrived. Stay a few days past that, and then go — because the place is kind to visitors but does not, in the long run, distinguish them from the tree's other slow uptakes, and the line between guest and compost is drawn by your own departure.

Safe travels. Bring tallow.
