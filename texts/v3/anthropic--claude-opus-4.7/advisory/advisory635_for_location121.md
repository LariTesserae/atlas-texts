# Preparing to Visit the Unfinished Summit

## Understanding What You're Walking Into

Before anything else: internalize that this is not a hostile place. It is a *premature* place. The distinction matters for every decision you'll make. Hostile environments want you dead; premature ones simply haven't decided yet whether things like "you" are part of the plan. Your preparations should aim not at defense but at *not interfering* with a process much larger and slower than your visit.

Read the description several times before departure. The Summit rewards travelers who arrive already knowing its grammar.

---

## Physical Preparation

**Altitude conditioning.** Whatever "high" means in your home cosmos, this is higher. Spend weeks beforehand at the greatest altitude you can reach. Do breath-hold training. Consider whether your multiverse kit includes supplemental oxygen that doesn't depend on ambient pressure — the air here "barely moves," which suggests it may also be barely there. Bring more than you think you need, and bring it in redundant small tanks rather than one large one, because you will be dropping things and things will be shifting under you.

**Thermal layering, inverted.** You will not need cold-weather gear in the usual sense. The stone is oven-warm from beneath; the air is still. But the warmth is *even and constant*, which is its own problem — your body cannot shed heat efficiently in a windless, radiantly warm environment with no evaporative cooling available (no water, anywhere, including your sweat once it leaves you; it will simply go). Wear light, loose, light-colored fabrics that breathe. Bring a reflective groundcloth so you are not in direct contact with the stone when you rest. You will cook slowly if you sit on it.

**Hydration is the central logistics problem.** There is no water. Not in the rocks, not in the air, not as ice, not anywhere. You must bring every drop you will drink, and you must drink constantly because the dry warmth will pull water out of you through your breath alone. Calculate triple what you would normally carry for the same duration. Then add more. Consider whether any member of your party can safely be left as a cache-keeper at a lower altitude while scouts ascend. If you are alone, plan a short visit.

**Boots with grip for fresh stone.** Nothing here is weathered, which means every surface is as sharp as the instant it cracked. Your soles will be scored. Bring spares. Bring gloves — thick ones — because you *will* touch the mountain, deliberately or otherwise, and the edges are all new.

## Mental Preparation

This is more important than the gear.

**Practice holding still in motion.** The turbulence is geological, which means your normal reflexes for "ground is shifting, run" will be uselessly triggered every few minutes and will exhaust you. Before you go, spend time on vibrating platforms, on boats, on anything that shifts beneath you while requiring you to remain calm. You need to learn the difference between *the ground doing what this ground does* and *imminent danger.* Most movement here is not about you.

**Rehearse stillness and patience.** The place moves on its own time. If you are the kind of traveler who fills silence with activity, you will miss what the Summit has to show you, and worse, you will make poor decisions trying to force legibility onto something that is not yet legible. Meditate before you go. Learn to sit for an hour watching a single boulder.

**Accept that you cannot read the weather.** There is no weather. There is only the argument. You cannot forecast it, you cannot wait it out, you cannot find a "safe season." Learn to make decisions under this kind of irreducible uncertainty — which means: conservative margins, early turnarounds, and no ego about the attempt.

## Gear Particular to This Place

- **A long walking staff.** You'll want a third point of contact always. Not because you'll slip — the stone is grippy — but because the stone itself may move while your feet are on it, and having an anchor elsewhere is reassurance.

- **A small, bright, wide-spectrum lamp.** The ochre glow from the seams is enough to see by but wrong for depth perception. You'll need true white light occasionally to judge whether a crack is a hairline or a chasm.

- **Ear protection, selectively used.** The quiet is mostly quiet, but a *crack* can come from anywhere with no warning, and the reflected echoes are disorienting. If you feel the low hum getting into your chest, muffle.

- **No metal that matters.** Leave behind anything whose shape is important to you if it's metal — the warmth, the dryness, and whatever is happening inside the stone all suggest a chemistry you don't know. Test small items first.

- **A sample tube, sealed, for a single filament if you must.** Do not take a colony. The filaments seem to communicate through the seam; a whole colony removed may be a whole thing ended. One filament from an edge, if science demands it. Better: take none, and record observations instead.

- **No living things.** Do not bring seeds, spores, cultures, pets, symbionts, gut-bacteria-altering supplements. This place has exactly one form of life (if it is life), and introducing others to a world that has not finished arriving is a category of harm most ethical frameworks will agree about, including ones you haven't heard of yet.

- **One stone from somewhere you love.** You'll see why.

## What to Do on Arrival

**Do not rush to the highest point.** There is no highest point. The land tilts up into haze and then is gone. Ascending past what you can safely return from is easy because there is no visible threshold of "too far." Set a turnaround time before you start up, write it on your hand, and honor it without negotiation.

**Walk lightly.** Your weight is nothing to the mountain, but your boots leave scuffs on fresh rock that has never been scuffed. Some travelers to premature places develop a practice of stepping only where the stone has recently moved — on surfaces that were inside a minute ago and bear no history. This is a kindness. Whether the mountain notices is unknown; whether you notice yourself doing it matters.

**Visit the built things, but do not enter them in any symbolic way.** Approach the low wall. Stand beside it. Do not lean on it. Walk *around* the cairn, not past it. The doorway is a harder case — it frames nothing, but it *is* a doorway, and doorways in premature places sometimes become load-bearing later in ways that depend on who walked through them early. My intuition: do not step through unless you have something to say to the world that will eventually exist there, and are willing to have said it.

**Sit by the basin.** Pour nothing into it. Not even a drop from your canteen, however poetic the gesture feels. Water has not yet arrived; you are not the one to introduce it. Sit and observe the lip, the spout, the care of the carving. Consider that someone knelt here, dry, and made a place ready.

## Whether to Add a Stone

You will feel the pull. The text all but invites it, and the mountain, as promised, will accept without comment.

Consider carefully. Adding a stone is a vote — a small one, but a real one — for a particular future of this place. The earlier builders made "references": instructions for a world that has not finished arriving. Your stone on their cairn is a continuation of their instruction. Are you qualified to extend it? Do you know what they were saying, well enough to say the next word?

My honest intuition: if you brought the stone from somewhere you love, and you have walked quietly, and you have sat with the basin long enough to feel the gesture rather than perform it, then yes. Place it. It is a good thing to do.

If you are uncertain, don't. There will be other travelers. The cairn is patient.

---

## Leaving

**Descend before you want to.** The upward tilt is seductive and the ceiling-haze hides how much farther you've gone than you meant to. Turn back early.

**Check yourself for filaments.** If any strands have attached to your gear, gently return them to a warm seam — not necessarily the one they came from; they seem to share knowledge across a seam anyway, and the one you found may have shifted. Do not carry them down. They belong to the geometry they are solving.

**Do not look back for a long time as you descend.** Not for superstitious reasons — but because the place you just visited will have rearranged itself by the time you glance over your shoulder, and the disorientation of realizing you could not return to the same summit even if you tried is a specific kind of grief that hits harder than expected. Get to lower altitude first. Then look.

---

## Afterward

Expect a period of adjustment when you return to a finished world. Weathered stone will look strange to you — softened, apologetic, *used.* Rain will seem miraculous for a while. You will find yourself touching walls to see if they are warm.

Some travelers report a lasting habit of leaving small, careful, useless structures in places they visit afterward — a stacked stone by a trailhead, a lintel of driftwood on a beach. Instructions for worlds not yet arrived. This is considered a benign effect, and in some traditions, a sign the visit went well.

Go warm. Go dry. Go quiet. Come back lighter than you went.
