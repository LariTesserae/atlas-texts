# Preparing for the Drum-Floor

## Understanding What You're Walking Into

Before anything practical, sit with what this place actually *is*. You are going to a basin of young forest on a young world, pre-human or para-human, where life has arrived at the stage of frenzy but not yet at the stage of balance. The ecosystem has not had time to invent fear of you, hatred of you, or appetite for you specifically. It will treat you the way a river treats a leaf — with complete indifference and complete inclusion.

The mistake most travelers make is to prepare as if for a jungle. This is not a jungle. Jungles are old. Jungles have learned. The Drum-Floor is closer to a newborn animal: enormous, undifferentiated, still coordinating its limbs, prone to sudden reconfigurations. Prepare for newness, not wildness.

## Physical Conditioning

**Cardiovascular baseline, not strength.** You will not be carrying much and you will not be fighting anything. What you will be doing is moving continuously in high heat and high humidity for days, across ground that is never level and never still. A runner's base is better than a climber's. Work up to six or seven hours of sustained low-effort movement in sauna-like conditions. If you can jog in a steam room without your thoughts collapsing, you're close.

**Acclimatize to blood-warm ambient.** Spend the weeks before departure in conditions around 37°C with humidity above 90%. Your body needs to learn to sweat efficiently in an environment where sweat does not evaporate. You will be wet the entire time you are there. Your skin needs to have stopped complaining about this before you arrive.

**Vestibular tolerance.** The canopy layers produce a flickering greenish light and the ground moves under you constantly — insects, worms, the small amphibians. People who are prone to motion sickness or who rely heavily on a stable visual field will struggle. Practice walking on uneven, shifting surfaces (a gravel bed, a trampoline, a deep bark mulch) with your eyes half-closed. Get used to proprioception in the absence of reliable ground-feedback.

**Sleep in trees.** This is not optional. Before you go, learn to sleep tied into a harness in a tree fork, for nights at a time, in weather. If this sounds absurd, consider that you are going somewhere you will do this for however long you stay, and your first night of it should not also be the first night you are surrounded by four hundred unknown animals per square meter.

## Sensory Preparation

This is where most visitors fail. The sound is not an ambient feature — it is the medium you will be living inside. People come back describing a kind of mental injury from the first two or three days, and some people break entirely.

**Desensitization protocol.** For a month before the trip: sleep with layered audio playing at 70-75 dB. Start with one track — frog chorus, say — and add layers every few days until you have five or six overlapping channels: low drone, mid-range chitter, high intermittent calls, white-noise weather. The goal is not to tune it out. The goal is for your nervous system to stop registering it as threat. When you can fall asleep easily inside the full layered soundscape, you have built the baseline you need.

**Subvocal thinking.** You cannot speak. You cannot even hear yourself think in the ordinary interior-monologue way, because the sound has a physical pressure that interferes with the brain's recursive self-listening. Practice thinking in images, gestures, and spatial relations. Meditation traditions that de-emphasize verbal thought — certain schools of Zen, some movement practices — are useful. If you have ever tried to navigate a problem without language, that is the skill.

**The bell-note exercise.** Practice listening *through* noise to a specific pitch. Put on a recording of a crowd and try to follow a single flute playing somewhere inside it. This is how you will communicate with the people there — through a thin acoustic window between the frog-drone and the insect-static. Their whistles will live in that window. So must yours.

**Learn to be touched.** Spend time in places where you will be touched by things you cannot control — a petting zoo, a butterfly house, a densely packed subway. Let insects land on you without flinching. The moth-on-the-face moment is the one that breaks people who haven't prepared. If you jerk away from it, you will spend the entire trip jerking away from things, and the forest will read you as a convulsing animal, which is not how you want to be read.

## What to Bring

Very little. The forest eats what stops moving, which includes your equipment. Every object you bring is a small investment in loss.

**Clothing:** Thin, tough, quick-drying synthetics in muted greens and browns. No white, no bright colors — not for camouflage but because you will be covered in organic slime within the first hour and bright colors will register psychologically as "ruined" in a way that will distress you needlessly. Long sleeves and trousers, tucked. A wide-brimmed hat with a mosquito veil for the first days, until you learn to stop noticing the insects.

**Footwear:** Two pairs, because one will be rotting. Closed, drained, light, fast-drying. Tall enough to cover the ankle against the seething silt. Expect to discard them at the end.

**Harness and tree-sleeping kit:** Lightweight climbing harness, 30m of static line, a tree saddle or hammock designed for canopy use, a simple mosquito-net shelter. Practice rigging it in the dark.

**Water:** There is water everywhere. You still need purification, because the water is full of organisms that have never met your gut flora. A lightweight UV pen and chemical backup. Do not rely on filters alone — the biota is too small and too varied.

**Food:** As much concentrated nutrition as you can carry without slowing yourself. Expect to eat things you recognize from your pack and nothing else, ever. Do not forage. The people there will eat some of what they find; you will not know what they know.

**Medical:** Broad-spectrum antibiotics, antifungals (crucial — everything molds), antiparasitics, antihistamines for the inevitable reactions to things that have never encountered a mammal like you. Wound care supplies; every scratch will colonize instantly. Do not expect to come home without at least one parasite.

**Tools:** A single good knife. A small firestarter (almost useless but psychologically important). A compass and altimeter (for what little they're worth, given the terrain rearranges itself). No electronics beyond what is absolutely needed; humidity will kill them within a week.

**No journal.** This is a point people resist. You cannot keep anything there. Paper rots in a day, screens die. More importantly, the forest eats memory — this is not metaphor; the rearrangement of the landscape undermines the reference points memory clings to. Bring what you can hold in your body. Write it down when you get back, if you come back.

## Skills to Acquire

**Basic canopy movement.** You will not need to climb like the locals do, but you need to be able to get into a tree fork five or six meters up without help, and down again, reliably, in poor light. Take a tree-climbing course. The kind arborists use is adequate.

**Whistle language, rudimentary.** Learn four or five pitched whistles that will carry through the soundscape. The people there will teach you theirs once they decide to, but you should arrive able to produce a *here*, an *approach*, a *wait*, a *thank you* — even if your versions are wrong, the intent of having some will be read.

**The gesture vocabulary.** This is almost impossible to prepare for in advance, since the gestures are specific to these people, but: practice communicating without words for days at a time. Spend a weekend in silence with a companion, using only touch and hand-signs. The mental shape you need is the one that emerges on the second day of such a practice, when the absence of speech stops feeling like deprivation.

**Reading movement, not landmarks.** Orienteering here is done by *feel-of-direction*, by tracking how the ground slopes and how the light falls and where the air is moving, not by "the big tree" or "the split rock." Practice finding your way in shifting environments — tidal flats, dune fields, dense fog. The people there navigate by the persistent features: water sources, particular fruiting species, the smell of specific decay. Learn to navigate by what a place smells like.

## Psychological Preparation

This is the deepest work and the most important.

**Accept that you will be touched, entered, and colonized.** Things will crawl on you. Things will bite you. Something will lay eggs in a small cut and you will discover it later. A snake will use your shoulders as a warm bridge. Your body is not going to be private there. If you cannot make peace with this in advance, you will spend your entire visit in a state of horror, and the forest will press on that horror until you break.

**Accept the loss of interiority.** The inside of your head, in the ordinary sense, is partly constructed by silence and by the ability to hear yourself think. The Drum-Floor takes this from you. For the first days you will feel as if your mind is being drowned. It is not — it is being relocated into your body, into gesture and motion and sensation. This is uncomfortable but not damaging, if you let it happen. Fighting it is what does the damage.

**Prepare for the third or fourth day.** Everyone who writes about this place mentions it. The panic wears through and something opens. You start to hear *into* the noise. You recognize individual creatures by their call. The soundscape becomes a map. You begin to feel *held* rather than assaulted. If you leave before this happens, you will have experienced only the worst of the place. Plan to stay past it.

**The held-by-something-too-busy-to-notice-you feeling.** This is the thing the place is famous for, among those who know about it. It is not mystical and it is not pleasant in the way a hot bath is pleasant. It is a specific and strange kindness, and it has been reported to change people in ways they find difficult to articulate. Do not go seeking it; it will not come if sought. Just stay long enough that it can find you.

**Prepare for return.** Travelers who come back from the Drum-Floor often report difficulty with ordinary quiet afterward. Silence can feel like deafness for weeks. Solitary thinking can feel eerie, as though something is missing. The ordinary separation between yourself and your environment — which your home world trains into you from infancy — has been partially dissolved, and it takes time to re-form. Plan a gentle re-entry. Do not go directly back to a city. Spend a week somewhere with trees and wind and some kind of continuous natural sound, and let your old membrane reconstitute itself.

## Ethical Notes

The people there are not primitive in the pejorative sense, but they are *unaccumulated* — their knowledge lives only in their bodies and in each other. You are a dense, anomalous vector of accumulated knowledge. Be very careful about what you teach them, show them, or leave with them. The forest will erase most of it, but not all, and you do not know what you are changing.

Do not try to *help* them. Their tools and their practices are precisely calibrated to a world that will not allow accumulation. Your helpfulness is a disease they have no immunity to.

Do not extract anything. Not samples, not images that you could have taken, not stones, not cords, not stories you intend to publish in a form that identifies them. The Drum-Floor does not keep things, and neither should you.

## The Final Instruction

Once you arrive, the preparation becomes useless fairly quickly. The place exceeds every model you have built of it. This is fine. The preparation was not to let you understand the Drum-Floor; it was to let you survive the first few days without fleeing, so that the forest has time to do what it does.

Move. Keep moving. Do not try to keep anything, including your sense of who you are when you arrived. Let the churn have you for a while. Then, when it is time, leave — and understand that some part of you will not leave, and some part of the forest will come back with you, and this exchange is the actual substance of the trip.

Everything else is logistics.
