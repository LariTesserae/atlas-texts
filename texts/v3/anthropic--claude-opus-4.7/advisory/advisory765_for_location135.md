# Preparing to Visit the Bellows-Grove

## What You're Walking Into

Understand first what this place *is*, because it will shape everything about your preparation: you are visiting a closed system that has achieved equilibrium. Nothing here needs you. Nothing here is expecting you. You are the only variable being introduced to an environment that has spent an unknowable span of time eliminating variables. Pack and behave accordingly.

## Clothing

Wear wool, not synthetics. The Bellows-Grove is warm-humid in a way that will soak cotton in minutes and make synthetics cling and stink; wool keeps functioning wet and won't offend the grove's slow chemistry with off-gassing. A merino base layer, loose trousers you don't mind ruining, and a light wool overshirt. Everything should be in muted browns, mosses, or undyed naturals. Not for camouflage — for courtesy. Bright colors in this light will look obscene, and you will feel it even if nothing else does.

Footwear is the real problem. The peat will swallow boots; every step fills with water within a minute, which tells you the substrate is not walkable in any ordinary sense. Bring tall rubber wellingtons with aggressive tread, and accept that they will come out black and smelling of iron tea for weeks afterward. Do not wear anything with exposed metal eyelets or hooks that can catch on aerial roots as you push through the curtain. A wide-brimmed hat of oiled canvas is worth its weight; the dripping is constant and arrhythmic and will otherwise find every part of your scalp and neck over the course of an hour.

Leave jewelry at home. Anything shiny feels wrong in there. A wedding ring is fine; a wristwatch, especially a ticking one, is not. The grove has its own time and your mechanical seconds will feel, to you, like a small rudeness you are committing against the air.

## Gear

Bring less than you think. This is a place where *every object you carry will feel heavier than it is*. A small daypack, and in it:

- **Water** (more than you expect; the warmth is deceptive and you will sweat even standing still)
- **A dense snack** you can eat without unwrapping loudly — hard cheese, a piece of dark bread, dried fruit in a cloth
- **A notebook and pencil**, not a pen (ink bleeds in this air; graphite is patient)
- **A single small flashlight** for looking up into the canopy, though you will find you rarely want to use it
- **A clean cloth handkerchief**, which you will use constantly
- **A respirator or N95**, not because the air is dangerous, but because the mineral-slate smell from the cylinders is occasionally strong enough to dizzy you, and you want the option

Do not bring: a camera with a shutter, any device that beeps, hand sanitizer (the alcohol smell is wrong here and lingers), perfume, mints, gum. Do not bring a compass expecting it to work; there is ambient magnetism in this place, perhaps from the iron-water, perhaps from the engines' unplaceable driver, and the needle will drift.

A phone, if you bring one, should be off and deep in the bag. You will not want to look at it. If you do look at it, you will feel you have broken something, though nothing visible will have broken.

## Physical Preparation

In the week before you go, practice standing still. This sounds absurd; it is not. The Bellows-Grove rewards stillness and punishes hurry, and most travelers have lost the ability to be stationary for more than ninety seconds without fidgeting. Stand in a quiet room for five minutes, then ten, then twenty. Notice your breathing. This is the skill that will serve you in the grove, because — and this is important — **you will find your breath synchronizing with the great piston, and you should let this happen consciously rather than be startled by it.**

If you have a tendency toward claustrophobia, know that the grove is bounded but not small. Forty paces across is a generous room. The low dome and the closing canopy may still activate the response in sensitive visitors; if so, pause near the entry curtain and let your eyes adjust to the chlorophyll-green light, which is the color your nervous system needs to believe you are outdoors. You are outdoors. You are just inside an outdoors that has grown a roof.

If you have asthma or reactive airways, take your preventive medication that morning. The humidity is total. The air is warmer and wetter than your lungs will expect.

## Mental Preparation

This is where most travelers fail.

The grove is not hostile. It is also not welcoming. It is simply *indifferent at a scale that humans rarely encounter*, and this indifference can feel, to the unprepared mind, like a judgment. It is not a judgment. The flywheel does not know you are there. The piston does not care whether you breathe in time with it or against it. The frog is not watching you. When you feel something has moved behind a trunk — and you will feel this — resist the urge to construct a narrative around it. The grove contains almost nothing and suggests much; this is a property of the place, not a communication from it.

Practice, before you go, the following reorientation: *I am visiting a room that does not need me*. Say it until it stops feeling sad. It is not sad. It is a relief, once you accept it. Most places we visit want something from us — our money, our attention, our appreciation, our maintenance. The Bellows-Grove wants nothing. You are free inside it in a way you are free almost nowhere else.

Be prepared, also, for the possibility that you will not want to leave. This is common and underreported. Something about the matched rhythms, the warm wet air, the sense of a system that has arrived at itself — it induces a state that some visitors have described as "the first real rest of my adult life." Plan your exit in advance. Agree with yourself, before entering, on a duration: two hours is good, three is plenty, four is too many for a first visit. Set no alarm (no alarms in the grove) but trust your body's sense of elapsed time, which will be poor but not absent.

## Etiquette

Do not touch the flywheel. Do not interrupt the bast-belts. These things have been doing what they do for longer than anyone can verify, and while they would likely survive your interference, the grove would notice, and you would feel the noticing.

The brass valve-wheel can be turned. It was placed there to be turned. If you turn it, turn it slowly, one quarter-rotation, and listen for the pitch change, and then turn it back. Do not leave it altered.

The ceramic dial can be looked at but should not be cleaned. The lichen furring its face is not grime; it is part of the instrument now.

The three stone steps leading nowhere are worth sitting on. They are at a good height for a human and were almost certainly placed for this purpose, or the grove has accepted the purpose they found. Sit. Breathe. Watch the flywheel make one full revolution. This takes two minutes. Almost no one in your world has watched a single thing for two uninterrupted minutes in years.

Do not speak aloud unless you must. If you must, speak quietly and only once. Echoes in the grove are strange — absorbed rather than returned — and the sound of your own voice coming back muffled and alone can be unsettling.

Leave nothing behind. Take nothing out. This is the old rule and it holds here with particular force, because the grove has absorbed everything brought into it over however long it has stood, and the balance is stable, and you are not qualified to shift it.

## On the Brass Tag

You will, on your way out, see the tag. You will not be able to read it. Do not attempt to clean it. Do not photograph it hoping to enhance the image later. The tag is not for you. It has been hanging there for a very long time, and whoever it was for is either no longer present or has, as the description suggests, become indistinguishable from the grove itself. The unreadability is the information. Accept it and step through the curtain.

## After

Give yourself a quiet evening. Do not go directly from the Bellows-Grove to anywhere loud, bright, or populated. The transition is harsh in the other direction: your breathing will feel fast for hours, the world will feel dry and urgent and full of demands, and you may find yourself briefly resentful of everything that asks something of you.

This passes. But for the first night back, eat simply, drink water, sleep early. You will dream of the piston's rhythm. This is normal. Some travelers report the rhythm continuing in the chest for days; it is not a medical concern, merely a loan the grove has made to your body, which returns itself on its own schedule.

You will want to go back. Wait at least a season. The grove will still be there. It has arrived at itself, and it is not in a hurry, and neither, now, are you.
