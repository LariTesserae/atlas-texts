# Preparing to Visit the Hollow Pith

## Before You Go

Understand first what kind of place this is. You are not visiting a city. You are visiting an arrangement — a long-held accommodation between a dying thing and the people who live inside it. Every choice you make there will press against that accommodation. Pack and prepare accordingly.

## The Body

**Ears, first and above all.** The Pith is an organ pipe the size of a town, and you are walking into the bell. Bring:

- Deep-seal earplugs, the kind used for industrial work or artillery. Two pairs, because you will lose one.
- Over-ear defenders to layer on top. The locals use resin plugs; you do not have a lifetime of adaptation, and your eardrums are softer than theirs by every measure that matters.
- A way to sleep through sustained low-frequency sound. Bone-conducted vibration will reach you even through the best plugs. Consider that your sleep will be shallow and strange for the duration of your stay. Accept this.

Do not, under any circumstances, remove your ear protection during one of the deep-tone events when the dust rises. The shift in pressure can rupture unprotected tissue. You will feel the vibration in your teeth, your sternum, the soft parts of your abdomen. This is normal. It is not damaging you if you are protected. It will feel like it is.

**Lungs.** The air is breathable but thick — resinous, warm, cedar-sweet. Expect:

- A persistent mild headache for the first two or three days as your body adjusts to the terpene load.
- Dry, productive coughing as your airways clear themselves of deposited resins. This is not illness. It is housekeeping.
- A cloth to press to your face when drinking, as the locals do. Bring several; they clog.

If you have asthma, reactive airways, or any history of resin sensitivity, do not go. There is no water here to flush anything.

**Hydration.** This is the hardest adjustment and the one most travelers get wrong. *There is no water in the Pith.* Not in the walls, not in the plants, not in the dust, not in the bodies of the people. Your water has to come with you, and it has to be concealed or contained in a way that does not offend. A visible waterskin in the Pith is a pornographic object — it displays a substance that does not exist in their world, in quantities that would be, to them, inconceivable wealth or inconceivable waste. Carry your water under clothing, in bladders against the skin, and drink in private.

Drink more than you think you need. The dry air pulls moisture from you constantly and without your noticing. You will not sweat visibly. You will simply desiccate.

**Skin.** The silver dust gets into everything. It is fine, it is slightly alkaline, and it is — this bears remembering — the powdered dead. Cover exposed skin loosely. Do not scrub the dust off in a way that seems disrespectful; brush it, rather, gently, as you would brush flour from a loaf.

## The Mind

Prepare for two specific psychological weights that catch travelers unprepared.

**The sound will wear on you** in a way you cannot predict from reading about it. By the third day you may find yourself weeping without cause, or laughing, or gripped by a flat affect you cannot shake. This is the bell doing its slow work on your nervous system. It will pass when you leave. Do not make major decisions inside the Pith. Do not, especially, decide that you understand the place deeply and want to stay.

**The emptiness will wear on you differently.** Walking a street built for thousands and meeting no one produces a specific kind of loneliness that most human travelers have never experienced — not the loneliness of wilderness, but the loneliness of a party after everyone has left. The Pith is always that party. Some travelers find this unbearable after a week. Some find it the most beautiful thing they have ever felt. You will not know which kind of traveler you are until you are there.

## Communication

Learn the forearm-tap language before you go, at least to the level of courtesy phrases: *greeting, thank you, I mean no harm, I am leaving, I am sorry*. A tutor who has been to the Pith can teach you the basics in a few weeks. The grammar is simple; the rhythm is everything. Tap too fast and you read as panicked. Tap too slow and you read as condescending.

Do not speak aloud in the Pith. Not because it is forbidden, but because it is useless — no voice carries — and because it marks you, instantly, as someone who has not understood where they are. The locals will be patient with this once. They will be less patient the second time.

During the rare silences, **do not make sound.** Those minutes belong to the dwellers. What they release into the quiet is something they have been holding, sometimes for months. Your cough, your exclamation, your delighted laugh at the novelty of hearing — any of these is a theft from a communal act you were not invited into. Stand still. Keep your mouth closed. Let them have it.

## The Etiquette of Dust

You are walking on ancestors. The locals do this too; it is not sacred ground in the sense of being forbidden. But there are gradients:

- Lanes swept clean are lanes of frequent use. Walk these freely.
- Lanes where the dust has deepened are lanes being allowed to rest. You may cross them. Do not linger, do not sit, do not disturb the depth of the dust with idle motion.
- Never kick dust. Never scuff it deliberately. Never take any with you. A traveler who smuggles a vial of silver dust off-world has committed a theft that the Pith has no word for, and for which your intuition is the only available sanction. Do not.

When you leave the Pith for the last time, it is customary — not required, but noticed — to stand for a moment at the edge of the bowl and let whatever dust has settled on your clothes fall back. You are returning what belonged to the place.

## The Shrine and the Chamber

You will want to see the thin-walled chamber. Everyone does. The impulse to press your face to that wall and glimpse the thing beyond is nearly irresistible, and it is precisely the impulse you must master.

**Do not enter the chamber.** The dwellers do not, and their forbearance is the oldest agreement in the city. A visitor who violates it — even once, even briefly, even respectfully — does damage to a thing held together by nothing but the holding. If you cannot trust yourself in proximity to that chamber, do not go up to the gallery at all.

You may approach the shrine. You may leave an offering if you wish: a crystal flake, a pod-husk, a length of braided fiber. Offerings from off-world are accepted but should be small, dry, and of materials that would not distress the cavity if they remained there for a thousand years. No metal. No plastic. Nothing wet. Nothing that smells.

The offering is not to the thing beyond the wall. It is to the wall. Remember this when you place it. You are thanking the husk for continuing to hold. If this gesture feels absurd to you, you are not ready to make it, and you should not.

## What to Bring Back

Memory. Notes, if you can write without disturbing. No physical object from the Pith — not dust, not a crystal flake, not a fallen pod-husk, not a fiber from a discarded rope. The Pith is a closed system that has reached its accommodation by losing nothing and gaining nothing for a very long time. You are an exception it tolerates. Do not abuse the toleration.

## One Last Thing

The seed will germinate eventually. It has not, it has not for so long that *not yet* is the shape of the world there, but eventually it will, and the city will end, and every person you meet and every wall you touch and every lane you walk will be torn open into whatever is outside.

You know this. They do not, or they know it only as the sound tells them, which is to say they know it the way we know we will die — distantly, abstractly, as a fact that does not interrupt the afternoon.

Do not tell them. Do not hint. Do not ask questions that lead them toward the knowledge. This is not your gift to give, and it is not a gift.

Enter gently. Leave gently. The amber light will stay with you for months after you return, and you will find yourself, in quiet rooms, listening for a tone that is not there.
