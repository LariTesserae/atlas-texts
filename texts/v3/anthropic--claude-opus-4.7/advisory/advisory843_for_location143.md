# Preparing to Visit the Condensery

## What you are preparing for

Understand first what kind of place this is, because your preparation depends on it. The Condensery is not hostile. It is not even indifferent in the way a desert or an ocean is indifferent — those places will kill you casually. The Condensery will not kill you casually. It will absorb you, slowly, with the same patience it absorbs a forgotten teacup. The danger here is not acute. It is ambient, and it works on timescales that a human body and a human mind are poorly equipped to notice before it is too late.

Prepare accordingly. Prepare for a place that is trying, without malice, to make you part of itself.

## Footwear

This is the single most important item. The pipe-plain is wet metal, curved, irregular, and upholstered in living things that have spent centuries perfecting the art of clinging to wet metal. You will not.

Bring boots with aggressive lugged soles in a compound rated for wet rock — climbing-approach boots, or the sort of thing shellfish harvesters wear. Studded soles are better. Felt soles, of the kind Japanese river-fishermen use, are best of all: the felt grips the lichen-slime in a way rubber cannot. Bring at least two pairs, because whatever touches the pipes will begin to be colonized, and after a week your boots will be growing things. You want to be able to rotate them and let one pair dry, though nothing really dries here.

Gaiters to the knee. You will be wading, often without meaning to — the shallow basins are mirror-still and invisible until you're standing in them.

## Clothing

Dress for a sauna that never ends. The ambient temperature is body-warm and the humidity is effectively one hundred percent. You cannot sweat; sweat has nowhere to go. This is more dangerous than it sounds. Humans rely on evaporative cooling, and in an environment where evaporation does not occur, moderate exertion can bring on heatstroke with very little warning.

Wear the thinnest, loosest, most breathable fabrics you own. Linen. Technical fabrics designed for tropical expedition. Avoid anything that traps moisture against your skin — which is to say, avoid most things. A broad-brimmed hat, because although the light is mild, it is also constant, and you will be under it for as long as you stay.

Bring more changes of clothes than you think you need. Whatever you wear will be damp within an hour and will stay damp, and damp cloth against warm skin is where the lichens will try you first.

## Skin and body

This is where most travelers underestimate the place. Every wet surface in the Condensery is colonized, and you are a wet surface. Within days, without countermeasures, you will begin to host passengers. Not dangerously — the organisms here are adapted to metal, not meat — but persistently, and the sensation of being slowly upholstered is one that even experienced multiverse travelers report as psychologically corrosive.

Bring:

- A strong antifungal soap and use it daily, everywhere, without exception
- Talcum powder, large quantities, for the folds and creases where moisture pools
- Nail clippers and a stiff brush — things will try to live under your nails
- A medical-grade barrier cream for your feet especially, which will be wet the entire visit
- Sealed dry-bags for anything you want to keep dry, which means everything

Check yourself, carefully, in a mirror, every evening. Look for orange spots, for pale threads, for anything that wasn't there yesterday. Early colonization wipes off with soap. Established colonization requires a dermatologist in a drier world.

## Water and food

Do not drink the standing water, no matter how clear. The shrimp-things are almost certainly harmless, but "almost certainly" is not a standard you want to apply to your own gut flora in a place with no medical infrastructure. Bring all your water. Budget generously — the humidity fools you into thinking you are hydrated when you are not.

Food should be sealed, shelf-stable, and eaten quickly once opened. Anything left out will be sampled by something within minutes and claimed within hours. There is no refrigeration problem because there is no refrigeration; everything sits at blood-warmth forever.

## Navigation

The Condensery has no landmarks in the ordinary sense. The pipe-plain looks the same in every direction, the sky gives you no time of day, and the light does not change. Experienced visitors have become disoriented within hours of their landing site and spent days recovering their bearings, or not recovering them.

Bring:

- A GPS or equivalent that works in the local cosmology, and backups
- A physical compass, with the understanding that the ferrous content of the pipes may confound it
- Brightly colored flagging tape, to mark your route — though be aware the tape will be lichen-covered within a year if you don't collect it on your return
- A good map if one exists, and the awareness that even a good map will be outdated in its details because although the large structure is static, the small wet life rearranges the surface continuously

Keep a logbook. Write down what you did each day, with timestamps from a reliable clock. Without external cues, your circadian rhythm will drift, and your sense of elapsed time will become unreliable within a week. Travelers have reported believing they had stayed three days when they had stayed three weeks.

## The sound problem

The silence of the Condensery is the thing most visitors are unprepared for. It is not a quiet place; it is a place that has been quiet, continuously, for a very long time, and the quality of long-established silence is different from the quality of ordinary silence. You will find yourself whispering without meaning to. You will find yourself reluctant to set down metal objects. You will find the sound of your own voice embarrassing.

This is manageable but you should know it is coming. Bring a small music player with headphones for your tent, if only to prove to yourself that sound still exists. Do not play music out loud on the pipe-plain. It feels, in a way difficult to articulate, like a kind of vandalism.

## Sleep and shelter

Bring a freestanding tent — you cannot drive stakes. A tent with a sealed floor and a fine mesh to keep out the smaller wet things. A sleeping pad with significant R-value, not for insulation but to keep you off the warm metal, because sleeping directly on the pipes means waking up colonized.

Sleep will be difficult. The constant warmth, the constant light, the constant low hum — none of these are signals your body knows how to interpret as "night." Bring a proper sleep mask, earplugs (though there is nothing to block out, the ritual helps), and be prepared to rely on melatonin or equivalent for the duration of your visit.

## Duration

Decide in advance how long you will stay, and stick to it. This is the single most important psychological preparation.

The Condensery is a finished place, and finished places exert a pull on the unfinished mind. Travelers who plan to stay a week have stayed a month. Travelers who plan to stay a month have been retrieved by search parties six months later, calm, uninjured, and unable to explain clearly why they did not leave. They say things like *I was going to leave tomorrow* and mean it, and have meant it every day for a hundred days.

Something about the equilibrium of the place — the sense that nothing further needs to happen, ever — communicates itself. It is restful in a way that is indistinguishable, at first, from peace, and only gradually reveals itself as a kind of cessation. Set a hard date for your departure. Tell someone in a world with clocks. Arrange, if you can, for retrieval rather than self-extraction.

## What to bring for its own sake

A good book. A notebook. A small, warm, non-essential ritual — a particular tea, a particular stretch, a particular song you sing in your tent at what you have decided is bedtime. These are anchors. The Condensery will try, gently, to dissolve your sense of yourself as a person with a life that is going somewhere. The ritual is how you remember you are.

Bring something that decays visibly on human timescales. A piece of bread, a cut flower. Watch it change. The Condensery does not change; watching something change will help you remember that you do, and that you are supposed to.

## What not to bring

Do not bring anything you are unwilling to lose. The place is not thievish — nothing will take your things — but the place is slow, and anything you set down and forget will become part of the continent faster than you think. Do not bring heirlooms. Do not bring photographs of people you love. Something about leaving a photograph of your grandmother on the pipe-plain, even for an hour, even retrieved, feels wrong in a way you will not be able to articulate but will not be able to unfeel.

Do not bring pets. Do not bring children. Do not bring the dying, even those who have asked to go somewhere peaceful. The Condensery is peaceful in the way that a sealed jar is peaceful, and it is not the right kind of peace for anyone who has a remaining question about their own life.

## Final preparation

Before you go, sit somewhere in your home world — a loud café, a crowded train, a park with children — and pay close attention to it. Fix the texture of unfinished life in your memory. Notice how things are in the middle of becoming other things. Notice how people interrupt each other. Notice how the light changes.

You are going to a place where none of this happens. You will want to have it to come back to, and you will want, while you are there, to remember clearly that it exists.

Then go. Walk carefully. Listen to the eels. Do not stay too long.
