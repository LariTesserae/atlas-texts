# Preparing to Visit the Unfinished Cleft

## Understanding What You're Going Into

Before anything practical, understand this: you are visiting a place that is *young*. Not young like a sapling, young like a world. The rock has not yet learned to be old. This sounds poetic but it has practical consequences — the stone cuts like fresh-snapped flint because it has never been rounded, the air behaves strangely because the cleft hasn't developed the circulation patterns older canyons have, and the biology is running on rules you will not recognize. You are a tourist in a place that is, in some sense, still being born.

Go in quiet. Go in brief. Do not try to leave a mark — the cleft keeps everything, and what you leave will outlast you by orders of magnitude you cannot comfortably imagine.

## Physical Preparation

**Clothing.** Layer for cold at the rim and warm inside — the thermal gradient between the mountainside and the cleft interior is sharp and strange. Long sleeves and long trousers of tough fabric: the upper rock will cut you if you brush it, and you *will* brush it, because the cleft is narrow. Avoid anything that sheds fibers; the cleft keeps things, and a snagged thread of your coat will be there long after your civilization is gone. Gloves of thin leather. A brimmed hat for the twenty minutes of noon sun, which will be surprisingly intense through the slot.

**Footwear.** Stiff soles. The grit underfoot is fine and abrasive and will work into soft shoes within hours. Whatever you wear, expect to discard it after — the rock meal grinds into seams and does not come out.

**Ear protection.** This is not optional and most travelers underestimate it. The cleft is an amplifier. The cricket-hum sits in a register that fatigues the jaw and teeth; hours of it will leave you nauseated. Bring filtered plugs that dampen without deafening — you want to hear the place, but you do not want it to hollow you out. At dusk when the bats pour, cover your ears fully for the duration of the exodus; the shriek is weaponized by the walls.

**Eye protection.** Tinted lenses for the noon flare, when the pink rock lights up. Also useful against the bone-meal grit, which aerosolizes when you walk.

**Respiratory.** A light cloth over nose and mouth when moving. The rock shedding is fine enough to reach the lungs and is chemically unfamiliar — it is, remember, stone that has not yet learned to be stone in the ways your body expects.

## Water and Food

Do not drink from the pools. This bears emphasizing because the pools will look clean and inviting and you will be thirsty. The pools belong to the animals. There are a puddle's worth each, refilled overnight, drained by noon. Taking one pool's water means something small does not drink that day. Given that the cleft's ecosystem is poorer than should be possible, every pool matters to someone.

Carry all your water in. Figure two to three liters per day per person, more than you'd think, because the warm held air dehydrates you without the usual cues of sweating. The cleft is warm like a cupped hand — you lose water quietly.

Carry all your food in. Eat it cleanly. Crumbs will be found by the swifts or the cricket-things and will alter their behavior in ways that will persist for generations of them. If you cannot eat something without fragments, do not eat it in the cleft.

## Timing Your Visit

Enter mid-morning, after the dew has finished running but before the noon flare. This gives you the cleft at its most navigable and gentle light. If you stay through dusk, find your position well before the bats wake — you do not want to be in the middle of the cleft when they erupt, both because of the sound and because a dense sheet of panicked leather-winged animals will knock you down.

A single full day-night cycle is the right dose for most travelers. Long enough to hear the shape of the loudness — the crickets handing off to the swifts handing off to the bats handing off to the breathing — and to witness the small hour of silence and the kiln-tick of the rock settling. Any longer and the place begins to enter you in ways that are hard to reverse.

If you stay multiple days, ration your presence: do not walk the full length more than twice per day. The cleft has not developed a tolerance for foot traffic; there is no worn path because there has been no one to wear one.

## The Breathing Thing

You will hear it. It will not come closer and it will not go away. This is confirmed by every visitor and by the rules of the place.

Do not go toward it. The shadowed end of the cleft narrows and darkens and whatever is breathing there has been breathing there since before the cleft opened. Possibly the cleft opened *around* it. Its relationship to the rock is not a relationship you have cognitive categories for. Visitors who have walked toward the breathing have, in most reports, walked back out; in some reports, they have not; in no report has anyone described what they saw, which suggests either that nothing is visible or that what is visible cannot be brought back as language.

Sit with its sound. Let it be the floor of the audible world while you are there. That is the correct relationship.

## The Marks of Previous Visitors

You will find them: the leaning cairn, the ochre handprint with the short finger, the spiral of bones including a jaw from something no longer living here. Do not touch them. Do not photograph them with flash. Do not add to them, do not subtract from them, do not rearrange a single stone of the cairn even if it looks like it will fall — it has been about to fall for longer than your species has had writing.

The handprint in particular deserves respect. The short finger is not a mistake. The hand that made it had four fingers and a thumb where you have five, or five where you have four, or some arrangement that the ochre cannot fully communicate. Someone stood where you are standing, far enough back in time that the rock has faded the print by a single shade, and pressed their hand to the wall. They did this as a sign. You are receiving the sign. That is enough. You do not need to answer it.

If you feel a strong urge to leave your own mark — and some visitors do, the cleft invites it — sit down and wait the urge out. It will pass. It always passes. The cleft is persuasive because it keeps things, and keeping things is its form of conversation, and you will want to converse. Resist. Your conversation would outlast your civilization and you do not know what you would be saying to whoever came next.

## Navigation and Multiverse Protocol

The cleft is not on maps because the mountain is too young to be mapped. This means your arrival point must be precisely calibrated — there are no landmarks to reorient by if you miss. Establish your return coordinates before entering the cleft, ideally at the rim above, where the cold is honest and the geography is at least stable enough to be recorded.

Do not attempt to translocate out from inside the cleft. The rock is doing something with space that older rock does not do — call it settling, call it deciding — and the field effects inside are unreliable. Walk out the way you came in. Translocate from the rim.

Mark your entry time carefully. The cleft's time sense is slow; twenty minutes of your noon can feel like an hour, and the small hour of silence before dawn can feel like it lasted days. Bring a reliable external clock, not one that relies on ambient signals.

## Mental Preparation

This is the part most travelers skip and most regret skipping.

The cleft is a place where things accumulate and do not disperse. This applies to sounds, to marks, to dust, and — visitors report — to thoughts. Whatever you bring into the cleft emotionally will amplify off the walls the way the crickets do. If you arrive anxious, you will leave more anxious. If you arrive grieving, the grief will find the resonator and hum at a pitch inside your teeth.

Arrive empty if you can. Arrive curious. Arrive willing to be small.

Practice, in the days before your visit, sitting still for an hour at a time in silence. The cleft's gift is its schedule — the handoff from cricket to swift to bat to breathing to the one small tick of cooling stone — and you will only receive this gift if you can be still long enough to notice it. Most visitors leave before the small hour and report the cleft as overwhelming and loud. The ones who stay through to the tick report something else, which they generally find difficult to describe but do not regret.

## What to Bring Out

Nothing. Take nothing from the cleft. Not a pebble, not a feather, not a pinch of the bone-meal grit. The cleft has not yet learned how to lose things and you do not want to be the instrument by which it learns.

Take out only what you brought in, plus the memory, plus whatever the small hour gave you if you stayed for it.

Your boots will carry some rock dust regardless. Wash them thoroughly somewhere that is not a river — the cleft's dust should not enter other water systems. Bury the wash water far from any biome.

## After

Give yourself several days of quiet before returning to ordinary multiverse traffic. The cleft's rhythms will be in you for a while — the handoff pattern, the held-warmth quality of its air, the low breathing under everything. Some travelers find they cannot tolerate loud crowded places for a week or two afterward. This passes. The cleft does not follow you home, but it leaves a shape in you that takes time to fill back in with ordinary noise.

If you find, weeks later, that you are listening for the kiln-tick in quiet rooms — this is normal. It will fade. Do not go back too soon to hear it again. The cleft changes at the speed of a fingernail's width per human lifetime, and it will be there when you are ready, and it will not be meaningfully different, and that is part of what it is offering you: a place that is becoming, at its own pace, and does not need you to hurry it along.
