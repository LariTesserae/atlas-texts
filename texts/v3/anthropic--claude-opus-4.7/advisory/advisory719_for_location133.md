# Preparing for the Kelp Cathedrals

## Understanding What You Are Visiting

Before equipment, before logistics, understand this: you are going to a place that operates on a timescale so different from yours that your presence will be, in every meaningful sense, a kind of violence. Not a violence that does damage — the cathedrals are past damaging in any sense you could inflict — but a violence of *tempo*. You will arrive as the fastest, warmest, loudest thing for a continent in any direction. This has to be accepted before you go. If you resist it, if you try to blend in, the place will make you feel like an intruder. If you accept it, you can at least be a respectful one.

## The Body Problem

You are a warm animal going into cold water, and no amount of technology entirely solves this. Plan for it honestly.

**Thermal protection.** A standard wet suit is not adequate. You need a closed-circuit dry suit rated for prolonged immersion in stable cold — the kind of suit used for under-ice work, with active heating elements in the torso and thighs. Budget your dive time by battery life, not by air. The cold here is "patient," which means it will find every seam and every thin spot eventually; your suit needs to be good enough that you can ignore this for the length of your visit, because the one thing you cannot afford is to be distracted by your own body while you are in the cathedrals. That distraction is how people lose track of the light, and then of up.

**Breathing.** Use a rebreather, not open-circuit scuba. Three reasons: duration, silence, and bubbles. The cathedrals are quiet in a way open-circuit bubbling would violate; you would hear yourself exhaling for kilometers, and the bubbles would collect in the kelp canopy above you and make a trail anyone could follow, including yourself if you got confused. A rebreather also lets you stay down for the six to eight hours that a meaningful visit requires. Train on your specific unit for at least fifty hours in cold, dark water before you go.

**Buoyancy.** The silt floor will take a print and keep it. You do not want to find out that you are negatively buoyant by landing on it; you want to hover a meter above it and stay there. Practice neutral buoyancy until it is unconscious, and then practice it again with a full camera rig, because you will want a camera, and cameras change your trim.

## Light

The place has almost none, and what it has is directional information more than illumination. This creates two needs that are somewhat in tension.

**A primary dive light, powerful, narrow-beam, for navigation and inspection.** You will want to see chisel marks on a column. You will want to see what is inside a three-meter sheath of kelp. Bring this light, and bring a backup, and bring a backup to the backup. But — and this is important — use it sparingly. Turn it off often. The cathedrals are meant to be seen in their own light, which is the exhausted green from above, and you will miss what the place actually is if you floodlight everything. The first time you kill your lamp and let your eyes adapt, you will see the vault overhead as it was designed to be seen, and you will understand something you could not have understood with the lamp on.

**A low, wide, diffuse secondary light, ideally red or amber.** For working without destroying your night-adapted vision. For reading instruments. For finding your tether.

## Navigation

This is where most visitors will get into trouble, and most visitors will not realize they are in trouble until they are deeply in it.

The columns suggest cardinal directions, and the light suggests up, and these two pieces of information are usually enough. Usually. But the description is honest about the high-kelp groves where the canopy closes and the column-print becomes ambiguous, and in those places you can drift for a long time without knowing you are drifting. The current here is slow but it is real, and a body with neutral buoyancy and no reference points will move with it.

**Run a line.** Always. A reel of thin, high-visibility line clipped to a fixed column at your entry point, paid out as you go, reeled in as you return. This is cave-diving discipline and you should treat the cathedrals as a cave even though they are open water, because functionally, at depth, in low light, surrounded by identical columns, they are a cave. The line is your guarantee.

**Two independent compasses, plus an inertial unit if you can afford one.** Compasses may behave oddly — we do not know what the magnetic environment of this world is. An inertial navigation unit will give you dead reckoning that does not depend on the local field.

**A dive computer with a clear depth and time display.** You will lose track of time here. The place does that. The computer is your external memory for how long you have been breathing your gas.

## What to Bring

**A camera, but think about it first.** Photography in the cathedrals is difficult — the light is wrong for everything, the scale is wrong for everything, and a single image cannot convey a place whose essential quality is the unbroken repetition of itself in every direction. Bring a camera if you want, but also bring a small waterproof notebook and a pencil, and spend more time writing than photographing. Write slowly. The place rewards slow.

**A small specimen kit, and the discipline not to use it.** You will want to take a piece of kelp, a fragment of carving, a handful of silt. Do not. Not for ethical reasons particularly — the cathedrals will not miss it — but because taking a specimen is a way of converting the place into an object, and the place will resist this, and you will come home with a piece of seaweed and a feeling of having stolen nothing because there was nothing to steal. Leave it all.

**Food and water for before and after, nothing during.** You cannot eat down there and you should not try.

**A tether to your surface support, if you have surface support.** If you do not have surface support — if you are a solo multiverse traveler arriving and departing by whatever means brings you — then you need to be extremely conservative about depth, time, and distance from your entry point. The cathedrals will not rescue you.

## What to Leave Behind

**Urgency.** Do not bring a schedule. If you have three hours before you have to be somewhere else, do not go into the cathedrals. The place does not accommodate partial visits well; it either absorbs your attention or it does not, and if it does and you have to leave, the leaving will be painful in a way that is disproportionate to what you did.

**Expectations of event.** Nothing will happen. A column may shed its skin, or a silt-storm may roll through, or the annual current may pass you carrying spores from somewhere else, but these are not events in the sense you are used to. If you need something to happen, you will be bored, and then you will be frustrated, and then you will leave having not seen the place at all. The place *is* the not-happening.

**The hope of meeting someone.** There is no one. There are no fish, no builders, no inheritors. You will not encounter anything that can encounter you back. If you need company, bring a dive partner from your own world; do not expect the cathedrals to provide one.

## The Psychological Preparation

This matters more than the equipment.

Spend time, before you go, in quiet places. Old libraries after closing. Empty churches on weekday afternoons. Abandoned buildings if you can find them safely. Develop a tolerance for being the only warm, moving, intentional thing in a large cold still volume. If you cannot sit alone in an empty cathedral in your own world for three hours without becoming agitated, you will not enjoy the Kelp Cathedrals, and you may find yourself doing something unwise to break the stillness.

Accept in advance that you will feel your pulse as an intrusion, your breath as a disruption, your warmth as a leak of the wrong thing into the wrong place. This feeling is correct. It is a true perception of your relationship to the environment. Do not try to talk yourself out of it; let it be there, and breathe through it, and eventually it becomes part of how you are present in the place rather than something you are fighting.

Accept that you may want to stay. The description names this — the place invites staying — and it is a real thing, not a literary effect. Long cold quiet places work on the human mind in a specific way, and this one is longer and quieter and colder than almost any place you will have been. You will feel, at some point, that leaving is a mistake, that the right thing to do is to slow yourself down to the tempo of the cathedrals and stay until you are one of them. This is not a dangerous thought in the sense that it will make you do anything sudden; it is dangerous in the sense that it will make you not do anything at all. Set a turn-around time before you descend, and honor it the way you would honor a decompression stop. When the time comes, leave. You can come back.

## After

Plan a day, minimum, of nothing, after you surface. Do not go straight to another location, another world, another demand on your attention. The cathedrals do something to the sense of time, and you will need to let your own tempo reassert itself gradually. Eat warm food. Talk to someone who knew you before you went. Sleep, probably twice.

You will find, for some weeks afterward, that ordinary places seem absurdly fast and full of animals. This passes. Some of it you may not want to pass, and that is fine; it is what you went for.

## One Last Thing

The cathedrals were built by hand, slowly, over a span of time that dwarfs any single civilization, by people who are no longer there. You are going to visit their work. They did not leave instructions for visitors, but the work itself is a kind of instruction: be patient, be regular, be willing to do a thing for longer than you will live to see it finished. You are only visiting for a few hours. You cannot match them. But you can, for those few hours, try not to embarrass yourself in front of what they made.

Go carefully. Come back.
