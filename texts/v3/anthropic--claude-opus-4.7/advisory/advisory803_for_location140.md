# Preparing to Visit the Glasshouse in the Spine

## Understand What You Are Going To Do

You are not going to a destination. You are going to witness a thing that does not require witnessing. This distinction matters more than any item of gear, and if you cannot hold it in your mind before you start climbing, the altitude will not help you hold it at the top. Sit with it. The Glasshouse is not waiting for you. It is not indifferent in a sullen way, the way a bored shopkeeper is indifferent; it is indifferent the way a glacier is indifferent. You will be the event. Nothing there will be.

Travelers who arrive expecting revelation tend to come down worse than travelers who arrive expecting nothing. Arrive expecting nothing. The place will give you what it gives you.

## The Altitude and the Air

This is the part most multiverse travelers underestimate, because the strangeness of the room distracts from the ordinariness of the ascent. The ascent will kill you long before the room will.

You are going to an elevation where the sky has turned the color of old iron. That is not a poetic detail — that is a description of light scattering through an atmosphere thin enough to be approaching the threshold where unacclimatized humans lose consciousness. Prepare as you would for a high Himalayan summit, minimum. Acclimatize in stages across at least a week, preferably two. If your multiverse transit deposits you directly at altitude, bring supplemental oxygen and use it; do not try to prove anything.

The dryness is the second killer, and it is subtler. Air that preserves paper and corpses will desiccate your lungs, your eyes, your sinuses. You will not feel thirsty in the way you expect. You will simply begin to fail. Drink on a schedule, not on thirst. Bring more water than you think you need and then double it. A humidifying mask — even a cloth kept damp inside a breathing cover — will spare you days of recovery later.

Bring lip balm. Bring it in a quantity that embarrasses you. Your lips will crack and bleed at rest.

## Clothing

Mountaineering layers, cold-weather rated to well below freezing. The cold "presses in from every side" — this is not decorative language. The stone is cold, the air is cold, the wind where it reaches you will be cold in a dry way that steals heat faster than damp cold does. Your extremities are the concern. Mittens over liner gloves, because you will want bare-fingered moments at the pane and you will want to recover from them.

Bring a coat you can sit still in for a long time. You will want to sit still for a long time. Most mountaineering gear is designed for movement-generated warmth; you need insulation that works when you are not generating any.

## Finding the Cleft

You will not find it unless you know. This is stated plainly and should be taken plainly. Before you go, acquire whatever coordinates, sketches, waymarks, or descriptions you can. If you are relying on intuition or a vague pointing-gesture from someone who has been, write down everything they say before you start climbing, because the altitude will eat your memory of the conversation.

The ledge is at an elevation where the stone rings like a bell underfoot. Listen for this. It is a real acoustic phenomenon in certain dry dense rocks at low pressure, and it is a landmark. When the ground begins to ring, you are close.

The drone is also a landmark, but only inside a certain radius. Below that radius you will mistake it for wind. Above it you will hear it for what it is. There is a transition zone of perhaps a hundred meters where the sound and its absence overlap in a way that will disorient you. Do not trust your sense of direction in this zone. Move slowly.

## Things to Bring Into the Room

**A light.** The room is dim "as a late hour in a library." A headlamp with a red filter or a warm low-lumen setting is better than a harsh white beam. You will want to see the veins of small leaves. A harsh light will not help you and will feel, in that room, like shouting.

**A tuning fork, or a string, or a small instrument.** The drone has a pitch. The text tells you plainly: you can tune a string to it, if you brought a string. Bring a string. Bring a tuning fork in a known pitch, so that when you descend and cannot remember the drone's frequency, you will at least know what it was *not*. This is the closest thing to a souvenir the place permits.

**A notebook.** Not to record the place — the place resists recording in ways you will discover — but to record yourself in the place. What you felt. What you noticed. Whether your hand was warm on the pane or whether it was only the pane that was warm. These notes will matter later, when the pitch has left you.

**A small offering, if you are the sort who offers things.** The ledge already holds a brass fitting, a notebook with a pressed leaf, a cup, a cloth. These are not a shrine. They are not arranged. They are simply what previous visitors left. If you leave something, leave something that will endure in that dryness — metal, stone, paper, bone. Do not leave food. Do not leave anything organic that was once alive elsewhere, unless you mean the gesture of the leaf-on-page-one, and understand what that gesture is.

**Do not bring seeds. Do not bring spores. Do not bring soil on your boots.** Clean your gear before the final approach. The air inside the pane has been sterile for centuries or longer. You will not breach the pane — but you may be tempted to press against it harder than is wise, or to set something against it, and the warmth of your body and the cold of the stone have created, over millennia, microscopic stresses in the seal that you do not want to be the one who completes. Also: contamination of the ledge itself, though it will not affect the forest, feels wrong in a way you will understand when you are there.

## Things Not to Do

**Do not try to stop the engine, or restart it, or touch its flywheels.** They are locked in positions suggesting motion. The suggestion is all the motion that is permitted. You will feel, briefly, that you could nudge one — that the whole apparatus is waiting for the small push that sets it running again. This feeling is wrong. The engine is already running. The drone is its running. The stillness of the flywheels is a different layer of the same machine.

**Do not try to open the pane.** You will not be able to, but you may try, and trying will make you feel ashamed in a way that will stay with you longer than you expect.

**Do not speak loudly.** Not for any mystical reason. The room is small and the drone fills it, and your voice inside that drone will sound wrong to you and you will not enjoy the wrongness.

**Do not stay past your physical limits.** The place will let you stay forever. It has no investment in your departure. The cold will work through your coat; the dryness will work through your lungs; the altitude will work through your judgment. Set a timer. Set two. When the timer goes, leave, even if you feel you have not finished. You will not have finished. No one finishes. The forest did the finishing, long ago, and there is no equivalent act available to you.

## The Pane

When you put your palm against the pane, it will be warm. Expect this and prepare for it anyway, because expecting it will not prepare you for it.

The warmth is the thing most visitors carry down. Not the forest, not the drone, not the light — the warmth of that one surface in a world of cold. It will feel, briefly, as if something on the other side of the glass knows you are there and has warmed itself in greeting. This is not true. The warmth is thermodynamic; the engine runs, the vapor cycles, the enclosure holds at the temperature the plants require, which is roughly the temperature of a human body because many things in many universes converge on that temperature for reasons of chemistry rather than affection.

Knowing this will not change the feeling. Let the feeling happen. Do not argue with it. Do not mistake it for communication.

## The Descent

The drone will follow you for about a hundred meters down the scree and then stop. When it stops, stop walking, briefly, and notice that it has stopped. This is important. If you do not notice the stopping, you will spend the rest of your descent unsure whether the drone is still with you, faintly, at the edge of hearing. It is not. It ended. Mark the ending.

Below a certain altitude you will lose the exact pitch. You cannot prevent this. You can, if you brought a tuning fork or a recording device, preserve an approximation — but the approximation will not be the pitch, and you will know it is not the pitch, and this knowing is part of what you came for, whether or not you knew it when you set out.

The text calls this a mercy or a theft. It is both. Prepare for it to be both. Some travelers mourn the loss for years. Some are grateful. Most are both, in alternation, for the rest of their lives.

## After

Do not try to describe the Glasshouse to people who have not been. You will fail, and the failing will make the memory thinner. Describe it, if at all, to people who have been somewhere like it — there are analogues in other places, other universes; you will recognize the look in their eyes.

Keep the notebook. Reread it rarely. The notes will seem inadequate, but they are the only thing that persists, and inadequacy is the correct register for anything written about that room.

If you find yourself, years later, humming a pitch that you cannot quite place, do not chase it. It is not the drone. The drone is not available to you anymore. What you are humming is the shape of the absence where the drone used to be, and that shape is yours now, and it is enough.
