# Preparing to Visit the Warmwater Conurbation

## Before You Go: Understanding What You're Walking Into

The first thing to internalize is that this is not a hostile environment, but it is a *saturated* one. Every sense will be occupied continuously from the moment you arrive. You will not have the luxury of a quiet baseline against which to notice new things. You must arrive already calibrated, because the city will not give you time to calibrate on arrival.

Spend at least two weeks beforehand training yourself to sleep through noise. Not just background noise — layered, structured, meaningful noise. Sleep with recordings of crowded markets, of industrial plants, of harbors. If you can still think clearly with all of it running, you are approaching ready. The standing roar of the Conurbation is not something you will tune out in a night; people report it taking a full week, and during that week you will be mildly dissociated, exhausted, and prone to poor decisions. Plan your itinerary accordingly — your first seven days should involve no commitments, no important transactions, no attempts to understand anything.

## Clothing and Body

Wear nothing that cannot get wet and stay wet. This is not a place where you occasionally cross water; you are in water, at varying depths, continuously. Your footwear is the single most important thing you bring. You want something that grips travertine and glassy pebble, drains instantly, has a sole thick enough that you cannot feel individual stones through it, and extends at least to mid-calf to keep filaments from wrapping your ankles when you stand still. Nothing that absorbs. Nothing that takes longer than a minute to dry when lifted out of the water. Bring three pairs. You will lose one and ruin one.

Your legs will be warm and wet for the entire duration of your visit. This produces problems that temperate-climate travelers do not anticipate: softening of the skin, loss of nail integrity, a particular kind of fatigue in the hip flexors from wading rather than walking. Toughen your feet for months in advance by going barefoot on rough surfaces. Keep toenails very short. Accept that your legs below the knee will look, when you leave, as though they belong to someone else.

Above the waist, wear loose light layers in materials that breathe. The air is warm and near-saturated; anything that traps sweat against the skin will chafe you raw within a day. Cotton is acceptable. Synthetic technical fabrics are often better. Bring a hat with a brim wide enough to keep condensation off your eyelashes — the mist constantly recondenses on any horizontal surface, including your face, and after some hours of this you will understand why the locals all wear something overhead.

Do not bring jewelry or anything with exposed metal fittings. The air is doing chemistry you cannot see, and while it is gentle with flesh it is not gentle with alloys. Copper will turn. Silver will blacken in days. Steel will pit.

## The Air and the Healing

You will notice, within hours, that small wounds stop bleeding faster than they should. By the next morning, cuts have closed. This is not something being done to you; it is the ambient state of the air. Do not fight it, but also do not test it. The air's tolerances have not been characterized by anyone you can consult. A small cut will heal; a deep wound is still a deep wound, and you will still need to manage it conventionally.

The more unsettling consequence is that you do not know what else the air is doing to you. Travelers report subtle shifts in mood, in dream content, in the sense of time passing. Some of this is the noise and the constant pearled light disrupting circadian rhythm. Some of it may not be. If you have strong reasons to keep your internal state stable — medication regimes, psychiatric considerations, cognitive work you need to remain capable of — consider whether this is your destination at all. The city breathes through its people. It will breathe through you.

Do not attempt to filter the air. You cannot. The working things are small enough to pass through anything you could carry, and in any case the whole enterprise of being in the city requires that you let its atmosphere in. A person in a sealed suit would be an object the city could not process, and would be treated as such — routed around, ignored, eventually disassembled for parts in some sense you would find deeply unpleasant.

## Food and Water

Do not drink the water, but understand that this is not because it is dangerous — it is because it is *occupied*. The filaments are everywhere in it, and while they will not hurt you they will establish themselves in your gut, and the consequences of this vary unpredictably. Buy sealed water from market vendors; it is cheap, it is everywhere, and the sealing process is trusted by locals, which is the only endorsement that means anything.

The pressed mineral cakes are safe for human digestion and are what most visitors live on. They are bland in a way that becomes interesting after about three days — your palate adjusts and begins to detect structure. Do not eat the filaments raw under any circumstances, but cooked filament preparations in market stalls are fine and often excellent. If something is being sold cooked and people are eating it in front of you, it is food. If something is being sold in a vial, it is probably not food even if it looks like it.

The soft-shelled crabs are not eaten and you should not eat them. This is not a superstition; it is an ecological arrangement. Eating one would not poison you but would be noticed, and you do not want to be noticed in that particular way.

## Money and Trade

Everyone is doing business. This is not hyperbole — there is no social mode in the Conurbation that is not transactional in some sense, though the transactions are often small, casual, and concerned with information or favor rather than goods. Arrive with things to trade. Physical items from elsewhere in the multiverse have good value here precisely because they are anomalies, and the markets appreciate anomalies. Data-thread of any kind is the closest thing to universal currency; acquire spools before entering and learn how they are read here, because the reading protocols differ from most places.

Do not try to understand the exchange rates in any formal way. Prices respond to the turbulent rearrangement of the districts. What cost one thing yesterday costs another today. Bargain casually, accept that you will overpay, and regard the overpayment as a tuition fee for being someone who has not yet learned the current shape of the trade.

## Navigation

Do not make maps. Do not rely on maps made by others. The district-scale shape of the city rearranges on timescales of days to weeks, and any map is a historical document. Instead, learn to navigate by the tower-clusters, which are long-stable, and by the position of the gates in the causeways, which move but are always signed.

Learn to read the patterning on the tower walls. You do not need to understand it fully — no visitor does — but recognizing which cluster you are in by the style of the patterning is a skill that comes after a few days of attention and is worth the attention. Locals use this automatically. Losing your cluster is the primary way visitors get truly lost, because all the water looks the same and all the causeways interconnect.

If you become disoriented, stop. Find a platform above the water. Watch the barge traffic for twenty minutes. The flow has logic, and the logic will eventually reveal the direction of the nearest trade hub, which is where you can ask for help. Do not wade in a direction you are not sure of — the water deepens unevenly, and while it will not hurt you, swimming in full clothing with your possessions is an experience you want to avoid.

## The Gates and the Large Things

When a gate in a causeway begins to open, move away from the causeway edge. Not in a panic — you have minutes, not seconds — but decisively. The barge traffic will already be diverting. Follow the barges. Whatever passes under the causeway does not harm people, but it displaces water, and the wave it produces on the far side is enough to knock an unprepared person off their feet. This is the main real physical danger you will face, and it is entirely manageable if you pay attention to the gates.

Do not try to see the thing that passes. Several visitors have reported after effects from the sight — not injuries, but lingering preoccupations, a sense of having been looked at in return. If you must look, look at the water, not at what moves under it.

## The Towers

Every tower is occupied by working processes you will not see and will not understand. The humming is the audible fraction of this. When the humming rises in pitch in a tower you are near, the tower is not angry and you are not in danger, but you are in the way of something. Walk to another tower. This is basic courtesy. Locals do this without thinking, in the same way you would step out of the path of someone carrying a heavy load.

Do not touch the tower walls for extended periods. A quick touch to feel the skin-like patterning is fine and expected. Leaning against a tower, sleeping against it, or trying to read the patterning for long minutes with your hand on it will eventually produce a response from the tower, which is not hostile but is *attentive*, and being attended to by a tower is an experience most visitors find difficult to integrate afterward.

## Sleep and the Pearled Night

You will not experience true darkness. The deep rose of night is brighter than most indoor rooms in other worlds. Bring a sleep mask, a heavy one. Bring earplugs even though they will not fully mute the roar — the roar in sleep is different from the roar awake, and the plugs help with the transition.

Accept that your sleep will be strange. Dreams in the Conurbation are reported to be unusually vivid and often shared in fragments — a traveler dreaming of singing may find, on waking, that the district was indeed singing, that the song crossed the basin, that they were hearing it through some channel they did not know they possessed. This is not dangerous, but it can be destabilizing if you are not expecting it. Expect it.

The pre-dawn trade bells will wake you. You will not sleep through them; no one does. Rise and find coffee, or the local equivalent, which is a hot infusion of mineral and filament that tastes like the air smells and is, after a few days, the thing you find yourself looking forward to most.

## Departing

Leaving is harder than arriving, and this surprises people. The saturation of the city becomes, by the end of even a short visit, the expected state of consciousness. The silence of other worlds will feel, for some days after you leave, like a kind of sensory deprivation. You will turn your head at nothing. You will miss the pearled light. You will, at odd moments, listen for singing.

This passes. Most of it passes. Some of it does not, and those who have visited the Conurbation tend to recognize each other across the multiverse by a quality of attention that the city leaves behind — a willingness to stand still in loud places, a comfort with things in continuous rearrangement, a slight sadness in silence.

If you cannot hear yourself think, you are in the right place. Bring that with you when you leave. It is the best thing the city gives.
