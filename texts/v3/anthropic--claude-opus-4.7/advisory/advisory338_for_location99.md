# Preparing to Visit Vost-Under-Ice: A Traveler's Guide

## First, understand what you're walking into

Vost is not a hostile place. It will not try to kill you. But it will, gently and without malice, undo several assumptions your body and mind have been making your whole life about how reality behaves. Prepare accordingly.

The central fact to internalize before you arrive: **you are going to a place where the air is partway through becoming water, where sound has been muted, where light has no direction, and where the distinction between weather and architecture is porous.** Nothing about this is dangerous in itself. The danger is that your nervous system, having no framework for it, may try to panic. Your job in preparation is to make panic unnecessary.

## The body: what to bring and what to do with it

**Clothing, in layers, in this order from the skin out:**

- A base layer of fine merino or silk — something that handles a continuous film of moisture against the skin without souring. You will not be *dry* in Vost. Accept this now. Bring four or five base layers and rotate them; a worn one can be wrung out and hung in your room, where it will not dry but will at least stop being actively unpleasant.
- A mid-layer that traps warmth even when damp. Wool, not down. Down is useless here; it will collapse into a wet mat within a day.
- An outer layer of something breathable and water-shedding. Do not bring a sealed rain shell. A sealed shell will trap your own condensation against you and you will stew. What you want is closer to what the locals wear: the waxed felt, or an analogue — oiled wool, a tightly woven wax-cotton coat of the old pattern. Breath goes out, water beads off.
- A hood or broad soft hat. You want something over your head not against rain (rain is not really what's happening) but against the constant gentle glazing of the air.

**Footwear.** Everything is wet slate and wet bonestone. The stone has been rounded by millennia of seepage. Traction is the entire question. Bring boots with aggressive soft-rubber soles — climbing-approach shoes or the equivalent — and break them in before you go. Leather uppers, well-dubbed. Two pairs, so one can rest while the other works. A rigid sole will betray you on the fluted grooves where meltwater travels.

**Gloves.** Thin wool liners under something water-shedding. You will touch the walls. You cannot help it. The walls are cold and moving and your bare hand will come away numb, and numb hands make mistakes on wet stone.

**Eyes.** This is the part travelers most frequently underprepare for. The light in Vost is diffused, flat, shadowless, cold. It comes from everywhere. It is not *bright* in the way desert sun is bright, but it is relentlessly even, and the eye is an instrument designed to find edges and directions. After about six hours your eyes will ache in a way you have not felt before, because they have been searching and finding nothing to fix on.

Bring two things: a pair of soft amber-tinted lenses (not dark, not polarized — amber, to restore some warmth the light refuses to give), and an eye mask for sleep. You will want to rest your eyes often in Vost. Carry the mask and use it for ten minutes every few hours in a doorway or niche. The locals close their eyes freely in public; it is not rude there.

## The lungs and the sinuses

You are going to breathe something that is not quite air. For the first day this will feel like a mild drowning — not acute, just an underlying sense that you are not getting what you expect from each breath. This passes. Your body adjusts within 36 to 48 hours. Know this in advance so you don't mistake the adjustment period for altitude sickness (though altitude sickness is also possible; Vost is high).

**Before you go:** practice slow nasal breathing for two weeks. Four seconds in, six seconds out. This trains your diaphragm to pull more efficiently and reduces the panic-flutter that the thick air triggers in unprepared travelers.

**Bring:** a small jar of a mentholated balm for under the nose at night, as the constant saturation can clog sinuses in people not used to it. Also a handkerchief — cloth, not paper, because paper disintegrates. You will be wiping your face frequently and paper will not survive the trip.

## Hearing and voice

You need to understand, before arriving, that **your voice will not work the way you expect.** You will speak and the sound will travel three paces and stop. You will instinctively raise your voice. Don't. This marks you immediately as an outsider and is considered, if not rude, then pitiable — the way someone shouting in a library is pitiable.

**Practice this in advance:** spend a day speaking only at close range and using gestures for anything farther. Get used to the feeling of being physically near the people you communicate with. In Vost, conversation is an intimate physical act. You stand close. You touch forearms lightly to get attention. You wait.

Learn, before going, a basic hand-sign vocabulary. The locals have a shared set of gestures for common needs — *water, sleep, yes, no, stop, help, thank you, which way, how much* — and any traveler's handbook will teach them. Do not improvise signs; some of yours will mean something else there and you will embarrass yourself or worse.

**Do not whistle.** I cannot find a rule that forbids it, but no one does it, and the one traveler I know of who whistled in a market was quietly led away from the stall and given water and a place to sit, as if he had done something medically alarming. Take the hint.

## The mind

This is the preparation most travelers skip and most regret skipping.

Vost does several things to the mind that are worth rehearsing against:

**The shadowlessness produces a mild dissociation.** Your sense of time, which is partly built on the movement of shadows and the angle of light, goes slack. Noon looks like morning looks like late afternoon. Bring a watch — a simple mechanical one, as electronics may behave strangely in the saturation — and consult it on a schedule. Eat on the schedule even if you are not hungry. Sleep on the schedule even if you are not tired. Your body's clock will not keep itself here; you must keep it for your body.

**The silent turbulence is hypnotic.** You will stand at a railing and watch the silver shift of the flyers, the sheeting water, the soundless movement of people across ledges, and you will realize an hour has passed and you have done nothing. This is not dangerous in small doses. It is pleasant, even. But set a timer, because the cold is patient and will find you if you stand still long enough.

**The thoughts-the-mountain-is-having.** On heavy-condensation mornings you may see a face form in the air. Do not react. Do not reach toward it. Do not speak to it. The locals' protocol is simply to stop what they are doing, lower their eyes slightly, and wait for it to dissolve. Imitate this. Whether these images are meteorological, neurological, or something else is not a question you will answer in a week, so do not try. Accept the local practice as your practice.

**The listeners.** You will see them in the walls. Small soft slow creatures with something like faces, watching. The locals do not discuss them; **you also do not discuss them.** Not even with other travelers. Not even privately in your lodging. This is the single strongest social taboo I can identify in Vost, and violating it will not get you hurt, but it will get you a kind of cold distance from the locals that will make the rest of your stay thin and unwelcoming. If you find yourself alone and feel an urge to *acknowledge* one — a nod, a glance — that is fine, apparently. But say nothing.

## Water, food, and the household economy

**Do not drink from any cistern without being offered.** Every cistern is a household, and every cistern contains a cistern-thing that is considered a family member. Drawing water without invitation is roughly equivalent to walking into a stranger's house and opening their refrigerator. You will be offered water often — the gesture is a flat palm held near the chest, fingers slightly cupped — and you should accept with the responding gesture (same hand position, brought forward an inch).

The water is clean. The cistern-things keep it clean. Do not ask how. Do not look too long into the cistern when you are drinking. Thank the household, not the water.

**Food** in Vost is simple: the shoulder-ewes provide milk, cheese, and occasionally meat; the lichens and sedges are boiled into thin stews; the pale flowers are not eaten by people (only by the ewes, and this is not something you should try to change). Travelers should bring some of their own food for the first two days while the gut adjusts — plain things, hard biscuits, dried fruit — and then eat what is offered.

**Never pick a flower.** I should not have to say this but travelers do it anyway. Every flower marks a death or a decision, and both are considered to belong to the person who made them. Picking one is theft of a kind that has no equivalent in most cultures.

## Navigation

The city rearranges itself. A neighborhood at breakfast is a different neighborhood at dusk. Do not rely on landmarks of the kind you are used to — a particular building, a particular stall — because these may have moved, been absorbed, or become a waterfall.

Instead, navigate by the **permanent features**: the thread of water at the top of the cirque (visible from most of the upper city), the orientation of the cirque walls (you can always tell which way is *in* and which is *out*), and the cisterns (which move rarely, being anchored to their things). Locals will give directions by cistern: *three cisterns down from the copper-furnace, then across the second overflow*. Learn to hear directions in this idiom.

Bring a notebook with waxed pages or oilcloth pages. Paper will not survive. Sketch as you go — not maps, which will be obsolete by morning, but *relations*: which cistern feeds which, which ledge cantilevers from which, which stair becomes a waterfall at which hour. These relations are more durable than the buildings.

## The copper-song

If you are in Vost when copper is being smelted — and you will likely hear about this before it happens; word travels by hand-sign across the cirque — go. When the fresh copper begins to cool it sings for about a minute, and this is the loudest sound in the city, and the only sound in the city that has any real carry. Children will be brought. Adults will stop what they are doing. Stand still, do not speak, do not move until the song has ended and the person who smelted it makes the small gesture that releases the crowd.

This is not a tourist attraction. It is a civic moment. You are being allowed to attend. Behave accordingly.

## Leaving

Plan your departure for a clear morning if you can; the fogs that roll through in minutes can trap you in the cirque for hours or days, and the trails down are not safe in fog. The locals will know when a clear morning is coming — ask the night before, with the gesture for *weather-tomorrow* (palm up, tilted slightly, eyebrows raised), and they will tell you.

When you leave, expect a strange grief. Travelers report this consistently. You will descend below the cloud layer, and sound will return, and shadows will return, and your voice will carry, and the air will become air again, and you will feel — oddly, sharply — that something has been taken from you. This passes in a day or two. It is not a sign that you should go back. It is just the nervous system releasing the grip it had to maintain while you were there. Let it pass. Drink warm things. Sit in a room with hard edges and directional light and let your eyes find corners again.

## Last thing

Somewhere in your luggage, carry a small token of a decision you have made — a marriage, a forgiveness, a promise to yourself, anything real. If, during your stay, you find yourself standing in a quiet place and wanting to leave something behind, speak the decision aloud (it will not carry; no one will hear) and leave the token on the bonestone. A flower may grow. It may not. Either way, you will have participated in the one thing Vost does that no other place does, which is to treat the decisions of a human life as events the earth itself records.

That is, in the end, why people travel to Vost. Not for the flyers or the light or the silence. For the chance that something they decided, while alive, might leave a mark on a mountain.

Go prepared. Go quietly. Come back changed, but not too much.
