# Preparing for the Stilled Heights

## Understanding What You're Entering

Before anything practical, understand this: the Stilled Heights is not a dangerous place in the usual sense. Nothing there will attack you. Nothing will fall on you, poison you, or hunt you. The danger is entirely one of *assimilation*. The plateau is a place that has solved itself, and it will extend the courtesy of that solution to you if you let it. Pack and prepare accordingly — which is to say, pack for a psychological expedition disguised as a physical one.

## Physical Preparation

**Altitude conditioning.** Spend at least two weeks acclimatizing at progressively higher elevations before arrival. The air on the plateau is thin in the ordinary sense *and* thin in a less ordinary one — it does not refresh itself. You will be breathing an atmosphere that has forgotten how to circulate. Bring supplemental oxygen, but ration it carefully: every breath of your own gas you release is a small disturbance the plateau will quietly compensate for, and you do not want to know what that compensation costs you.

**Warmth without bulk.** It is cool but not cold. Dress in layers of muted color — not for camouflage but because bright dyes feel obscene there, and you will want to avoid feeling obscene. Avoid synthetics that rustle. Wool, linen, soft leather. Nothing that announces your motion.

**Footwear.** Soft-soled. The plateau yields under pressure and keeps the record. You will leave footprints that will outlast every civilization you have ever heard of. Choose your boot tread as you would choose an epitaph.

**Eat beforehand, not during.** Bring water, yes — small sips only — but do not eat on the plateau. The act of digestion is a louder, more transformative process than you realize; you will feel your own gut as a vulgar engine against the silence. Fast the morning of your visit and eat heavily when you return.

## What to Bring

**A timepiece you trust** — ideally two, mechanical, independently wound. You will need external proof that time is passing, because the shadows will not give it to you and your body will stop giving it to you after about the first hour. Check them often. Set an alarm for departure and do not negotiate with it when it sounds.

**A written note to yourself.** Something like: *You came here from somewhere. Someone is waiting. You intend to leave at [time]. You have agreed, in advance, that the feelings you are having now are not reliable data about whether to leave.* Put this in your breast pocket. Read it whenever you feel calm in a way that seems to have no source.

**One small object to leave.** This is the most important item in your pack. You will want to leave something. The cairns invite it, the place invites it, and refusing the invitation is both possible and, for some travelers, costly in ways that are hard to articulate. Bring something you have made with your own hands — not something expensive, not something meaningful in a showy way. A knot you tied. A scrap of cloth you wove, however clumsily. A word written on a leaf. The plateau does not honor wealth. It honors the fingerprint of attention. Bring one thing, place it in one empty hollow, and do not leave more. The travelers who leave second offerings often leave a third, and a fourth, and eventually themselves.

**A partner, tethered.** If your multiverse license permits, travel with one other person, and bring a light cord — not a rope, nothing so dramatic, just a length of soft line connecting you at the wrist. The cord is not for physical safety. It is so that when one of you begins to settle, the other can feel the slackening and tug. Agree in advance: *if I tug, you leave with me, no discussion.* Write this agreement down. Both sign it.

**A mirror, small.** Every twenty minutes, look at your own face. Settling shows first in the eyes — a kind of quieting of the micro-saccades, a stillness in the gaze that is not calm but *resolution*. If your reflection looks too composed, leave immediately.

## What Not to Bring

- No recording devices. They will work, but what they record will be wrong in ways that will trouble you later. Photographs from the plateau develop as still lifes of places the photographer has never been. Audio recordings play back as rooms.
- No firearms, no blades beyond a small utility knife. There is nothing to defend against, and the plateau treats weapons as a category error. Travelers who draw weapons there have reported the weapons feeling suddenly apologetic in their hands.
- No books you care about. Reading is fine, but the plateau reads back, and books opened there sometimes return home altered by a few words, here and there, in ways only their owners notice.
- No music. Do not play music. Do not hum. The plateau has achieved its silence at a cost you are not authorized to negotiate on its behalf.

## Behavioral Protocols

**Move continuously but slowly.** The great risk is falling into the compensating pattern — making your motion one of the small motions the plateau absorbs. To avoid this, walk with deliberate *asymmetry*. Vary your pace. Change direction for no reason. Stop, then start again before you have finished stopping. Be, in your small way, the thing the plateau has outgrown. Be a little ugly about it. Graceful travelers are the ones who do not come back.

**Do not sit down.** Sitting is the beginning of settling. If you must rest, lean against nothing, crouch briefly, rise again. Do not lie down under any circumstances. Do not close your eyes for more than a blink.

**Do not count things.** The regularities are real, and verifying them is a form of agreement with them. Every cairn you measure, every cushion-plant interval you confirm, is a small ratification. Notice the regularities; do not confirm them. The distinction matters.

**Do not approach the animals.** Not because they are dangerous but because they are not. Their aliveness is a pressure you can feel at twenty paces; at two paces it becomes a request, and the request is for you to join the compensation. A moth landing on your shoulder is a courtesy you cannot afford.

**Do not look for the still humans.** You have been told they are there, looking like stones. Your natural instinct will be to try to find one, to verify, to understand. Resist this. Recognizing one is a form of being recognized by it. The travelers who find the still humans report that the still humans seem, briefly, to be waiting for something — and whatever that something is, you do not want to provide it.

**Speak aloud, occasionally.** Say your own name. Say the name of someone who loves you. Say what you had for breakfast three days ago. The plateau tolerates speech — it is too small a disturbance to fully absorb — and your voice is one of the strongest anchors you have to the world where things still happen. Do not speak in whispers. Speak in an ordinary voice. Feel slightly silly about it. The silliness is protective.

## Duration

**No more than ninety minutes on your first visit.** This is not a suggestion. Set your alarms. The plateau does not feel long while you are there; it feels exactly as long as you have been there, which is a different thing, and a dangerous thing. Ninety minutes is long enough to see the place. Longer is long enough for the place to see you.

## Departure

When your alarm sounds, leave immediately, without a last look. The last look is how the plateau catches most of the people it catches. There is always one more detail to resolve, one more cairn to approach, one more herd to walk past. Resolve nothing. Leave questions unanswered. You can come back — most travelers do come back, once — and the plateau will be identical, because of course it will.

As you descend, you will feel a strange sorrow, almost a grief, for about the first hour. This is normal. It is the feeling of re-entering a world where things happen, where loss is real, where your footprints wash away. Some travelers describe it as homesickness for a home they have never had. It passes. Eat something heavy. Talk to someone about something trivial. Let the ordinary noise of causation fill you up again.

## A Final Note

Some travelers ask whether the plateau is sacred. The honest answer is that *sacred* is the wrong category. The plateau is not holy; it is finished. The humans who built the cairns were not worshipping. They were acknowledging. They had found a place that had arrived somewhere the rest of the universe is still traveling toward, and they left small tokens in the way one leaves a coin on a milestone: not in offering, but in recognition.

Leave your token. Acknowledge what has been acknowledged. Then go back down into the unfinished world, where the wind still moves, and be grateful, for as long as you can manage it, that things are still becoming other things.

That is the whole of the preparation. The rest is discipline, and luck, and the small stubborn fact of wanting to go home.
