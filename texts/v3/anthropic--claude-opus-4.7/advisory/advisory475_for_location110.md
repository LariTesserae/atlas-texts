# Preparing to Visit the Shelf Cities

## What You Are Walking Into

Before anything practical, understand the shape of the trip. This is not a destination in the ordinary sense. There is nothing to see in the way we usually mean seeing — no landmark that rewards arrival, no monument whose photograph will satisfy the ache of having gone. The Shelf Cities are a country-sized mood. What you are buying, with your preparation and your time and the small suffering of your body, is days of walking through a place that will not acknowledge you and is not lonely for your acknowledgment either. Most travelers who do not understand this in advance come back shaken in a way they cannot articulate, because they were braced for wonder and received instead a long quiet indifference, which is a different and harder gift.

Go if you are at a point in life when being small is tolerable. Do not go during grief — the plateau will neither console you nor distract you, and the scale of its unconcern will scrape at a wound that wants scraping but should not have it. Do not go during an ambitious phase, either; you will resent the place for refusing to be impressed by you. Go, ideally, in a fallow season, when you are between things, when your inner weather already matches the plateau's patient cycling.

## The Body

The altitude is real and the air is thin. Arrive through a staging world at a comparable elevation and spend at least a week there before crossing over — longer if you are past forty, longer still if you smoke or carry extra weight. The coolness-that-is-also-pressure of the damp thin air is harder on the lungs than dry cold; you will find yourself breathing faster than you expect even at rest, and this accumulates as a kind of low exhaustion that no single night's sleep will clear. Expect to be tired in a way you have to make friends with.

Your skin will be damp the entire time you are there. This is not a discomfort that gets worse but it is also one that never goes away. Pack as if for a long cool mist: merino base layers (several, because drying anything is slow), a tightly woven wind shell that breathes, a brimmed hat that sheds water without channeling it down your neck. Avoid anything waterproof-to-the-point-of-sealing; you will sweat inside it and end up wetter than the plateau would have made you. The trick is layering that manages moisture rather than excluding it, because exclusion is not possible here.

Your feet need serious thought. The flagstones are slick with film, the causeways run with thin sheets of afternoon rain, the basins are deeper than they look. Bring boots with aggressive lugs and waterproof membranes you trust, and bring a second pair you can alternate so that one is always drying (slowly — nothing dries quickly here). Wool socks, many pairs. Blister treatment that works on damp skin, which is a different problem than blisters on dry skin. Gaiters are worth their weight.

Eat more than you think you need to. The cold-damp burns calories in a sneaky way. Bring dense foods — nut butters, hard cheeses, cured meats, oily fish in tins, chocolate, olive oil to pour on things. The plateau offers no foraging. The reeds are not food. The cushion plants are not food. The lizard is not food, even if you could catch it, which you could not.

## Water

Paradoxically, water is the thing to think hardest about. The plateau is wet everywhere and drinkable nowhere without treatment — the mineral content of the weeping seams is high, the basins collect whatever the mist carries, and the slow pace of everything means biological contamination has had leisure to develop its own strange ecologies. Bring a pump filter you trust, a backup chemical treatment, and the willingness to use both in series. The basins in the plazas are the easiest source and often the cleanest-looking, but prefer water caught fresh from the afternoon rain in a clean vessel spread flat during the rainfall hours.

Drink more than thirst tells you. The thin air lies about hydration.

## Time

Plan for longer than seems reasonable. The plateau is days across in any direction and there is no shortcutting it. Shelter within the cities is plentiful — every town offers thousands of empty rooms — but none of them were built for sleeping in the way bodies like yours sleep, and you will want a lightweight tent or bivy to set up inside a room that has a roof of reasonable integrity. Sleeping in the open is possible but the afternoon rain and the evening cold argue against it.

Budget at least two weeks of walking. Three is better. A week is not enough to understand anything and you will come back having seen the cities the way one sees a painting from a moving car.

Walk slowly. There is no reward for covering ground quickly and there is a penalty, which is that the plateau's scale only reveals itself to a pace that matches its own. Aim for ten to fifteen miles a day at most, with long midday stops during the rain hours, when you sit under a lintel and watch the water do its work on stone that has been accepting water for longer than your species has existed.

## The Mind

This is the part most travelers underprepare for.

The silence is not like other silences. It does not press on you the way desert silence does, nor hold you the way forest silence does. It simply declines to be broken. You will find, after two or three days, that you stop talking aloud even to yourself, because the single small returned echo from the nearest wall is worse than saying nothing. Bring something to read, on paper — screens feel wrong here in a way that is difficult to explain but universal among visitors. Bring a notebook. Writing is one of the few human activities the plateau permits without quiet mockery.

Do not bring music. I know the temptation. Resist it. Music in headphones on the plateau is a form of flinching and you will regret having flinched.

Expect, at some point around the fourth or fifth day, a kind of mental event that different visitors describe differently but which seems to be the same event. It arrives as a sudden hollow feeling, usually in a plaza or at a parapet, and it is the moment your sense of being a protagonist gives up. People have wept during this. People have laughed. People have sat down in the damp and not moved for an hour. It passes. What comes after it is the good part of the trip, and most of why to go. But you cannot skip to it, and preparing for it means mostly agreeing in advance that you will let it happen when it happens rather than fighting it.

Travel with one companion or alone. Groups of three or more fail here — the social dynamic cannot survive the scale and the quiet, and parties fragment acrimoniously. One other person, chosen carefully, is ideal: someone you can be silent next to for hours without the silence becoming a problem to solve. A spouse of long standing. A sibling you actually like. A friend whose company you have tested in bad weather. Not a new lover. Not a colleague. Not anyone you are trying to impress or anyone trying to impress you.

## Things Not to Do

Do not try to claim a room, mark it, improve it, or return to it. The cities do not care but you will start to, and the caring is a trap. Sleep where you sleep and move on.

Do not dig, chip, or take stone. This is not because anything will punish you — nothing will — but because the impulse to take a piece of the plateau home is an impulse to make the place smaller than it is, and smallness is the one thing the plateau cannot sustain without becoming something else.

Do not approach the edges casually. The cloud-seas below look soft. They are not soft. The drop is real and the parapets, such as they are, were not built with your balance in mind. Stay well back unless you have specifically come to a high quarter designed for viewing, and even then, sit rather than stand.

Do not try to decode the cities. The doorways do not mean anything. The avenues do not lead anywhere. The causeways are not messages. Travelers who arrive with theories leave with different theories and none of them are right, because the cities are not the kind of thing that theories are about. Let them be what they are.

Do not photograph extensively. One camera, used sparingly, for a few images you will want later as anchors for memory. Photography at scale here becomes a way of refusing to be present, and the plateau punishes refusals of presence with a dullness that follows you home.

## Coming Back

Plan a soft re-entry. Do not schedule meetings for the week after you return. The plateau's pace will still be in your body and ordinary human tempo will feel frantic and false for some days. Cook simple meals. Walk in a local park. Let the speed of the world you came from rebuild itself around you gradually rather than all at once.

Some travelers find they have brought something back that they cannot name and do not want to lose. Others find they have left something there that they did not know they were carrying. Both are normal. Neither requires action. The plateau is not a place one has a relationship with — it is not interested in relationship — but one's own self, having been exposed to it, continues to settle for weeks afterward, the way a room settles after a heavy object has been moved through it.

Go in late summer of whatever world you stage through. Bring warm socks. Drink the rain. Walk slowly. Come back when you are done, not before.
