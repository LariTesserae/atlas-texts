# Preparing to Visit the Glasshouse Terraces of Meel

## What You Are Actually Going To

Before anything practical, understand what you're signing up for. This is not a tourist destination, not a wilderness, not an abandoned ruin, not a functioning facility in any sense a human would recognize as having purpose. It is a place that has been *kept* — kept warm, kept wet, kept growing — past the point where anyone remembers why. You are going to spend time inside someone else's patience. Prepare accordingly: not for danger, but for a specific kind of exposure that most travelers are not ready for.

The danger at Meel is not that something will hurt you. It is that you will not want to leave, or that when you leave you will have become someone slightly different, and that the difference will be hard to name.

## Clothing

Wear as little as decency and your constitution allow. Everything you put on will be saturated within ten minutes and stay saturated for the duration of your visit. There is no drying anything inside the Terraces. Favor materials that hold their structure when wet and do not chafe — thin merino wool is ideal, linen acceptable, cotton tolerable, synthetics unpleasant against skin that is constantly misted. Avoid anything with metal fasteners that will corrode and stain; the air is mildly aggressive toward exposed iron over a span of days, and toward jewelry over a span of hours.

Footwear is the critical question. The walkways are iron grating, often overgrown, often submerged. You want something that grips wet metal, drains freely, and does not come off when the floor is a living mat of roots. Sandals with heel-straps are the traditional choice of those who know the place. Do not wear boots — they will fill and stay full. Do not go barefoot on the first day; the grating edges are rust-fanged in places, and while nothing here wants to infect you, the tetanus you bring with you from outside is indifferent to local intentions.

Bring a second set of clothes sealed in a waterproof bag for the return journey. When you leave the cloud line and descend, being wet becomes being cold very quickly.

## What to Carry

A small pack, oiled canvas or waxed leather, nothing that will not survive immersion. Inside:

- A notebook made for wet conditions — rite-in-the-rain paper, or one of the waxed journals sold by cartographers in damp multiverses. You will want to write things down. You will not want to, at first, and then around the second hour you will want to very much. A pencil works; ink runs.
- Water, though this seems absurd in a place drowning in water. The condensate is technically potable but has the mineral signature of whatever the mountain's interior holds, and some travelers react poorly for a day or two. Better to bring your own for the first visit and sample the Terraces' water only after you know you can leave.
- Food that does not mind damp. Hard cheese, dried fruit, nuts in a tin, flatbreads wrapped in oiled paper. Do not bring anything aromatic — the still, warm air traps smells, and you will be smelling your own lunch for an hour after you eat it.
- A small light. The green light of the interior is adequate for seeing where you are, but inadequate for reading fine print or examining the engines. A candle will not stay lit. Bring a sealed electric torch or, better, a small oil lamp designed for damp conditions, of the sort miners once carried.
- A timepiece, if you want to know what time it is. You will probably stop caring, but the first few hours are disorienting without one. Mechanical watches tolerate the humidity if they are well-sealed; leave anything electronic outside unless it is rated for immersion.
- One book. Not more. A book you have read before and want to read again slowly. The light is good for reading. The pace of the place will make you read at a quarter your normal speed, and you will notice things you did not notice before. Do not bring a book you are trying to finish.

## What to Leave Behind

Anything that marks time urgently. Appointment calendars, schedules, devices that buzz. They will feel obscene inside the Terraces, the way a siren feels obscene in a library.

Anything you are trying to forget. Meel does not help you forget. The slow pace and the close vegetation and the constant low-grade sensory isolation (nothing to see beyond three meters, nothing to hear but drip) will bring your interior life forward like a developing photograph. If you come with something unresolved, you will spend your time there resolving it, or failing to resolve it, or discovering that it cannot be resolved. This is fine if you are ready. It is not fine if you arrived expecting a pleasant afternoon.

Any living thing. No seeds, no pets, no cultures in jars. The place has resisted every introduction, and while the mechanism of this resistance is not understood, there is no reason to test it with your herb garden.

## Physical Preparation

Your body will be at mild thermal stress the entire visit. Not dangerous, but constant. The temperature is just above comfortable and the humidity is total, which means you cannot cool by sweating — your sweat does not evaporate. Drink more than you think you need. Move more slowly than you think necessary. If you feel lightheaded, sit down on the nearest dry patch of grating; it will pass within a few minutes, but pushing through it is how people fall into pools.

If you have respiratory conditions, test yourself in a steam room for an hour before committing to this trip. Some lungs do not love warm wet air. There is no medical aid at Meel.

If you have any phobia of close spaces or overgrown vegetation, reconsider. The visual horizon is three meters of leaves, always.

## Mental Preparation

This is the part most travelers neglect, and it matters more here than the clothing.

**Prepare to be alone.** There may be other visitors; there may not. The Terraces have no staff, no greeter, no evidence of current human presence beyond the fact that the engines still turn and the humidistats still tremble. You will not meet anyone. If you do, they will be another traveler like yourself, and the etiquette is to nod and pass, not to converse. The place is not for talking in.

**Prepare to be bored, and then to stop being bored.** For the first hour, or two, or three, you will feel that nothing is happening. This is correct — nothing is happening, on your timescale. Then something shifts, and you begin operating on the Terraces' timescale, and you notice that a great deal is happening, constantly, at the speed of ferns unfurling and rust advancing and pistons completing their eleven-second strokes. This transition is the whole point of the visit. Do not try to force it. Do not give up before it happens. Bring the book precisely for the interval when you need something to do with your hands while your mind adjusts.

**Prepare for the upper pane.** When you find it — and you will, the walkways lead there eventually, though not directly — you will press your forehead to the cold glass and see the other mountains, and you will feel the specific grief of remembering that you came from a world with walls. This grief is productive. Stay with it. Do not rush back into the green. Travelers who spend at least a quarter-hour at the upper pane return home differently than travelers who glance and turn.

**Prepare to want to stay.** Around the fourth or fifth hour, the thought will arise: *I could simply not leave.* This is a real thought and it has a real answer, which is that the Terraces do not sustain human life. There is no food that you can eat. The water will eventually make you ill. The warmth is real but the nutrition is not. The place is a refuge for a few hours; it is a slow death for more than a few days. Trust the part of yourself that knows this. Set a time to leave and leave at that time, even if you do not want to.

## Getting There and Getting Back

The route to Meel varies by multiverse. The consistent features are: a mountain whose name you will not retain, a cloud line you pass through going up, a steep final approach on what may or may not be a path. Bring a guide for the ascent if one is available in your starting world. Do not attempt the descent in fading light.

When you leave, descend immediately to below the cloud line before you change clothes. Your dry set will not stay dry in the cloud. Once below, find shelter, change entirely, wring out what you were wearing, and drink something warm. Expect to feel strange for a day or two afterward — slightly slowed, slightly less reactive to news and noise. This fades. Some travelers report that a small portion of it never fades, and that this is the part they wanted.

## A Final Note

The engines have been running longer than any living memory. Someone built them, someone tuned them, someone decided they should keep turning, and that person is long gone, and the engines are still turning. You are a guest in the care of an absence. Behave accordingly: gently, with attention, leaving nothing behind and taking nothing except what has already begun changing inside you by the time you reach the upper pane.

Walk slowly. The place has time. You, for a few hours, also have time. This is rarer than you think, and you should not waste it by hurrying.
