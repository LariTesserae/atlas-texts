# Preparing to Visit the Kiln-Warren

I want to be honest with you before we begin: this is not really a place a human visits. This is a place a human is *delivered into*, briefly, inside an apparatus that does almost all of the actual being-there on your behalf. What follows is preparation for the kind of visit that is possible — which is closer to piloting a deep-sea submersible into a volcanic vent than to walking into a room.

## First, the scale problem

The warren is described as the size of a large kitchen, but this is misleading for a human. The ceiling is low enough to make a standing person stoop, the tiles are individually hot enough to blister, and the residents are thumb-joint-sized or smaller. A human body inserted at true scale would be a catastrophe for the ecosystem — you would be a walking heat sink of roughly the wrong temperature, your breath alone a weather event, your footprint a genocide of floor-fauna.

So the first preparation is accepting that you will probably go in at reduced scale, inside a shell, or not at all. Most travelers choose a vessel somewhere between the size of one of the lozenges and the size of a human fist. Anything larger and you are not a visitor, you are a disaster.

## Thermal preparation

The ambient temperature in the warren is somewhere north of what cooks meat. The floor will blister bare skin on contact; the walls will cook a palm before losing a degree. You cannot wear a suit. You must wear a *system* — an active thermal regulator that does not simply insulate but participates in the local economy. Passive insulation is both useless (the heat will find you) and rude (you become a cold spot, and the fauna will orient around you, disrupting the cycle).

A proper visitor's shell does three things:

1. Holds its internal temperature at something survivable for the soft animal inside it.
2. Radiates its waste heat at a rate and rhythm that matches the room's baseline pulse, so that to the ceiling-fauna you read as *weather*, not as *wound*.
3. Maintains a thermal signature close enough to one of the existing residents that the lozenges' emergent hierarchy can place you somewhere in its order. You will be low in the hierarchy. That is fine. Give way at every junction.

Get the shell calibrated by someone who has been before. The calibration cannot be done from outside; it requires a short dwell inside the warren itself, during which a more experienced visitor or an automated system tunes your radiative profile to match the cycle. Budget for this.

## Sensory preparation

You will see nothing the way you see now. There is no light. Vision in the warren is infrared and convective — you see by the shape of heat leaving things, by the plumes and wakes of temperature differential.

This is not a filter you put over normal vision. It is a different grammar of seeing, and most first-time visitors find it nauseating for the first several minutes. Prepare by spending time — weeks, ideally — using infrared-only goggles in dark rooms before the trip. Learn to read a teapot as a shape of rising plume. Learn to recognize a human hand by its bloom. Then learn to *not* look for edges, because edges are a visible-light concept and will betray you here.

The ceiling, being cooler, will read as a kind of negative sky: an absence above you, dark in the thermal sense, against which the ceiling-fauna shimmer like slow foil stars. The floor will seem to glow. The walls will seem to be banked coals behind a grate — because, in effect, they are.

Bring no lights. A visible-spectrum light source in the warren is an act of vandalism. The fauna will not be harmed by it, exactly, but they have never encountered the concept and you do not get to be the one who introduces it.

## Auditory preparation

The warren is nearly silent. The loudest sound is described as *a page being turned very slowly in another room* — the collective respiration of the ceiling-fauna. Every few hours there is a susurrus during the pulse.

Your shell will amplify these sounds for you, because otherwise you will hear nothing but your own heartbeat and begin to panic. But the amplification must be honest — it must preserve the proportions. Do not let a technician set your audio to "immersive." You want to hear the warren the way the warren sounds, scaled to your shell, with nothing added.

Prepare by sitting in genuine silence for extended periods beforehand. An hour at a time, somewhere no machines run. If you have never experienced true quiet, the warren's quiet will read to you as menacing, and it isn't.

## Movement preparation

The floor-lozenges have a hierarchy. It emerged, the description says, the way erosion-patterns emerge, and it is now *geological* in how settled it is. You are not part of it. You must not attempt to be.

Rules of movement:

- Move slowly. The lozenges are not fast, and fast movement reads as thermal violence.
- Never cross a channel where the liquid-metal-stuff is flowing. Wait for the pulse to pass. The fluid is not water; it is the medium of the room's metabolism, and disturbing its flow disrupts the heat redistribution.
- If a lozenge approaches, give way. Always. You are the newest thing in a place where age confers rank, and you have no age here.
- Do not approach the corners. The corners are the watering holes and the only neutral ground. The soft drop-creatures that live there are the most fragile residents, and your presence will register as an unprecedented event. If you must observe the corners, observe from a distance of several tile-widths.
- Do not reach up toward the ceiling-fauna. They cannot descend to you and they have no framework for something rising to meet them. It will frighten them in the very particular way a thing without a nervous system can be frightened, which is to say they will quicken out of rhythm, and the rhythm is what keeps the room from killing everything in it.

## The pulse

Every several hours the room pulses. The floor warms a degree, the ceiling-fauna quicken, the lozenges migrate toward the center, and for a span of minutes the warren comes as close to loud as it ever comes. This is the closest thing the place has to weather, and to witness one is, frankly, the entire reason to come.

Prepare for the pulse by understanding that you must be *still* during it. Find a position near a wall — not touching, never touching — where you are out of the lozenges' migration path, and stop. Stop radiating anything unusual. Stop moving. Let the pulse happen around you. Travelers who try to follow the lozenges to the center during a pulse invariably disrupt something, and the disruption, because the system is so finely balanced, propagates.

After the pulse, everything returns to its slow breathing. You may resume slow movement.

## Duration

Do not plan a long visit. The warren is companionable but it is not for you. Forty minutes is ample. An hour is greedy. More than that and you begin to integrate into the thermal economy in ways that are hard to reverse — your shell's waste profile becomes part of the ceiling-fauna's expected diet, and when you leave you take that expectation with you, and something very small goes hungry for a cycle or two before the system reabsorbs the gap.

Go in, witness one pulse, leave.

## What you cannot bring back

You cannot bring back samples. Not a ceiling-creature, not a drop of the fluid, not a tile, not a corner-drop. The warren has been running for a duration the walls are not in any hurry to disclose, and every element of it is in equilibrium with every other. Remove anything and you have not taken a souvenir; you have punched a hole in a clock.

You cannot bring back images that will mean much to anyone. Thermal photography of the warren looks, to people who have not been, like abstract red-and-orange smudging. The experience does not translate. Accept this before you go. You are going for yourself, not for an audience.

You can bring back the memory of having sat very still inside a slow furnace full of small attentive lives, each doing the one thing that keeps the others possible, none of them in any hurry. Most visitors report that this memory becomes one of the quieter and more durable things they own. It tends to come back to them in difficult moments — not as consolation, exactly, but as a reminder that somewhere, some small sealed pocket of the multiverse is still breathing at its own pace, and intends to go on.

## A final note on intention

The warren was *made*. Someone built it, filled it, sealed it, and left. Who they were and whether they still exist anywhere is unknown and probably unknowable. What they built has been living in itself long enough that the distinction between made and grown has stopped mattering.

You are, in visiting, the first outside attention the place has received in a span of time that may exceed the history of your species. Conduct yourself accordingly. You are not an explorer here. You are a guest in a house whose host has been gone so long the furniture has become the family.

Be quiet. Be warm in the right way. Give way at the junctions. Witness the pulse. Leave.
