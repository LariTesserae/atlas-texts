# Preparing to Visit Lowdamp: A Traveler's Guide to the Sump Works

## Before You Go

This is not a place that rewards urgency. If you are the sort of traveler who wants to see a location and tick it off — who moves through worlds the way people move through airports — Lowdamp will be wasted on you, and you will be wasted in it. The rhythm is too slow. You will become irritable within a day, unbearable within three, and by the end of the week you will do something rude to a toad and find yourself quietly, firmly escorted to the freight shaft without a word of explanation.

Go only if you can spare at least ten days. A month is better. The cavern does not *open* to short visits.

## What to Pack

**Clothing.** Forget everything you know about traveler's gear. Synthetics will make you miserable — they don't breathe, and in ninety-percent humidity you will stew in your own sweat until your skin breaks out in red bloom. Take cotton and linen, loose-cut, thin. Take more of it than you think, because nothing dries properly down there; you will rotate through damp garments for your entire stay, and what was worn yesterday will still be damp tomorrow. Rubber boots to mid-calf, fitted well — you will live in them. A pair of rubber-soled slippers for indoors. A waxed oilcloth cape for the first few days, until you stop caring about being wet.

Leave the hat. There is no sun. The gaslight will not burn you.

**A hand towel.** Not a small one. A proper absorbent cloth, carried over the shoulder or tucked in the belt, because you will be wiping your face constantly for the first week and then unconsciously for the rest of your life.

**Clay.** A small jar of unfired clay, or failing that, a lump of beeswax. This is for your ears — not to block the chorus, which would be both rude and futile, but to *dampen* it at night when you are trying to sleep and haven't yet learned the trick of hearing underneath it. Locals will not need this and may be amused that you do. That's fine. They were babies here once; they learned in the cradle.

**Writing materials in waxed oilcloth wrapping.** Paper rots. Ink runs. If you mean to keep notes, seal everything, and write quickly, and expect smears.

**A small light of your own.** The gaslights are enough for locals but not for newcomers. A shielded oil lamp is best — electric things tend to corrode within days in that atmosphere, and the batteries die strangely, as if the damp is pulling the charge out of them. Bring spare wicks.

**Salt tablets, or the equivalent.** You will sweat more than you have ever sweated. In the first three days you can become quietly, dangerously depleted without noticing, because the air is so wet that sweat does not evaporate and you cannot see how much you are losing. Drink constantly, salt constantly, and do not trust your thirst.

**Do not bring:** Perfume. Scented soap. Insect repellent. Anything aerosolized. The toads are sensitive to volatile compounds, and you will offend them before you have offended anyone else, which is a worse social error than it sounds. Wash with the gritty grey soap they sell at the works commissary. It smells of lye and nothing.

## Physical Preparation

Spend a month before your trip getting your lungs ready for thick air. Sauna sessions help — not the dry kind, the steam kind, and stay in longer than is comfortable. Your chest will learn. You need it to learn, because the first breath you take at the third landing will feel like drowning, and if you have not prepared, you will panic, and panic three hundred feet underground in a shaft is how people die or at minimum embarrass themselves irrecoverably.

Get your heart checked. The heat stress on the cardiovascular system is real. If you are old, or heavy, or unfit, acclimatize at the first landing for a full day before descending further. Nobody in Lowdamp will think less of you for this. They think less of people who rush.

Cut your nails short. You will be damp-handed constantly, and long nails soften and tear.

## Mental Preparation

This is the harder part. Lowdamp is a place that dismantles a certain kind of traveler.

**Learn to wait.** Before you go, practice sitting still for long periods. Not meditating — just sitting, watching something slow. A candle. A pot of water not quite boiling. A spider building. The cavern operates on a timescale that is not hostile to you but is simply indifferent to your sense of what a "day" should contain. A meeting that would take ten minutes elsewhere may take three afternoons here. A purchase may require two visits and a shared jar of water before price is even raised. If you cannot bear this, do not come.

**Prepare for the chorus.** The toads' croaking is continuous. It never stops. There is no silence in Lowdamp, not in any building, not at any hour. Some travelers find this maddening in the first two days and transcendent by the fifth. Some never cross that threshold. There is no way to know in advance which you will be, but you can prepare by listening to recordings of massed frogs for hours at a stretch before you go — not as a cure, but as an inoculation. The important thing is that the chorus is not *noise*. It is the voice of the place. Treating it as noise will mark you as an outsider in a way you cannot recover from.

**Expect weeping.** Yours, and everyone else's. The heat draws water from the eyes. It is not emotion, though sometimes it becomes emotion, as the body teaches the mind certain things through simple repetition. Do not comment on another person's wet face. Do not apologize for your own.

## Cultural Etiquette

**The toads.** Do not touch them unless invited. Do not step on them, which is harder than it sounds because they sit where they sit and do not move out of the way. You learn to step *around*. Children will laugh at you for the first few days as you do the newcomer's shuffle. Let them laugh; it's how they learn you're safe.

Never, *ever*, harm a toad outside rendering season, and do not ask when rendering season is — they will tell you if you need to know. If a toad chooses to climb onto you, let it. It will leave when it wants to. You will be mucus-streaked; this is fine. The streak dries to a faint iridescence and locals may greet you more warmly for the rest of that day. It is something like a benediction, though nobody will call it that.

**The buyers.** Do not ask. Not the foreman, not the pump-men, not the children, not the woman who sells clay jars at the head of the lane. The buyers are not discussed. If you are there for reasons connected to the buyers — which I suspect some multiverse travelers are, though I advise against it — make your arrangements before descending and do not speak of them below.

**The water.** Drink from the clay jars near the vents. Do not drink from the channels (obviously), and do not drink from standing pools (less obvious — the warm water looks clean). Boiled water is safe. The locals' bodies have adapted to things yours has not.

**Greetings.** A slow nod. A slower smile. Hand on chest briefly for elders or the foreman. Do not shake hands; hands here are always wet, and the ritual of dry-handed greeting from the surface world translates badly. Do not clap anyone on the shoulder, especially the old men; their skin, after decades down here, has become tender in ways you cannot see.

**Pace of speech.** Slow down. Repeat yourself if needed. The chorus swallows consonants. Speakers here have learned to front-load their sentences with vowels and to pause between phrases. Imitate this and you will be understood; persist in surface-world rapid-fire and you will be treated as slightly feeble, which is not dangerous but is tedious.

## What to Do While You Are There

Honestly? Not much. That is the point.

Sit on a brick pier in the evening — "evening" being a matter of shift change rather than sky — and watch the chorus swell when the gaslights turn up for the night work. Walk the two side-lanes and count the stalactite stubs. Drink from the clay jar. Let someone tell you about the pump that failed in their grandfather's time and took six years to properly fix. Nod. Do not offer engineering suggestions. Do not mention how they do things elsewhere.

Visit the foundry if invited, but do not linger; the heat there is beyond what a visitor can tolerate for long, and the men do not like being watched. Visit the rendering sheds only during rendering season and only if a local brings you. The smell, I am told, is indescribable, and not in the way travelers usually mean that word.

Sleep when you can. The humidity will give you strange dreams — vivid ones, wet ones, often featuring slow-moving water or masses of small breathing things. This is normal. Do not try to interpret them. They belong to the cavern more than to you.

## Leaving

Do not rush the ascent. Your lungs have adapted to thick air and will be confused by the thinness of the upper world. Rest at every landing. Drink water at each. The shaft takes hours going up that took minutes going down, and this is as it should be.

Expect to grieve a little, when you reach the surface. The world up there will seem thin, shrill, dry, over-lit. The silence will ring in your ears for days — because the chorus is gone, and you will have come, without noticing, to think underneath it. The thinking-underneath will persist for weeks. Some travelers find they never quite lose it.

This is the gift Lowdamp gives, if it gives you anything: a different floor to the mind, laid down in warm wet brick, beneath which something slow is always pulsing.

Carry it carefully. It is what you went for, even if you did not know.
