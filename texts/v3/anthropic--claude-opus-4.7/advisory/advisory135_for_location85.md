# Preparing for a Visit to the Seventh Terrace, Cauldron-Ninety-Four

## Before You Go

Understand first what kind of place this is. It is not a factory, though it industrially produces; not a garden, though it is tended; not a wilderness, though it is alive in ways no one fully controls. You are visiting a womb that has been running for seventy years. Carry yourself accordingly. Loud tourists are not turned away — they simply find, after a few hours, that they have nothing to say.

Give yourself at least three days on the terrace. One day is not enough to stop listening for your own world's sounds. The quiet here is not empty; it is full of very slow things, and you cannot hear slow things while your ears are still braced for traffic.

## Clothing and Body

**Footwear: none.** I cannot stress this enough. The foremen walk barefoot not for ritual but because leather genuinely rots in a week, and rubber soles slip on the copper dimples and crush the mosses that have taken decades to establish. Bring your feet. Trim your nails before you arrive — a torn nail in that warm wet environment will fungate within a day, and the frogs carry something mild but tenacious that likes open skin. If you cannot bear to be barefoot, the terrace-keepers will loan you a pair of woven hemp slippers that dissolve gracefully into the compost when worn out. Accept them with thanks and do not try to keep a pair as a souvenir; they will liquefy in your luggage.

**Clothing: linen or raw silk, loose, undyed.** Synthetic fabrics sweat badly in the greenhouse weight and, more importantly, shed microfibers that the ecology has no idea what to do with. Wool is too warm. Cotton works but mildews quickly. Bring two sets so one can dry while you wear the other, though "dry" is an aspirational word here. Nothing dries. You will be permanently damp. Accept this on arrival rather than fighting it for six hours first.

**Leave behind:** perfumes, scented soap, deodorant with fragrance, insect repellent, sunscreen, hair product. The hover-midges breed in the dimples and a single person with citronella can sterilize a square meter of micro-ecology for a season. Wash with the grey soap provided, which is made on-site from the foundry's tallow and has no scent beyond a faint iron note.

**Bring:** a small towel of your own, a wide-brimmed hat of woven plant fiber (the drip from the ceiling pipes is constant and will drive you slightly mad if it keeps landing on your scalp), and, if you are prone to ear infections, beeswax plugs for sleeping.

## Acclimation

The altitude is real. Four thousand meters is not trivial. Spend two days at the foundry-village below the Cauldron before ascending — drink their bitter tea, which is dosed with something from the red fern, and sleep badly for a night as your body recalibrates. Ascending directly from the lowlands will give you the shakes by hour three and you will remember nothing of your first day.

The air at the terrace is saturated. Breathing feels like drinking, the description is exact. If you have asthma, pulmonary problems, or a recent respiratory infection, do not go. This is not a place where you can be evacuated quickly; the crane that lowers finished cliffs also lowers unconscious tourists, and it takes forty minutes to rig.

## What to Bring For the Mind

A notebook with waxed pages — ordinary paper pulps in an hour. A pencil, not a pen. Something to read that you have already read, because new text will not hold you here; your attention will keep drifting to the trellises. A small folding stool if your knees are bad, because there are no chairs on the seventh terrace and the foremen consider this a feature.

Do not bring a camera. You will be tempted. Resist. Photographs of the terrace are uniformly disappointing — the density reads as clutter, the light is always wrong, the scale collapses. What you want to preserve is not visual. Sketch instead, badly, in pencil. The act of looking long enough to draw a single fern frond will teach you more about the place than a thousand photographs.

## Protocols of the Place

**Do not pick anything.** This should be obvious but visitors do it anyway, always apologetically, always "just one leaf." The ninety-one species are in a balance that took decades to find. A single orchid-flower removed is a small murder.

**Do not touch the unfinished clay.** The matrices on the trellises are at various stages of setting; the oils from a human hand can derail a chemistry that has been accreting for years. The finished cliff by the east stair you may touch, gently, with the back of your hand. Many visitors do. It is warm. It feels like stone that remembers being alive.

**Do not try to see the frogs.** They live in the fissure and they do not want to be seen. Bringing a light into the fissure is considered the gravest possible discourtesy. Sit near the cliff at the thermal transitions — roughly an hour after dawn and an hour before dusk, when the slurry-pipes shift temperature — and listen. You will hear the click. One click. That is the whole encounter. Do not wait for more.

**Walk the same path.** The foremen maintain a route across the terrace that has been walked so long the moss has given up on it. Use this path. Do not explore. The temptation to push through the leaves into some unseen corner is strong and must be refused; you will crush things that have no names yet.

**Speak quietly or not at all.** The foremen communicate mostly in gestures. Match them. Your voice carries strangely in the curved iron space and will disturb the hover-midges, who navigate by air pressure.

## A Harder Preparation

You should sit, before you go, with the question the place poses. The Cauldron may have replaced the world's cliffs, quietly, over a century. The hike you took last summer to that scenic ravine — was it born or was it made? Was it shipped there by crane in sections and installed while the nearby village slept?

You will not resolve this question on the terrace. You will only feel it more acutely. Some visitors find this liberating — if all landscape is made, then making landscape is a holy craft, and the foundry-masters are priests of a kind. Others find it unbearable and leave weeping. A few become very quiet for a long time afterward and return home to discover they can no longer enjoy a hike without listening for the hum of deep machinery beneath the trail.

Decide, before you go, which kind of person you are willing to become. You do not get to choose, exactly, but you can prepare the ground.

## On Leaving

When you descend, you will carry spores. This is unavoidable and not a problem — the ecology of the terrace has been seeding the lowlands for seventy years and the world has absorbed it. But rinse your hair in the grey soap before you take the lift down, and leave your hemp slippers in the composting bin by the east stair. Do not bring anything living out with you deliberately. The frogs, especially, should stay where they are, growing slowly larger, their call descending by quarter-tones into a register no one has heard yet.

## One Last Thing

Listen for the frog on your last evening. Sit by the fissure at the thermal transition. Do not bring a light. Do not speak. Wait for the single click.

Whatever pitch it is when you hear it — remember it. That note is the clock of the place. If you return in ten years, the click will be lower, and you will know how much of your own life has passed by how far the frog has descended. This is one of the few honest timepieces in the multiverse. Most travelers, upon hearing it, decide to come back.

Bring warm socks for the descent. Your feet will have forgotten shoes.
