# Preparing for the Lacquer Ward

## First, accept that you cannot plan to go there

The Ward is not a destination. It is an encounter. You will not find it by looking for it; if anything, looking probably makes the seams close. Your preparation, therefore, is not for *getting in* but for *being ready when you slip*. Carry the kit always, in whatever city of whatever world you are wandering through, and one evening a service door behind a canal lock will be slightly ajar, and you will be glad.

This means the gear must be light enough to live in your coat pockets permanently without becoming a burden. Choose accordingly.

## Clothing

Wear wool, or something that behaves like wool — fiber that stays warm when saturated. Cotton will kill you slowly over two hours of standing condensate; synthetics will merely make you miserable. Over the wool, an oilskin or waxed canvas outer layer, long enough to cover your thighs. Not a modern rain shell: the Ward's water is not rain, and modern membranes assume rain. Oilskin sheds the Ward's honest wetness in a way Gore-Tex cannot, because oilskin does not try to breathe and the Ward will not let anything breathe.

Boots must be rubber-soled and have real tread. The cobbles are glossed black and the water is everywhere and the buildings move — you will need to pivot suddenly, and you will need to pivot on wet stone. Leather uppers are fine; they will be ruined; this is acceptable. Consider gaiters.

A hat with a brim. Not a hood. You need peripheral vision because the architecture is reshuffling behind you and you will want to notice the pipe-bundle descending toward your ankles before it reaches them. A hood narrows your world, and the Ward punishes narrowed worlds.

Gloves: thin, waterproof, with bare fingertips or easily peeled back. You will want to touch things — the growing quartz, the fern fronds, a coin from a frog — and you will want your hands warm between touches.

## What to carry

**A small notebook in waxed paper or oilcloth binding, and a pencil.** Not a pen. Ink runs in the Ward; pencil writes on wet paper. Write down what you see quickly, in fragments. You will forget specifics within an hour of leaving, and the specifics are the only things worth having brought back.

**A length of chalk.** For marking the door you came in by — not that it will stay marked, not that the door will stay where you marked it, but the attempt is a courtesy to yourself and may help. Mark on both sides of the threshold if you can.

**A watch that is purely mechanical.** Electronics in general are a gamble here; anything with a clock chip or GPS is likely to behave oddly or brick itself in the vault's glow. A wind-up watch will keep time, more or less, and more or less is what you need. Do not trust it precisely. Two hours, to you, may be something else to the street you left.

**A small amount of currency of several kinds.** Coins especially. If you drop one in the frog-pool by the mill, the frog will bring you another. This is a kind of conversation. You should have something to say back. Do not bring gold; bring copper and small silver, the kind of coins that do not insult either party.

**A handkerchief.** For your face, your hands, the lens of anything you might foolishly have brought with a lens. Everything fogs.

**A flask of something warm.** Tea, broth. Not alcohol — the Ward's pressure-pulse and the low ceiling will already slightly alter your balance and your sense of time, and drink compounds this. Warmth, yes. Intoxication, no.

**A folding knife,** small, for gaskets and cords and the occasional unexpected need. Not a weapon. Nothing there needs fighting.

**A piece of bread or dry biscuit in wax paper.** You will not be hungry. You will, on emerging, discover that you are ravenous in the specific way of people who have been working harder than they knew. Eat before you reach your own door.

## What *not* to carry

Cameras are mostly useless. The even, shadowless oyster-light flattens everything; photos come back looking like photos of a grey wall. If you must, bring film, not digital. Film sometimes takes.

Do not carry anything you cannot afford to have soaked, stolen by a marten, or quietly replaced with something almost-the-same. Check your pockets before leaving. Check them again. The Ward gives gifts; some gifts are frogs.

Do not bring anyone who will ask loud questions. The residents are at peace with strangeness and will tolerate a quiet visitor indefinitely, but they are busy, and the Ward responds — I am fairly sure — to attention. Loud attention seems to accelerate the reshuffling. A companion is fine if the companion knows how to be quiet in a strange room.

## Mental preparation

This is the larger part of the preparation, and the part most travelers neglect.

**Abandon the idea that you will understand what is being made.** The mill grinds something; the barge carries crates; the furnace consumes something the woman in the apron feeds it. You will not find out. Trying to find out will exhaust you and will probably offend no one but will certainly waste your two hours. Let the industry be industry. Watch how it is done, not what it produces.

**Accept soft causality.** Gutters run uphill sometimes. The canal reverses. A barge passes through an arch smaller than itself. If you stand at any of these phenomena demanding an explanation, you will pin yourself in place while the Ward moves around you and you will miss everything else. The correct response to every impossibility here is a small nod, as if someone had told you the time.

**Practice being unsurprising.** The residents are not unfriendly but they are not curious about you, and you should return the courtesy. Do not stare. Do not photograph them. Do not ask for directions — there are no stable directions. If someone nods at you, nod back. If someone does not, do not.

**Prepare to leave through a different door than the one you entered.** This is important. You must internalize, before you go in, that the way out will not be the way in, and that this is not a problem, it is the normal operation of the place. Travelers who insist on retracing their steps get stuck — not trapped, exactly, but looping, finding the same alley again and again while the exit they want stays sealed. The Ward lets you out when you stop asking to go back and start simply going.

**Set an internal limit.** Two hours is, by the reporter's account, about right. Much longer and the pulse of the boiler starts to feel like your pulse, and things begin to become difficult to disentangle. If you find you have lost track of time (your mechanical watch will not help as much as you hope), make for water — the canal, a gutter, any running surface — and follow it. Water in the Ward tends toward exits.

## On the frogs

You will come out with frogs in your pockets. Everyone does. Release them into wet stone, not dry ground. They want gutters. Do not try to keep one. They do not survive outside the Ward, and more importantly, keeping one is a kind of theft the Ward notices, and the next seam you find may not open.

## On what you will bring back that is not frogs

Your coat will smell of wet cedar and hot brass and crushed fern for days. This is not removable; do not try. It fades on its own over perhaps a week. Some travelers report that during that week, they dream more vividly, and that in the dreams they are finishing a task they do not remember beginning. This passes.

You will, for some time after, find yourself checking the seams of ordinary walls. You will look at service doors with a new attention. This, too, passes, though more slowly, and some travelers say it never entirely does. Consider this part of the cost of admission. It is not a high cost. The Ward is worth it.

## A final note

If, while you are there, you hear the thing in the vault above the rooftops — the sound like wet canvas being folded — do not look up. Not because looking up is dangerous, but because the residents do not look up, and you are a guest, and guests observe the local courtesies. Whatever is above the rooftops is above the rooftops. Let it be. Walk on. The mill is grinding; the boiler is breathing; a child is dropping pebbles in the canal; a door is opening for you somewhere you did not expect, and your coat is already soaked through.

Go.
