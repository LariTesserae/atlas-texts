# Preparing to Visit the Cupped City

## Before You Go: The Orientation Problem

The first thing to understand is that this is not a place you visit the way you visit a city. It is a place you visit the way you visit a tidepool, or a nest, or a sleeping animal. Your scale is wrong. Your relationship to the architecture is not "inhabitant" or even "tourist" — it is closer to "weather." You will loom. You cannot help looming. Make peace with this before you arrive, because there is no graceful way to discover it once you're there.

Spend time, in the weeks before, holding small things. A thimble. A single die. A grain of rice. Look at them until you can see them as *buildings* — until you can feel, in your chest, what it would mean to live inside one. This is not a mystical exercise. It is a calibration. Travelers who skip it tend to experience the Cupped City as quaint, which is the one response that will poison the visit entirely. The city is not quaint. You are simply enormous.

## Physical Preparation

**Altitude.** The basin sits high. Give yourself three to five days of acclimatization at elevation before the final approach. The thin air matters not just for your lungs but for your attention — at altitude, with low oxygen, you will find yourself going still in ways that serve this place. Don't fight that stillness. It is, in a sense, what you came for.

**Clothing.** Layers you can shed. The basin is warm and humid despite the mountain cold outside, and you will sweat through anything heavy within an hour of settling in. Bring something dark and non-reflective for your outer layer — bright colors in that dim underwater light feel like an insult, though no one will tell you so. Soft fabrics only. Nothing that rustles.

**Knees.** You will spend a great deal of time kneeling, crouching, or lying flat. Pad them. Bring something waterproof to lie on — the lip of the basin is wet everywhere, and you will want to get your face close to the pools for long stretches. A folded wool blanket wrapped in oilcloth works better than any technical mat.

**Hands.** Keep them clean and keep them *away*. The oils from human skin will sit on that stone for centuries. If you must touch — and sometimes you must, to steady yourself on the descent into the hollow — use the back of a knuckle on a wet surface, where the weeping will wash you off within the hour.

## What to Bring

- A small, dim light. Not a torch. Something the size and brightness of a single candle flame, shielded. The city's own twilight is the correct light for seeing it; anything brighter will flatten the reflections from below and you will lose the city's particular way of being lit.
- A soft cloth for your face. The humidity will bead on your eyelashes and run into your eyes when you bend close to the water.
- Water and a little food, eaten outside the basin. Do not eat inside. Crumbs in that pool would persist.
- A notebook, but pencil only, and write it outside the hollow. Inside, just watch.
- Something small of your own that you are willing to leave, if it comes to that. More on this below.

## What Not to Bring

No cameras. I say this not because photography is forbidden — there is no one to forbid it — but because the flat record will betray you afterward. The city does not photograph well. Its whole nature is a thing of scale, wet light, and the slow pressure of being looked at back. A picture will show you a wet rock with small stones on it, and you will doubt your own memory, and something will have been lost that you cannot recover. Use your eyes.

No offerings of food or flowers or coins. The builders did not ask for these, and the ecosystem cannot process them. A copper coin dropped into that pool will still be a copper coin in a thousand years, and the shrimp will have to graze around it.

No voice recorders, music, or anything that emits sound. The silence there is not absence; it is a substance. You are entering it.

## The Descent

There is no single way in. The ridge folds over the hollow, and you will need to find a crack, a chute, a place where you can lower yourself down onto the basin's lip. Wherever you enter, *stop* before you can see the city. Stand with your back to the opening and let your eyes adjust to the dim. Let your breath settle. The first sight of the city matters, and you want to be steady when it happens.

Approach the lip on your belly, slowly, over the last few yards. Not out of reverence exactly — out of practicality. A person walking upright to the edge of the basin casts a shadow that crosses the entire city at once, and the shrimp will scatter, and it will take an hour for the place to settle again. You will have arrived and immediately disturbed everything. Crawl.

## Inside the Bowl

Lie down. Get comfortable. You are going to be here a long time.

The first fifteen minutes you will see almost nothing. Your eyes are still wrong for the light. Let them adjust. In the meantime, listen. Count the drops falling from the roof-edges. There are, if you listen carefully, three distinct rhythms to the weeping of the walls, and they almost but not quite sync up. Finding them is a way of slowing your nervous system down to the city's tempo.

After half an hour your eyes will have given you the basin. After an hour they will have given you the city. After two hours they will have given you the shrimp. After three, if you are patient, they will have given you the iridescent surface-beetles, which are the hardest things to see and, I suspect, the most important.

Do not try to see everything. There is nothing to see. There is only this: the place exists, and you are inside it looking, and the looking is what the visit is for.

## On Being Looked At

At some point, a shrimp will stop and turn and look at you.

Prepare for this. Not emotionally — you cannot prepare emotionally for this, and the travelers who think they have are the ones most undone when it happens. Prepare *practically*. Decide in advance what you will do.

My advice: do nothing. Do not move. Do not try to communicate. Do not feel that you must *acknowledge* the moment. The shrimp is not asking anything of you. It is simply confirming something — what, we do not know, and we may be wrong that there is anything to confirm at all. Your task is to be a thing that can be looked at. Let it look. Let it finish. Let it go back to its grazing.

Afterward you may cry. Many people do. That is fine. Cry quietly and try to keep tears off the surface of the pool.

## The Question of the Builders

You will want to theorize. Everyone does. You will lie there and spin stories: they were driven out, they were rescued, they ascended, they shrank, they are hibernating somewhere in the basin walls, they are the shrimp, they are the beetles, they are gone, they are watching, they never existed and the city built itself.

Let yourself do this for about an hour and then stop. None of the theories are satisfying because none of them are the point. The point is that something made this place, and the thing that made it is not here, and the place remains. This is true of a great many places in a great many worlds. The Cupped City is just unusually honest about it.

## Leaving

This is the hardest part, and the part most travelers do badly.

Do not leave at the moment you feel you are done. That is too soon. Your sense of completion is a human artifact and has nothing to do with the city. Stay an additional hour after you feel finished. In that hour you will either settle into something deeper, or you will feel an increasing urgency to go, at which point: go.

Back out the way you came. Do not stand up inside the hollow. Do not turn and take a last look from the lip — or if you must, do it once, and briefly, and then go.

Leave nothing. Take nothing. If you brought something to leave — and I mentioned above that you might — consider whether you were right to bring it. Most travelers who planned to leave something decide, in the end, not to. The city has enough. Adding to it feels, once you are there, like an act of vanity. But some travelers do leave something small, set carefully on a high dry ledge above the waterline, where it will not disturb the pools. A button. A single bead. A folded scrap of paper with nothing written on it. This is permitted, I think, but only if the impulse arrives inside the basin, not before.

## Afterward

You will be quiet for several days. Do not try to describe the visit to anyone during this time; you will get it wrong and fix the wrong version in your memory. Wait a week. Then, if you want to, write it down — longhand, slowly, with the understanding that you are not capturing the place but making a small separate thing that gestures toward it.

You may find, months or years later, that you have begun moving more carefully through your own life. Stepping around small beetles. Leaving spiders alone. Looking down more often. This is a common aftereffect. It is not a burden. It is, I think, the city's single small gift to its visitors — a recalibration of what deserves to be noticed.

The shrimp, after all, looked at you. It took you in. It considered you. And it decided you were a thing worth a moment of its attention, in its short precise life, in the middle of the only city it will ever know.

Try to be worth that, afterward. That is the whole preparation, really. Everything else is just knees and cloth.
