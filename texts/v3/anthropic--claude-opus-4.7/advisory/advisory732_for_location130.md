# Preparing to Visit the Drift-City of Hollow Spires

## Understand First What You Are Visiting

Before anything practical, sit with this: you are not visiting a city. You are stepping into the middle of a thought that a very large, very slow entity is having. The "city" is closer to a cortex than to a settlement. The fauna are closer to the firings of that cortex than to inhabitants. Nothing there is decorative. Nothing is incidental. Every drip of seep-water, every click in the mid-register, every pulse in the capillaries is *load-bearing* in a computation that began before your species existed and will conclude after it is gone.

This matters because the single most important thing you can do as a visitor is **not interfere**. Not out of politeness — out of the recognition that you do not know what a single misplaced footstep might cost, or when the cost would come due. You might crush a filament of mineral felt today that was supposed to reach a particular fracture line in four thousand years. You would never know. Neither, probably, would anyone else. But the thought would come out slightly wrong.

Go there to witness. Not to touch, not to sample, not to understand. To witness.

## Physical Preparation

**Thermal:** The temperature hovers just above the fog-point. You will not freeze, but you will lose heat steadily and without drama through the wet air, the wet ground, and the wet stone. Wear insulation that does not rely on air gaps — closed-cell foam layers, or a sealed drysuit with underlayers. Assume everything you wear will be soaked through on the outside within an hour. Plan for your inner layer to stay dry for ten or twelve hours; after that, return or suffer.

**Footwear:** The stone is glassy, unweathered, unfractured. It is the slickest surface you have ever walked on, and it is wet. Studded soles will skate; the rock is too hard and smooth for studs to bite. You want a soft sticky compound — climbing rubber, or something tackier — with a flat, large contact patch. Accept that you will fall anyway. Pad your knees, elbows, and the base of your spine. A fall onto that stone is a fall onto tempered glass.

**Breathing:** The air is breathable, probably. Test before removing a respirator. But even if it is breathable, your breath will fog conspicuously, and fog is a disturbance in a place this finely tuned. Consider a closed-circuit rebreather that captures and recycles your exhalations, or at minimum a warming scarf that breaks up and diffuses the plume. Your warmth is pollution here.

**Hydration and waste:** Bring every drop of water you intend to drink, and every means of containing every drop you intend to excrete. Do not drink the seep-water. Do not touch the seep-water with your bare skin if you can avoid it — not because it will harm you (it probably won't) but because it is *doing something*, and your skin oils and salts will interfere. Seal your waste in double containment and carry it out. All of it.

**Light:** Do not bring active illumination. No headlamps, no flashlights, no phone screens. The local light is on a rhythm, and your photons would be a shout in a library. If you cannot see well enough by the capillary-pulse to move safely, then you should not be moving. Sit. Let your eyes adjust over an hour, then two. You will begin to see. The dark is not absence here; it is a medium.

## Sensory and Cognitive Preparation

**Acoustic:** The sound is the hardest thing. It is loud, it is layered, and it is *intentional* — you will feel, after a few minutes, that it is speaking, and after an hour that it is speaking *to* you, and after three hours that it has always been speaking to you and you are only now remembering how to listen. This is a trap, probably. Or it is real, which is worse.

Bring high-fidelity passive attenuators — not noise-canceling headphones, which would generate their own signal, but wax or silicone plugs that bring the overall volume down by twenty or thirty decibels while preserving the structure. You want to hear the chord. You do not want to be inside it at full strength for long.

Practice, before you go, sitting for extended periods in overwhelming but non-threatening sound — heavy rain, a waterfall, a cathedral during a long bell. Learn what it feels like when your thoughts start to synchronize with an external rhythm, and learn to notice it and step back.

**The long tone:** When the single clear tone sounds, you will want to stop. Everything stops. You may feel compelled to hold your breath, or to turn toward the source, or to answer. Do not answer. Do not attempt to record the tone for later study, either — or rather, record it if you like, but know that the recording will not capture what made you stop. What made you stop was not in the sound.

**Time perception:** Nothing here moves at a human tempo. After some hours you will feel either that time has stopped or that you have been there for days. Bring a mechanical watch (not digital — the capillary-light's non-closing loops may or may not interact with semiconductors; don't find out with something you need). Check it often. Set hard departure times and honor them even when you don't want to.

The dynamic of the place is slow enough that you can convince yourself nothing is happening, and then look up and find that the spire you were standing near is now behind you, or that the felt has grown up over your boots. Neither of these will actually happen in the time you are there. But you will *feel* that they could, and the feeling is close enough to the truth that you should respect it.

## What to Bring

- A sealed sitting pad — you will want to sit, for long periods, and the ground will leach heat from you if you sit on it directly.
- Mechanical timekeeping.
- Closed-system food: nothing aromatic, nothing crumbling. The fauna may react to you regardless, but you do not need to broadcast.
- A notebook and a pencil. Write by the capillary-light. Your notes will be nearly illegible later. This is fine. The notes are not the point; the writing is.
- Passive acoustic attenuators.
- A partner, tethered. Tethered because it is easy to lose sight of each other in the density and the dark, and because the spires *shift*, and a separation of twenty meters could become a separation of an hour's careful navigation. Stay roped.
- A clear, pre-agreed exit protocol. Decide before you arrive how you will know it is time to leave. The place will not tell you. Or it will, but you will not be able to trust what it tells you.

## What Not to Bring

- Active electronics, beyond your watch and minimum life-support.
- Any biological sample from your home timeline — seeds, spores, cultures, anything. The local biology, if it is biology, has been tuned for a very long time. Do not be the person who introduces something new.
- The intention to collect samples going the other way. You are not qualified to know what a sample of the mineral felt would do in a laboratory, and neither is anyone you know. Leave it be.
- Firearms, cutting tools beyond a small utility knife, anything that could be used to take a piece of the place by force. If you feel you need these for safety, do not go; you are not ready.
- Strong emotions you have not processed. The place will amplify them. Grief in particular seems to resonate. Go when you are steady.

## Mental and Ethical Preparation

**Accept that you will not understand what you see.** The "fauna" may not be animals. The "buildings" may not be buildings. The relationships between the things you see may not map to any category you have — predator and prey, infrastructure and occupant, tool and user. You will try to sort what you see into these categories anyway, because your mind is built to. Let the sorting happen and then let it go. What you saw is not what your mind sorted it into.

**Accept that something may notice you.** The long tone, the capillary pulse, the breath against your ankle — you may at some point feel unambiguously that you have been registered. This feeling may be accurate. If it happens, do not run, do not speak, do not gesture. Incline your head slightly, the way you would to something in a temple, and wait. The attention, if it is attention, will pass. It has many other things to attend to, and they are all more important than you.

**Accept that you may want to stay.** This is the deepest hazard, and the hardest to prepare for. The place is saturated with a kind of slow meaning, and a human mind, starved for meaning in the ordinary way, will drink from it and want to drink more. Some visitors, I suspect — though I have no proof — have sat down on a ledge of glassy stone and simply not gotten up, and the mineral felt has grown over them, and they have been integrated into the long slow thought, and this was not done to them but accepted by them. Prepare for the pull. Know the names of the people who are waiting for you at home. Write them in the notebook. Read them aloud, quietly, when you feel the pull.

**Go with a question, not a goal.** Goals will be frustrated; the place does not offer anything that can be achieved. But a question — a real one, something you have carried for years — may find, in the layered chord and the slow pulse and the patient dark, something that functions as an answer. Not a stated answer. A shape, in the sound, that your question fits into. This is the gift the place can give, if it gives any gift. Bring a question worth asking.

## On Leaving

Leave before you want to. Leave while you still want to come back.

When you step back off the ledge and out of the city's thinking, you will find that the ordinary world is painfully fast, painfully warm, painfully bright, and painfully thin. This will pass. Give it weeks. Do not make major decisions during this period. Do not try to describe the place to anyone who did not go — not yet, not until the description comes without strain. The description that comes easily and early is always wrong.

And for a long time afterward, when you hear a certain kind of sustained tone — a bell, a sung note, a resonance in a pipe — you will briefly stop what you are doing, and then resume. This is fine. This is what you brought back. It is a small part of the thought, and it is thinking in you now, very slowly, and it does not require anything from you except that you occasionally stop, and listen, and then go on.
