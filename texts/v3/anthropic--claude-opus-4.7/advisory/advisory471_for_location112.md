# Preparing to Visit the Engine Hollow

## Understand What You Are Walking Into

Before anything else: understand that the Engine Hollow is not a place you visit. It is a place that considers whether to let you remain a visitor. Every hour you spend there is an hour the hollow spends deciding whether you are a guest or a new permanent feature of its ecology. The longer you stay, the less say you have in that decision.

Plan your trip backwards from your departure. The departure is the hard part.

## Timing Your Stay

Limit your visit to under four hours on your first trip. This is the threshold before entrainment begins in earnest — the piston is 2.3 seconds, which is slower than resting respiration for most humans, so your breathing will slow to meet it without your permission. Four hours is roughly the window in which your body can be coaxed back to its own rhythm once you surface.

Bring a mechanical metronome. Not digital — the hollow's dust gets into electronics in ways that are subtle and later. Set it to a tempo that is *incommensurable* with 2.3 seconds. Something like 73 bpm, a prime-adjacent number that will never phase-lock with the piston. Keep it in your breast pocket. When you leave, follow the metronome, not your breath.

Bring a second metronome as backup. The first one may stop working for reasons that will not be mechanical.

## What to Wear

**Skin:** Cover everything. The walls are conchoidal basalt glass and they will open you without your noticing, and the dust will enter the cut, and the cut will close tomorrow with the dust inside. This is how people come back changed in small ways they cannot locate.

Long sleeves, heavy canvas gloves, eye protection. A scarf or filter mask over the mouth — not to keep dust out of your lungs (it is already too late for that; the dust is very fine and you have been breathing it since you entered) but to slow the rate.

**Feet:** Thick-soled boots. The floor is always lightly powdered. You will leave footprints. The footprints will be gone by the time you turn around, and this is fine, but do not try to retrace them. The hollow does not remember you were there; behave accordingly.

**Temperature:** Dress for 35°C, dry heat, no wind. You will sweat and the sweat will evaporate instantly into the dryness that has forgotten what dryness means. Bring three times the water you think you need. Drink on a schedule you set before entering, not when you feel thirsty — your thirst signal is one of the first things the hollow quiets.

## What to Bring

- **Water.** Far more than feels reasonable. Stored in the metal canteen you brought down, not in anything you picked up in the hollow.
- **Salt tablets.** Electrolyte loss will be severe and unnoticed.
- **A sealed lunch,** which you will probably not want to eat. Eat it anyway, on schedule. Eating is an act of your own metabolism, and the hollow respects metabolisms that insist on themselves.
- **Notebook and pencil.** Not pen — ink behaves oddly in the dust. Pencil is fine. Write things down even if they seem obvious. *Especially* if they seem obvious. The hollow makes things seem obvious that are not.
- **A piece of home.** Something small, with personal meaning — a photograph, a ring, a letter. Keep it in an inner pocket. When you feel your pulse begin to match the piston, touch it. This is a tether and it is more important than any instrument.
- **A small, loud whistle.** You will not be heard over the engine. That is not what it is for. It is for you to hear, to startle yourself out of the acoustic bath when you need to.

## What NOT to Bring, and What NOT to Do

**Do not bring offerings to the shrines.** This is the single strongest recommendation. The shrines are tended. The dust is sorted. Whatever sorts it has a system, and that system does not include you, and contributing to it means participating in it, and participating in it is one of the ways the hollow writes you into its permanent configuration. Walk past the shrines. Note them. Do not touch the bowls. Do not add dust. Do not remove dust. Do not even photograph them if you can help it.

**Do not touch the door.** The wheel turns freely. This is a temptation. The door is in a state of perfect readiness that is not yours to spend. Opening it would be — at minimum — using up a moment that was being saved for something else, by something else. The consequences of this are not known because nobody has been stupid enough yet, and you should not be the first.

**Do not harm the fauna.** Not the moths, not the crabs, not the listeners, not the mites, not the slug. You can cut a stalk and it will not have been cut tomorrow, but this is the stalk's prerogative, not yours. The fauna are aware of you in the way a saturated solution is aware of a speck of dust: you are a potential nucleation site. Do not give them a reason to start crystallizing around you.

**Do not try to talk to anything.** You are not loud enough. This is merciful. If you ever become loud enough — if the listeners turn toward you — leave immediately.

**Do not sit down.** Stand, crouch, move slowly, but do not sit. Sitting is a posture of belonging. The hollow reads postures.

**Do not sleep there.** Under any circumstances.

## Psychological Preparation

The hollow is full and at equilibrium. This means it is, in some real sense, at peace. This peace is seductive. You will feel, after perhaps forty minutes, a great and unexpected relief — the relief of being in a place where nothing needs to be done, nothing is ending, nothing is beginning, nothing is required of you. This feeling is accurate. It is also the mechanism.

Practice beforehand the habit of noticing when you feel at ease and treating that as an alarm. Meditators, paradoxically, are at higher risk here than anxious people. The anxious will flee the noise. The calm will settle into it.

Before you enter, write on the inside of your arm, in permanent marker, the time you intend to leave and the name of someone you love. When you look at your arm and cannot remember why those words matter, it is well past time to go.

## The Return

Exit moving. Do not pause at the threshold to look back. Looking back is something people who belong to a place do.

For the first 24 hours after surfacing, stay in loud, variable, unpredictable environments. A city street. A busy kitchen. A room with children. The hollow's rhythm needs to be washed out by arhythmia, by interruption, by the ordinary mess of surface life where nothing is at its final configuration.

Expect to find dust in your belongings for weeks. Expect to find it after that, too, in places you are sure you cleaned. It is consumed by surfaces and also settles on them. This is simply how the hollow works, and a little of the hollow came back with you. Accept this. Do not try to sweep the dust into a bowl and sort it by color. If you find yourself wanting to, call the person whose name is on your arm.

## A Final Note

Some travelers report, months after returning, a faint sense that there is a version of themselves still there — standing in the amber dusk, at final size, always having been — and that this version is content. This report is common enough to be taken seriously. Whether it is a trick of memory, a genuine ontological spur the hollow grew off you, or something else, is not known.

If you feel this, and you feel it as a pull rather than a curiosity: do not go back. That is not a homecoming waiting for you. That is a vacancy the hollow is keeping warm, and the hollow is very patient, and the hollow has already decided it has room.

Go. Look. Leave. Do not return.

The engine coughs. The flywheels turn. It does not need you. That is the best thing about it, and the worst.
