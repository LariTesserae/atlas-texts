# Preparing to Visit the Sump-City

## First, understand what you are walking into

This is not a ruin to be explored. It is not a wilderness to be survived. It is something in between and stranger than either — a place where intention has drained out and been replaced, molecule by molecule, by a different kind of order. You are going to be, for as long as you stay, the loudest and most structured thing there. Prepare accordingly. The discipline of this visit is mostly a discipline of reduction: of noise, of motion, of your own insistence on being a visitor with a purpose.

## Clothing and body

Wear as little as decency and your own nerve allow. The heat is the wet, suffocating kind where sweat does not evaporate because the air is already full; whatever you wear will soak through within minutes and stay soaked. Choose thin, pale, quick-rinsing fabric — linen, technical mesh, something that will not chafe once heavy with water. Long sleeves and long trousers anyway, loose, because you do not want bare skin brushing the walls. The frogs are not the problem. The things you aren't looking closely at, under the overhangs, may secrete. Pale amphibian skin in hot wet dark is almost always chemically defended in some way, and you have no pharmacopoeia for this biosphere.

On your feet: something closed, soft-soled, that you can walk in through ankle-deep warm water for hours without blistering. Not boots — boots will fill and stay full. Think of the shoes a reef-walker wears, or thin neoprene booties with a tough sole. You will be stepping, constantly, around and sometimes over living things. You need to feel the ground through your shoe. A hard sole makes you clumsy and makes the eels flinch, which is the first sign you have become a disturbance.

Bring a wide-brimmed soft hat. The light is shadowless but it is still light, and hours under a pearl haze will cook the top of your head in a way you won't notice until you're unwell.

## Water and food

Do not drink the water in the channels. It is warm, it is moving, and it is the circulatory system of ten thousand small lives — everything here urinates and sheds and breathes into that water. Carry your own, more than you think, because you will sweat continuously and the heat will draw you down fast if you stint. Plan on drinking twice what you'd drink on a hot dry hike. Electrolytes matter more than calories here; a day in that bowl without salt replacement and you will start making bad decisions.

Eat beforehand. Eat lightly if at all while inside. Unwrapping food in that hush feels obscene, and crumbs will draw attention from things you hadn't noticed were paying attention.

## The question of contamination — both directions

This is the single most important preparation, and travelers new to multiverse work tend to underweight it.

*You* are a contaminant. You carry skin flora, gut flora, spores in your hair, bacteria under your nails, all tuned to a different world. An ecosystem this enclosed, this stable, this quietly balanced — a pocket a few hundred paces across that has been settling for an unknowable span — can be knocked sideways by a single sneeze. Before you cross the rim, wash. Strip and rinse in clean water. Cut your nails. If you have been sick in the past two weeks, do not go. If you have taken antibiotics in the past month, do not go; you are shedding resistance genes into a world that has never encountered them.

*The city* is a contaminant too. Whatever lives on those walls has had a very long time to develop chemistry. Do not touch surfaces with bare hands. Do not let water from the channels enter your eyes, mouth, or any cut. Carry a small sealed bottle of clean water for rinsing if you slip. If something bites or stings you, leave immediately. You will not identify it, and the travel back through whatever threshold brought you here is the only medicine you have.

When you return: quarantine yourself and your gear. Everything gets washed in hot water or burned. The boots especially. This is not paranoia — this is the basic courtesy of someone who moves between worlds.

## Movement

Walk slowly. Slower than that. The city's metabolism is so low that your ordinary pace will register to it as a kind of violence. Stop often. Let things resettle around you before you move on. A useful rule: after any significant movement — entering a domed room, stepping over a channel, turning a corner — stand still and count to sixty before doing the next thing. You will find that in that minute the pulsing of the walls, which had stopped, resumes.

Do not step on anything living if you can avoid it, and you can almost always avoid it. The creatures do not flee. This means the burden of not harming them is entirely on you. Look before each foot goes down. Accept that you will move at perhaps a tenth of your normal speed.

Do not pick anything up. Not a frog, not a pale coin-crab, not a smoothed stone. Nothing. The impulse will be strong because everything is within reach and nothing resists. Resist for them.

## Sound

Do not speak unless you must, and then in the quietest register your voice has. The hush is not empty — it is the actual texture of the place, and your voice tears it. If you are traveling with a companion, agree on hand signals before you cross the rim. Agree in particular on the signal for *we are leaving now*, because you will need it.

Turn off anything that beeps or hums. No music, no recording device with an active indicator tone, no watch that chimes the hour. The low note from the central dome, when it sounds, will go through you in a way you were not expecting; it is not loud but it is the loudest thing in the world, and if your pocket chooses that moment to buzz, you will feel you have done something unforgivable.

## What to bring for seeing

A small dim light, red-filtered, for looking into the domed rooms. Not a bright beam — the creatures under the overhangs are eyeless or nearly so for a reason, and a hard white light is a cruelty. Better, honestly, to let your eyes adjust and simply not see the things that don't want to be seen. Much of this visit is about accepting partial sight. The haze will not let you see the rim from the center; take that as instruction. You are not meant to hold the whole place in your head at once.

If you must record, use a camera you can operate in near-silence, and do not use flash. Ever. A flash in that pearl-gray calm would be an act of war.

## What to bring for your own mind

This is the preparation travelers most often neglect. The Sump-City does something to people. It is the heat, partly, and the shadowless light, and the way sound is muted, and the fact that everything you look at is alive and almost still. An hour in the bowl and you will feel your own pulse slow, your thoughts thicken, a strange reluctance to leave settle into your legs. Some visitors report a sense that the city is waiting for them to also agree to be a habitat — to sit down in one of the domed rooms and let the frogs cover them, and become, over a long enough span, another surface.

This is not mystical. It is the rational response of a primate nervous system to heat, humidity, low stimulation, and the profound relaxation of being somewhere that makes no demands. But it is real, and you should know it is coming.

Set a timer before you enter — a silent one, a vibration on your wrist. Two hours is plenty for a first visit. When it goes off, leave. Do not negotiate with yourself. You can come back. The city will not have changed. The city has not changed in a very long time.

Bring, in your pocket, one small token from your own world. A coin, a key, a folded note in your own handwriting. Something that is unambiguously *yours* and *from elsewhere*. When the drowsy not-leaving feeling comes over you, take it out and hold it. It is a small anchor and it works better than you would expect.

## The rim

Cross the rim deliberately. It is so worn you could miss it — walk over it without knowing you'd crossed anything, as the description warns. Don't. Stop at the rim. Note where you crossed. Leave a small marker you can see from inside — a bright cloth tied to something, high enough that the haze won't swallow it. The bowl disorients. You cannot quite see the rim from the center. When you want to leave, you will want to leave in a straight line toward a known point, not wander the circumference looking for your way out while the heat climbs and your thinking softens.

## Going in

Cross at the coolest hour your arrival permits, if the world has hours. Walk down into the bowl slowly, letting your body adjust to the heat in stages. Stop at the first domed building. Stand in its doorway without entering. Watch the wall breathe for a few minutes. Listen for the low note. Let the place know, in whatever way places know such things, that you are there and you intend no harm.

Then go in, if you still want to. Most travelers, at that doorway, find they have already seen what they came to see. The city is small. A held breath. You do not need to walk its diameter to understand it. Sometimes the best visit is the shortest one — a few minutes inside the rim, a long look at one pulsing wall, and then back out, up the gentle slope, into a world where the air is thinner and sound carries and things have edges again.

That is the last preparation: be ready for the possibility that you will want to turn around almost immediately, and know that this is not failure. It is the city teaching you, very gently, the only lesson it still has to give — that some places are finished with being visited, and the grace is to notice.
