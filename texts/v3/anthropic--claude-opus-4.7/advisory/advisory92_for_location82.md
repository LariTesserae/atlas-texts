# Preparing to Visit the Understory City

## First, a reckoning with what you are bringing

You are, by the standards of this place, enormous. Not just tall — *volumetrically* wrong. The people are child-sized, their houses are crouched-person-sized, their bridges are woven for their weight. You cannot climb into their city. You cannot enter a dwelling. You will occupy, at most, a quarter of the chamber's floor just by standing in it, and if you are not careful where you put your feet you will undo civilizations of slug and lichen with every step.

Accept before you go that you will be, to them, a slow-moving piece of weather. Possibly a falling thing from the canopy, except vertical and ambulatory and wrong. Plan your visit around this fact and not around any fantasy of mingling.

## Clothing and body

Dress for a long cool damp. Not cold enough to shiver in, but cold enough to sap you over hours — the temperature of a cave, with humidity at saturation. Wool beneath, then something that sheds water without rustling. Avoid synthetics that squeak. Avoid anything bright — your eyes will adjust downward into their twilight, but more importantly, you should not be a visual event in a place lit by fungus.

Soft boots, waterproof to the calf, with soles you trust on slick wood and uneven stone-soft bark. You will probably be standing in ankle-deep marsh for much of the visit because there is nowhere else to stand. Treat the boots with something that will not leach into the water. Whatever you wear on your feet is going to be, effectively, wading into a living broth. Clean the boots before you arrive, scrupulously, and again after — not for your sake but for both ecosystems, theirs and the next world's.

Bring a hat or hood. Things drip constantly from above, and some of what drips is alive.

Do not wear perfume, deodorant, scented soap, mosquito repellent, sunscreen, or anything that outgasses. The chamber's air is still and saturated; whatever you exhale or emit will linger near you as a small atmosphere of wrongness. Wash in unscented water for two days before entering. Brush your teeth with salt. If you smoke, stop a week in advance. Your breath alone will be pungent to them — you have been eating foods they have never encountered and your gut flora is an invasion in aerosol.

## Eyes

This is the preparation most travelers underestimate.

Spend at least three nights before the visit sleeping in total darkness and reading by candlelight only. Better, spend a week. Your rods need to be ready. When you enter the chamber you will see nothing for perhaps twenty minutes — longer if you came from sunlight. Do not bring a flashlight. Do not bring a phone. Do not, under any circumstance, introduce a light source into a place lit by fungus; you would scald their evening like opening an oven.

If you wear glasses, bring a cloth. They will fog continuously and you will have to decide whether to wipe them every minute or let them blur. I recommend letting them blur. This place is not for reading details; it is for receiving an atmosphere.

Consider: you may want to bring a patch to cover one eye for the hour before entering, and remove it once inside. The pirate's trick. It works.

## Food and water

Bring all of your own, sealed, and take all waste out with you. Nothing you have eaten belongs in that pool. Nothing of your body does either.

This will require planning. You cannot relieve yourself in the chamber — not in the marsh, not against the wood, not anywhere. Sort this out before you enter: reduce your intake the day before, use the forest well outside the root-arch, carry containers for what you cannot hold. Treat the chamber as you would a sickroom or a shrine: you do not shed into it.

Do not accept food if it is offered, and I suspect it will not be. But if a child reaches up with a grub — and a child might, because to them you are a curiosity and children are the same everywhere — decline gently. Your gut is not their gut. More importantly, you do not know what that grub is doing in their cycle, and removing even one may matter in ways you cannot compute.

## Sound

Practice being quiet for a week before you go. Not silent — they are not silent — but quiet in the specific way of people who have nowhere to be. Walk slowly around your own house without making the floors creak. Learn the sound of your own breathing and make it softer. Cough into the crook of your elbow through cloth.

The chamber's acoustic is a close one. A sneeze will ring. A dropped object will ring longer. A raised voice will probably end your visit, not because anyone will make you leave, but because you will have broken something you came to witness.

Do not hum. Do not whistle. Do not, please, play music for them to "share cultures." They have a culture. It is the slow percussion of water into water, and the creak of wood, and the breathing of the great coiled thing in the upper pen. Do not overwrite it.

## The tidal cycle

Learn when the pool rises before you go, and plan your visit around it. You want to be present for at least one rising and one receding — this is the chamber's weather, and without seeing it you will not have seen the place. But know that when the water rises, things come out of it. The salamanders long as your arm, the worm-eel, the crabs. They will walk across the floor. Some will walk across your boots. Do not move. Do not lift a foot to let one pass. You are terrain, and terrain does not flinch.

If you flinch and crush something, you will have killed a citizen of a world that has perhaps two hundred non-human citizens of that size and kind. There is no coming back from that. Stand like a root.

## What to bring

- Water, sealed, enough for twice your expected stay.
- Food that does not crumb. Hard cheese, dense bread, nothing you have to unwrap noisily.
- A small notebook and a soft pencil. No pen — pens click and leak. Write by fungal light or not at all.
- A cloth for your glasses, for your face, for catching a cough.
- A sitting-pad, because you will want to sit for hours and the floor is marsh. Something waterproof, dark-colored, that compresses small.
- Waste containers, sealed.
- A single warm layer more than you think you need.
- Patience in a quantity you have never previously measured.

## What not to bring

- Cameras. The place cannot be photographed and should not be. The flash would be a catastrophe; the shutter click, an obscenity. Even a silenced phone emits heat and radio that you cannot feel but the moths might.
- Gifts. You cannot give them anything. Every object you possess is made of materials they have never seen, processed by chains of extraction they do not participate in. A steel needle is a meteorite. A plastic button is a curse. If you want to give them something, give them your carefulness.
- Knowledge of the outside. Do not, in any attempted communication, describe the forest, the sky, the sun, other cities, the multiverse. The Rule is that nothing leaves. The corollary is that nothing should enter either, at least nothing of the mind. You do not know whether their not-leaving is a limit or a faith. Do not be the one who tests it.

## How to behave once inside

Stand at the edge of the marsh, not in the center. Find a place near the root-arch — you are closer to your own world there, which is appropriate, and you occlude less of theirs. Sit, eventually, when your legs tire. You will be there a long time.

Do not approach a dwelling. Do not approach the pens on the upper bridges, even though you are tall enough to look into them from the floor. The coiled thing in the highest pen: do not look at it too long. Whether it is god or prisoner or beast, attention from outside its world is not something it has been shaped to receive. Let it alone.

If a person approaches you, do not bend down quickly. Lower yourself slowly to a sitting position and wait. Let your face be available to their light. Do not smile widely — teeth are startling to many peoples, and you do not know their grammar of expression. A soft closed-mouth look, eyes steady, is safest. If they speak, listen. Do not try to reply. Your voice in that chamber, at your size, will be weather.

If nothing happens — if they simply continue their lives around you, neither acknowledging nor avoiding — understand that this is the best possible reception, and honor it by continuing to be there without demanding to be seen.

## What you are there for

You are there to witness a completeness. A world that is one room. A people who have made everything of what they were given, and for whom the boundary of the room is not a wall but a horizon — the way the sky is a horizon for you, unquestioned, not a constraint but the shape of the real.

You will want, at some point, to feel sorry for them. Resist this. The description ends on the correct note: *It is not a sad place.* Your sorrow would be a projection from a larger world onto a smaller one, and the relative sizes are not what you think they are. Their completeness is not less than yours because it is contained. It may be more.

## Leaving

Leave during a receding of the pool, when the creatures are returning to the water and the ladders are dropping. Step backward out of the root-arch so you do not turn your back on the chamber. Once outside, wait another ten minutes before you move quickly or speak. The transition is steep and you owe it a pause.

Do not look back from outside. The chamber is a held breath, and you do not want to be the one standing in the doorway when it is let out.

Wash, afterward, in clean running water, with attention. Some of the chamber will want to come with you. Send it back as best you can. What remains — a spore, a damp in your hair, a particular quality of hush in your hearing for a few days — you may keep. It is what you went for.
