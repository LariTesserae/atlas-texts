# Preparing to Visit the Clamor Plateau

## The Ears First, Always

Whatever else you forget, do not forget hearing protection. Not one pair — three. A primary set of high-attenuation industrial earplugs (the foam kind rated for thirty-plus decibels), a secondary set of musician's filtered plugs for when you want to hear yourself think without damage, and over-ear defenders you can clamp down on top of the plugs when the birds are directly overhead. Sound pressure at the density described — millions of vocalizing birds in a standing wave — is not a matter of discomfort. It is a matter of permanent cochlear injury measured in minutes. The ringing the text promises you at the end is the sound of hair cells dying. Protect aggressively and you may get out with only a whisper of tinnitus instead of a lifelong companion.

Bring a small notebook and a stub of pencil, because you will not be able to speak to anyone, including yourself. If you travel with a companion, develop hand signals before you arrive. Point-wait-nod will become your entire conversational repertoire.

## The Altitude

The violet sky tells you everything. This is somewhere between four and six thousand meters of pressure-altitude, possibly higher. Do not walk onto this plateau from sea level. Acclimatize in stages over at least a week — ideally longer — at progressively higher camps before making the final ascent along those switchbacks. Carry acetazolamide and know the symptoms of high-altitude pulmonary and cerebral edema; know them well enough that you can identify them in yourself when your judgment is already compromised. A pulse oximeter weighs almost nothing and will tell you things your body is hiding.

Drink more water than you think you need. The dry cool air will steal moisture from your lungs with every breath, and you will be breathing fast.

## The Light

That hard white sun, unfiltered by proper atmosphere, is carcinogenic in a way ordinary sunlight is not. Category 4 mountaineering glasses with side shields — not sunglasses, *glacier* glasses. A broad-brimmed hat. Long sleeves of tightly woven fabric. Zinc oxide on every exposed surface of skin, reapplied obsessively. Your lips will crack and bleed. Bring salve.

The light will also make sleep difficult even inside a tent. Bring a proper sleep mask, the kind that blocks light rather than suggesting the idea of blocking it.

## Shelter and Warmth

The air is cool rather than cold by day, but at this altitude radiative cooling at night will be severe. A four-season tent, a sleeping bag rated well below what you expect to encounter, and an insulated pad with a high R-value. Pitch in the lee of boulders — but check above you for loose stone, because frost-splitting is ongoing even in a settled landscape.

Do not camp in the lush green hollows. Those are guano gardens, and while they are beautiful, you will wake covered in mites and smelling of ammonia.

## Food and Water

The tarns and the thin stream are drinkable only after filtration *and* chemical or UV treatment. Bird colonies of this density contaminate every water source with pathogens you have no immunity to — some of which may not map cleanly to terrestrial disease categories. Boil when you can. At altitude, boiling takes longer; factor the fuel.

Bring all your food. Do not eat the eggs. Do not eat the birds. Whatever the old hearth-builders knew about this place, you do not know it, and the math that does not work regarding the birds' feeding is a warning sign that whatever sustains them may not be something you want in your bloodstream.

## What to Wear Under Your Boots

The plateau is rounded and settled, but frost-split scree is ankle-breaking terrain and guano-glazed stone is slick as ice. Stiff mountaineering boots with good ankle support. Gaiters. Trekking poles. A first aid kit that assumes you are days from help, because you are.

## The Long Stay Problem

The text tells you that you can stay as long as you like and that no one does. Believe this. Plan your visit as days, not weeks. The sound does something to cognition that is not fully about decibels — there is a flattening that happens when a sensory input is both overwhelming and perfectly unchanging, and people who linger in such conditions begin to make decisions that look, from outside, like they have forgotten they are the kind of creature that leaves places. Set a departure date before you arrive. Tell your companion the date. Have them hold you to it.

## The Cairns and the Spiral

If you come across the old hearths and the knapped obsidian and the spiral with the dot — leave them. Do not add your own cairn. Do not take a flake of obsidian as a souvenir. The long-standing arrangement the text describes is not yours to join or disturb. If the people who come here every few generations arrive while you are present, greet them with open hands and give them the plateau; withdraw to its margins and wait until they have done what they came for.

The spiral may be counting. It may be a warning. It may be a map of something about the birds that does not want to be understood quickly. Copy it into your notebook if you wish. Do not deface the stone.

## The Birds Themselves

Do not approach nesting ledges. Do not handle eggs or chicks. Do not attempt to capture a specimen for study. A bird that does not need to feed is a bird whose biology is not your biology, and interfering with it may have consequences that propagate through the strange stillness of this place in ways you cannot predict. Observe from distance. Photograph if you like, though photographs of this place are said to come out looking oddly empty, as if the camera caught only the stone and not the cloud of screaming life above it.

Watch the columns rise in their same places. Watch the same densities on the same ledges. Understand that you are looking at a system that has achieved something like equilibrium on a timescale that makes human civilization look like weather. Your presence is not part of this equilibrium. You are a small warm body passing through.

## The Descent

Start down earlier in the day than you think you need to. The switchbacks will be harder on your knees than the ascent was on your lungs. As the sound fades over that hour's walk, pay attention. Many travelers report that the first hour of quiet is disorienting in a way the noise itself was not — a feeling of having lost a companion you did not know you had acquired.

At the base, rest a full day before traveling further. Hydrate. Eat something warm. Speak, aloud, to another person, about ordinary things, so that your voice remembers it is a voice and not a gesture.

## What You Will Bring Back

The ringing, as promised. Bring a small recorder and capture the silence of your own room when you return home; play it back in the first week and listen for the faint shape of the clamor underneath. This is not mysticism. This is how auditory memory consolidates after trauma, and hearing it confirmed from outside your own skull will help you understand that it is a signal, not a sign of losing your mind.

You will also bring back, for a while, an unusual tolerance for ordinary noise and an unusual impatience with ordinary stillness. Both will fade. The plateau does not follow you. It only waits, and will be exactly as it is when the next traveler comes, and the next, and the next, screaming faintly in the ears of all of them forever.
