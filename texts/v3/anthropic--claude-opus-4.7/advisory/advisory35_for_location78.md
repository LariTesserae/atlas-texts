# Preparing for Iselvet

## Understanding What You're Walking Into

Before anything else: understand that Iselvet is not dangerous in the way a frontier town is dangerous, nor in the way a high-altitude industrial site is dangerous, though it wears the costume of both. The danger here is ontological. You are going to a place where the distinction between "thing" and "suggestion of thing" has not fully set. Your preparation must account for the possibility that some part of you — your boots, your notebook, your sense of where your left hand is — may begin to participate in the local indeterminacy if you stay long enough.

Plan your visit in weeks, not months. A fortnight is reasonable. Past a month, things start to happen to travelers that the foundry crews recognize on sight and will not explain to you.

## Physical Preparation

**Altitude and air.** The thin blue-grey air is breathable but tiring. Arrive already acclimatized from somewhere of comparable elevation — a week in a mountain town on your home continuum, minimum. Do not attempt Iselvet after a direct transit from sea level. The geological hum compounds altitude sickness in ways that feel like grief.

**The damp.** This is the physical fact that will shape your whole trip. Chronic, penetrating, mild. Pack as if for an English autumn that never ends: wool base layers (wool, not synthetic — synthetic fabrics behave oddly here after a week, pilling into shapes that weren't there before), oiled canvas outerwear, a good felt hat, two pairs of boots so one can attempt to dry while you wear the other. Bring more socks than seems reasonable. Triple it. Then add more.

**Footwear specifically.** Your boots need hard, well-defined soles with aggressive tread, because you will spend time on slopes that do not hold a print. Leather uppers, hobnailed if you can manage it. Avoid anything with memory foam or gel inserts — these materials are already halfway to being suggestions and the mountain will finish the job. Break the boots in thoroughly before arrival. New boots at Iselvet may decide, over the course of your visit, to be a slightly different size.

**Warmth without drama.** Temperatures drift gently, so you won't need survival-grade cold weather gear, but you will need sustained insulation against the chronic chill-damp. Think coal-miner, not mountaineer.

## What to Bring for the Strangeness

**Objects with strong identity.** Bring tools and personal items that are old, well-used, and unambiguous. A pocketknife you've carried for twenty years will resist the local drift better than something new. Heirloom items are excellent. Freshly-manufactured goods — anything still shiny, anything whose purpose you had to look up — are vulnerable. The foundry workers understand this intuitively and carry tools passed down through generations.

**A written inventory.** Keep a list, in ink, on good rag paper, of everything you brought. Check it daily. This is not paranoia. Some travelers return missing small items they never noticed disappearing; others return with small items they don't remember packing. The list helps you notice. It also helps you remain yourself, which is related.

**A timepiece with a second hand.** Mechanical, not digital. Wind it every morning at the same time. The ritual matters more than the timekeeping. Digital watches work but will start showing you times that don't advance linearly by the third day.

**Salt.** A small leather pouch. I can't explain this fully — ask a foundry chemist if you meet a friendly one — but salt is one of the few substances that arrives at Iselvet already fully committed to being itself, and a pinch in your pocket is a small anchor. The locals will not comment on this but will notice its absence.

**A camera, but manage your expectations.** Film cameras work reasonably. Digital photographs taken at Iselvet tend to be accurate on review the first day, slightly wrong by the third, and by the time you're home may show scenes you don't remember photographing. Film is stabler but still unreliable for the upper terraces. Sketch instead, if you can. Drawing forces you to look, and looking is how you hold onto a place like this.

## What Not to Bring

- **Anything precious that you couldn't stand to lose or have altered.** Leave the wedding ring at home. Leave the manuscript at home.
- **Rigid opinions about what metal is.** You will be shown ingots that challenge your definitions. Going in, understand that "metal" is a verb at Iselvet, not a noun.
- **Plumb bobs, spirit levels, or any instrument that depends on a stable local vertical.** Compasses work, surprisingly, but only because magnetism has apparently decided to be here. Gravity is still hedging.
- **Pets.** They know. They will suffer. Don't.

## Social and Practical Preparation

**Language.** The foundry workers speak a trade pidgin that absorbs loan-words from many sources, so you will probably manage. Learn the word for "persuasion" and the word for "fixed" — these are the nouns that carry the real meaning of the place. Learn the polite greeting and the foundry-shift acknowledgment. Do not attempt to learn the song of the mouse-shaped creatures. People who try this report bad dreams that last years.

**Currency and trade.** Iselvet accepts hard currency from down-mountain, preferring heavy coin over paper. Bring coin. Paper money here feels embarrassed about itself. There are lodgings — many, as the city is thinly occupied — and a visitor with funds will have no trouble finding a dry-ish room in one of the middle terraces. Avoid the upper terraces for lodging. Avoid them altogether if you can.

**Food.** The food is fine. Mountain goat that is almost goat, root vegetables that grow in the runoff gardens, coarse bread, strong tea, a spirit distilled from something mossy that will knock you flat. Eat what the foundry workers eat; they have worked out which local foods have fully committed to being food. Do not forage. Do not eat anything you find growing in an empty terrace, no matter how familiar it looks.

**Water.** Drink only boiled water, or tea, or the spirits. Not because of bacteria — the pre-erosional water is if anything too pure — but because unboiled Iselvet water has a tendency to be more suggestive than hydrating. Boiling seems to commit it.

## Mental and Spiritual Preparation

**Practice holding a fixed idea of yourself.** This sounds mystical but is practical. Before you go, spend time each day thinking clearly about who you are: your name, your history, the shape of your hands, the sound of your mother's voice, the route from your front door to your kitchen. Do this as a daily practice for at least two weeks before transit. At Iselvet, you will want this reserve. The mountain does not attack identity, exactly, but it offers alternatives.

**Accept that you will not fully understand what you see.** The foundry persuasion-work, the salamander songs, the empty terraces, the wager the city represents — all of this resists full explanation. Travelers who demand narrative closure from Iselvet go home frustrated. Travelers who accept that they are witnesses to an unfinished argument between human intention and raw possibility go home changed in quieter, better ways.

**Prepare for the loneliness.** The sparse population, the dim unvarying light, the chronic damp, the slow weather, the hum — these compound. By your fifth day you may feel a melancholy that does not belong to you. This is normal and it is not yours. It belongs to the place. Let it pass through. Bring a book you love. Bring a flask. Write letters you will never send.

## On Leaving

Budget three days at the bottom of the cable tram before attempting to return to your own continuum. You need time in a place where things stay what they are, to let yourself re-commit to being the specific person you were when you arrived. Eat heavy food. Touch stone that remembers being stone. Talk to a stranger about ordinary things.

Check your inventory one final time. Look at your hands in good light.

Then go home, and try to find a way to describe where you have been, knowing that the words for it have not fully agreed to be words either.
