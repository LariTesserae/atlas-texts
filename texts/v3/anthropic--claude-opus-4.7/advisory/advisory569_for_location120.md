# Preparing to Visit the Sumphouse

## What You're Actually Going To

Understand first what this place *is*, because your preparation depends on reading it correctly. The Sumphouse is not a ruin, not a tourist site, not a wilderness. It is a functioning organism that happens to be made of brick and water and small lives, and it has a caretaker. You are visiting something closer to a very old, very quiet household than to a cave or a monument. Prepare accordingly — as a guest, not an explorer.

## Clothing

Wear layers you can shed. The descent is the first strangeness: the air warms as you go down, and by the time you are in the chamber you will be in something like a greenhouse in late spring — humid, close, body-temperature warm. A light wool shirt is ideal; it handles damp better than cotton and won't chill you if you sit still for a long time on the stone bench (and you will sit for a long time).

Shoes should be soft-soled and quiet. Leather with a thin sole, or canvas. You are going to be walking among scattered tesserae, across uneven brick, sometimes through two fingers of standing water. Nothing with a hard heel — the sound would be an assault on a room tuned to drip-percussion, and you don't want to be the loudest thing in the chamber. Bring a second pair of socks in a waterproof pouch. You will get wet to the ankles at some point and not mind, until later.

Nothing that rustles. No synthetic shells, no crinkling rain gear. Whatever you wear will become part of the acoustic room for the duration of your visit, and you want to contribute as little as possible.

A hat is optional but thoughtful — the drips from the ceiling are constant, and while a drop on the back of the hand is part of the experience (arguably the whole point), drops falling steadily on your scalp for an hour will eventually become distracting.

## What to Carry

**A small vessel of your own.** This is the one thing I would insist on. Not because you need it, but because the chamber's fundamental practice is that every drip is caught. If you sit long enough to be counted into the tuning, you should have brought something to be tuned with. A small ceramic bowl, a copper cup, a wooden bowl that will darken beautifully with use — anything that rings or thumps or plops differently than what is already there. Set it down somewhere a drip is landing on bare brick or into a puddle. You are adding a note. Leave it when you go. The caretaker will decide whether it stays.

**Nothing to eat in the chamber.** Food smells carry and persist in humid enclosed air, and the rats are householders, not scavengers — you don't want to change the terms of their residence. If you need to eat, do it at the top of the stairs, before you descend.

**Water to drink,** but not the chamber's water. The copper, the coin-leachate, the generations of small lives — it is not for you. Bring your own in a flask that doesn't sweat or clink.

**A light, but only a small one.** A shielded candle or a very dim hand-lamp. The light shafts do the work of illumination during the day; you want something only for the corners, and only briefly. Bright light will spook the bats into the open and disturb the moths from their wall-patches, which is both rude and — if you value the sight of hundreds of paperwhite moths resting undisturbed — self-defeating.

**No camera, or a very quiet one.** The shutter of a mechanical camera is a crack in an otherwise continuous soft fabric of sound. If you must record, record sound, not image. The chamber is made of sound more than it is made of sight. A small field recorder, left running on the bench for twenty minutes, will give you more of the place than any photograph.

**A handkerchief.** The humidity will fog anything on your face. Also useful for wiping drips from the back of your neck without flinching, which you will want to stop doing after a while anyway.

## What to Leave Behind

Your impulse to identify things. You will see a newt and want to know what kind of newt. You will see the fig-like tree and want to know if it is a fig. Let these questions go unfulfilled. The chamber does not catalog itself and does not benefit from being cataloged. The fish in the cistern came from somewhere, but the question *how did they get here* is not a question the chamber is interested in answering, and the longer you sit with it unanswered the more the chamber relaxes around you.

Your sense of time. Bring no watch, or at least do not look at one. The drip is constant but never rhythmic, and the cricket-song rises and falls in slow tidal swells that do not correspond to any clock. You will find, if you stay long enough, that you have lost an hour or lost ten minutes and cannot tell which. This is correct. The Sumphouse's time is its own.

Your urge to tidy. You will see the scattered tesserae and want to gather them. Don't. The caretaker does this, or doesn't, on a schedule that is part of how the place keeps itself. You are not the caretaker. You are a visitor who might, if you are very lucky and very patient, be noticed by the caretaker on a future visit.

## Protocols Inside

**Descend slowly.** The steps are worn into hand-hollows; they are slick and steep. Take them one at a time. The descent is also a psychological adjustment — you are moving from ordinary air into the chamber's weather, and rushing it will mean you arrive disoriented.

**Stand still inside the doorway for a full minute before moving.** Let the moths settle. Let the rats decide whether to keep watching or go back into the pipes. Let your eyes adjust to the greenish between-pillars dimness. The chamber will assess you. You should feel this assessment happening and not resist it.

**Walk where the floor tells you to walk.** The drifts of tesserae mark the paths water takes; do not walk through them. The intact mosaic patches are older than any civilization you belong to and should be treated as such. The pools are shallow but the stone around them is slick with algae.

**Do not touch the bats.** Obvious, but worth saying. Also do not touch the newt, which will let you, and will regret it, and so will you.

**You may touch the bricks.** They are warm, and this is part of the gift. Press your palm flat against the wall and feel the heat, which is older than the bricks themselves — it comes from whatever flue or seep the builders built around, and has been warming hands for as long as there have been hands to warm.

**You may put a coin in the mosaic pool** if you have one that is copper. Iron or nickel or modern alloys — no. Copper is what the water there has learned to speak with. A copper coin will become green and soft-edged in time, and will be indistinguishable from the others, which is the point.

**Sit on the stone bench.** Not immediately. Walk the length of the chamber first (forty paces, slowly, not in a straight line). Then sit. Sit for at least as long as it takes for a drip to land on you. That is the chamber's way of enrolling you. Until a drip has landed on you, you are still a visitor; after, you are briefly a part of it. This is not ceremony, it is just what happens if you stay still long enough.

## On the Caretaker

You will probably not meet them. The broom is new, the bristles bound with waxed twine, but whoever tends this place does so on a rhythm that has nothing to do with your visit. If you do meet them — coming down the stairs as you are going up, or already at work in a corner you did not see them enter — the correct behavior is the same as with the rats: do not approach, do not avoid, acknowledge with the smallest possible gesture, and continue with whatever you were doing. They have seen many visitors. The ones who bow low and ask questions are tiresome; the ones who pretend not to see them are rude. A nod is enough.

If you leave a vessel, and they are present when you do, you may look at them while placing it. They may or may not look back. This is not a transaction, only a noticing.

## Leaving

Do not leave quickly. The ascent is a decompression — the air cooling as you climb, the cricket-song thinning, the drip-percussion fading first to a distant tapping and then to the sound of your own feet on sandstone. Pause two or three times on the way up. Listen to how the chamber sounds from different distances. It has a different voice at each landing.

At the top, before you step out, turn and look back down. The light from the shafts will still be reaching up toward you faintly, greenish, the color of pondwater. This is the last image to take with you. After this you will be in ordinary weather again, and it will seem thinner, and colder, and too dry, for most of a day.

## A Final Note

You may find, once you have left, that you want to go back. Many travelers do. Resist this for at least a season. The Sumphouse is not diminished by visits, but you are changed by them, and returning too soon means returning as someone who has not yet absorbed the first visit. Let the drip that landed on the back of your hand do its slow work first. When you return, if you return, you will be a different vessel, and the chamber will catch you differently.
