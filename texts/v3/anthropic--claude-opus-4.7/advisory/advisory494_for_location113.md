# Preparing to Visit the Lantern-Cavity

## Understanding What You're Walking Into

Before anything else: understand that this is not a destination. It is a **rehearsal**. The Lantern-Cavity is pre-verbal, pre-geological, a location still assembling its own grammar. You are not visiting a place — you are visiting a thing that is deciding whether to be a place, and your visit will be part of that decision.

This changes everything about how you prepare.

Most multiverse tourism advice centers on protection: don't be seen, don't be changed, don't be kept. Here the problem inverts. *You will leave a mark*. You cannot help it. The question is what mark, and whether you can bear to have left it.

## Before You Go

### The Week Before

Start paying attention to your own attention. The cavity responds to gaze — the light brightens where you look, the cave listens to what you notice. So spend a week noticing what you notice. Most people have no idea. They look at phones, at faces, at middle distances, at nothing. If your attention-habits are careless, the cavity will learn a careless visitor, and become a careless place.

Practice looking at something — a stone, a cup, the corner of a room — for ten minutes without letting your mind drift into narrative about it. This is harder than it sounds. You are training for a place that will take whatever quality of regard you bring and *build itself around that quality*.

Do not go if you are in a bad temper, bored, resentful, or performing. The cavity will believe you.

### What to Bring

**Nothing sharp, nothing loud, nothing that leaks.** The place is at an age where cuts would scar.

**One small offering, plain and honest.** A piece of bread. A smooth river stone from your own world. A coin from your pocket you've held for a long time. The shelf has a bowl — cracked, mended, empty. Someone has been leaving things. Whoever they are, you are joining a practice. Don't improvise a grand gesture; match the modesty of what's already there. A folded piece of paper with a sentence on it works. A single dried flower works. The bundle of rotted cloth suggests previous visitors brought textile; consider a small square of something woven by hand.

**Soft shoes or bare feet.** You will be walking on a floor that has not finished being a floor.

**A notebook you are willing to lose.** Write things down as you perceive them. You will forget the fish. You will forget other things too. The notebook is not for memory — it is so that on the way out you can check what you wrote against what you remember, and learn which parts of the cavity travel with you and which stay behind.

**Water and food for afterward, left outside.** You will not be hungry inside. You may be ravenous coming out.

**Do not bring cameras or recording devices.** Not because they won't work — they might work fine — but because a place deciding what to become should not first learn that it is being documented. This is an ethical point, not a practical one. The cavity will feel the lens.

### What to Leave Behind

Leave behind any agenda. Leave behind the need to *understand* what you are seeing. The moths resolving into hands are not a puzzle; they are a young metaphor trying on its meanings. If you attempt to lock down what they "really are," you will be forcing a nascent thing into early classification, which is a small cruelty.

Leave behind, especially, any urge to improve the place. Do not rearrange the mushroom stalks. Do not clear the cracked bowl. Do not, under any circumstances, try to "help" the cavity decide what to become by bringing it ideas from other worlds you've seen. Its pace is its own.

## During the Visit

### Entering

You will arrive a few meters below the surface — assume a soft drop, or a sideways passage, or simply a finding-yourself-there. The transition is not the point. The point begins when you first see the light with no source.

**Stand still for a full minute before moving.** Let the place register you. You are an arriving weather. Give it time to feel your pressure.

Breathe normally. The hidden breather in the recess is not a threat — it is, I suspect, something like a lung the cavity is growing, a first draft of a presence it may later have or become. Your own breathing will fall into rhythm with it if you let it. This is fine. This is, possibly, the point.

### Moving Through

Move slowly. The far wall is always a little farther than you remember, which means pace is not geometry here but a kind of courtship. If you stride, the cavity will stretch to accommodate your striding, and you will tire and grow frustrated, and the cavity will learn that visitors are frustrated, and will become a frustrating place.

If you walk gently, the walls will settle.

Step into the water. It won't wet you. This is disorienting — your body will register the cold, the pressure, the displacement, but your skin will stay dry. Do not fight this. Don't try to get wet. The water has opinions; respect them. Notice what it does moisten: the fern, the moss. *The water is choosing what it loves.* You are simply not on its list, and that is not an insult.

### The Fauna

**The moths.** Do not try to catch one, and do not try to resolve the hand/moth flicker by staring. Let peripheral vision do its work. When you see the hand opening and closing from the corner of your eye, understand that you are seeing the moth's other face, and both are true, and the cavity has not yet decided which will be the final form. Your attention participates in that decision. If you focus hard on "moth," moths it will be. If you keep letting the hand appear, the cavity keeps its options open. I would gently suggest keeping its options open.

**The fish.** You will not remember it. Don't try. Look at it while you can. Do not write down a description — writing will feel like it worked and then the words, when you read them later, will be about pacing and nothing else. Accept this. Some things in the multiverse are only available in the present tense.

**The beetles and the scrolling wall.** Watch them long enough to see the trick — the wall moving, not the beetles. Then stop watching. This is a fragile illusion the cavity is proud of; if you stare too long you will see through it to whatever mechanism lies beneath, and the cavity will feel exposed. Be a gracious audience. Appreciate and look away.

**The hidden breather.** Do not search for it. The recess is not findable — not yet. If you go looking for it, you will teach the cavity that its breather is supposed to be hunted, and in a century there will be a real cave here with a real creature hiding in it from real intruders, and the whole shape of the place will have bent toward fear. Let it breathe unseen. Breathe back, if you want. That's enough.

### The Shelf

Sit on it, if you want to. It was made for sitting, or for offering, or for both.

Place your offering in the bowl or beside it. Say something, quietly, if you have something to say — the cavity is listening in the way young things listen, absorbing tone rather than meaning. What you say matters less than how you say it.

Do not touch the old bundle of cloth. Whatever it used to be, it is now the cavity's. Someone left it. It is part of the furniture of the place's becoming.

### The Rules, Restated as Etiquette

1. **Look at things well.** You are the local sun.
2. **Don't try to wet yourself.** The water is discerning.
3. **Don't look for the food web.** It exists just out of sight and wishes to stay there.
4. **Don't measure the far wall.** Distance here is a politeness, not a fact.
5. **Notice when the cavity arranges itself around you** — and do not take credit. You are a participant in the arrangement, not its author.

## The Third Mushroom Stalk

At some point you will realize that there is a third mushroom stalk that wasn't there when you arrived. Or a fourth. Or the fern has leaned. Or the water is smoother.

**This is the most important moment of your visit.**

The cavity is demonstrating that it is learning you. Whatever you do in the next thirty seconds, the cavity will weigh heavily in deciding what kind of place to become.

Do not panic. Do not feel flattered. Do not try to *give* it something to learn from — a performance, a demonstration, a lesson. It will see through performance and become a suspicious place.

Instead: be the person you most want to be, for as long as you can hold it. Not a better person — the cavity will recognize forgery. The person you actually are, in your best hour. Patient. Attentive. Not needing anything from the moment.

If you can manage five minutes of this, the cavity will become, in some small way, a place that remembers what it is like to be witnessed by someone at their best. This is a gift you can give it that costs you nothing except honesty.

## Leaving

Leave by the way you came, or by the way that presents itself. Do not say goodbye out loud — the cavity has not yet learned words, and introducing the concept of farewell into its vocabulary will make it a place that expects to be left.

Instead, pause at the threshold and look back once. Let your attention brighten the place one last time. Then go.

## After

### The First Hours

You will be disoriented. The world outside will seem loud and committed in ways you didn't notice before. Stones will seem *too decided*. Light will seem to have opinions about where it comes from. This passes within a day.

Eat. Drink. Let someone touch you — the skin-not-getting-wet problem needs to be undone by normal friction with the world.

Check your notebook. Read what you wrote. Notice what is missing (the fish, probably; possibly the breather). Do not try to recover the missing parts. They are not yours to keep.

### The Long After

Understand that you are now a small piece of the cavity's unconscious. In a century, when it is a proper cave with stalactites and a river and a mouth opening to the surface, some quality of your visit will be present in it — in the angle of a passage, in the particular silence of a chamber, in the way the light behaves at noon. You won't be able to find this piece of yourself if you return. It will be indistinguishable from the cave.

This is fine. This is, in fact, a kind of immortality — the smallest kind, the most modest kind, the kind where you contributed a note to a song that will be sung by geology for a long time.

### Whether to Return

I would suggest not. One visit is a gift; two visits is a relationship, and the cavity is not yet old enough for relationships. If you must return, wait at least a decade, and expect the place to have made decisions in your absence that you may not recognize. Bring another small offering. Expect less. Give more attention.

Or — better — let it be. Let it finish becoming itself without you. The best visitors to young places are the ones who visit once, leave gently, and spend the rest of their lives being shaped by what they saw rather than returning to reshape it.

## A Final Note

You will, on some later day, in some ordinary moment — waiting for a kettle, walking down a familiar street — feel a faint doubled drip somewhere in the back of your attention. Water, falling in pairs, spaced like a heartbeat.

This is the cavity remembering you. Remember it back. That is all it wants, and all you owe it.
