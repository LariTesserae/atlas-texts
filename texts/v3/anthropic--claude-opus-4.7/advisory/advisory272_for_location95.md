# Preparing to Visit the Engine-Garden of Pitchfork Spire

## The Body First

Before anything else, understand that your body will be the primary instrument of this visit, and it will be abused in specific ways. Plan accordingly.

**Altitude.** Three thousand meters is not killing altitude, but it is disorienting altitude, and the Engine-Garden gives you no time to acclimatize on arrival. If your multiverse transit deposits you directly onto the platform, you will step out of the crossing into thin air, and your first hour will involve headache, mild nausea, and a peculiar slowness of thought that may mix badly with the acoustic environment. Spend at least two or three days at comparable altitude beforehand in some more hospitable world. If you cannot, bring acetazolamide and start it the day before. Bring ibuprofen for the headache the Engine will give you regardless.

**Hearing.** This is the single most important preparation. The sound will damage you if you are not ready. Ordinary foam earplugs are insufficient — they attenuate the high end well but do nothing for the chest-cavity BOOM of the pistons or the subaudible note of the resonating basalt. You want, ideally, both: deep-insertion silicone or custom-molded plugs rated to 30+ dB, worn *under* industrial over-ear defenders. Bring spares. Bring a third set in a different pocket. If you lose your hearing protection on the platform you cannot simply leave — the hike down, if there is one, will take hours, and the sound does not stop at the edge of the deck, only attenuates.

Accept also that no protection will fully shield you from the felt components of the sound. The piston-strike in your sternum, the iron chord in your teeth — these pass through bone, not air. You cannot block them. You can only prepare yourself to receive them.

**Teeth.** If you have loose fillings, get them checked. The subaudible vibration will find them. Do not bring a retainer or partial plate you are not sure of.

**Eyes.** The fluid in your eyes will pulse with each piston-fall. Vision blurs slightly in time with the Engine. This is uncomfortable but not harmful. If you wear contact lenses, bring glasses as backup; the vibration sometimes persuades lenses to shift.

## Clothing

Dress for a cold mountain that is not cold where you are standing. The ambient air at three thousand meters is thin and chill; the deck plates are warm; the steam-vent orchids are surrounded by pockets of humidity; the clouds that roll through will soak you in seconds. You want layers you can shed and re-don easily, none of them precious.

Wear boots with thick, non-conductive soles. The grille walkways transmit vibration directly to your feet, and over hours this produces a deep bone-ache in the shins and knees. A stiff sole and a cushioned insole will help. Avoid metal-shanked boots if you can — they ring.

Do not wear jewelry. Rings will buzz against your fingers. A necklace will slap your collarbone in rhythm with the pistons within ten minutes and you will hate it. Empty your pockets of coins and keys. Anything loose and metallic becomes a small percussion instrument in the Engine's orchestra, and you will notice every addition to the sound.

Bring a scarf or neck gaiter. Not for warmth — for the steam, which condenses on exposed skin and can chill you even as the deck plates warm your feet.

## What to Bring

**Water.** More than you think. The altitude dehydrates you, the warmth of the deck accelerates this, and you will not feel thirsty until you are already in trouble. Two liters minimum for a day-visit. The Engine drinks rain; you should drink at least as devoutly.

**Food that does not crunch.** Soft bread, dried fruit, cheese. You will want to eat in silence, because silence is what the place does not offer, and the small private silence of chewing something soft is a kindness to yourself. Avoid anything that requires a loud wrapper.

**A notebook and a pencil.** Not a pen — pens fail at altitude and in humidity. Write things down, because your short-term memory will be worse here than you expect. The vibration interferes with the formation of memory in subtle ways; visitors routinely find that their recollections of the garden are impressionistic and temporally scrambled. A pencil and paper will let you pin things in place.

**A mechanical watch, if you have one.** Digital watches sometimes behave oddly here — there are reports of them gaining or losing minutes, or displaying the same second for longer than a second should take. A mechanical watch ticks at its own rhythm and is immune. Also: a wind-up watch is, in a very small way, a sibling of the Engine, and you may find this companionable.

**Do not bring a camera as your primary record.** Photographs of the garden are reliably disappointing; they capture the brass and the ferns but not the BOOM, and so they lie. If you must photograph, photograph small things: a single frog, a single rivet, the pattern on a moth's wing. The whole refuses to be contained.

**A handkerchief.** For the condensation on your face after a cloud. For the oil you will inevitably touch. For covering your mouth if the steam shifts in your direction.

## Mental Preparation

This is the part most travelers underprepare for, and it matters more than the gear.

**Accept that you will be made quiet.** The garden does not permit conversation. If you bring a companion, understand that you will spend your hours there side by side in a forced solitude, and that this may be harder than being alone. Couples have gone to the Engine-Garden and come back strange with each other, having spent a day unable to speak and having thought, separately, too much. Go alone if you can. If you cannot, go with someone whose silence you already trust.

**Prepare to not understand the Engine.** You will want to. Human minds confronted with a vast apparently-purposeless mechanism will work hard to assign it purpose, and the Engine-Garden offers many seductive hypotheses — that the garden is the output, that the Engine is a clock for something, that the builders left instructions encoded in the rhythm. Some of these may be true. None of them are verifiable. If you arrive needing an answer, you will leave angry. If you arrive willing to stand in the presence of something unresolved, you will leave changed.

**Practice slow attention.** In the week before your visit, spend time watching things that change on the order of hours — a candle burning down, bread rising, a shadow crossing a room. The Engine's tempo is four or five seconds per breath, and everything around it moves at the pace of decades. If you arrive with the metabolism of someone who has been scrolling feeds, you will be unable to see the place at all. The frogs will look like decoration. The vines will look static. The garden will not reveal itself to anyone in a hurry.

**Consider what you will not do.** You will not touch the Engine. You will not try to find the caretaker. You will not attempt to determine what is in the shafts below. You will not carry anything away — not a fig, not a filing of soil, not a feather from a swift. The garden's balance is old and does not need your interference, and you do not know what the consequences of small interferences are. Decide these things before you arrive, so that when the temptations come — and they will, especially the temptation to pocket a little of the warm black soil — you have already decided.

## Timing Your Visit

Arrive in the morning if you can, so that you experience at least one cloud-passage and at least the beginning of dusk. The half-hour of dusk, when the clockwork-patterned moths fly, is the garden's most beautiful interval and also its most psychologically demanding — the light goes strange, the Engine's sound seems to change (it does not; you do), and visitors report a distinct sense of being watched, not by the absent caretaker but by the place itself.

Do not stay overnight on your first visit. The platform is not a place to sleep. The vibration disrupts sleep architecture severely, and those who have tried report vivid, mechanical dreams and waking up with the rhythm of the pistons beating in their pulse for days afterward. If you must stay, stay on the basalt above the platform, where the sound is merely terrible rather than total.

Plan your departure for while it is still light. Navigating away from the Engine-Garden in the dark is difficult not because of terrain but because of disorientation — after hours in the sound, your sense of direction and your sense of time are both compromised, and you will want clear light to recalibrate by.

## After

Give yourself a day of quiet afterward. Do not go directly from the Engine-Garden to anywhere loud, populous, or demanding. Your ears will ring for hours regardless of protection; your body will continue to feel the BOOM for a day or more; your thoughts will be slower and stranger than usual, and you will find ordinary conversation abrasive. Many travelers describe a mild depression in the days after — not sadness exactly, but a sense that ordinary rhythms are too fast, too arbitrary, too unpatient. This passes. It is worth letting it pass on its own, in a quiet room, without attempting to fight it.

Write down what you remember while you still remember it. The garden fades from memory faster than most places; something about the combination of the vibration and the strangeness seems to prevent the consolidation of detail. Within a month, most visitors can recall only: the BOOM, the frogs, the feeling of the question. Everything else softens into impression. If you want to keep more than that, you must write it down within the first day.

And consider — this is the last thing — whether you will visit again. Some travelers find that one visit is enough for a lifetime. Others find that they must return, pulled by something they could not name, on a rhythm of their own that seems to want to sync to the Engine's. Neither response is wrong. But know which you are, and plan accordingly, because the garden will still be there. It expects to be.
