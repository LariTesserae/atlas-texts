# Preparing to Visit the Worn Plateau

## Body First

You cannot fake your way onto this place. The altitude will find out. Spend at least three weeks at progressively higher elevations before you arrive — real mountain time, not pressurized cabin time. Your blood needs to learn to carry more. Even then, bring supplemental oxygen for the first days, and accept that you will move slowly, think slowly, sleep badly. This is not a place for exertion. The plateau rewards patience, and your body will force patience on you whether you want it or not.

Bring sun protection of a seriousness you would bring to altitude on your own world plus a margin. The thin air and the near-vacuum violet of the zenith mean the UV is punishing even when it feels cold. Glacier glasses, not sunglasses. Zinc on every exposed surface. A broad hat, and a scarf for the back of your neck. You will not feel burned until you are badly burned.

Dress for just-above-freezing with constant damp. This is a specific and miserable climate to dress for — colder would actually be easier, because you could trust snow to stay snow. Here everything beads and runs. Wool next to skin. A genuinely waterproof shell, not merely water-resistant. Gaiters. Bring twice the socks you think you need and a way to dry them, because there is no sun-baked rock to lay them on; the stone is cold and the moss is wet.

Your boots matter enormously. The moss is slick when pressed, and the lichen rings are not to be walked on carelessly — more on that below. You want stiff soles that distribute your weight, not aggressive lugs that tear.

## Supplies

Bring your own water in the quantity you need for the first several days, then learn to collect from the tree-fog and the beaded needles. The pools are drinkable but you should not drink from them — not for any reason of contamination, which is not the problem here, but because the pools have a relationship with the plateau that you are not party to and should not interrupt. The water that runs along the blue-green needles and drips from their tips is fine. Catch it in a clean vessel. It tastes faintly of metal and of something older than metal.

Food: bring it all. Nothing here is for eating. The succulents contain antifreeze; the ferns are slow in a way you do not want to take into your body; the moss is a clock and should not be chewed. Pack dense, warm-tolerant, freeze-tolerant rations. Expect your appetite to diminish from altitude; pack for what you will actually eat, plus emergency reserves.

Bring good paper and pencils. Electronics work unpredictably on the plateau and fail entirely within some distance of the bare flats — distances that vary, and that you do not want to discover by walking until your instruments stop. Pencil on paper is reliable. Make a habit of writing the date and your best guess of the time at the top of every page, because your sense of both will drift.

Bring a small, heavy object you care about — something you will notice the weight of. A stone from home, a ring, a locket. You will want a fixed reference for yourself that is not your shadow.

## How to Move

Walk slowly. Not just because of the altitude. The plateau's tempo is not yours, and the longer you spend there the more you will feel your own speed as a rudeness. Match what you see. The ferns uncurl at a pace you can watch; watch one for a full minute before you enter a new area. It is a way of announcing yourself.

**Do not walk across the lichen rings.** Skirt them. Each ring is thousands of years of growth, and while the plateau itself does not seem to mind, you will know what you have done, and the knowledge will sit badly. If you must cross, cross at the narrowest point, step lightly, and do not come back the same way — the scuff will be visible for the rest of your life and longer.

Keep track of your shadow. This sounds absurd until you are doing it. When your shadow begins to lag, you are closer to a flat than the visible landscape has told you. A quarter-second of lag is a warning. A half-second means turn around. Do not try to measure more precisely than this. Do not experiment with how far in you can go. The thing inside you that "understands without being told" is older than your curiosity and should be obeyed.

The same applies if sounds arrive before their causes, if the horizon tips upward into a dish, if the moss patterns begin to repeat at multiple scales in a way that feels *designed*. These are not puzzles to solve. They are signs posted in a language your body reads before your mind does.

## In the Cities

You may sleep in the cities. The builders do not mind shelter being used as shelter. Choose a room with a roof slab intact, sweep it with your hand before you lay out your bedding, and leave it cleaner than you found it.

**Do not disturb the threshold bowls.** If you find one empty or nearly empty, fill it with rainwater — this is welcome, and may be the most useful thing you do on your visit. Do not empty a full one. Do not take the needles or the pebbles from any bowl. Do not replace them with your own offerings unless you understand what you are offering, which you do not.

If you see a fallen stone from a wall, you may set it back. Match the grain. Do not cut new stone.

If you see one of the keepers — the figure at the edge of vision, gathering a stone — do not approach, do not call out, do not photograph (your camera will likely not resolve them anyway). Lower your eyes briefly as a courtesy and continue what you were doing. They have been keeping this place for a long time and do not need your attention. If one approaches you, stand still. Offer nothing. Accept nothing. They will most likely pass.

Do not sleep in the central plazas. Sleep in a side room, against a wall, with your small heavy object where you can touch it without looking.

## The Flats

Do not go to a flat. This needs to be said plainly because the flats exert a real pull on a certain kind of traveler — the kind who reads a description like this and thinks *yes, but I would be careful*. You would not be careful enough. The builders learned this across a span of time that makes your whole civilization a brief weather, and they built their cities at the distance they built them because that distance is the answer.

You may walk toward a flat until your shadow begins to lag. You may stand at that distance and look. You will see: moss in patterns that make your eyes tired, a thinning toward bare stone, a horizon that seems to cup slightly, and beyond that, not much. Whatever you expect to feel there, feel it at that distance and turn back. Do not kneel. Do not leave anything. Do not speak aloud, and if you have been speaking, stop before you turn around.

## The Pools

You may look into the pools. You should, at least once — they are part of what this place is. Kneel at the edge. See your face, the violet sky behind you, the depth. You will notice, behind or beneath or alongside these, *something else*. The description is right: do not look at it too long. How long is too long is a thing you will know. Two or three breaths is plenty. Stand up and walk away without turning to look again.

Do not lean over a pool far enough that you could fall in. Not because you would drown — they may not be pools of water in the sense you mean — but because the relationship between you and what is in them should remain one of distance.

## Nights

The nights are long, cold, and clear, and the sky will show you that you are not on a planet. This is one of the most important things the plateau has to show you, and also one of the hardest to carry home. Prepare for it the way you would prepare for any experience that rearranges you: bring warm drink, bring a companion if you can, bring a practice for afterward — writing, prayer, silence, whatever you use to metabolize large things.

The curvature is not the curvature of a sphere. Do not try to work out the geometry in the moment. You will have decades to think about it later if you wish. At the time, just look, and let your eyes do what they do, and do not try to name it.

## What to Bring Home

Nothing physical. No stones, no needles, no moss, no water. The plateau does not seem to punish taking, but things taken from it do not do well elsewhere — they go grey, or they stop being what they were, or they become heavy in ways that cannot be measured but can be felt. Leave the plateau on the plateau.

Bring back your notes. Bring back whatever you understood. Bring back the small heavy object you brought with you, and notice whether it feels different in your hand.

## A Final Orientation

The Worn Plateau is not dangerous in the way that predators or storms or crevasses are dangerous. It is dangerous in the way that a very old, very patient, very slightly wrong place is dangerous: by being itself, continuously, while you are inside it. Most visitors come back. Those who do not mostly did something specific they were warned against — went too far into a flat, drank from a pool, disturbed a threshold, spoke aloud in a bad place.

Go expecting to be changed a little, on a long timescale, in ways you will not notice for years. This is what the plateau does. It is what it has done to everything on it, including the mountains, including the builders, including the question of what the surface is a surface of. You will get a small dose of the same weathering. That is the visit. That is what you went for.

Keep the bowls full where you find them low. Walk softly. Come back.
