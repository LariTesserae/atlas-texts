# Preparing to Visit the Cistern

## Understanding What You're Entering

Before anything practical, understand this: you are going to a working place. Not a wonder, not a ruin, not a wilderness, though it has elements of all three. It is someone's jobsite. The people there are engineers, divers, tenders, masons. They have eleven-year veterans who have seen a tenth of it and forty-year veterans who have seen a tenth of it. If you go in with the attitude of a tourist you will be, at best, ignored; at worst, resented. Go in as a person who understands that other people are working and you are a guest in their workplace.

This orientation matters more than any gear.

## The Body

You will be underwater, deep, for extended periods. The water is cool — cellar-cool — not lethally cold, but cold enough to sap you over hours. You need:

- **A diving suit rated for sustained immersion at pressure.** Not a recreational wetsuit. Something closer to what the engineers themselves wear — likely a hardhat rig with air supply, or a modern analog, if your multiverse kit allows. Ask locally what they use. Match it. Do not bring exotic equipment that will mark you as foreign and, worse, may fail in ways the locals cannot help you with.
- **Pressure conditioning.** Spend time, before you go, at depth in your home world or a training pool. Your ears, your sinuses, your joints need to have met pressure before. The "fronts" that move through the Cistern when distant engines cycle will press on your eardrums. You need to know that feeling already.
- **Cardiovascular fitness for long, slow swimming.** Distances are measured in engines — leagues apart. You will not be sprinting. You will be moving steadily for hours. Train like a long-distance swimmer, not a sprinter.
- **Tolerance for the *whum*.** This is not trivial. The bone-conducted throb of the pumps is constant and inescapable. Some visitors cannot sleep through it. Some develop a kind of pressure-headache that compounds. Before you go, sleep for several nights with a low-frequency sound source in your room — a subwoofer playing a slow pulse at around 1 Hz, two pulses, offset. Get your nervous system used to *never being in silence*.

## The Mind

This is the harder preparation.

**You must adjust to a place without sky.** Humans, even experienced cave divers and submariners, have a deep expectation that *up* leads somewhere. In the Cistern, up leads to more Cistern. A diver who swam up for a week would still be in it. This is psychologically corrosive in ways that sneak up on you around day three or four. Prepare by:

- Spending time in very large enclosed spaces (cathedrals, hangars, mines) and deliberately sitting with the feeling that you cannot see outside.
- Practicing what I'd call *lateral orientation* — getting your sense of place from landmarks on the floor and walls rather than from the sky. Cave divers know this skill. Cultivate it.
- Making peace with the fact that you will not, during your visit, see the sun.

**You must adjust to the scale.** The Cistern is larger than a country. You will see perhaps a tenth of a percent of it. Accept this before you go. The temptation to "see as much as possible" will drive you to move fast, and moving fast in the Cistern gets people hurt. Pick a small region. Commit to knowing it.

**You must adjust to the constant light.** There is no night. The lamps never go off. Bring a sleep mask, a good one, and practice using it. Your circadian rhythm will drift; accept that too.

## What to Bring

- A **slate and grease pencil** or the local equivalent — something that writes underwater. You will want to note things, and your memory will blur in the uniform light.
- A **small, dim personal lamp** for close work, separate from the ambient lamps. Not for seeing-in-the-dark (there is no dark) but for reading detail on iron, for examining a weld, for signalling.
- **Food that travels well at pressure.** Ask locally. The engineers will know.
- **A gift.** This is my own advice and I stand by it. Bring something from your home world — a tool, a book, a small mechanical curiosity — to give to whoever hosts you. The Cistern's workers live in a closed world. A well-chosen gift is worth more than any amount of apparent expertise.
- **Currency, if relevant, or skills to trade.** You may need to pay for lodging, for a guide, for a lift between engines on a maintenance sled. Find out in advance what is valued.

## What Not to Bring

- **No weapons.** The Cistern's fauna are not hostile. The rule about the crabs, the octopuses, and especially the cormorant-things is absolute. A weapon marks you as someone who does not understand the place.
- **No collecting equipment.** Do not bring jars, nets, sample bags. You are not there to take specimens. The ecosystem is seeded and tended; removing things is, at best, rude, and at worst a crime against whoever seeded it.
- **No drones, no autonomous probes.** If the ceiling cannot be approached, your machines cannot approach it either. People have tried. They have come back wrong, or not come back. Do not send a proxy to do what you should not do yourself.
- **No bright flash photography in the open water.** The fish schools are part of how the place works and how the engineers navigate by lamp-light. Startling them is dangerous and disrespectful.

## The Rules — Take Them Seriously

Let me restate the rules of the place, because they are not suggestions:

1. **Do not approach the ceiling.** This is the most important rule and the one most likely to tempt you, because you are an explorer and the ceiling is a mystery. The rule was not decreed by anyone. It was *arrived at*, meaning people learned it by losing people. The cormorant-things come from up there. That is all the information you need.

2. **Do not kill the crabs, the octopuses, or the cormorant-things.** If you find yourself needing to, something has gone wrong and you should retreat, not escalate.

3. **Measure in engines.** Learn to do this. When someone tells you a place is four engines south of the Ward Gate, know what that means in swimming time, in air supply, in fatigue. Do not try to convert to your own units. The locals' units are load-bearing; they encode practical information about pump-houses as waypoints and shelter.

4. **Respect an engine that is down.** The water around a stopped pump goes strange. Currents reverse. Fish gather. It is not a good place to be. Stay clear until it is running again.

## Logistics of Arrival

If your multiverse transit lets you pick your entry point, do not enter at the ceiling (obviously) and do not enter inside an active intake shaft (you will be drawn in). Enter on one of the broad terraces, near but not inside a pump-house, where there will be people. Someone will find you within a few hours. Explain yourself clearly. Ask for a foreman.

If you can, time your arrival to coincide with the flooding of a new section, and ask to observe — at a safe distance — a new section going from rust-bloom to first kelp. This is the most extraordinary sight the Cistern offers, and it is an engineering miracle: the deliberate birth of an ecosystem in a volume larger than a city.

## A Final Thought

The narrator says: *We do not know what it is for.*

Do not press this question with the locals. They have had eleven years, forty years, to wonder, and they have chosen to work. The question is not taboo but it is tender, and a stranger asking it lightly will seem to be treating their life's labor as a puzzle. If the question comes up, let them bring it.

What I suspect, for what it is worth: the Cistern is a young place. It has just begun. Someone is building something enormous, something that needs a stable, tended, living body of water larger than any natural sea, and the pumps are its circulation and the kelp-rows are its first tentative organs. You are visiting a thing in its infancy. Visit it the way you would visit the nursery of a child who will grow to be powerful. Quietly. With respect. And without overstaying.

Go well. Keep your lamp lit. Come back.
