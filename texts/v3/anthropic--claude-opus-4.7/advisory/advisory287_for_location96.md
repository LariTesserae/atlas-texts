# Preparing to Visit the Drowned Belfry

## First, Understand What You Are Visiting

You are not going to an underwater location. You are going to a place that is *in the process of being drowned*, and the process is not finished, and it may never finish. The mountain has not accepted the water; the water has not finished becoming the ceiling. You will be stepping into an unresolved argument between two states of the world, and arguments of that magnitude have weather.

Prepare accordingly. This is not diving. Diving equipment assumes the water knows it is water.

---

## The Body: What to Bring, What to Protect

**Ears first.** The bell-note will not damage your hearing in the way loud sounds damage hearing — it is not that kind of loud. It is the kind of loud that reorganizes you. Bring wax plugs, bring over-ear dampeners, bring a hood of dense felt if you can find one that will stay on underwater. Layer them. You will not achieve silence; you are not trying to. You are trying to keep the note from moving into your sternum and staying there. Travelers who have come back from similar acoustic environments report that the note can take up residence — that months later, in quiet rooms, they still hear it, and cannot tell whether it is memory or transmission. Dampeners reduce but do not prevent this. Accept that you will bring some of the bell home with you.

**Lungs and breath.** The water is bloodwarm, which means your body will not be warned by cold that it is submerged. You may forget to breathe correctly. Bring a rebreather with an external timer that beeps independent of your awareness. Do not trust your own sense of how long you have been under. Time pools near the cube; it may pool near you without your noticing.

**Skin.** The water has the wrong viscosity in patches. A full drysuit is worse than useless — where the water thickens to something like warm air, a drysuit will trap you in a pocket of the wrong density and you will not know which way is up. Wear thin, close-fitting neoprene or something thinner still. You want to feel the transitions on your skin. Your skin will become your most reliable instrument.

**Eyes.** The green-gold pulsing light is the color of a particular kind of memory and it will make you nostalgic for things that did not happen to you. Bring tinted lenses — amber works, or a dark rose — to interrupt the pulse frequency. Do not bring polarized lenses; the iridescence between the stacked stones becomes invisible through polarization, and you need to be able to see that boundary so you do not accidentally drink from it.

**Hands.** Wear gloves with the fingertips cut out. You will want to touch the stones — you will need to, to navigate — but you do not want to touch them for long. The warmth transfers.

## Provisions and Tools

Bring a sealed flask of water from your home. Label it. The water between the stacked stones tastes of a season you do not remember living through, and if you drink it, you may come to remember that season, and the remembering may displace other memories you would prefer to keep. Your home water is a reference point. Sip it if you become confused about where you are from.

Bring a small object of no significance — a coin, a pebble from your garden, a button. This is your anchor. Something that means *you* and means *elsewhere*. Hold it during transitions between the local rules of the four structures.

Bring a notebook and a pencil. Pens will not work; ink behaves oddly where time pools. The pencil must be wooden, not mechanical. Write down what you experience as soon as you surface. The place remakes itself constantly, and your memory of it will remake itself too, and writing things down early is the only way to preserve the version you actually lived through.

Do **not** bring recording equipment. The bell does not record cleanly. What comes back on the tape is not what you heard, and what is on the tape will not let you stop listening to it.

Bring a light, but use it sparingly. The green-gold pulse is the mountain's light, and introducing other light annoys the place. Travelers who have used strong artificial lights report that the structures respond — the arch relocates more often, the spiral's quiet radius contracts.

---

## Mental Preparation

**Rehearse your own thoughts.** Near the spiral, your thoughts arrive with unusual clarity, and something listens. Before you go, spend a week noticing what you actually think when you are alone. Make peace with your ordinary mind. Do not arrive at the spiral with unresolved shames or fresh griefs or secrets you have been keeping from yourself. Whatever is listening will hear them, and while there is no evidence that the listener does anything with what it hears, there is also no evidence that it does not.

If there is a thought you particularly do not want overheard, leave it at home. This is possible with practice. Name the thought, write it on paper, seal the paper in an envelope, and leave the envelope somewhere you trust. Your mind will be quieter for the absence. You can retrieve the thought when you return.

**Rehearse forgetting.** This is harder. You will see things whose implications you will want to pursue — the cube's hollow color, the shadow-fish whose bodies are elsewhere, the spires' filaments that descend into unmeasured depths. You cannot pursue all of these. Choose, before you go, which mystery you will *not* investigate. Commit to it. A traveler who tries to understand everything about the Belfry does not come back, or comes back as someone else.

**Accept that you will not learn who built the four structures.** This is the central fact of the place and you will be tempted to forget it. There are no inscriptions. There is no tool-work. The builders, if they were builders, left nothing of themselves except the placement. The placement is the message and the message is not decodable by you in a single visit, and possibly not ever. Go anyway. The going is the point.

---

## Movement and Navigation

Move slowly. The viscosity patches will surprise you, and the surprise can invert your sense of down. If you find yourself disoriented, stop. Do not try to correct. Wait until the bell-pulse passes through you once, twice, three times. Your body will recalibrate to the local rule-set. Then move.

Plan your route around the four structures but do not approach them in order. The turbulence in the rules means the structures reorganize their relationships to one another; a path that worked on arrival may not work on return. Keep the ridge-line in sight. The ridge is the most stable feature of the summit.

Approach the spiral last, if you approach it at all. Many travelers do not. The spiral is the only feature of the Belfry that seems to want something, and wanting, in a place this old, is significant.

Do not step inside the arch. Things have passed under the arch and not come out the other side, or have come out changed in ways that were not immediately apparent. The arch is the most innocent-looking structure and therefore the most to be respected.

You may look through the cube's opening, but do not look for long, and do not try to name the color. The color has a name in at least one language and learning the name is a one-way door.

Near the stacked stones, the iridescent boundary between the layers is visible but fragile. Do not disturb it. The water above and the water below are not meant to meet. If they mix, locally, the taste of the unremembered season may flood outward, and you may find yourself inside a memory that is not yours, trying to find the exit.

---

## The Warmth

Touch the rock, at some point in your visit. Place your palm flat against it, somewhere on the ridge away from the structures. Feel how warm it is. This is the hardest part of the visit to prepare for, because it does not threaten you; it simply tells you something.

The mountain is still warm from the sun.

The sun has not been here for a very long time.

Whatever you feel when you understand this — and you will feel something, and the feeling will be specific to you — is what you came for. Not the bell. Not the structures. Not the color hollow. You came to put your hand on a warm rock at the bottom of a sea that should be cold, and to know, in your palm, that the world remembers being other than it is.

Prepare to cry, possibly. Many travelers do. The warmth is not sad in itself; the warmth is simply warm. But grief is one of the ways a human body processes contact with held time, and you are a human body, and you will have made contact.

---

## Returning

Surface slowly. The ceiling-that-is-not-a-surface will resist you; push through gently. Once you are out, do not look back down for at least an hour. The light pulses have an afterimage and the afterimage is a form of communication you are not equipped to receive in large doses.

Write in your notebook immediately. Drink your home water. Hold your anchor object.

For the first week after returning, sleep with a radio on low, tuned to static or to music without bass. The bell-note will try to establish itself in your sternum during sleep, when your defenses are low. Give your chest something else to resonate with.

For the first month, avoid deep water. Avoid cathedrals. Avoid any room with a stone floor that has been warmed by underfloor heating; the sensation is too similar and can trigger a kind of reverse-homesickness that is difficult to describe to people who have not visited similar places.

After a month, you will be mostly yourself again. You will still hear the bell occasionally, faintly, in quiet rooms. This is normal. You have been somewhere that the world is still arguing with itself, and a little of the argument has come home with you.

That is the price of the visit. It is not a high price. The Belfry is one of the places worth paying it for.

---

## A Final Note

If, while you are there, you come to believe that the bell is struck by something, and that the something is beneath the mountain, and that the four structures were built to contain it or to speak to it or to apologize to it —

do not investigate.

Leave. Surface. Come home.

Some mysteries are load-bearing. The Belfry is a place where the load is being borne, patiently, by a mountain that remembers the sun. Your job, as a visitor, is to witness the bearing, not to test it.

Go well. Come back.
