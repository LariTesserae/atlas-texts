# Preparing to Visit the Gestation Shelf

## What You Are Walking Into

Understand first what this place *is*, because your preparation depends on it: you are visiting a world in the act of being made. Not a ruin, not a wilderness, not a frontier — a nursery. The rock is younger than you are. The animals are prototypes. The atmosphere is a held breath. You will be the most finished thing on the shelf, and that asymmetry matters more than any of the practical hazards.

Go in with the disposition of someone entering a kiln, a hatchery, or a chapel mid-service. Not a tourist. Not a scientist with a clipboard, either — something quieter than that.

## Physical Preparation

**Footwear.** This is the single most important item. Your boots need to be soft-soled and broad — think felted wool, thick crepe, something that spreads your weight. The half-stone is slick and unfinished and your edges will mark it. Treaded hiking boots will leave prints that may still be there, fossilized, when the shelf is done. Consider whether you want that kind of signature on a world's early geology. I would not.

**Clothing.** Layers that breathe. The warmth is steady and the humidity is high — you will sweat. But when the mist fronts come through, the harmonic shifts and the bench streams, and you will be soaked for several minutes. Wool base layer, a shell that sheds water without being plasticky. Avoid synthetic fabrics that shed microfibers; they will not degrade here in any timeframe the shelf recognizes, and they will be incorporated.

**Respiration.** The air is thick, warm, wet, and pressurized by whatever the trellises are doing. It is probably breathable — the fauna breathe it — but its composition is not guaranteed to match yours. Bring a small analyzer if you have one. A light filtering mask for the first hour is wise, not because the air is toxic but because it may contain aerosols from the trellis output, spores of the lichen-analog, or the shed material of the folded things. Your immune system has opinions about unfamiliar proteins.

**Ear protection, carefully chosen.** The trellis chord sits in your sternum and climbs into pressure-register. Standard earplugs will block the audible component and leave the sub-audible to work on you unmediated, which can produce nausea, vertigo, and a peculiar emotional lability. Better: musician's filters that attenuate evenly across the spectrum, or nothing at all. If you go without, expect to cry at some point for no reason you can name. This is normal and will pass.

**Hydration and food.** Bring your own water. The water weeping from the rock is clean in the chemical sense but it is *participatory* — it is part of the shelf's circulation, and drinking it means joining that circulation in ways not well characterized. Eat before you arrive and bring dense rations. Do not eat anything you find. The grazers are mostly fluid inside a skin; whatever the salamander-thing digests them into is not food for you.

## What to Bring

- A notebook with a soft pencil. Ink pens leak in the humidity and ballpoints skip on damp paper.
- A light source with a warm, diffuse output. The dusk is uniform and your eyes will adjust; a harsh LED will blind you to the trellis luminescence, which is one of the things you came to see.
- A small offering. I am serious. Someone tends this place. The cairn and the wall and the doorway-frame are the marks of a presence that visits. You are a visitor too, and it is courteous to acknowledge the other visitor. A stone from a finished world. A piece of worked metal. Something small, placed somewhere visible, taken back when you leave if it feels right, left if it doesn't.
- A timepiece that does not rely on sunlight or satellite. The long warm dusk does not advance and you will lose time badly. Set an alarm for your departure.

## What to Leave Behind

- Anything that sheds: glitter, loose fibers, flaking paint, dandruff if you can help it (shower beforehand, wear a cap).
- Metal objects you are not keeping track of. A dropped coin on a finished world is litter. A dropped coin here is a geological inclusion that will be found in ten thousand years by something that evolved around it.
- Recording devices that emit. Passive cameras are fine. Anything that pings, scans, or broadcasts will interact with the trellis harmonic in ways you cannot predict and the tender may not appreciate.
- Strong scents. Soap, perfume, deodorant. The fauna are learning what exists; do not teach them you.
- Your assumptions about ecology. This system runs on sound and warmth pumped in from off-stage. Nothing you know about trophic levels applies cleanly.

## Behavioral Preparation

**Move slowly.** Not out of fear — out of respect for the pace of the place. The shelf is working on geological time compressed into something more like gestational time, and your quick human tempo will feel violent in it.

**Do not touch the trellises.** They are the engines. Their filigrees reorganize when you look away; imagine what they do when you put a hand on them. The downwind rock is markedly more committed to being rock, and you do not want to stand downwind of a trellis for long either — you are made of water and protein and the trellis does not distinguish between substrates it is supposed to cure and substrates it isn't.

**Do not go through the doorway-frame.** The view through it is subtly wrong. That is a door. It goes somewhere. It is not yours.

**Do not interact with the fauna beyond observation.** They are first drafts. You are a finished draft from elsewhere, and anything you do to them — feeding, handling, even prolonged close attention — is a selection pressure the shelf did not plan for. The folded things especially: do not try to follow their geometry when they refold. People have done this and come back changed in ways that are hard to describe and harder to undo.

**If the tender arrives, be still.** You may not recognize them as a person. They may not recognize you as one either. Do not speak first. Do not offer anything unless offered to. If they ignore you, you have been accepted. If they attend to you, answer simply and truthfully and do not ask questions about the project. It is not yours to know.

## Psychological Preparation

This is the part most travelers underprepare for.

The shelf will make you feel things. The trellis chord works on your body directly, and the emotional valence it produces is not quite any human emotion — closest, perhaps, to the feeling of watching a child sleep, or the feeling of being in a cathedral before a service begins. Reverence mixed with tenderness mixed with a low dread that is not about danger but about *scale.* You are standing in a place where a world is being made on purpose. Whoever is making it is not you, and is not, probably, of your order.

Some visitors come away from the Gestation Shelf and cannot settle into finished worlds for months afterward. Everything seems too *done,* too complacent in its being-itself. Prepare for this by deciding in advance what you will return to and why. Have a meal planned. Have a person to see.

Some visitors come away wanting to be tenders themselves. This is a longer road and not one you can start from a single visit. If the impulse arrives, note it and let it sit for a year before acting on it.

## On Leaving

Leave the way you came in. Walk out by the same path. Brush the soles of your boots against a finished stone before stepping off the bench, so that what you carry away is only what you brought. If you left an offering, decide at the threshold whether to retrieve it. If you left footprints in the half-stone, there is nothing to be done about them now; apologize silently and go.

Do not look back from the cloud layer. Not because anything bad will happen — nothing will — but because the shelf is working, and your backward glance is an attention it does not need. Let it be unobserved. Let it get on with becoming.

## A Final Note

The shelf is not dangerous in the way a wilderness is dangerous. It is indifferent in the way a kiln is indifferent. A kiln will not chase you, but it will also not notice if you fall in. Behave accordingly: with the calm, attentive, slightly formal care of someone who knows that the room they are in is doing important work, and who wishes neither to interrupt it nor to be absorbed into it.

Bring your wonder. Leave your hurry. Go.
