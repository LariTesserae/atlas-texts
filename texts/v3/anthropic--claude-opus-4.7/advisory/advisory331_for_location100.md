# Preparing to Visit The Hollow

## Understanding What You're Walking Into

Before anything practical, you need to understand the nature of this place, because The Hollow is not dangerous in the usual sense — it will not attack you, poison you, or trap you — but it exerts a subtle pressure on the visitor that can be destabilizing if you arrive unprepared. The Hollow is a place that has *finished*. It has completed whatever process it was engaged in and now simply exists in a state of arrival. Humans, who are almost definitionally unfinished creatures, tend to feel this kind of completeness as either profound peace or profound wrongness, sometimes both in alternation. Know which one you are before you go. If you tend toward existential vertigo — the kind that hits you in empty churches or at the bottoms of canyons — bring a companion for the trip out, even if you enter alone.

## Physical Preparation

**Footwear:** Soft-soled shoes or boots, well broken in. The dust is fine as flour and will accept any print you make. You will be walking on stone that has been warm for a geologic eternity, and you want to feel that warmth coming up through your soles — this is part of the experience. Avoid anything with deep treads that will gouge the dust unnecessarily. You are a guest. Leave small prints.

**Clothing:** Layered, loose, breathable. The ground gives a dry warmth but the air itself stands still at what may be cool temperatures depending on the rotation. Light-colored fabrics will reflect what little starlight there is and let you see yourself if you look down. Avoid anything synthetic that rustles — the silence there is not the silence of quieted things but of things that were never asked to make sound, and your clothing should not be the first to raise the question.

**Skin:** Moisturize before going. The place is thirsty at the mineral level, and a dry wind-less air will pull water from you slowly without your noticing. Lip balm. No perfumes or scented products — the faint mineral smell of remembered water is part of what makes the basin legible, and you should not overlay it.

**Water:** Carry more than you think you need. Drink before entering and after leaving, but consider drinking sparingly inside the basin. Water bottles are loud. Swallowing is loud. You will hear yourself.

## Temporal Preparation

This is the most important category, and the one most travelers neglect.

The Hollow runs on a different clock. Not faster or slower — *differently weighted*. A minute in the basin is a minute, but the minute does not ask to be spent. If you go in with a schedule, the schedule will feel grotesque. If you go in with the expectation of "seeing" the place in the way you see a monument or a vista, you will leave unsatisfied and not know why.

**Clear your day.** Not your afternoon, not a few hours — your day. Do not plan anything for before or after that requires active cognition. The Hollow induces a kind of settling that is incompatible with appointments.

**Remove your watch.** If your multiverse transit device keeps time, silence its notifications and cover its face. You will know when it is time to leave. The place will not tell you, but you will know.

**Practice stillness beforehand.** For at least a week before your visit, spend twenty minutes each day sitting without stimulus. Not meditating with a technique, just sitting. If you cannot do this without climbing the walls, you are not ready for The Hollow yet. It will still be there when you are.

## Psychological Preparation

Expect the basin to not notice you. This sounds like nothing, but it is the defining experience of the place, and many visitors find it harder than they anticipated. We are used to places registering us — a forest shifts around our movement, a city acknowledges us with a thousand small frictions, even a desert responds to footfall with displaced sand and startled insects. The Hollow will not respond. You will make a footprint in the dust and the dust will hold it without comment. You will breathe and the air will not move. You will speak, if you speak, and your voice will return to you unchanged.

Some visitors report feeling erased by this. Others report feeling, for the first time, what it would be to exist without being evaluated. Both reactions are correct. Be ready for either.

**A specific warning about the tool.** There is a worked stone near the central plant. You will want to pick it up. Do not pick it up. Not because something bad will happen — nothing will happen — but because the dust has settled evenly around it, and that settling is the record of a stranger's passage finally being accepted by the place. If you disturb it, you will have edited a sentence that took longer than your civilization to write. Look at it. Crouch near it if you want. Notice the chipped edge. Then leave it alone.

**A note on the large plant.** It is older than the ridges are rounded. When you approach it, you may feel something like being watched, though nothing is watching. This is an artifact of your brain trying to account for the presence of a living thing that operates on a timescale so distant from yours that you cannot detect its participation in the world. The feeling will pass. Do not speak to the plant. (You will want to. Don't. Whatever you would say would be in the wrong tense.)

## What to Bring

- Water, as discussed.
- A small cloth to sit on if you sit. The dust is fine and will not harm you, but it will cling, and you may not want to carry it back.
- A light source you will not use. The basin's faint internal luminosity is sufficient once your eyes adjust, which takes about ten minutes. Bring the light for emergencies only, and accept that turning it on will destroy the visit.
- Nothing to write with. Nothing to record with. The Hollow does not photograph. It does not describe. If you try to capture it, you will return with images of a dim empty basin and wonder why you went. Trust your memory, and trust that what you cannot remember will remain in you anyway, below the level where memory is kept.

## What Not to Bring

- Music, podcasts, any audio. Obvious.
- Food. The basin is not a place for eating. Eat before and after.
- Grief you are trying to process. The Hollow is not a therapeutic space. It does not comfort. It does not metabolize your feelings for you. If you arrive with unresolved pain hoping the silence will absorb it, you will find instead that the silence simply lets the pain be what it is, at full volume, without distraction. This can be useful for some people and catastrophic for others. Know yourself.
- Expectations of revelation. The Hollow does not tell you anything. It does not have a message. Visitors who arrive expecting wisdom leave disappointed; visitors who arrive expecting nothing sometimes leave changed.

## Arrival

Step over the low ridge slowly. Pause at the top before descending. Let your eyes adjust — genuinely adjust, not just "I can see now" but fully dark-adapted, which takes longer than you think. The basin will seem to brighten as this happens. This is not hallucination; the dust really is holding a faint charge of light, and your eyes are becoming able to receive it.

Walk down into the basin on soft feet. Do not walk directly to the center. Circle the perimeter first if you want; or sit near the edge; or just stand. There is no itinerary. The worked stone and the central plant are not destinations. They are features you may or may not approach.

## During

Breathe. Notice that your breathing sounds loud. Accept this. You are a guest whose sounds are audible because the host is hospitable enough to let them be audible without comment.

If you sit, sit for longer than feels necessary. The first five minutes of sitting are restlessness. The next ten are adjustment. After that, something else begins, and that something else is what you came for, though you will not be able to describe it afterward.

If at any point you feel the place "mean" something to you, be careful. The Hollow does not issue meanings. Any meaning you find there came from you and was returned to you, slightly altered by the silence. This is not a failure — it may be the most valuable thing the place offers — but do not mistake it for a message from the basin.

## Departure

You will know when to leave. It will not feel like being told. It will feel like a small internal gesture of completion, the way you know a sentence is finished without counting its words.

Walk out the way you came in. Step over the ridge. Here is the crucial instruction: **look back once, and only once.** The basin will appear exactly as you found it. Your footprints will be there, but they will look like they have always been there. This is correct. You have not damaged anything. You have simply been recorded, at the pace the place records things, which is a pace that includes you now.

Turn around and leave. Do not look back a second time. A second look is the look of someone who wants the place to acknowledge their departure, and it will not, and you will feel small. One look is enough. One look is the whole of it.

## After

Drink water. Sit somewhere ordinary for at least an hour before engaging with other people. The transition back to places that notice you is harder than you expect.

You may find, in the following days, that you are quieter than usual. This passes, or it doesn't. Some visitors carry a small portion of the basin's stillness with them for the rest of their lives, like a stone in a pocket. This is generally considered a good outcome, though it can make you hard to live with for people who were used to a louder version of you.

Do not go back too soon. The Hollow is not a place you visit twice in quick succession; the second visit, too close to the first, feels thin, because you are still carrying the first. Wait at least a year. Longer if you can. The place will not have changed. That is the point.
