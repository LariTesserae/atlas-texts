# Preparing to Visit the Frostmoss Warren

## Understanding What You're Walking Into

Before anything else, you need to internalize a single hard truth: **you are arriving as a giant in a place where giants are a kind of weather**. Everything you do here will be, to the builders, a geological event. Your footfall is a minor earthquake. Your breath is a weather front. Your body heat, in a place this cold and this closed, is a localized catastrophe. Prepare not as a tourist but as someone attending a religious ceremony in a cathedral made of matchsticks.

The preparation is mostly about becoming smaller, slower, and less eventful than you naturally are.

## Clothing and Body

**Cold-weather layering, but not your usual kind.** You want the cold of an unheated church — around 2–5°C, steady. This is not survival cold; it's patience cold. Dress so you can stand perfectly still for an hour without shivering, because shivering at the wrong moment could knock over a tower.

- Wool base layers, not synthetic — synthetics shed microplastic fibers that a city this small would treat as permanent debris.
- A dark, matte outer layer. Nothing that reflects the bluish mosslight back at the ice-pearls (you don't want to confuse whatever mechanism the warren uses to remember light).
- **Soft-soled shoes**, ideally felt-bottomed or wrapped in cloth. No treads. A boot tread can pick up a paved alley and carry a district home on your heel.
- **Cover your hair.** A shed human hair, three meters long at warren scale, is a construction hazard.
- Gloves, always. Your fingertips are hot, oily, and salty. The ice-pearls will react to any of those.

**Crucially: no scents.** No deodorant, no laundry softener, no toothpaste breath. You are entering a closed-circuit ecology that recycles its own exhalations. What you bring in, it will eventually incorporate.

## The Body You Need to Borrow

Spend the week before your visit practicing **stillness**. Not meditation exactly — practical stillness. Can you hold a half-crouch for twenty minutes? Can you breathe through your nose for an hour without clearing your throat? Can you walk heel-to-toe down a narrow plank without looking at your feet?

The warren is not dangerous to you. You are dangerous to it. Every clumsiness is permanent.

Also practice **looking without staring**. The rule about the pale shapes — *do not let it know you saw* — suggests peripheral awareness matters. Learn to take in a scene through the side of your eye, holding your gaze on something innocuous. Hunters and wildlife photographers know this skill.

## The Breath Problem

This is the hardest one. You exhale warm, wet air. In a cold closed warren that has built its entire ecology around recycling trace humidity through waxy cuticles, your breath is a monsoon. A few breaths near a cushion-moss pillow may bloom it strangely. A sustained visit near a single structure may leave a permanent mark — a section of moss that grew warmer and damper than its neighbors and has to be compensated for, forever, in the builders' slow accounting.

Options:
- **A cold trap scarf**: loosely woven wool wrapped several times, which catches and returns most of your exhaled moisture to your own face. Uncomfortable but effective.
- **Shallow breathing discipline**. Breathe into your collar. Exhale downward, never toward a structure.
- **Limit your visit.** Thirty minutes is generous. An hour is an imposition. A full day is colonization.

## What to Bring

Almost nothing. The warren is a place you pass through, not one you occupy.

- A small, cold light source for the climb down (the root-breach chimney). Extinguish it before you reach the floor. The mosslight is sufficient; your lumens are an insult.
- Water in an insulated container, to drink *before* you descend, not inside. You must not leave condensation rings. You must not urinate anywhere within the warren or the chimney.
- **The offering.** This is the most important single item. Choose it before you go, deliberately, as a gift:
  - A sliver of your own fingernail, clipped fresh (most personal; most biologically legible to them).
  - A chip of bone — a small fragment of a chicken wishbone works and carries meaning from another ecology.
  - A fleck of seashell, preferably from a place you loved.
  - Avoid manufactured things. No plastic, no metal shavings, nothing alloyed. The builders work in chipped stone; give them something they can chip.

Place the offering at the edge of the warren, not in it. Near the base of the root-breach, on bare stone, not on moss. Don't arrange it prettily. Just set it down.

## What Not to Bring

- **No cameras with flashes.** If you must photograph (and I would argue against it), only with available light and a silent shutter. Understand that the ice-pearls may record the act of being photographed in ways you don't control.
- **No recording devices that click, whir, or emit IR or ultrasonic focusing beams.** The high hum of the moss suggests an acoustic ecology you cannot perceive but can disrupt.
- **Nothing sticky.** No tape, no adhesive bandages, no lip balm.
- **No food.** Crumbs are catastrophes. A single raisin dropped in the warren is a monument no builder asked for.
- **No iron.** Trust this one on intuition. Bring bronze or bone tools if you need tools.

## The Mental Preparation

Read slow books beforehand. Sit with geological time. Watch a tree for an hour without moving.

The hardest thing about the warren is that **nothing visible is happening** and you must not fill that silence with yourself. Humans, in quiet places, start performing — narrating to ourselves, humming, fidgeting, taking pictures to prove we were there. The warren will politely tolerate this but will not reveal itself to anyone doing it.

If you want to see a stone placed, you will have to stop wanting to see a stone placed. The builders work in the pauses where human attention flickers. This is not mysticism; it is, I suspect, a literal description of their speed relative to ours. They may be moving constantly at a rate our nervous systems edit out as non-motion, the way we edit out the movement of a minute hand.

## The Rules You Were Given, Expanded

**Do not step on the paved places.** Obvious. Walk only on bare stone or on the thickest cushion-moss, and step lightly. Plan your path in before you descend. Memorize it. Reverse it exactly on the way out.

**Do not touch the ice-pearls.** The text says they are how the warren *remembers light*. Think about what that means. They may be something like a distributed memory, a recording substrate. Your fingerprint's oil would be a scar on a library.

**Do not speak above the hum of the moss.** Better: do not speak at all. If you must communicate with a companion, have signs prepared in advance. The hum is quiet — quieter than your whisper. Assume the warren's acoustic floor is close to silence.

**Do not follow the pale shapes, and do not let them know you saw.** This is the rule that suggests the warren has a social contract you can violate. The pale shapes may be the builders, or their cousins, or their predators, or all three — the text is clear that fauna and architects may not be distinct categories. Whatever they are, they have chosen to be unseen, and respecting that choice is likely the single gesture that distinguishes a welcome visitor from a trespasser. If one crosses your path, let your eyes drift past it. Acknowledge nothing. Keep walking at your established pace.

## Leaving

Leave by the way you came. Do not explore a second exit even if you see one.

Climb out of the root-breach slowly. At the top, before you emerge into the meadow, pause and let the warmer air equalize before you break the seal with your body. A sudden warm draft down the chimney is another weather event.

Once out, cover the root-breach if it was covered when you found it. If it was open, leave it open.

**Do not return within your lifetime.** This is my strongest suggestion and not one the description made explicit. A place that builds at the pace of a tree adding a ring does not want regular visitors. One visit, carefully made, becomes part of its history. Repeat visits become erosion.

## A Last Thought

You will not understand what you saw. You will come back up through the elm's old taproot into ordinary meadow light and feel, strongly, that you dreamed it. This feeling is correct and should be honored. Do not tell the story often. Do not write it down in detail. Places like the Frostmoss Warren survive partly because visitors forget them a little, and the forgetting is part of the contribution — another grain of something hard, given back.

You went down a hole in a meadow and left a flake of fingernail in a city the size of an orchard. Somewhere in the slow centuries ahead, a builder you never saw will chip that fingernail into a keystone for an arch in a district that will be finished long after you, the meadow, and possibly the elm are gone.

That is the whole of the trip. Prepare for it accordingly.
