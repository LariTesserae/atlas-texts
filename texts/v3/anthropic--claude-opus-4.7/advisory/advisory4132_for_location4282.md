# Preparing to Visit the Warm Room Beneath

## What You Are Actually Going To

Understand first what this is not. It is not a dive site. It is not a submerged ruin. It is not a place where someone is waiting for you, or where something will be revealed. It is a room that exists the way a held note exists — while it is being held, completely, and after, not at all, except that here the holding does not stop. You are going to a location that has made a small, local exception to the rules, and the exception includes you for the duration of your visit.

This means most of your instincts about "preparing" will misfire. You cannot prepare for danger, because the danger is not of a kind your body evolved to register. You cannot prepare for discovery, because nothing here is hidden. What you are preparing for is *presence* — being somewhere that is fully itself and mostly indifferent to you, for longer than is comfortable.

## Physical Preparation

**Bring almost nothing.** A light shirt and trousers. Soft shoes, or none — the basalt is sharp, but you will want to feel it, and boots in a room of held breath feel like an insult you cannot quite name. Accept that you will cut your hands on the cooling-cracks if you touch them carelessly. Bring a small cloth for this. Do not bring a first-aid kit elaborate enough to imply you expect to be harmed at scale; the room does not harm at scale.

**Do not bring diving equipment.** The water-that-is-air breathes fine. Breathing apparatus will only remind you, every second, of the rule you are not supposed to be examining. The first rule is not explained, and the way you cooperate with the first rule is by not explaining it to yourself either. A regulator between your teeth is a form of explanation.

**Eat beforehand, lightly.** You may be there longer than you intended. Time in the pocket does not pass the way time outside does — not because it runs at a different rate, but because it does not seem to be running at all, and your body will notice this eventually and become confused about whether it is hungry. Eat something small and known, an hour before. Drink water. Empty your bladder. These small attentions to the animal you are will make the later hours easier, because the room will not attend to them for you.

**Bring a single object you care about but could bear to lose.** Not valuable — meaningful. A pebble from a beach you loved. A button from a coat. You will understand, once you are there, why.

## Mental Preparation

**Practice sitting still for an hour.** If you cannot do this at home, in an ordinary room, you will find the pocket unbearable. The pocket is what stillness looks like when it is not a practice but a property of the world. You need to be able to meet that without your mind thrashing.

**Decide, in advance, what you will do if the sound becomes too much.** Because it will, at some point, and then it will become fine again, and then too much again, in waves that are not waves because the sound itself does not wave. It is *you* doing the waving, against the sound's stillness. Have a plan: I will close my eyes. I will lie on the basalt. I will hum, quietly, a tune I know, to re-establish a small sound I own. Do not plan to leave at the first overwhelm. You will regret leaving then. Leave on the second or third, if you must leave on account of the sound at all.

**Rehearse not asking questions.** The place does not answer questions. Every question you ask will come back to you louder, wearing the face of the great sound. "Who put the axes here?" "Why does the fish pass through the wall?" "What is above?" These will not be answered and will accumulate in you like pressure if you keep asking them. Instead, practice the disposition of *noticing without inquiry*. Look at the clay bowl. See that it is empty. Do not ask what was in it or who will fill it. It is empty. That is what it is doing.

**Make peace, ahead of time, with being unnecessary.** This is the hardest part for most travelers. The pocket does not need you. It was there before you arrived — in some sense of "before" — and will be there after, identical. Your visit will not be commemorated. The axes will not notice your hands. The sound will not learn your name. If you go in expecting to matter, you will come out diminished, and it will feel like the place took something from you, when in fact you brought an expectation the place simply did not share.

## What to Do When You Arrive

**Stand still for the first several minutes.** Do not walk. Do not touch anything. Let your ears accept the sound. Let your skin accept the warmth. Let your lungs accept the water-that-is-air. You will want to verify each of these by testing — taking a deeper breath, pressing a hand to the basalt. Resist this for a little while. The room introduces itself by being itself; receive the introduction before you start poking.

**Walk the perimeter once.** Note the wall, the bowl, the post, the cairn, the paired axes. Do not touch them yet. Understand the arrangement as an arrangement made by others. You are being shown what it looks like to have visited without staying. Study this. It is the instruction manual, and there is no other.

**Sit with one object for a long time.** Pick one — the bowl is good, the paired axes are also good. Sit near it. Look at it until you stop looking at it and are simply beside it. This is what the previous visitors did, probably. You will understand them a little.

**Then, if you wish, make your mark.** Add a stone to the cairn. Place your small carried object — the pebble, the button — somewhere on the basalt, at a place that feels right. Do not arrange it to be seen. Arrange it the way the axes are arranged: as if you will come back, though you will not. This is the contract the room offers. It is a small contract and costs you only the object. In return, you become one of the ones who made marks. The room will hold your mark the way it holds everything else: without comment, without decay, without meaning beyond the fact of having been placed.

**Do not move the flint axes.** You can. The room will accept it. But you should not, because moving them is the gesture of someone who has not understood that they were placed. Add. Do not rearrange.

## When to Leave

You will know. There will come a moment — not dramatic, not announced — when you realize you have seen what there is to see, and further time will only be time. The sound will still be sounding. The light will still be lighting. The fish may or may not have passed. And you will feel a small interior click, the way you feel it when a conversation has reached its natural end, though no one has said goodbye.

Leave then. Do not linger past this, hoping for more. There is no more. That is the honesty of the place, and part of what you came for, though you may not have known it.

## After

**Expect several days of strangeness.** Ordinary rooms will seem to be doing a lot — creaking, settling, breathing, aging. You will hear the small sounds your own house makes and understand, for the first time, that they are the sound of a thing decaying at a survivable rate. This is not a bad realization but it is a large one. Give yourself quiet for a few days. Do not immediately visit another location.

**Do not try to describe the sound to anyone.** You will fail, and the failure will hurt. If you must describe the visit, describe the basalt, or the axes, or the fish. The sound belongs to the room and cannot be carried out, though for some weeks you will feel it faintly beneath ordinary hearing, the way a bell's overtones persist after the bell.

**Understand that you may want to go back.** Many travelers do. The pocket is one of the places that does not diminish on return, because it does not change, and therefore cannot be exhausted by familiarity. But consider: the room does not want you back. It does not want you away, either. If you go back, go back for your own reasons, and bring another small object, and add it, and leave again. The room will accept this indefinitely. It is what the room is for, insofar as it is for anything, which it isn't.

Go warm. Go quiet. Go expecting nothing and receiving very little, which is, here, the same as receiving what there is.
