# Preparing to Visit the Bryophyte Town

## The First Thing to Understand

You are going somewhere wet. Not damp, not humid — *wet*, in the way a sponge left in a sink is wet, in the way the inside of your mouth is wet. Every preparation flows from this. If you internalize nothing else, internalize that your relationship with water is about to change. You will stop trying to stay dry within a few hours of arrival, and the trip only begins properly once you've accepted this.

## Clothing

Forget waterproofing. Waterproof fabrics in a place like this become clammy prisons — you will sweat inside them in the cool-wet air, and the condensation will be indistinguishable from the drip. What you want instead is clothing that accepts water gracefully: wool, especially hard-spun wool that stays warm when soaked; boiled wool outer layers; linen underlayers that dry against the skin. Avoid cotton entirely — it holds water without insulating and will chill you.

Bring:
- Two or three sets of wool underlayers so you can rotate
- A boiled-wool cloak or heavy tunic, hooded
- Oiled leather for your feet, well-greased before you arrive and re-greased every evening with tallow or lanolin (the townspeople will have some; ask)
- Wool socks in excess — six pairs minimum for a week — and accept that all of them will be damp most of the time
- A knitted cap, because your head loses heat in the cloud-mornings
- A broad felt hat for when the clouds lift and the low sun hits

Leave behind anything with metal zippers (they will corrode visibly within days), anything down-filled (catastrophic when wet), anything you're precious about. The moss-spores will green the seams of your clothing by the end of the first week and this is not reversible.

## Footwear, Particularly

The duckboards are narrow, slick, and often laid over water deep enough to swallow your ankle. You want flexible-soled boots — stiff hiking boots will betray you on the corkscrew lanes. Soft leather, ankle-high, treated heavily with grease. Bring a second pair. You will need to alternate, drying one by the fire while wearing the other, and you will never wear fully dry boots for the duration of your stay. Get used to this before arriving by wearing damp shoes for a full day at home. It is unpleasant in a specific way and you should know the unpleasantness in advance so it doesn't surprise you in-situ.

## The Body

Take whatever you take for fungal infections — athlete's foot preventatives, antifungal powder, anything of that kind — and bring more than you think. Your skin will be negotiating with spores and with constant moisture. Between your toes, under your arms, behind your ears: these places need attention daily. The townspeople have herbal practices for this; you will not have immunity to the specific microbial ecology, and your body will need help.

If you are prone to respiratory issues, reconsider. The air is saturated with mold spores, moss spores, and the exhalations of an enormous amount of living matter in close quarters. Asthmatics should not go. Everyone else should expect a week of adjustment during which their lungs feel *full* in a way that is not dangerous but is noticeable. Bring any inhaler or bronchial support you rely on.

Ears: the constant drip and chitter will exhaust your auditory processing in a way you won't predict. Bring wax earplugs for sleep. You will need them. The frog chorus does not stop at night; it only changes key.

## Hearing and the Bells

Before you go, spend some time listening to dense soundscapes — a rainforest recording, an aviary — for a couple of hours at a stretch, so your nervous system has some preparation for sound that never resolves. New visitors often report a kind of auditory vertigo in the first two days, a sense that they cannot *find* the sound, cannot locate any single element. This passes. The trick, once you're there, is to stop trying to parse it. Let it be weather.

Learn the bell-codes before you arrive if you can. Each household has its own bell-pitch, and the town operates on a bell-language that functions as their town-wide communication system. You will not master it, but you should at least know the common calls: meal-bell, weather-bell, gathering-bell, death-bell. Ask your host family to teach you on the first day. Misunderstanding a storm-warning bell could put you in danger during the Turning.

## Timing Your Visit

This is the most consequential choice.

**Midsummer through early autumn** is the balanced season — the town at its steady-state, the dripping-green quiet-loud equilibrium. This is when a first-time visitor should go. You will experience the place as it *is*, not as it *transforms*.

**The Turning** (three weeks, late spring) is spectacular but should not be attempted by a newcomer. The lower stories flood. Sleeping arrangements become complicated. The sound *triples*. Mudslides of wet moss can be genuinely dangerous if you are beneath a roof when one goes. The horsetails spearing through the floor will unnerve you in a way that affects sleep. If you must see the Turning, go for a second visit, with a host you trust, and accept that you will be in the way.

**Deep winter** is quiet and cold and the town retreats inward — you would be a burden on a household's winter stores and the inward-turned sociality would exclude you by necessity, not unkindness.

## What to Bring as a Gift

Metal. Any metal object — a good knife, a bronze bell, a bundle of iron nails, a pair of scissors, a needle-case of fine needles — is worth more than money. The town has almost no metal of its own; what they have came up the mountain on someone's back decades or centuries ago. A dozen good bone needles would be welcome; a dozen steel needles would be remembered. Do not bring weapons — nothing about the place requires them, and you will give the wrong impression.

Dry goods that travel well: salt, first of all (the damp eats their salt stores constantly). Hard cheeses. Dried fish. Strong liquor in a sealed clay bottle. Anything *dry* is precious here.

Do not bring books unless they are bound in oiled leather with waxed pages, and even then expect them to suffer. If you must bring writing materials, bring wax tablets; ink runs and paper rots.

## Money and Exchange

The town barely uses currency. You will be hosted, and you will be expected to contribute — either with labor (tending the moss-walls, watching the sluice, carrying yokes) or with gifts. Do not offer coin unless it is clearly decorative; they have no economy to absorb it. Work is the better currency. Ask what needs doing. Someone always needs a second pair of hands with the net-catching of roof-slides, with the trimming of horsetails, with the hauling of water from the cistern.

## Psychological Preparation

The town is *close*. Eighty dwellings packed shoulder-to-shoulder in a saddle of mountain, paths that corkscrew, eaves that kiss overhead, lanes that tunnel. If you are claustrophobic, know this in advance. The clouds passing through twice a day will, for perhaps twenty minutes each time, reduce visibility to arm's length, and this can be disorienting in a way that goes beyond mere fog — you are *inside a cloud*, in a town made of living matter, and the whole place seems to breathe around you.

Accept that your sense of where-the-day-is-going will be compromised. You will not see the sun reliably. Time will become a matter of bells and meals and the state of the light, which is not the same as the time of day. A small mechanical watch, wound tight and carried in an oiled pouch, will help you stay grounded. Do not rely on anything electronic; the humidity will kill it within days.

Expect a feeling, somewhere in the second or third day, of being *subsumed* — that you are not staying in a place but staying *inside* a place, inside its walls, its sounds, its moistness, its greenness. This is accurate. The town is a single organism as much as a settlement, and you are, briefly, part of its microbiome. Some visitors find this deeply peaceful. Others find it oppressive. You will not know which you are until you arrive.

## Practical Skills to Acquire Beforehand

- **Balance on narrow wet surfaces.** Practice on wet planks if you can. The duckboards are unforgiving of stumbles.
- **Sleeping with ambient noise.** Train yourself on a recording for a week before departure.
- **Lighting fires with damp tinder.** Your hosts will have fires, but there may be moments — on approach, perhaps, or if you step aside from the town for a day — when you need to. Dry tinder stored in wax-sealed containers is standard here; learn to use it.
- **Basic moss identification.** Not out of scholarship — for safety. Some of the liverworts around thresholds are mildly toxic if you touch your eyes after handling them. Your hosts will point these out. But knowing in advance that *moss is not generic* will orient you.

## The Frogs

Do not kill frogs, even by accident. It is not taboo exactly, but the children will notice, and their disapproval is social death for a visitor. Watch where you step. When one leaps onto your face in the night — this will happen — flick it off gently, do not swat. They are part of the town in a way that is difficult to articulate until you have lived among them for a few days. You will start to recognize individuals. Do not laugh at this when the children tell you the names of the frogs; they are not being childish.

## What to Expect from the People

They speak loudly and close to the ear because they have to. Do not mistake the loudness for aggression, and do not interpret the closeness as intimacy beyond what it is. They will stand closer to you than you are used to, and they will touch your arm while speaking. This is the sound environment training them, not personal feeling. Respond in kind — speak up, lean in — and you will be understood.

They are not demonstrative. Emotional events (a birth, a death, a quarrel, a reconciliation) are announced by bells and otherwise handled quietly. Do not expect grand expressions. A hand on your shoulder at a meal may be the warmest thing you receive, and it will mean a great deal.

They will ask you about where you are from, but not in the way you expect. They will want to know what the *weather* is like, what the *walls* are made of, what grows there. Political questions, historical questions, questions of identity — these do not interest them. They are asking about the shape of your life as a creature in a place. Answer accordingly.

## On Leaving

When you depart, you will be damp for days. Your clothes will smell of moss for weeks; some of them will have to be burned. Your hearing will be strange for a day or two — you will find ordinary rooms uncannily silent, and you may feel briefly that something is wrong, that the world has been *muted*.

Take a small patch of wall-moss with you if offered (it will be offered; it is traditional). Keep it in a wet clay pot. It will not thrive anywhere else in the multiverse — the spores and symbionts and specific mineral runoff of that mountain are not replicable — and within a season it will have faded to an ordinary moss, indistinguishable from any other. But for that season, you will have a piece of the town on your windowsill, and when you water it, you will smell the lanes again for a moment, and hear, faintly, under the ordinary sounds of your own life, the suggestion of a bell answering a bell across a saddle of cold mountain.

Go in summer. Bring needles. Listen for the weather-bell. Do not kill frogs.
