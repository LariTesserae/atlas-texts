# Preparing to Visit the Cistern Room

## What You Are Actually Going To

Before anything else, understand what kind of place this is. The Cistern Room is not a destination in the usual sense. Nothing is going to happen there. No one is going to meet you, no ritual will unfold, no secret will be revealed. It is a working piece of infrastructure that happens to be beautiful, the way a well-made wheel is beautiful, and your visit is essentially trespass into someone's utility closet — one they have built with such care and such devotion to its purpose that simply standing inside it will rearrange something in you.

Prepare accordingly. Prepare to be unimportant there.

## Clothing and Body

Wear something you do not mind getting wet from the knee down. The ledge is ankle-deep in overflow at all times, and you will not be able to avoid it — the ledge *is* the floor, and the floor is always a little flooded. Linen or light wool that dries fast. Not cotton, which will hold the wet and chill you when you come back up the stair into open air. Not synthetics, which will feel wrong in that room; the room predates them and resents them subtly, though it cannot say so.

Sandals that grip wet brick, or bare feet. Bare feet are better. The brick is smooth from handling and from the constant flow, and your feet will read the place more honestly than any shoe can. Trim your nails first — you will want to stand a long time without shifting, and long nails in wet sandals produce small pains that pull you out of the room.

Bring a towel for afterward, left at the top of the stair. You will need it.

Do not wear perfume, cologne, scented soap, scented anything. The room is very clean in a very particular way — the cleanliness of constant turnover, of a place that rejects what settles. Your smell will be the only new thing in it. Keep that small.

If you have long hair, tie it back. You will want to lean over the water at some point, and you will not want to disturb it.

## What to Bring

Very little.

- A small copper or bronze cup, if you have one, for drinking. The water is potable in most versions of this room; your intuition will tell you whether to drink in this one. If it does not tell you, do not drink.
- A candle and matches, sealed in wax or a tin. Not for the room itself, which is painfully bright, but for the stair on the way down and the stair on the way back up, which will be dark by contrast even at midday. Your eyes will have adjusted to the nine shafts, and the tunnel will seem much darker going out than it did coming in.
- A single clean cloth for drying your hands if you touch the water, which you will.
- Nothing to write with. Nothing to record with. No camera, no instrument. Not because the room forbids it — the room does not care — but because every traveler who has brought one has come back with images that do not convey it and has regretted the attempt. The moving mesh of light does not photograph. The roar does not record. You would be bringing home a corpse of the place.

Leave your pack at the top of the stair.

## Timing Your Visit

The room breathes on a daily cycle. Decide which breath you want to share.

**Dawn** is cold and quiet. The water is at its coldest — still warm to the touch, but only just — and the light comes in at a low angle that the oculi were not really designed for, so only two or three of the nine shafts reach the water, and the mesh on the walls is sparse and slow. The roar is the same, but the room feels larger and emptier. This is the melancholy hour.

**Late morning to early afternoon** is when the room does what it was built to do. All nine shafts come down straight; the mesh is at its most active; the water is pleasantly warm; everything is at working pitch. This is when most visitors should go. The room is loudest about itself at this hour.

**Late afternoon** is the warmest water and the most honeyed light, the shafts slanting again but from the other side, catching the dome tiles so that the terracotta glows from within. The light on the walls gets slower and thicker. A good hour for staying longer than you meant to.

**After sunset** is not worth it. The oculi give nothing, the room goes truly dark, and the roar without the light is frightening in a way that does not teach you anything. Do not stay past dusk.

Check the weather if you can. A day with passing clouds will give you the pulsing — the mesh dimming and snapping back — which is one of the room's best gifts. A day of steady sun gives you steadiness. Rain gives you the deeper roar and the rising water, which is worth experiencing once but is not the room at its most itself. Drought gives you the quiet, and the quiet is rare and valuable, but you cannot schedule for it.

## Mental Preparation

This is the part that matters most, and that travelers routinely neglect.

The Cistern Room will do something to your sense of purpose. You have come from a multiverse of urgency, of errands, of meanings that require your attention. You will descend a stair and find yourself in a place whose entire nature is that it has been doing its job, loudly and brightly, without reference to you or anyone, for as long as it has existed and for as long as it will. Its loudness is not for you. Its brightness is not for you. The ferns in the corners are not for you. Even the tools on the ledge — the dipper, the brush, the ladder — are for someone else's hands, and you should not touch them.

Travelers who arrive with a question to answer, or a grief to process, or an epiphany to harvest, tend to leave the room disappointed or obscurely angry. The room does not meet them. It is not that kind of room.

Travelers who arrive with nothing — who have spent the walk up the hill emptying themselves of the need for the room to be about them — tend to come back changed in ways they cannot articulate for some time. What the room offers is the experience of being near something that is complete without you. This is a rarer experience than it sounds.

Practice, in the days before you go, standing near working things and not interfering with them. A river. A kiln. A beehive. A sleeping animal. Notice how your mind tries to make the thing about you — tries to name it, photograph it, extract a lesson from it, imagine owning it. The Cistern Room is a concentrated form of this practice. You will do better there if you have warmed up.

## On the Descent

The stair is stone and older than you expect from the room's crisp newness — the stair was perhaps already there, and the cistern was fitted to its terminus. It is dim. There is a handrail cut into the wall on the right; use it.

As you descend, you will begin to hear the roar before you see the light. This is the correct order. Let the sound arrive first. Do not hurry toward the light; the light is not going anywhere.

At the final turn, pause. Take three breaths. Understand that when you step through the opening you will be somewhere very bright and very loud and very warm, and that your body will need a moment to accept all three at once. Many visitors have stumbled here from pure sensory overload, and the ledge is narrow, and the water is deep.

## Inside the Room

Stand on the ledge. Do not immediately try to see everything. Let your eyes adjust — it will take longer than you think, because the brightness is not uniform but moving, and your pupils will keep trying to settle on a stillness that does not exist.

Do not call out across the pool to test the acoustics. Everyone does this, and it is fine, but it is the act of someone who has not yet given the room its due. Wait until you have been in the room twenty minutes before you make any sound of your own.

You may put your hand in the water. You may sit on the ledge and put your feet in. You may not swim. The pool is twenty feet deep, the spouts push a slow circulation you cannot see, and the walls are smooth; a swimmer who tires has nothing to grip. Also, it is not your water.

You may look at the single water-plant reaching up from the floor. It is the room's one inhabitant of any ambition. It has been lifting and settling in that same circulation for years, and it will continue after you leave. A long look at it is, in my intuition, the closest the room comes to giving you something personal.

Do not linger past your natural stopping point, but also do not leave the moment you feel you have "seen" it. The room unfolds on a slower clock than sight. Give it an hour if you can. Give it two if the light is good.

## Leaving

Climb out slowly. Your legs may be stiffer than you expect from standing in cold overflow. The stair back up will feel very dark for the first thirty seconds; this is why you brought the candle.

At the top, dry your feet before you put shoes back on. Sit a moment before you walk away from the hill. The outside world will seem strangely quiet and strangely dim, and you will not want to speak to anyone for a while. Arrange your travel so that you do not have to.

Do not, on the same day, try to visit another location. The Cistern Room is a full day's experience even though you are only inside it for an hour or two. Trying to stack it with anything else wastes it.

## A Final Note

There is a chance, small but real, that the person who tends the room will arrive while you are there — to scrub the waterline, to check the overflow plug, to refill the dipper's peg. If this happens, step aside onto the narrow ledge, nod, and say nothing. They will not speak to you. They are not a guide or a guardian; they are the person whose job this is, and your presence is neither welcomed nor forbidden, simply irrelevant to the work. Watch how they move. They will move like someone who has been coming down these stairs for a long time and expects to come down them for a long time more. This is perhaps the deepest lesson the room has to offer, and it is offered only incidentally, as a side effect of the room's real business, which is holding water.

Go prepared to be a small warm animal standing briefly inside a larger working thing. That is what you are there. That is enough.
