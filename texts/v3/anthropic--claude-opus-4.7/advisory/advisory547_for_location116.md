# Preparing to Visit the Upper Foundries of Hesh

## Before You Go: The Mental Preparation

Start by accepting, weeks in advance, that you are going somewhere that will not quite match its own descriptions. Most travel guides work because the place stays still while you read about it. Hesh will not. Read about it anyway, but hold what you read lightly. The travelers who do worst in Hesh are the ones who arrive with a firm picture of what they expect and then spend their whole visit trying to reconcile it with what they find. The ones who do best arrive curious and a little slow.

Spend a few evenings practicing stillness. Sit somewhere quiet and just listen — not to anything in particular, but to the ambient hum of wherever you are. Hesh rewards this kind of attention. If your baseline is noise and hurry, you will miss most of what is actually happening there. The city's signal is low and patient and you need receivers tuned accordingly.

If you are the kind of traveler who likes to "do" places — to tick off sites, to photograph, to move — consider whether Hesh is really for you. It is not a bad place for such travelers, but they will find it frustrating, and the frustration will make them loud, and loud is the wrong frequency for Hesh. The city does not punish loudness so much as gently decline to reveal itself to it.

## The Body

**Altitude.** This is the most important physical preparation. The air is thin — genuinely thin, not just atmospherically "thin" in the literary sense. If you live at sea level, arrive at least four days early at some intermediate elevation. A week is better. You will be walking a lot, and much of the walking involves stepping up and over and across things, and you do not want to be gasping. Gasping is undignified in Hesh; people will look, politely, and then look away.

**Breath.** Even after acclimatization, you will find yourself breathing harder than you expect. Learn, before you go, to breathe through your nose. The air in Hesh carries particulates — fine metallic dust, condensation, the exhalations of the foundries — and your nose is a better filter than your mouth. Some visitors pack lightweight scarves of loose-woven silk or cotton, which work well and are considered by locals to be a reasonable, even respectful, choice.

**Feet.** Bring boots. Not hiking boots — those mark you as someone expecting wilderness. Bring something that splits the difference: sturdy, closed-toe, with soles thick enough to handle hot metal grating but flexible enough to let you feel what you are standing on. You will be walking on surfaces not designed for walking: zinc plates, riveted access hatches, the tops of pipes, the flanges of boilers. Rubber soles are essential; some of what you cross will be warm, and leather alone will not insulate. Break the boots in first. Blisters in Hesh are a slow misery because the city is large and the distances, as noted, are not always what they were.

**Hands.** Bring gloves — thin, leather-palmed, the kind a mechanic would wear. You will touch things. You cannot help it. The city is full of surfaces that invite a hand: warm copper, cool brass, smooth cast plate. Bare hands will get grimy with a very specific fine dark dust that does not wash out of cuffs easily and that sometimes, inexplicably, tastes of something you remember.

**Eyes.** The brightness is not sun-brightness; sunglasses designed for glare will do little. What you want is something with a slight amber or rose tint that softens the peculiar quality of the light without darkening it. The locals wear small pince-nez with faintly tinted glass; you can have a pair made before you go, or simply borrow this aesthetic with a modern pair. The light can induce a kind of low-grade trance if you stare into it for too long — this is one of the ways people lose afternoons in Hesh. Tinted lenses reduce the effect without eliminating it.

**Ears.** You will not need ear protection despite the industry. You may want, instead, very small cotton plugs for sleeping, because although the foundries are quiet, they are not silent, and the sub-audible humming that you feel in your sternum will, the first few nights, keep you up. After the third night you will not notice it. By the end of the trip you will miss it when you leave, and for some weeks afterward your own house will seem unaccountably hollow.

## Clothing

Layers, in natural fibers. Wool, linen, cotton. Synthetics sometimes behave oddly near the light-columns — they can pick up static, or develop faint iridescence along their seams, or, in rare cases, release a smell of their own manufacture, which is unpleasant and disorienting. Wool is the safe choice. A good wool shirt, a vest, a jacket you can remove when you pass near a warm boiler-wall and put back on when you step into the shadow of a cooling tower. Temperature in Hesh is highly local; you can cross from too-warm to too-cool in three paces.

Muted colors. Not out of fashion but out of practicality: the fine dark dust settles on everything, and white will not stay white past the first afternoon. Grays, browns, dull greens, dark blues. You will see locals in soft ochres and umbers and the occasional deep red. Avoid pure black — it shows the pale condensation-dust that settles on you near the flues, which is somehow worse than the dark dust. Avoid pure white for the opposite reason.

A hat with a small brim. Not for sun — for the occasional upward drip from a flue or gutter above you. Hesh is plumbed vertically as well as horizontally, and something is always condensing somewhere overhead.

A good bag. Canvas, with real buckles, not zippers. Zippers occasionally stick in the damp, and Hesh has a lot of small damps.

## What to Bring

**A notebook,** but use it sparingly. Writing things down in Hesh is a peculiar experience. Some things you write will read differently the next day. This is not the city playing tricks on you; it is your own memory recalibrating to a place that does not quite sit still. Write anyway. Do not reread obsessively.

**A small, simple watch** — mechanical if possible, with no digital anything. Time in Hesh is stable, mostly, but electronics sometimes behave oddly near the light-columns, and the last thing you want is to be checking a dead phone in a city where asking directions is its own adventure. Know that the watch will occasionally seem wrong. Trust it anyway; it is almost certainly right.

**A canteen.** Water is scarce, remember, and although the inns provide it, you cannot count on refilling between districts. A liter, minimum. Learn to drink in small sips.

**Cash in local denomination.** Hesh has a currency; exchange before you arrive, as no one will take your home currency and the exchanges within the city keep irregular hours and operate on principles that are not always explained. The coins are heavy and thin and have the weight of real metal. Carry them in a small pouch; they rattle in pockets and the sound attracts the delayed pigeons, which are harmless but startle easily.

**A gift,** small and useful. If you stay with a family or are shown any hospitality, it is customary to leave something — not valuable, just *considered*. A good bar of soap. A small jar of honey from your home region. A pencil. Nothing electronic, nothing perfumed heavily, nothing that trumpets its origin. The gift should be able to sit on a shelf and be useful over years.

**Salt,** a small tin. This is an old traveler's habit and you will find reasons for it.

## What Not to Bring

Cameras are permitted but mostly useless. Photographs of Hesh tend to come out correctly composed but subtly wrong — the light doesn't register, or it registers as something else, or the image will show a chimney you are sure was not there. This is not prohibition, just futility. Bring a small sketchbook instead. Sketching slows you down to the city's rhythm, and the results, while imperfect, are more honest.

Do not bring strong perfumes, colognes, or scented products. The air carries smell further than you expect and it lingers. A strongly-scented visitor becomes a weather event in whatever district they pass through. Unscented everything.

Do not bring firearms. There is no threat in Hesh that a firearm would address, and the locals find weapons baffling in the way one finds a fork baffling at a soup course — technically applicable, deeply beside the point.

Do not bring a dog. Your dog will not understand the clinkerhounds and the clinkerhounds will not understand your dog. There will be no aggression, just a mutual failure of category that will leave everyone uneasy. Cats travel better but you should not bring a cat either. The cats of Hesh are already negotiated into the city's systems in ways your cat cannot slot into.

## Etiquette

**Move slowly.** This bears repeating. Everything in Hesh moves at the pace of geology. A brisk walk marks you as foreign more surely than your clothes or accent. A brisk walk also, practically, is dangerous, because the footing is not always what it appears.

**Do not remark on the oddities.** If a street seems longer than it was yesterday, or if you catch a glimpse of a face inside a light-column, or if a shadow falls in the wrong direction, do not exclaim. Do not ask locals about it. Do not point. The residents consider remarking on these things unlucky, and beyond that, it marks you as someone who has not done the minimal reading. If you *must* discuss something strange, discuss it privately with a fellow traveler, quietly, and preferably indoors.

**Do not touch the stovehair.** The glassy black grass in the vents is not dangerous, but it is considered — not sacred exactly, but *set aside.* Leaving it alone is one of the city's small contracts with itself.

**Do not feed the animals.** The pigeons will eat from your hand, but they are already in an arrangement with the city and your bread complicates it. The clinkerhounds will not eat human food anyway and may cough at you in what seems like reproach.

**Do not try to bathe in the channels.** Water is prized. A traveler who dips their hands to wash causes no lasting harm but will be remembered, for years, as the one who did that.

**Greet people you pass on narrow catwalks.** A small nod is enough. The city is densely packed and on some passages you will have to turn sideways to let someone by. The nod is the acknowledgment. Failing to offer it is considered cold.

**Listen when someone speaks quietly.** Locals speak at low volume because sound carries. Leaning in to listen is normal and not considered intimate or strange. If you cannot hear and must ask them to repeat, do so, also quietly.

**Do not ask about the slow reduction.** The older workers will not tell you what it is. Younger workers will not tell you what it is. Innkeepers will not tell you what it is. You may come to some private understanding of what it is, over the course of your visit, but you should not speak this understanding aloud, to anyone, including yourself. Especially including yourself.

## Lodging and Food

Inns in Hesh are small, quiet, and built into the walls of larger structures — a few rooms above a foundry's administrative offices, say, or a guesthouse tucked beside a cooling tower that keeps it at a pleasant temperature year-round. Book ahead if you can; arrivals without reservations are accommodated but the accommodating takes time and negotiation. The beds are narrow and warm. The linens smell of the city, which is to say faintly of metal and something green you cannot identify.

Food is simple and good. Much of it involves the metal-tolerant vegetables — the tin-leaved rhubarb is common, stewed with a little sweetener, and is better than it sounds. There is a bread, slightly sweet, which the bakeries produce in low pale loaves. There is fish from the gutters, which also sounds worse than it is; the silver fish are clean and delicate and taste of nothing in particular, which is part of their charm. There is a soup, served in every inn, whose recipe is local and which you will not be told. Eat it. It is good.

Drink the water. It is safe. It tastes faintly mineral, faintly of the city itself.

Do not expect meat in quantity. The clinkerhounds are not food and there is no grazing land, obviously. There are some small birds kept for eggs, some fish, and occasionally something like a small game bird brought up from below the mountain. Travelers who require lots of meat should prepare themselves for reduced intake and consider it part of the experience.

## What to Do

**Walk.** This is the main activity. Pick a direction, move slowly, look up and down and sideways. Do not plan routes; you cannot, because of the distance problem. Instead pick a general intention — "I will move toward the high end of the city" — and let the route happen.

**Sit.** Every district has benches, usually copper, usually warm. Sit on them. Watch the light-columns. Watch the moths, if it is dusk. Let the sub-audible humming settle you. Two hours on a bench in Hesh is not two hours lost; it is, quite possibly, the entire purpose of having come.

**Visit a foundry floor,** if a local will take you. Most will allow visitors for short periods, provided you are quiet and do not interfere. What you will see is less dramatic than you expect and more meaningful than you can articulate. You will not understand what is being reduced. That is fine.

**Watch the moths at dusk.** At least once. Stay for the full cycle, from first emergence to full spiral. The patterns on their wings, seen up close through a borrowed glass, are one of the things you will think about for the rest of your life.

**Visit a garden.** The formal terraced gardens are open to respectful visitors. Sit near the bell-vines. When the wind is right, they ring. This is not a metaphor.

**Attend, if you can, the tram-bell.** There is a tram that runs along one of the middle terraces. Its bell, when it rings, carries across districts. Catching the tram is pleasant. Catching the sound of the bell from a distance, while sitting still somewhere, is better.

## Leaving

Plan to stay longer than you think. A week is a minimum. Two weeks is better. A month is when you start to understand things you will not be able to explain to people back home. Beyond a month, you risk not leaving, which is not dangerous exactly but is a decision you should make deliberately rather than arrive at by drift.

When you do leave, leave slowly. Descend the mountain at a walking pace if you can, by the low aqueduct road. A fast descent gives some travelers a kind of pressure headache that is not purely altitudinal. The city seems to prefer being left gradually, and you will feel better for honoring that.

For some weeks after your return, the world will seem a little too loud and a little too fast. This is normal. Your baseline has shifted. Do not fight it. Sit in quiet rooms. Listen to your own house. Look at the light in your kitchen and consider whether it is also, in its way, being made by something, somewhere, patiently.

And the lichen grows. And the bells ring when the wind is right. You carry a little of the hum with you now, in the sternum, where these things live. Take care of it. It is the part of Hesh that came home.
