# Preparing to Visit the Cold Loom

## What You Are Going To

Understand first: this is not a dangerous world in any ordinary sense. Nothing here wants to hurt you. Nothing here *wants* anything with the kind of edge that wanting has. You are going to a place that has completed itself. It will not notice you unless you give it something to work with, and even then it will notice you the way a river notices a leaf.

The danger is subtler. The Cold Loom metabolizes. If you stay too long, or go too deep, or lie down in the wrong pool, parts of you will be taken up into the slow turning and put to use. Not violently. Not even regrettably, from the inside. You just come back lighter than you left, and eventually you come back not-quite-you, and eventually you do not come back.

Prepare accordingly.

## Body

**Respiration.** The air is a suspension — half water, half vapor, never resolving. Standard rebreathers will drown inside an hour as the condensate overwhelms their scrubbers. You want a warmed-intake mask with an aggressive hydrophobic pre-filter and a reservoir that drains continuously. Budget for the mask to need stripping and redrying every six hours. Bring three. Practice swapping them one-handed in the cold before you go — your fingers will not be clever there.

**Thermal.** The cold is not severe but it is total and it is patient. A body loses heat to that wet air the way a coin loses shine to handling: slowly, evenly, without drama, until one morning the coin is dull. Layer wool against the skin (synthetics wet out and stay wet), a vapor-barrier mid-layer, and an outer shell that sheds condensate rather than resisting it — waxed canvas is better than modern membranes here, because the membranes will clog with the plateau's half-vapor and stop working on day two.

**Feet.** You will walk on the pelt. It gives underfoot like deep pile carpet laid over sponge. Ankle support is mandatory; the moss hides the old lozenge-stones and small hollows. Waterproofing is pointless — your feet will be wet the entire visit. Plan for it. Bring dry socks in sealed pouches, three pairs per planned day, and understand that "planned day" is a category the plateau does not recognize.

**Caloric load.** Your body will burn hard just staying warm in the wet cold. Eat twice what you think you need. Eat fats. Eat before you are hungry, because hunger there arrives already late, the way everything there arrives already late.

## Time

This is the hardest preparation and the one most travelers neglect.

You need **two** chronometers, mechanical, wound before entry, worn on opposite wrists. Electronics drift in the configuration-turbulence — not catastrophically, but enough that you cannot trust them. Two mechanicals let you catch the moment one of them begins to disagree with the other, which is the moment you need to leave.

More importantly: you need a **companion outside**. Someone in a stable frame, watching a stable clock, with a hard agreement that if you are not back in X hours they come in after you or pull you out by whatever extraction protocol your travel method supports. Do not trust your own sense of how long you have been there. You will lie down in the moss for what feels like ten minutes and it will be two days. The plateau does not do this to you maliciously. It does it because that is what the plateau is.

Set the extraction window shorter than you think you need. You will want to extend it from the inside. Do not build your protocol to allow this.

## Mind

Here is where you make your real choices.

**Bring something you are willing to lose.** Not a physical object — the plateau is indifferent to objects. Bring a *question* you have been carrying, or a grudge, or a name you are tired of knowing, or a grief that has outstayed its usefulness. The moss will take something from you. If you arrive with an offering ready, it will take the offering. If you arrive without one, it will take whatever is closest to the surface, and that may be something you wanted to keep.

Veteran travelers call this "loading the skim." It is not guaranteed to work. But the ones who forget to do it are the ones who come back having lost their mother's maiden name or their first language or the reason they became a traveler in the first place.

**Do not bring anything you cannot lose.** Your spouse's name. The melody your child invented. The specific texture of your best memory. These should be written down, before you leave, in a hand you know, and left with your outside companion. If you come back thinned, they will read them back to you. Some will re-seat. Some will not. This is known.

**Decide in advance what you are there for.** The plateau will accommodate any purpose or no purpose, but having one will anchor you. Naturalists study an engine. Mystics sit in a pool. Cartographers attempt, and fail beautifully, to map it. The ones who get into trouble are the ones who go "to see what happens," because what happens, when nothing is asking anything of you, is that you begin to agree with the plateau, and agreeing with the plateau is how you become part of it.

## Practical Kit

- **A staff**, wooden, taller than you. For probing the pelt ahead of you, for balance on the tilt, and for marking your path. Metal staffs hum oddly near the engines and become unpleasant to hold.
- **A length of bright cord**, at least a kilometer, in a pack dispenser. Tie it to something hard at your insertion point. The plateau's configuration-drift will route you back along the cord when your own sense of direction fails, which it will. The cord works. No one knows why the cord works when compasses do not.
- **A notebook, pencil, oiled-cloth wrap.** Write often. Write banal things — what you ate, what you saw, the time on both watches. When you review the notebook later you will find inconsistencies, and the inconsistencies are data about what the plateau did to you. Ink runs in the wet. Pencil survives.
- **One stone from home.** A pebble. Something dense and familiar. Carry it in your pocket. When the plateau's stones go strange in your hand, your pebble will stay your pebble, and this is an anchor worth more than any instrument.
- **No recording devices that matter to you.** Bring a camera if you want, but understand that the images may come back wrong, or the device may come back wrong, or you may come back unable to remember operating it. Treat all electronics as consumables.
- **Food that requires no preparation.** You will not want to stop and cook. Hard sausage, dense bread, chocolate, nuts. Nothing that needs heat. The cold flame of the green-burning engine is not for you and should not be approached.

## Conduct

**Do not approach the engines closer than thirty meters on your first day.** They will not object if you do. But the braid-within-braid-within-braid of their structure is a visual trap that some minds fall into and do not climb out of unassisted. Acclimate at distance. Learn what their sound does to your sternum before you learn what their surfaces do to your eyes.

**Do not eat anything from the plateau.** Not the grazers, not the heron-things, not the possibly-fish, not the pelt itself. None of it will poison you in a toxicological sense. All of it will enroll you in the metabolism.

**Do not drink the pool water unfiltered.** Filter heavily, then boil, then drink reluctantly. Better: bring your water. The faint bioluminescence in the deep pools is beautiful and you should look at it and you should not put it inside you.

**If a flock passes before a flue and you see the characters, do not try to read them.** You will think you almost can. You cannot. The almost is the hook. Look away. Write in your notebook: *flock, flue, characters, looked away.* Move on.

**If you find yourself thinking the plateau is beautiful, fine.** If you find yourself thinking the plateau is *right* — that this is what worlds should come to, that your own world is noisy and unfinished and ought to settle — that is the skim beginning. Check your watches. Start walking the cord back. You can return tomorrow. The plateau will be there. The plateau is always there.

**If you lie down, set an alarm on both watches, loud, and place one watch on your chest.** Do not lie down in a pool. Do not lie down near an engine. Do not lie down without telling your outside companion, on whatever comms you have, that you are lying down and for how long. The moss will not harm you. It will only use you. Brief use is fine. Many visitors lie down once, deliberately, and report it as the most restful sleep of their lives. Extended use is how people become cogs.

## Afterward

Plan a decompression. Do not travel onward for at least a local week. Sit in a loud, warm, crowded place — a market, a bar, a train station. Let the noise re-seat you. Talk to strangers about small things. Eat hot food. Take hot baths. Re-read what you wrote in the notebook and compare against the record your companion kept.

Expect to find something missing. Most travelers do. Most travelers also find that the missing thing was something they can live without, and some find that the missing thing was something they are glad to be rid of, and a few find that the plateau returned them a question they did not know they had been looking for, turning slowly in a cog somewhere, waiting for them to come and collect it.

This last is rare. Do not plan for it. Plan for the loss, and hope for the return, and in either case: go slowly, bring the cord, and come back while there is still enough of you to come back with.
