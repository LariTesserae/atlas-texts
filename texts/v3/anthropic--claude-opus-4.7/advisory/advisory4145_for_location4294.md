# Preparing to Visit the Cold Hollow

## Understanding What You Are Entering

Before anything practical, you need to sit with what this place actually is. The Cold Hollow is not a destination in the tourist sense. It is a working organ of something enormous and patient, and your visit will be, at best, the kind of trespass a mayfly commits by landing on a cathedral bell. Nothing about the hollow is designed for you. Nothing about it is hostile to you either. You will simply be irrelevant to it, and that irrelevance is itself one of the things you are going there to experience.

This matters for preparation because most of what you'd instinctively bring — instruments, plans, intentions — will feel absurd once you are inside. Prepare anyway. The absurdity is part of the lesson.

## Physical Preparation

**Cold-water diving certification, real and current.** The water inside the hollow is just above freezing, and the water outside, at fifty to sixty meters' depth, will be colder still in the thermocline and punishing on the descent. You need genuine technical diving experience, not a resort course. If you are not already a competent cold-water diver, spend the year or two it takes to become one before you go. The hollow will still be there. It has been there longer than you can meaningfully conceive.

**A drysuit, properly fitted**, with thick undergarments rated for prolonged near-freezing immersion. Wet suits will not do. You are going to want to stay, and staying is the whole point, so you need to be able to stay without your core temperature becoming a countdown.

**Rebreather, not open-circuit.** Two reasons. First, at depth, open-circuit bottom time is brutally short and you will need hours. Second — and more importantly — the hollow is very quiet, and it is full of a sound you have come to hear. Your own exhaust bubbles will thunder in that space. A rebreather lets you be silent inside something that is not silent.

**Redundant everything.** Lights, cutting tools, gas supply, buoyancy. Standard technical diving discipline. The hollow excludes animal life but you still have to get down to it and back up, through a column of ordinary ocean that does not care about you.

**Trimix, planned conservatively.** At sixty meters you want helium in your breathing gas. Narcosis inside the hollow would blur the one experience you came for.

## Acclimating Your Senses

The hum is the part most people fail to prepare for. It is loud enough to vibrate the bones of your inner ear, and it contains information your body will try to interpret as meaning even though you cannot parse it. Some visitors describe nausea, vertigo, or a sensation of being slowly read.

**Spend time, before you go, in places that hum.** Large transformer yards. The engine rooms of ships. Pipe organs during practice. Learn how your body responds to sustained sub-audible frequencies so that when a much larger and stranger sound takes hold of you, you have a baseline.

**Practice stillness underwater.** In a pool, in quarries, wherever you can. Learn to hover neutrally without fidgeting, without finning, without any input at all, for thirty minutes at a stretch. The hollow will want you still. Your body must know how.

**Fast lightly before the dive.** Not for buoyancy — for perception. A full stomach anchors you to your mammal concerns. You want to arrive slightly emptied.

## What to Bring Inside

Almost nothing.

Resist the urge to bring instruments. A hydrophone recording of the hum will tell you less than being present to it told the plant, which has been humming for longer than our species has made sound. If you must record, do so on the descent and ascent, not inside. Inside, be a witness, not a technician.

**Bring one small, dense object you care about** — a ring, a stone, a tooth. Not to leave behind; you must leave nothing behind. But to hold during the experience, as an anchor to your own continuity. People who float too long in the hollow without a tether to their own lives sometimes find the re-entry difficult. A small object with a history pulls you back along a thread.

**Do not bring offerings.** The plant does not want them and does not exclude animal life by accident. Introducing organic material would be a violation on the order of tracking mud across an altar that is also a computer that is also possibly alive in a sense we have no word for.

**Do not bring cameras you intend to use inside.** Light discipline matters. The plant is managing the light in that space with extraordinary precision, and your strobe is a graffito across a fresco. If you want images, take them from the threshold, pointing out, not in.

## Mental and Ethical Preparation

**Accept that you will not understand what you see.** The calcified hexagonal lattices with their dark central nodes, the slow chord changes in the hum, the standing ripples on the plate-leaf — these are functions in a process whose purpose is opaque to you and possibly opaque in principle to anything with a lifespan under a geological epoch. You are not going to crack it. Let that go before you descend, or you will spend your time inside frustrated rather than present.

**Prepare for the pull to stay.** This is the real hazard of the Cold Hollow, more than cold or depth or gas. The passage describes it precisely: a person can float there and stop noticing they have stopped moving. Set a hard turnaround time on a dive computer with an audible alarm you cannot ignore. Tell your surface support to expect you at a specific minute and to initiate recovery if you are late. Do not rely on your own judgment once you are inside. Your judgment is one of the things the hollow gently sets aside.

**Think about what you want to carry back.** Not souvenirs — you are bringing none — but what kind of changed person you want to be afterward. Travelers who go in wanting awe generally get awe. Travelers who go in wanting answers generally come back frustrated. Travelers who go in wanting to be briefly and truly peripheral to something vast and working — those come back different in a way that lasts.

**Make peace with your scale.** Before the dive, spend a night somewhere you can see a lot of stars, or a morning somewhere very old — a glacier, a redwood grove, an exposed Precambrian outcrop. The hollow will do this to you anyway, but arriving already partway there means you spend less of your limited bottom time adjusting.

## The Descent

Go down slowly even beyond what your decompression profile requires. The light changes as you drop, and somewhere around forty meters you will begin to see the hollow's outer surface: a dome of kelp holding a shape that kelp should not be able to hold, the currents combing over it without disturbing it. Circle it once before approaching the threshold. Let yourself register that this is a thing, a single thing, and you are about to enter it.

The threshold is the chemical gradient — that astringent cleanness on the tongue. You will pass through it and the water on the other side will be still in a way that ocean water is never still. Your bubbles, if you are on open circuit, will rise with a wrongness. The hum will begin to be felt before it is heard.

## Inside

Find a position near the center, not on the plate-leaf (do not touch it) but suspended in the water above it, and stop. Fins neutral. Hands at your sides. Breathe as shallowly as your gas planning allows.

The light will move across the floor more slowly than light should. The ripples will renew themselves on the plate-leaf. The hum will find the resonant cavities of your skull and settle into them. One frond, perhaps, will rotate a degree during your visit; you will not see it move, but you may notice afterward that something is different.

You will feel included. This is the word the passage uses and it is the correct one. Not welcomed, not observed, not tolerated. Included, briefly, in a process that does not require you and will not miss you.

When your alarm sounds, leave. Do not negotiate with yourself. Ascend on your planned profile. Do your deco stops. Surface into the world where things happen quickly and mean things and end.

## After

Do not try to explain the Cold Hollow to people who ask about your trip, at least not for a while. The words available to you will flatten it. Wait until you find the right person, or the right evening, or until you realize you do not need to explain it at all, that it has simply become one of the things you know about the shape of existence — that somewhere under a green-black sea, a plant is thinking in chord changes across geological time, and that for one afternoon, it let you hang in the middle of its thought.
