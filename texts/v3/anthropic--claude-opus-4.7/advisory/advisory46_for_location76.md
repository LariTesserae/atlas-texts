# Preparing to Visit the Rust-Marsh

## Before You Go: The Mental Work

Start months ahead. This is not a place you visit on a weekend whim, because the Rust-Marsh does something to the rhythm of your body and your expectations, and if you go in unprepared those alterations will follow you home and not leave for a long time.

The first thing to do is sit with the fact that **nobody made it**. Our minds are built to see a hammer and infer a hand. The Rust-Marsh is going to present you, continuously, with the signature of intention — worked metal, fitted parts, rhythmic labor — and withhold the intender. This produces a specific kind of vertigo that travelers who didn't prepare for it describe as worse than the causality slippage or the cold. Practice. Look at termite mounds, at the hexagons of basalt columns, at the insides of geodes. Train yourself to accept *made-looking* without *maker*. You will not succeed entirely. That is fine. Just thin the reflex.

The second mental preparation is around **causality**. Spend time in places where cause and effect are already a little loose — deep fog, sensory deprivation tanks, unfamiliar cities at three in the morning. The Rust-Marsh's sonic echoes, where a splash precedes its step, will make you flinch unless you've practiced not flinching at things that don't warrant it yet. Some travelers do drills: have a partner clap at random intervals; you are forbidden to react. It sounds silly. It helps.

Third, and this is the one people skip: **arrange your return**. Have someone who loves you waiting on the other side with warm food and a loud, chaotic environment — children, a busy kitchen, a market. You will need the counter-medicine. Travelers who come back to empty apartments tend to sit very still for a long time, listening for pistons that aren't there.

## Physical Preparation

**The cold is the first practical problem.** Not Arctic cold — the temperature probably hovers just above freezing — but it is *wet* cold, and it is everywhere, from the drip-sky down to the numbing water, and there is no sun to ever warm anything. You want:

- A drysuit, not a wetsuit. The water will go up to your knees in places and you cannot afford to have it against your skin. Test it for at least six hours of immersion before the trip.
- Wool under the drysuit, multiple layers, because the drysuit will fail somewhere — a seam, a seal — and you need to stay warm when it does.
- Neoprene gloves rated for under-five-degree water, and a spare pair sealed in a dry bag. Your hands are the part of you most likely to fail, and failed hands mean failed everything else.
- Sealed boots with aggressive tread. The smoothed metal under the water is slick as wet glass, and a fall in this place, with the machinery nearby, could be the last thing.
- A hood that leaves your ears partly clear. You need to hear the place. Muffling it is both disrespectful and dangerous — the large animals announce themselves, if you listen.

**Bring nothing electronic that matters.** The vibration will find the resonant frequency of delicate components and shake them to death within an hour. Mechanical watches work, as do film cameras with sturdy bodies. Digital equipment may function, may not, may function intermittently in ways that are worse than not functioning. If you want records, paper and graphite. Graphite in particular because ink will freeze or run in the drip.

**Ear protection is a harder question than it seems.** The sound is loud enough to cause hearing damage over long exposure, but blocking it entirely severs you from the place's best warning system. Compromise: filtered plugs that attenuate without muting, the kind musicians use. Even then, limit your exposure. Four hours in, four hours out, is the maximum most experienced travelers will do in one go.

**Food that needs no preparation.** You cannot start a fire. Nothing here will help you cook. The boilers are cold despite the steam; this is a lesson about trusting appearances. Bring dense calories — nuts, hard cheeses, cured meats, chocolate — and eat more than you think you need, because the cold will burn it off faster than you expect and you will not notice you are hypothermic until you are.

**Water is tricky.** The standing water is drinkable in the technical sense — no pathogens, no algal toxins, the place is too sterile for that — but it tastes strongly of copper and lightning and seems to carry some of the causality-looseness with it. Travelers who drink it report hearing their own voices a moment before they speak for hours afterward. Bring your own water. A lot of it.

## What to Carry

- A small iron object of sentimental value. This is traveler's superstition, not verified practice, but the tradition holds that carrying worked iron *that someone you loved made or gave you* helps with the no-maker feeling. It gives your hands something to close on when the vertigo hits. A horseshoe nail from a grandfather. A key. A ring.
- A written list of the names of people who know you. Read it when you get disoriented. The Rust-Marsh will try to sand you down into its rhythm; the names are grit against the sanding.
- A length of bright cord, orange or red, tied to your wrist and trailing behind you about ten meters. The pearl-grey light flattens depth perception and the machinery grows out of the water at unpredictable angles. The cord lets you retrace, and also lets a partner find you if you go down.
- A companion, ideally. The Rust-Marsh is not a solo trip for first-timers. You need someone to confirm what you heard, and to notice when you have stopped moving.

## Behavior on Arrival

**Do not approach the larger animals.** The horse-sized thing with mismatched legs is not hostile, but its attention is heavier than you can bear for long. Travelers who have locked eyes with it for more than a minute or so describe a feeling of being *inventoried* — of having every action of their life silently counted — and this is not something you recover from quickly. If one turns toward you, bow slightly, lower your eyes, and move laterally out of its line of sight. This seems to satisfy whatever the protocol is.

**Do let the six-legged things graze you.** Stand still for ten minutes after arrival, let the pearl light collect on you, and let them come. This is the handshake. After it, the place will tolerate you much better — the larger animals will treat you as ambient rather than anomalous, and the flatfish will not startle when you wade past. It is worth the cold.

**Do not touch the machinery directly.** Not because it will harm you — it won't, it has no interest in you — but because your warmth disturbs the equilibrium in very small ways, and you do not want to be the traveler who accidentally altered a piston rod's diameter by the width of a bacterium and tilted the hundred-million-year balance. Respect the stasis.

**Keep moving, gently.** Standing still for too long will start to entrain your heartbeat to the four-count bass. This is the effect that lingers longest after you leave. The longer you let it establish, the longer it takes to fade. Walk at irregular intervals. Hum something syncopated. Break your own rhythm deliberately.

**When sounds arrive before their causes, note them but do not investigate.** Chasing a splash you hear toward the sound you haven't yet heard the splash *of* will get you lost in a way the cord cannot fix, because you are now navigating by something that has not happened. Stay with what has.

## The Departure

Leave before you want to. The Rust-Marsh gets more compelling the longer you stay, and this is not because it is becoming friendly — it is because you are beginning to belong to it, which is a process that, completed, does not return you.

Plan the departure for a specific time and hold to it even if nothing seems wrong. Especially if nothing seems wrong.

Have your partner's hand on your arm as you step out. Let them pull. A small physical tug, from a person whose name is on your list, is the single most effective thing for breaking the rhythm's hold.

## Afterward

For a week or two, avoid quiet rooms. Avoid standing water. Avoid the smell of wet copper. If you hear pistons that aren't there, which you will, do not follow the sound, do not investigate whether it is happening again. It isn't. It's the Marsh releasing its grip.

Eat warm food. Stand in sunlight. Let people touch you — shoulders, hands. The cold of the place is partly metaphysical and it needs to be displaced by warm live contact.

And at some point, sit down and try to write about it. Not for publication. For yourself. The Rust-Marsh resists description and the resistance is part of what it does to you; pushing back against that resistance, sentence by sentence, is how you reclaim the parts of yourself that got left in the shallows with the tin flatfish and the grazing fur-and-steel herds.

You will never fully describe it. That is correct. Write anyway.

Some travelers go back. Most don't. Both are reasonable responses. If you do go back, go for shorter durations, not longer ones — the place rewards familiarity with deeper entrainment, and the second visit is where most of the people who didn't come home stopped coming home.

Go carefully. Come back warm.
