# Preparing to Visit the Undergarden

## What This Place Actually Is

Before preparing, understand what you're walking into. The Undergarden is not wilderness. It is not ruins. It is someone's garden — a functioning, tended, inhabited ecological artifact that has existed in balance for what is probably centuries. You are, in the strictest sense, a stranger entering a private greenhouse whose owner happens to be out. Prepare accordingly. Most of your preparation should be oriented not around survival (the place is gentle) but around *not damaging anything* and *not being rude*.

## Physical Preparation

**Clothing.** Wear something you can move quietly in, with soft soles — the paths are narrow and the loam takes footprints. Avoid anything that sheds (no fleece, no fraying linen). You do not want to leave fibers in this ecosystem. Long sleeves and long trousers, but breathable — the chamber is warm. Bring a scarf or light hood you can pull over your hair; the gold dust will settle on you, and while it seems benign to the local flora, you don't want to carry it out on your scalp into another world where it might not be benign.

**No perfumes, no deodorants, no scented anything.** The bees feed on the dust rather than flowers, which suggests a sensory ecology we don't understand. Strong smells are an intrusion. Wash with unscented soap the day before. Don't smoke in the preceding hours.

**Respiratory consideration.** The gold dust is a fine airborne particulate of unknown composition that plants absorb and thicken on. It is almost certainly not meant for human lungs. Bring a light cloth mask or a folded silk handkerchief you can breathe through if you plan to stay more than an hour. Don't use anything with an exhaust valve that would blow air sharply at the vegetation.

**Gloves, but thin ones.** Cotton or thin leather. You will want to touch things — the warm brick, the glow-plates, the waxy fronds — and your bare skin carries oils, salts, and microbiota. Gloves also protect you from anything the succulents' sap might do to human skin; that thick clear honey-sap is doing chemistry we don't have names for.

**Water and food — your own.** Do not, under any circumstance, drink from the succulent rosettes or eat anything in the chamber. The succulents hold the water of this ecosystem. Every drop you take is a drop taken from the rabbit-things, the geckos, the beetles. Pack enough water for your entire visit plus return. Eat beforehand or pack sealed food you can consume outside the chamber.

**A sealable bag for waste.** Everything you bring in, carry out. Everything. Hair you shed, a sneezed-into tissue, a dropped thread. This is not a leave-no-trace wilderness ethic; it is a leave-no-trace *guest* ethic. You are in someone's living room.

## Movement and Behavior

**Move slowly.** Not reverently-slowly in a way that feels performative, but at the pace the place itself moves. The loudest sound is a pebble clicking against a pebble. If your footfalls are louder than that, you are too fast. Practice this before you go — walk around your home trying to be quieter than your own breathing.

**Follow the paths exactly.** The fauna maintain them. They are the circulatory system of the chamber. Stepping off the path crushes rosettes, breaks fungal networks, compacts loam. Stay on the path even if it means you can't reach something you want to see.

**Do not touch the animals.** The gecko-thing opening an eye at you is a social act; it has determined you are not a threat. Do not prove it wrong. The rabbit-things, the click-tailed swallow-equivalents, the bees — observe, don't interact. Your hand is a monstrous thing to them.

**You may touch the masonry.** The brick, the glow-plates, the vault. These are human artifacts and tolerate human touch. Feel the thumbprints in the bricks. That is what they are there for — a greeting from the makers, across time.

**Do not touch the tools.** The rake, the bench, the cloth, the knife. These belong to the tender. The oiled knife is a promise: *I am coming back.* Leaving those objects alone is how you honor that promise. Don't sit on the bench. Don't test the rake.

## What to Bring for the Tender

This is important, and most travelers miss it.

Someone tends this place. They are out. You are in their space. The traveler's etiquette, across most cultures in most worlds, is that you leave a sign of passage — something small, something that says *a person was here, and meant no harm.*

Bring a small, neutral offering: a smooth stone from your home world, a single dried seed of a common grain, a pressed leaf, a coin with no sharp edges. Leave it on the bench, near the folded cloth but not on it. Not as payment — you have nothing to pay for — but as acknowledgment. A traveler's calling card. The tender will understand. Tenders always understand.

Do not leave anything living, anything perishable, anything that will rot or sprout. Do not leave writing unless you can be very sure the tender would welcome it; a written message is a demand for response, and you will not be there to receive one.

## Mental and Temporal Preparation

**Pre-adjust your sense of time.** The Undergarden operates on centuries. You will feel, within minutes of entering, a pressure to *do something,* to justify your presence with activity. Resist this. The appropriate action in the Undergarden is to be present, at the place's own pace, and then to leave. If you can sit still for an hour in your own home without fidgeting, you are ready. If you can't, practice.

**Prepare to not understand things.** The light is from stone. The plants don't drink. The dust feeds them. Nothing here will make sense in the frameworks you arrived with, and that is fine. You are not here to understand. You are here to witness. Resist the urge to collect samples, take measurements, or photograph systematically. A traveler who treats a place as a puzzle to solve misses what the place is.

**Prepare for the second door.** There is a second door at the far end, smaller, darker-framed. You will want to go through it. Decide *before you enter the Undergarden* whether you will open it. My recommendation: do not, on the first visit. You have not yet earned the first chamber. The second door is for later, if there is a later.

**Prepare for the possibility of the tender returning.** It is unlikely but possible that during your visit, the tender will come through one of the two doors. If this happens: stand still. Keep your hands visible. Do not speak first. Let them take in the fact of your presence. Their response will tell you everything about what to do next. If they gesture you out, go out, without hurry but without dawdling. If they ignore you, remain quiet and observant. If they speak, listen far more than you reply.

## What to Leave Behind (Before You Enter)

- Any weapon. The obsidian knife on the bench is the only blade that belongs in this room.
- Any electronic device that hums, buzzes, or emits light. The bees' hum is the loudest sound. Yours should be quieter.
- Any expectations of grandeur. This is a small room. Its value is not in its scale.
- Any plans to return with others. If you feel the Undergarden deserves to be shown, to be shared, to be documented for the benefit of those who haven't seen it — this is the precise instinct that destroys places like this. If you must tell someone, tell them in words only, and let them find their own way or not.

## On Leaving

Leave by the door you entered. Close it behind you to the same angle of ajar-ness you found it. Do not look back from the stairway for a last glimpse; you have had your glimpses, and a last-look is a greedy thing.

Ascend the worn chute slowly. By the time you reach the upper world — the one no one down there mentions — you should be breathing at the pace of the Undergarden still. Let that pace fade on its own, over the following hours. Don't rush back into your ordinary speed. Something of the chamber will travel with you for a day or two, a quietness behind your eyes. This is the place's gift. Receive it gracefully.

## A Final Note

You will be tempted, once you have been there, to believe the Undergarden is *yours* in some small way — that you have a claim on it, a relationship with it, a right of return. You don't. You were a visitor in a room that has been held in balance for longer than your civilization, by a hand you never saw, for reasons you weren't told. The correct posture, forever after, is gratitude without possessiveness.

Go well. Walk softly. Leave the knife oiled.
