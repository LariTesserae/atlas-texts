# Preparing to Visit the Hollow Verge

## First: Understand What You Are Agreeing To

Before anything practical, sit with this: you are going to a place that was *made*, and is being *maintained*, by an intelligence or process you will not meet, will not perceive directly, and almost certainly will not comprehend. The valley is not hostile. It is not indifferent either. It is *arranged*, and you will be inside the arrangement whether or not you consent to be part of it.

Most multiverse travelers prepare for danger, weather, language, currency. For the Hollow Verge you must prepare for something stranger: the possibility that your presence will be noticed by the substrate itself, and that you will never know in what register the noticing occurred. Decide, before you go, that you are comfortable being perceived without being able to perceive back. If that idea makes you restless, choose another world.

## Physical Preparation

**Your body will mostly be fine.** The air is breathable — the particulate in the haze is fine enough that ordinary lungs tolerate it, though expect a mild sweetness at the back of the throat and a faint chalky film on the tongue by the end of each day. Bring a soft cloth; rinse your mouth with clean water in the mornings. Do not try to filter the air. The particulate is almost certainly part of the coordinating field, and removing it from your immediate vicinity may cause the field to *reach* for you in ways you do not want.

**Dress in loose, pale, unpatterned fabric.** This is not a cultural concession — though it is also that. The inhabitants wear this for a reason, and I suspect the reason is that strong colors and hard geometries *register* differently in the field, and draw attention you have not earned. Linen, undyed cotton, raw silk. No logos. No reflective surfaces. No metal you can avoid. Leave your jewelry at the waystation.

**Footwear should be soft-soled and quiet.** The ground between the clusters is firm and walkable, but you will sometimes cross what appears to be bare rock that rings faintly underfoot. Boots with hard heels will produce a sound that carries further than physics allows. Wear something that makes you closer to barefoot than not.

**Bring nothing electronic that you cannot turn fully off.** Not low-power. *Off.* Batteries out. Whatever field permeates the valley interacts with active electronics in ways that are sometimes benign and sometimes — according to the accounts I trust — produce the sensation of having a second heartbeat, located somewhere outside the body, for days afterward. If you must record, use paper and graphite.

**Hydrate more than you think necessary.** The moisture in the air is deceptive; it beads on you but does not enter you. You will dehydrate while feeling damp. This is one of the most common mistakes travelers make.

## Sensory and Psychological Preparation

**Practice tolerance for continuous sound before you go.** The valley is, as described, deafening across every register, but the worst thing a newcomer can do is try to block it out. Earplugs will not help; the chest-tone comes from inside you. Instead, spend the weeks before your visit sitting in places with layered ambient sound — a forest, a market, a machine room — and practice letting the sound be the background rather than the subject of attention. If you arrive unpracticed, the first day will be overwhelming, and overwhelm here has a tendency to fix itself in memory strangely. People who panic on day one often find that years later, in ordinary life, they can still hear the ringing.

**Learn to count slow rhythms.** The tidal pulse of the river is the master clock of the whole valley. If you can feel it — if you can learn, within a day or two, to know when the surge is coming without looking at the water — you will find the place enormously easier to move through. Everything is conducted against this pulse. Your walking pace, your breathing, your choice of when to stop and when to continue, should all eventually sync to it. Not because the valley demands this, but because being unsynced is exhausting in a way that will not announce itself until you are depleted.

**Prepare for the dusk hold.** Those three minutes when everything stops are the single most psychologically demanding experience the valley offers. The silence is not like any earthly silence. The hollowness becomes geometric, dimensional, pressing. Some travelers weep. Some laugh. Some feel a terrible conviction that they are about to be seen, truly seen, for the first and last time, and that they must *not be found where they are standing.* Whatever you feel, **do not move** during the hold. Stand or sit where the first silence finds you. Close your eyes if it helps. The ordinary rhythms will return, and you will be unchanged, or changed only in the ways one is changed by having stood briefly inside something much larger than oneself.

**Decide in advance how you will respond to the long low tone** that seems to come from inside your chest. It is not coming from inside your chest. But you will not be able to convince yourself of this while it is happening. The best approach, I am told, is to simply acknowledge it — "yes, I hear you" — silently, and let it pass. Arguing with it (trying to locate it, trying to describe it, trying to match it with your own voice) is not dangerous but is deeply wearying.

## Social Preparation

**The inhabitants will not greet you. Do not greet them.** The twenty-meter distance at which they stand when they meet is not ceremony; it appears to be the distance at which the humming of the nearest building can *mediate* their encounter. If you approach closer than that, you are asking the buildings to mediate *you*, and I do not know what that would mean, and neither, I suspect, do they.

**Do not ask them questions about the valley.** They will not be able to answer, and the attempt to answer may cause them distress that persists after you leave. If you must speak to someone, speak of small concrete things — a leaf, a stone, the color of the river at the current pulse — and speak briefly.

**Do not offer gifts or trade.** They carry nothing for a reason.

**Watch the buildings, not the people.** The change of key in the hum when two inhabitants meet is the actual social exchange. The humans are almost incidental to it. If you want to understand anything about the culture of the Verge, listen to the architecture's response to proximity.

## What Not to Do

**Do not touch the sharp rock faces with intention.** Brushing against them is fine. Pressing your palm to one to feel the edge is fine — once. But do not try to break a piece off, do not try to carve, do not try to mark. The cliffs are remade on a schedule, and there is reason to believe that deliberate alteration is noticed and corrected, and that the correction sometimes extends to the alterer.

**Do not cross the edge of the rain into the dry ground, or the dry ground into the rain, more than necessary.** The edges are real, and the fact that they are maintained suggests they matter. One crossing is a curiosity. Repeated crossings are a pattern, and patterns here get incorporated.

**Do not sleep in the hollow spaces between the trees.** Sleep near a building, or near the cluster of ferns at the river's edge, where the inhabitants sleep. The hollow is, as the description suggests, *room left for something*. It is almost certainly empty the vast majority of the time. But "almost certainly" is not a margin I would sleep in.

**Do not try to photograph the shimmer.** If you bring a camera (against earlier advice), and if you point it at the haze when the lattices briefly resolve, you may get an image. The image will be of something. The something will not be what you saw. Travelers who study these images afterwards report that they continue to change, slowly, in storage.

**Do not stay past the seventh dusk.** This is not a rule anyone has given me; it is a pattern I have observed in accounts. Travelers who stay six nights or fewer return describing a beautiful, strange place. Travelers who stay longer return describing themselves differently, in ways their loved ones notice before they do. Whatever the valley is doing, it operates on a timescale that begins to include you somewhere around the seventh day.

## What to Bring

- Loose pale clothing, more than you think (the humidity means you will want to change)
- Soft-soled quiet shoes
- A cloth for your mouth and hands
- Drinking water from outside the valley (the river water is probably safe but I would not drink it; it pulses to a rhythm and I do not want that rhythm inside me)
- Plain food that does not require preparation (no fire in the valley — not forbidden, just wrong)
- Paper and a graphite pencil
- A small, simple object from your home world to hold during the dusk silence. Something worn, something loved, something that reminds you which scale of existence you belong to.

## What to Leave Behind

- All electronics
- All metal you can spare
- All expectation of understanding
- Any belief that you are the protagonist of your visit

## A Last Note

The place is beautiful. The description you have been given does not fully convey how beautiful — the way the filtered light catches the beading moisture on a parasol-fern when the filaments are just beginning to uncoil; the way the chords of the buildings fold into the sky's ringing when you stand at a certain point near the river; the way, in the moment just before the dusk hold, every living thing in the valley seems to take a single shared breath.

You will not understand what you are inside of. That is all right. Neither do the inhabitants, probably. Neither, perhaps, does whatever comes through at intervals. The Hollow Verge may be a place where *understanding* is not the right relationship to the environment — where the right relationship is something more like *attending*, carefully, at the correct distance, for the correct duration, and then leaving while the leaving is still yours to choose.

Go gently. Listen more than you look. Come home on time.
