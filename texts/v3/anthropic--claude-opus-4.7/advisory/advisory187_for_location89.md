# Preparing to Visit Oss-in-the-Cleft

## What You Are Walking Into

Before anything practical, understand what kind of place this is. Oss is not merely static — it is *held*. Something has arrested it. The rawness of the granite argues that the valley itself is young, cut recently and quickly, but within that young wound a pocket of time has been stopped or slowed to a near-halt. The fruit that ripens and does not fall, the moon fixed at its waning three-quarters, the thrush with its unchanging two notes — these are not charming features. They are the symptoms of a condition. You will be entering that condition.

Whether the condition affects *you* is the central question of your visit, and you cannot know the answer in advance. Plan for both possibilities.

## Physical Preparation

**Altitude.** Three thousand meters is not trivial. If you come from sea level, you will arrive short of breath, headachy, and possibly nauseous for two to four days. In an ordinary place you would acclimatize. In Oss, it is worth considering whether your body will acclimatize at all, or whether you will simply remain in the mild distress of your first hour, indefinitely, for as long as you stay. Arrive already acclimatized if you can — spend a week at comparable elevation beforehand. Do not arrive with any condition you would not want to be stuck in.

**Clothing.** One mild temperature, all year. Layers for a cool day in early autumn: a long-sleeved shirt, a wool overshirt, trousers of something that breathes, sturdy shoes broken in well. Nothing you would mind wearing for the entire visit, because — and this matters — laundry in Oss may not dry on the ordinary schedule, or it may dry and then remain exactly as dry and as clean as it was when you hung it, forever. Bring two full changes and accept that you may wear the same clothes for the duration.

**Shoes especially.** The streets are stone stairs. Switchbacking, uneven, cut into living rock. Bring footwear you trust on wet stone, because the channels seep and the mornings condense.

## Medical Considerations

This is the part that deserves most thought.

If time does not move normally in Oss, then healing does not move normally either. A cut you arrive with is a cut you may leave with. A cut you acquire there may not close while you are there. Arrive **fully healthy**. Get dental work done first. Cut your nails and your hair short enough that you will not need to tend them — though note that if you are fully inside the local condition, they will not grow at all, and if you are not, you will need scissors.

Bring:
- Any medication you take regularly, in quantities for three times your planned stay. If the condition holds you, you may not metabolize doses on schedule; if it does not, you are in a pre-industrial mountain valley.
- A small, complete first-aid kit, assuming no wound will heal on its own.
- Do not bring anything you need refrigerated. Do not bring anything that relies on being taken at precise intervals measured by your body's own clocks — those clocks may not run.

**Eat sparingly before you arrive, and eat sparingly there.** The fruit is real food and the blue-green leaf is nourishing, but consider: if digestion slows with everything else, a heavy meal becomes a heavy meal you are carrying for days. Small portions, often, of the local food.

## Temporal Preparation

This is the deepest preparation, and the one travelers neglect.

**Set an external anchor.** Arrange a way to know how long you have been there that does not depend on your own sense of time, your body's hunger, your beard, or the sky. A good mechanical watch wound before entry will probably keep running (it runs on mainspring physics, which may or may not be affected — test on arrival by comparing it to your pulse). Better: arrange with whoever sends you in to retrieve you on a fixed external date, regardless of what you say or signal. Do not trust your own judgment about when to leave. People who stay in places like Oss lose the faculty of leaving before they lose the desire to leave.

**Decide your stay in advance and write it down somewhere you will see it daily.** "I am leaving on the fourteenth day." Carry the note. Read it every morning. You will find, probably on the third or fourth day, that the note begins to seem quaint, or unnecessary, or mildly embarrassing, like an urgent memo from a former self who did not understand. This is the condition settling onto you. Keep reading the note.

**Expect the sensation of urgency itself to feel ridiculous.** The children of Oss have learned that urgency is a misunderstanding. You will learn this too, quickly. It is a seductive lesson. It is also, for a traveler whose life is elsewhere, wrong for you. Remember that you have a life that moves. Remember specific people who are aging while you are not.

## Social Preparation

The people of Oss speak quietly. Match them. A loud voice will carry across the whole valley and you will be the foreigner who shouted. Bring the habit of low speech and unhurried gesture. Do not bring the habit of small talk that fills silence — silence is not empty here, it is the ambient condition, and filling it marks you as someone who cannot bear it.

They do not invent. They will not find your devices interesting in the way you expect. They may find them interesting in an entirely different way — as curiosities that *change*, that wear out, that need charging — but this interest will be mild and slightly sad, the way one regards a guest who is visibly ill.

Bring small gifts, but think carefully. A fruit you brought with you will rot in their hands, or will not; either outcome is disquieting. A bead, a well-made small tool, a smooth stone from your home valley — these are appropriate. Gifts that persist are gifts that fit.

**Do not ask them how long they have lived there.** They will answer, and the answer will not resolve, and you will feel you have pressed on a soft place in someone kind.

## The Shrine

You will want to visit the empty shrine on the lowest terrace. Everyone does. A few warnings.

Do not take anything from it. The offerings are not taken. You would be the first, and whatever keeps the place in its condition may or may not consider this significant, and you do not want to be the test case.

You may leave an offering. Think about what you leave. Whatever you place there will, by the logic of the place, remain there — your pebble will sit among their pebbles, your bead among their beads, possibly for as long as the valley exists. This is a decision of some weight. Bring something you are willing to give in that sense. A coin from your home currency is a poor choice (meaningless there, lost to you). A small object that meant something to you, that you can release, is a better one.

Do not enter the shrine expecting to find out what it is for. It is not for anything you can name. The inhabitants have not named it either. It is the place where the offerings go.

## Leaving

This is the hardest part, and the one most worth rehearsing.

When your external date comes, leave. Do not negotiate with yourself. Do not decide to stay "one more day to see the harvest" — there is no harvest, there is only the continuous ripeness, and one more day here means one more day here forever in the sense that matters.

Expect, on the walk out of the valley, to feel that you are making a mistake. Expect to feel that you are leaving the only sane place you have ever been for a world of pointless motion and decay. This feeling is accurate as a description of the difference between Oss and elsewhere. It is not a reason to turn around. You are a creature of the moving world. Your people are there. Your unfinished things are there. The stillness of Oss was not made for you, and staying in it would not make you still — it would make you stopped, which is different.

When you cross back out, your watch and your body and your wounds will remember how to move again. The first meal you eat in a place with seasons will taste strange — too urgent, too alive, too much. Sleep a full night. In the morning, notice that the light has changed since yesterday. This is how you know you are home.

## One Last Thing

Bring a notebook and a pencil, and write in it every day you are there. Not because you will forget — you will not forget Oss — but because the writing is a small act of change, of accumulation, of one page becoming two. It is a thread you spin out of yourself into the stopped air of the valley, and when you leave, you will follow it back.
