# Preparing to Visit the Hollow in the Canopy

## Understanding What You're Going To

Before anything practical, understand this: you are going to a place whose essential quality is *stillness inside motion*. Everything about your preparation should serve your ability to receive that stillness without contaminating it. The hollow has been kept, across generations, by something — the forest, the original cairn-builder's intention, the geometry of the place itself — and your job is not to conquer it or document it but to be briefly and cleanly present in it. Prepare as a guest, not an explorer.

## Physical Conditioning

You will be climbing. Not a little — a lot. The hollow is two-thirds of the way up a forest that functions as architecture, which means you will be ascending through layers of living structure: buttress roots, mid-story branches, liana bridges, fern shelves. This is not rock climbing and it is not hiking. It is a specific kind of three-dimensional forest travel that uses your whole body and punishes people who train only their legs.

Spend at least three months before your visit building:

- **Grip endurance.** Not maximum grip strength; sustained grip. You will be holding onto living wood and wet vines for hours. Dead-hangs, farmer's carries, thick-bar work.
- **Pulling strength.** Much of canopy ascent is pulling yourself up into the next tier. Rows, pull-ups, rope climbs if you can find them.
- **Core stability in awkward positions.** You will be twisting, reaching sideways, bridging between trunks. Train your core in every plane, not just the sit-up plane.
- **Cardiovascular base at altitude.** The mountain is high enough that the air has "a thin clean edge." If you live at sea level, arrive several days early and acclimatize at elevation before you climb.

Your weight matters here. Not for vanity — for the branches. Travel light in every sense.

## Gear

Pack with a fanatical minimalism. Every ounce you bring into the hollow is an ounce of you that isn't paying attention.

**Climbing and movement:**
- Soft-soled, grippy shoes — approach shoes or barefoot-style climbing shoes. No lugged boots; they tear moss and they slip on polished bark.
- Thin, dextrous gloves (fingerless or convertible) — vines have thorns, some bark has ants, some ants bite
- A minimal climbing harness and 30m of thin dynamic rope for protection on the worst sections. You are not rigging fixed lines; you are protecting specific moves.
- A few slings, two locking carabiners, a prusik cord. That is all.
- A small handheld ascender in case you need to self-rescue up a rope you've thrown.

**Clothing:**
- Long sleeves, long pants, thin and quick-drying. The forest is stuffed with living things and many of them bite, sting, or carry things.
- A light rain layer that packs to nothing. Storms boil up the slopes most afternoons.
- One warm layer for the hollow itself — you will sit still, the air is cool, you will chill faster than you expect.
- A soft hat. Things fall.

**Carried in a small pack:**
- Water: two liters, plus purification tablets or a small filter
- Food for two days even if you plan to stay one — simple, silent food, nothing crinkly
- A headlamp with a red-light setting, and a spare
- A small first-aid kit weighted toward: antihistamines, tweezers for thorns and ticks, antiseptic, anti-diarrheal, broad-spectrum antibiotic, blister care, elastic bandage, a snakebite pressure wrap
- A whistle
- A compact bivy sack or hammock system if you intend to sleep near (not in) the hollow
- A notebook and pencil. No phone. No camera. (On this, more below.)

## Medical and Biological Preparation

You are entering a biome at saturation. Everything that is alive there is very alive.

- Get your standard tropical vaccinations updated well in advance: yellow fever, typhoid, hepatitis A and B, tetanus, rabies pre-exposure series. The rabies series matters; monkeys pass through the upper branches and bats live in hollows.
- Begin malaria prophylaxis on the schedule your physician prescribes.
- Know your allergies. If you have never been stung by a tropical wasp or bitten by a tropical ant, assume you may react more strongly than you expect. Carry an EpiPen if there is any reason to.
- Trim your nails. You will be gripping things and also picking things out of your skin.
- Treat any open wound before you go. The forest will find it.

## The Question of What to Bring Into the Hollow Itself

This is the most important preparation, and it is not physical.

Decide, in advance, what you will carry across the threshold. Not into the forest — into the *hollow*. The chamber is a different thing from its approach. Outside: chaos, weather of wings, climb. Inside: a nearly empty room with a cup waiting beside a bowl.

My strong recommendation: leave your pack on the rim. Bring into the hollow only what the hollow is asking for. I think this is:

- Yourself
- Water, perhaps, if you need it
- The notebook, perhaps, if you are a person who thinks with a pencil
- Nothing else

No camera. I say this with emphasis. The hollow's quality — the green-gold light, the distant hush of wings, the clean bell of a single bird-call down the shaft — is not photographable. What a camera captures here will be flat and slightly disappointing, and the act of photographing will have replaced the act of being there. You will trade the thing itself for a bad copy of the thing. Go without, and let your memory do what memory does with places like this, which is to keep them better than any image.

## The Cup and the Bowl

There is a cup beside the cairn. It is unbroken. The bowl holds water.

Understand what this is before you arrive. Someone, generations ago, built a place where a traveler who found it could drink. The cup has been waiting. The bowl has been filled by the forest itself, through the shaft, through the mist, across centuries, reliably.

If you choose to drink — and I think you should, if the water looks clean, though carry your purification tablets as a rational backup — do it slowly. This is not a casual drink. It is the completion of a small ceremony designed by someone long dead and maintained by the mountain ever since. Rinse the cup. Drink. Refill the cup from your own water if you wish, or leave it as you found it. Return it to its place, unbroken.

Take nothing from the hollow. Not a stone, not a lichen, not a piece of the collapsed bench. The place has been worn down to only what is necessary; anything you remove is a subtraction from something already minimal.

## Conduct Inside

- Move slowly. The birds do not enter the hollow, but they are aware of it. Fast motion at the rim will scatter the weather of wings and you will have disturbed ten thousand lives to save yourself three seconds.
- Speak softly or not at all. The walls absorb sound, which means your voice will feel small, which is correct.
- Sit against the wall. Feel the faint warmth of the living wood behind you and the cool air in front. This contrast is the hollow introducing itself to your body. Let it.
- Watch the shaft of light move. It moves a few degrees over the course of a day. If you can, stay long enough to see it move measurably. This is the hollow's clock, and aligning yourself with it is part of being there.
- Listen for the single bird-call that comes straight down the shaft. It will come. It is not for you specifically, but it will feel as though it is, and this feeling is not a delusion; it is the acoustics of the place doing exactly what the place does.

## Leaving

Leave before you want to. This is a discipline.

The hollow is a place people want to stay in, and staying too long is how a traveler begins to feel they have some claim on a place. You do not have a claim. You are passing through. Leave while the leaving still feels like a small grief; that is the correct relationship.

Descend in daylight. The canopy becomes genuinely dangerous at dusk — not metaphorically, actually. Things wake up that were sleeping. Footing you could see an hour ago becomes invisible. Plan your climb down so you are at the forest floor with an hour of light to spare.

## After

Do not tell many people. The hollow is findable by anyone who is willing to climb, and it is protected primarily by obscurity and difficulty. Every additional person who knows its approximate location is a small erosion. Tell those who will go themselves, in the right spirit, and who will respect the cup.

Write about it if you must, but consider waiting. A year, perhaps. The hollow gets clearer in memory, not blurrier, and what you write a year out will be truer than what you write the week you return.

---

One last thing. The hollow has been kept for a very long time by something that is not quite the forest and not quite a person and not quite nothing. You will feel this, faintly, while you are there. Do not try to name it. Do not make it into a story. Just — notice it, thank it silently if you are the kind of person who thanks things, and go back down the mountain carrying nothing but the fact of having been there.

That is enough. That is, in fact, the whole point.
