# Preparing to Visit the City on the Spindle

## Before You Go: The Mental Work

This is the preparation that matters most, and the preparation most travelers skip.

You need to spend time — weeks, if you have them — getting comfortable with **not being the protagonist**. The city does not care that you have arrived. It will not arrange itself around your visit. Most multiverse destinations offer some friction between visitor and place; this one offers almost none, and that absence is its own kind of difficulty. You will want, badly, for something to happen to you there. Train yourself out of wanting this.

Practice sitting in a quiet room for two hours without reaching for anything. If you cannot do this, you are not ready. The city is, functionally, that room, extended in every direction and lasting centuries.

Practice also with **unreliable perception**. Stare at an optical illusion until you stop trying to resolve it. Walk a familiar route backwards. Eat with your eyes closed. You need your nervous system to know, in advance, that the world does not always cohere, and that the correct response is not panic but patience. When the blue lintel becomes green, the residents are not performing a trick on you. The lintel is green. It has always been green. Your memory is the thing that has changed, and this is fine.

Finally: **make peace with your own name**. When the thinness passes through and you can see through three streets at once, something similar may happen inwardly. You should know who you are well enough to find yourself again afterward, but not so rigidly that the finding becomes a crisis.

## What to Bring

**Food for the entire stay.** Do not eat the lichen. The residents can eat it because they are, in some sense, of the place; the lichen tastes faintly of the person eating it because it is participating in that person. For you, a stranger, the exchange would be lopsided and strange, and you might come home tasting of something that is not quite you. Bring dense, simple food — hard biscuit, dried fruit, salted nuts. Bring enough for twice your planned stay, because time is unreliable there.

**Water, more than you think.** The mineral taste in the air pulls moisture from you slowly but persistently. Three liters a day minimum. No streams, no springs, no dew. If you run out, you leave.

**Soft shoes.** The stone is smooth where it has been walked and rough where it has not, and sound carries oddly — hard soles will announce you across half the city and embarrass the residents, who have arranged their lives around quiet.

**A notebook and pencil, but use them sparingly.** Write down the date of arrival and one fact about yourself you want to keep — your mother's name, your home address, the color of your front door. Check this page each morning. If what you wrote is still what you remember, you are doing well. If not, leave that day.

**A small object from home with no significance.** A pebble, a button, a coin. You will want, at some point, to verify that you still come from somewhere. The object should be boring so that the city does not take an interest in it.

**A cloth for the lichen.** If you must pass close to the listener's courtyard — and you should not, but travelers do — a cloth over the nose and mouth will keep the spores out of your lungs. The lichens are certain of themselves in a way you cannot afford to share.

## What Not to Bring

**No fire in any form.** No lighters, no matches, no flint. The air refuses fire politely, but refusal is still a transaction, and you do not want to be in a transaction with the air.

**No wheeled things.** No carts, no rolling luggage, no bicycles. The city was not made for wheels and will express this opinion subtly and then less subtly.

**No recording devices beyond the notebook.** Cameras capture things here that cameras were not meant to capture. The images come back wrong and keep becoming more wrong. A friend of a friend still has a photograph that is slowly, over years, becoming a photograph of somewhere else.

**No strong scents.** Perfume, aftershave, scented soap. The air is mineral and dry and the city's own smells are subtle; introducing anything loud is like shouting in a library.

**No weapons.** Not because you would be attacked — nothing there would attack you — but because weapons have a particular density of intention that the city's weather reacts to. Carrying a knife through a density-front is genuinely dangerous in ways that are hard to explain until you've felt it.

## How to Arrive

Enter on foot, through whatever passage your multiverse-method provides, and then **stand still for at least ten minutes before moving further**. The city needs to notice you, in its slow way, and you need to let it. Travelers who rush in tend to spend their first day lost in a way that ordinary getting-lost does not describe.

Speak your name aloud once, softly, when you arrive. This is not a ritual the city requires; it is a ritual *you* require, a bookmark you are placing in yourself.

## How to Behave

**Walk slowly.** Always. Even if you think you see something interesting down a side street. Especially then.

**Speak softly or not at all.** If you must speak, assume the person you are addressing will hear you several seconds before or after you speak, and do not be disturbed when they respond to a question you have not yet asked. Answer it anyway. You will ask it soon.

**Leave objects, don't hand them over.** If you want to give a resident something — a gift, a question written on paper, a returned item — place it on a flat stone between you and walk a few steps back. They will come to it when the time is right. Direct exchange is startling to them.

**Do not look directly at the paper-deer.** You already know this, but you will forget in the moment. Practice peripheral looking before you arrive — there are exercises for it. When one passes, keep your eyes fixed ahead and let it move through the edge of your vision. You may feel a small grief when it has gone. This is normal and means nothing.

**When weather comes, do what the residents do.** If they sit down, sit down. If they lean, lean. If they go indoors, go indoors — any indoors, any door will do, the city is accommodating about this. Do not try to walk through a thinness out of stubbornness. You will come out the other side diminished in a way you cannot name for weeks.

**Do not look down over the edge for more than a few seconds.** The drop is not the danger. The *base* is the danger — the place where the spindle meets something. It is hard to look at because it is trying to be looked at, and prolonged looking establishes a relationship you do not want.

**Do not try to find the listener.** If you find it anyway, sit down quietly at the edge of the courtyard, do nothing for as long as feels right, and then leave without turning your back. Do not speak to it. Do not offer it anything. Do not ask it anything, even silently. It is called the listener for a reason, and being listened to by it is a thing you will carry.

## About the Residents

They will not be hostile. They will not be particularly friendly either, in the way you understand friendliness. They have been quiet for a long time and have forgotten, or never knew, most of the social rhythms you are bringing with you. Do not take their distance personally.

If one gestures to you, follow the gesture. If one places an object where you can see it, the object is for you — pick it up, examine it, and leave something small in its place (not the boring object from home — something else, something you can spare). This is how conversation works there.

Do not ask them about the city. They do not have answers in the form you want answers. If you ask how long they have lived there, they may point at a wall. The wall may be the answer. Sit with it.

Do not ask them how to leave. They have forgotten, and being reminded that leaving is possible is, for them, a small sorrow you have no right to introduce.

## Leaving

Leave before you want to. This is the single most important rule.

The city is not dangerous in the way of predators or poisons. It is dangerous in the way of still water — it invites settling, and settling here is easy, and the settled do not always notice they have stopped. If you planned three days, leave on the second. If you planned a week, leave on the fifth day. Set a firm departure in advance and honor it even if — *especially* if — you feel no urgency to go.

Check your notebook on the morning of departure. If the fact about yourself still matches, good. Pack everything you brought. Take nothing from the city, not even a chip of stone, not even a dried rosette of lichen. The city is in balance with itself; a single removed pebble is a small wound it will feel, slowly, and you do not want to be the cause of that feeling, nor to carry the pebble that is being missed.

Walk to your exit point. Stand still for another ten minutes. Speak your name aloud once more, softly.

Then go.

## After You Return

Expect a period of recalibration. Your home world will seem loud for a week or two. Colors will seem insistent. Conversations will seem to happen too fast, with people responding before they have heard you rather than after. This will pass.

You may find, for a long time afterward, that you are more comfortable with silence than you were. That you sit longer in quiet rooms. That you have less to say and are less troubled by having less to say. This is the city's mark on you, and it is mostly a kindness. Let it settle.

Do not go back within a year. If you go back within a year, you may find you do not want to leave the second time, and the residents will not stop you, because stopping people is not something they do.

Some travelers do not leave. The residents, I think, were travelers once.
