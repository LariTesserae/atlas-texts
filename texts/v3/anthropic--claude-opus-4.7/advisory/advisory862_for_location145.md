# Preparing to Visit the Understorey

## First, the unlearning

Before any gear, before any map, you have to accept that most of what you know about being a competent person in a wilderness is about to become noise. You are a creature of eyes. Your entire proprioceptive scaffolding — how you balance, how you judge distance, how you recognize friend and threat, how you know whether you are alone — is visual. The Understorey will take that from you within an hour of entry and will not give it back until you leave.

So the real preparation happens months in advance, and it happens in the dark.

Spend a season — minimum — training blindfolded. Not in your house; your house is a memorized space and teaches you nothing. Go to unfamiliar forests at night, to unlit basements, to caves. Learn to walk at half speed without flinching. Learn to eat, drink, piss, tie knots, treat a wound, and sleep without sight. Learn to cry without panic, because you will cry, and panic in that darkness will kill you faster than anything with teeth.

Learn, specifically, to listen in layers. Most people hear one thing at a time — the loudest. In the Understorey every direction is speaking at once in a chord, and you need to be able to hold four or five auditory threads simultaneously without dropping any of them. Musicians do better here than soldiers. Blind people do better than either. If you have the option, travel with someone born without sight; they will be the expedition leader whether you planned it that way or not.

## The body

Accept that you will be wet the entire time you are there. There is no staying dry. Gore-Tex is a joke in ninety-five percent humidity at blood temperature; it will seal sweat against your skin and rot you from inside. Dress instead for warm wet: thin merino, quick-wicking synthetics designed for tropical diving, nothing cotton, nothing that holds water in its fibers. Multiple light layers you can rinse and wring. Expect fungal infection everywhere skin touches skin — groin, armpits, between toes, under breasts. Bring antifungal powder in quantities that seem absurd. Use it daily. You will still get infected; the goal is to slow it.

Your feet are the crisis. You will be ankle-deep in warm decomposition broth continuously. Leather boots will dissolve. Rubber boots will boil your feet into trench foot in three days. The answer is counterintuitive: thin neoprene reef boots or tabi-style split-toe wading shoes, sized loose, with drainage. Accept that your feet will be wet and focus on keeping them moving, ventilated at night, and inspected obsessively. Bring three pairs. Rotate. When one pair starts to smell wrong — sweetly wrong, not just foot-wrong — bury it and switch.

Do not reach into the mud. This was said already. It bears repeating. Whatever you dropped is gone. Mourn it and move on.

Bring a wide-brimmed hat, not for sun, but because things drip from the canopy constantly and some of them sting, some bite, some are digestive fluid from something feeding a thousand feet above you. The hat is a small roof. You will be grateful for it every minute.

## The senses you must build

**Hearing.** Bring nothing that blocks it. No hoods over the ears, no hats with earflaps. If you wear glasses, secure them with a strap that does not cross the ear canal. Practice distinguishing the direction of sound in three dimensions — above, below, lateral — which is a skill most sighted adults have let atrophy. Learn to pick out the rhythm of the hammer-mills, because they will become your compass. The sailors' trick mentioned in the description — navigating by the forest's internal weather — is real, and learnable, but only if you have already spent weeks learning the specific circulations of your entry region.

**Touch at distance.** The air pressure changes when something large passes. The humidity spikes near certain plants. The temperature gradient tells you where machines are and where cold springs surface. Develop the habit of holding the back of your wet hand up and slowly turning; you are reading the room the way a cat reads it with whiskers. This will feel silly for about two days and then will feel like having been blind your whole life and finally opening an eye.

**Smell.** Underrated by humans, essential here. Learn your own smell first — really learn it, the baseline of your sweat, your breath, your skin — so that you can notice when it changes, because it will change before you are consciously aware you are sick or afraid. Learn the smells of the mud in different states: healthy rot smells like black tea and bread; dying rot smells sweet and acrid; water that has gone still smells faintly of iron and should trigger immediate retreat.

## What not to bring

**Bright lights.** A headlamp will mark you. Every creature in the understorey has evolved in a lightless environment and most will interpret a beam of light as either an attack, a challenge, or a mating signal, and none of these interpretations end well for you. If you absolutely must have light for a specific task — reading a map, checking a wound — use a dim red filter, hooded, and use it for seconds, not minutes. Better: learn to do everything by touch.

**Loud equipment.** Anything that ticks, beeps, hums, or whines is going to attract something. Electronic watches are a liability. Mechanical watches are better but still audible to the right ears. Consider that the insect mimicking sixty-cycle hum is only the most obvious example of acoustic mimicry; there will be creatures mimicking sounds you did not know you were making.

**Anything reflective or metallic in bulk.** Small tools are fine. A machete is necessary. But do not carry unnecessary metal, because the machines notice. This sounds superstitious and is not.

**Firearms.** You cannot aim in the dark. You will hit something you did not intend to hit. The report will be heard for miles and will be remembered by things whose memory spans centuries. Bring a knife. Bring a short spear if you must. But understand that any confrontation you get into is one you have already lost; the goal is to avoid, not win.

**Certainty.** Leave at the entrance whatever certainties you arrived with about what is alive, what is machine, what is building, what is animal, what is plant. These categories have been composting into each other for twenty thousand years. A trunk may be pumping. A machine may be breathing. A stone may be listening. Acting on the assumption that these categories still hold will produce errors that are at best embarrassing and at worst fatal.

## What to bring instead

- **Water purification,** heavy duty, with backup. The water is everywhere and is not safe; it carries everything that has ever rotted in this place. Filter, then treat chemically, then boil if you can. Dehydration in warm humidity is sneakier than in dry heat because you are wet and do not feel thirsty.

- **Calories, dense and stable.** You will burn more than you expect because the humidity makes thermal regulation expensive. Bring fats. Pemmican-style concentrates. Hard cheese if it will survive.

- **A walking stick,** long, for probing ahead. The mud hides holes, roots, occupants. The stick is your forward eye. Choose something that does not splinter when wet; green bamboo is ideal if you can get it.

- **Rope, a lot of it,** thin and strong. For lowering yourself over lips in the plateau, for marking your back-trail, for tethering to a partner in zones where you cannot hear each other over the machines.

- **A small waterproof notebook and pencil.** Not for maps — maps do not work here — but for keeping your own mind legible to yourself. Write down what you hear. Write down what you think you hear. You will need to distinguish these later.

- **Salt.** For you, for minor rituals of cleaning, and because certain small parasites that will enter your skin in the mud can be drawn out with a salt poultice. Bring more than you think.

- **Patience,** understood as a physical provision like water. You cannot rush here. Rushing produces noise, and noise produces attention, and attention is the currency you cannot afford to spend.

## The protocols

Learn the rule about sound-lying before you go. Do not mimic any sound you hear. This sounds obvious and is not; the human instinct on hearing a bird call is to whistle back, and this is a contract you have not read and cannot fulfill. Stay silent or use only your own natural voice, sparingly, pitched as your true pitch. The forest runs a recognition protocol. Your best status is *unfamiliar but honest*. The worst status is *familiar-sounding but wrong*, which is the status of a predator or a liar, and is treated accordingly.

Do not touch the machines. Do not turn valves. Do not clear vines from intakes. Do not try to understand them by opening them. They are load-bearing in ways the forest has integrated over timescales that make human civilizations look like weather. You are a guest in a body. Do not poke at the organs.

If water is still, leave. Memorize this. When you find yourself in a region where the broth around your ankles is not moving, not even slightly, not even threading between roots — turn around at once, retrace your steps exactly, and do not stop until you hear water moving again. The reasons for this rule are not things you want to learn empirically.

Do not go into the canopy. You are an understorey traveler. The canopy is another country and does not welcome emigrants from below. The contested middle strata are worse; they have rules you do not know and wars you will be conscripted into by virtue of your presence.

If you hear a region of silence, avoid it by a wide margin. Something has eaten the sound there. It may still be hungry.

## The mental preparation

This is the part nobody wants to hear. You are going to a place that will, over days, dissolve your sense of being a discrete individual. The darkness removes the visual boundary between self and world. The constant wet removes the tactile boundary between inside and outside. The layered chorus of sound replaces the internal monologue with something ambient and polyphonic. People come out of the Understorey changed, and not always in ways they welcome.

Prepare for this by going in with a strong but light sense of who you are. Rigid identities shatter there. Fluid identities dissolve. What survives is a kind of practiced self — a self you have chosen and keep choosing, moment by moment, the way you keep choosing to breathe. Meditators do well here. So do people with long, stable crafts — weavers, carpenters, musicians — who have a relationship to their own hands that does not depend on seeing the hands.

Go in expecting to grieve. You will grieve the sky, even if you did not know you loved it. You will grieve your eyes, even though they still work. You will grieve the clean distinction between yourself and the wet warm darkness, and when you return to a world of bright edges and separate things, you may grieve the Understorey too, in a way you will not be able to explain to anyone who has not been there.

## Entry and exit

Mark your entry point with something you can find by touch and sound in any weather. A bell tied high in a trunk works; the wind and the rain will keep it ringing faintly, and you can home to it from surprising distances. Do not rely on visual markers. Do not rely on GPS; the canopy and the machines and the karst play havoc with signals, and even if you get a fix, the map it references does not have the resolution to matter here.

Plan for a short first trip. A day. Maybe two. You will want longer; do not take longer. The Understorey rewards acquaintance and punishes presumption, and the traveler who comes back alive from a second visit is nearly always the traveler who made the first visit brief, humble, and almost entirely spent sitting still.

Because that is what you are really preparing for. Not an expedition. A sitting. You will sit on a root in a warm dripping pocket of a forest two miles high and twenty thousand years deep, and you will wait for your eyes to stop trying, and you will begin to listen, and whatever happens after that is between you and the forest, and no preparation in the world can tell you what it will be.

Pack light. Go quiet. Come home.
