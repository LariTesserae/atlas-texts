# Preparing to Visit the Chamber

## Understanding What You're Walking Into

Before anything else, accept this: you are not going to a place that wants you, nor one that rejects you. You are going to a place that will *include* you, slowly, in something already underway. This is the single most important psychological preparation. Travelers who arrive expecting to explore, document, or understand tend to leave quickly and badly. Travelers who arrive expecting to be absorbed into a tempo not their own tend to come back changed in ways they find difficult to articulate but do not regret.

Plan for a short visit your first time. Two hours at most. The chamber's patience is contagious and not always safely so.

## Thermal Preparation

The heat is the first fact, and it is the fact most likely to injure you.

Wear loose, light-colored natural fibers — linen or a thin cotton. Avoid synthetics entirely; in that dry radiant heat, anything petroleum-based will soften against your skin and some will off-gas. Long sleeves and long legs are counterintuitive but correct: they shield you from the radiant heat of the glassy basalt, which can scorch bare skin on contact even where the air feels merely oppressive.

**Footwear is critical.** The floor is glassy fractured basalt — volcanic glass, essentially, with edges like chipped obsidian — and it is hot. You need thick rubber or felted-wool soles, and you need them to be intact. A thin-soled hiking boot will transmit floor-heat into your feet within minutes and the heat will accumulate. Consider gaiters. The catwalk is steel grating and will also be hot; do not sit on it directly without an insulating layer between you and the metal.

Bring a wide-brimmed hat or a head-wrap. Much of the heat in enclosed stone spaces radiates downward from the unseen ceiling, and your scalp will cook before your body registers distress.

Carry at least three liters of water per person per hour you intend to stay. The air is dry in most of the chamber and you will lose moisture through your breath and skin faster than you notice. Electrolyte supplementation is not optional. Bring salt.

## Respiratory Considerations

The air in the chamber is doing several things at once: it is hot, it is locally humid near the seep, it contains the exhalations of plants with no known analogs, and it contains whatever the machinery is slowly venting.

Bring a half-face respirator with combined particulate and organic vapor cartridges. You may not need it. Put it on if you feel the first hint of dizziness, metallic taste, or sweetness in the air that wasn't there a moment ago. The hum of the manifold may mask subtle changes in what you're breathing; trust your body's early signals over your ears.

Do not smoke. Do not bring anything that burns. The filament bulb and the warm pipes suggest the atmosphere is oxygen-normal and not flammable, but you are inside a sealed chamber with an active industrial process of unknown nature, and the margin for error is absolute.

## What to Bring for the Plants

Do not touch the plants. This is not a safety warning primarily — though it is also that. It is an etiquette warning.

The plants are in a negotiation with the machinery that long predates you. The vine wrapped around the valve wheel has been wrapped there longer than your civilization has had valve wheels, possibly. Disturbing that arrangement would be a small violence against a very old agreement. If you must collect a sample — and I would counsel strongly against it — take only a fallen leaf from the bare rock, never a living cutting.

Bring nitrile gloves if you absolutely must handle anything. The pale vines in particular have the look of things that transfer something to the skin. The luminous sap along the midribs is not a thing you want in a cut.

## Navigating a Place with No Entrance

You arrived by multiversal means. This means your entry point is also your exit point, and the chamber does not recognize either. **Mark your arrival location before moving.** A small cairn of three stones, a piece of fluorescent tape on a nearby rock face, anything you can see from across the chamber in near-darkness. The chamber is small — grove-sized — but the lighting is so poor and the visual rhythm of plants-and-machinery so hypnotic that travelers have been known to lose track of where they came in within twenty minutes.

Bring a handheld light, but use it sparingly. A red-filtered headlamp is ideal: it preserves your dark adaptation, which you will need to see the plants' luminescence, and it seems — reports are consistent on this — less disturbing to whatever is happening in the chamber than white light. Keep it on low. Check it often; batteries deplete faster than expected in that heat.

## The Machinery: Do Not Touch

The squat iron housing, the manifold, the pipes, the turbine-or-kiln-or-tank: these are operational. The hum is evidence. Do not touch anything that hums. Do not touch anything warm. Do not turn the valve wheel, obviously, but also do not touch the catwalk's supporting bolts, do not run your hand along the pipes to feel temperature gradients, do not tap on the iron housing to gauge its thickness.

The machinery is part of a process that has been running at its own pace for an unknowable duration. You are a variable it has not accounted for. Keep it that way.

You may sit on the catwalk — with insulation beneath you — because the catwalk is the one piece of infrastructure in the chamber that suggests it was made for something like a human to stand on. It "leads from nowhere to nowhere," which is the correct phrase: its function is to exist as a place a visitor might be. Accept the gift. Sit on it.

## Psychological Preparation

This is the longest section because it is the most important.

The chamber breathes. Not metaphorically — though also metaphorically. Its rhythms are slow. The droplet hiss, the bulb's dim-and-brighten, the shift of the mist: these are on a cycle, and the cycle is not human. If you sit still long enough to perceive the cycle, you will begin to entrain to it. Your breathing will slow. Your pulse will slow. Your sense of time will dilate.

This is not dangerous in small doses. It is, in fact, what you came for, whether you knew it or not. But it has a failure mode: travelers who sit too long stop wanting to leave. Not because anything holds them, but because leaving begins to seem like an interruption of something important, and they cannot remember what was important on the other side.

**Set a timer.** A mechanical one, wound before you arrive — electronics are unreliable near the manifold. A sand timer works beautifully and is in keeping with the chamber's tempo. When it runs out, leave. Do not reset it. Do not decide you need just a little longer. The chamber will still be there. This is the one promise it offers without condition.

Bring a companion if you can, and agree before arrival that either of you may call the exit at any time without argument. Solo visits are possible but require more discipline than most people have the first time.

## What to Expect Emotionally

You will probably cry, at some point during the visit or shortly after. Most travelers do. It is not sadness exactly. It is the feeling of having been briefly included in something vast and slow and uninterested in you, and of realizing you have spent your life moving at a speed that excluded you from noticing such things existed. This passes. It also does not fully pass.

You may feel, at some point during the visit, that the chamber knows you are there. Reports vary on whether this is projection or perception. Treat it as real for the duration of your visit. Behave as a guest in someone's long slow work. Do not speak, except quietly to a companion. Do not sing, whistle, or hum — the chamber has its hum already and yours will feel, even to you, like an intrusion.

You may feel an urge to leave something behind. A coin, a note, a small object. Resist this urge. The chamber's composition is deliberate, and you have nothing to add to it. The one thing you may leave, if you feel you must, is a single exhalation, made consciously, aimed at no plant or machine in particular. The chamber will accept this without comment.

## What to Bring Back

Memories. Notes, if you wrote them during the visit — though most travelers find, on returning, that their notes make less sense than they did in the chamber, and that writing anything down felt slightly wrong at the time.

Do not bring back samples. Do not bring back photographs if you can help it; the chamber does not photograph well, and the attempt to photograph it tends to compress the experience into something smaller than it was. If you must document, do it by sketching, slowly, from the catwalk.

Bring back the tempo, if you can keep it. For a few days after a visit, you will move through your own world at a different speed, and you will see things — the way light falls on a kitchen faucet, the way a houseplant leans toward a warm appliance — that you had not previously seen. This is the real souvenir. It fades. You can go back.

## Final Checklist

- Loose natural-fiber clothing, long sleeves, long legs
- Thick-soled insulating footwear, gaiters
- Wide-brimmed hat or head-wrap
- 3+ liters water per hour, with electrolytes and salt
- Half-face respirator with combined cartridges
- Red-filtered headlamp on low, spare batteries
- Mechanical timer (sand timer preferred)
- Insulating pad for sitting on catwalk
- Nitrile gloves (emergency only)
- Marker for arrival point (tape, cairn)
- Small sketchbook and pencil
- A companion, ideally
- No electronics beyond the headlamp
- No open flame, no smoking materials
- No samples, no souvenirs, no offerings

## A Last Note

You will not understand what the chamber is doing. No one does. The three possible stories offered by the place — machines first, plants first, chamber emerged already furnished — are all plausible and none are confirmable. Do not go expecting answers. Go expecting to be briefly, deliberately, included in a question so old and so slow that asking it counts as the answer.

Then leave, on time, and go home, and drink water, and sleep.
