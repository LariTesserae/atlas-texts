# Preparing to Visit the Sump Grove

## First, Understand What You're Visiting

This is not a destination in the ordinary sense. It is a slow organism the size of a chapel, and you are proposing to step inside it. Most of your preparation should be oriented around a single question: *how do I enter a living system without becoming a disturbance it will take a decade to forget?*

The grove will not hurt you. It will not test you. But it keeps, as the description says, its own slow books, and the entries you make in those books on your visit will persist long after you're gone. Prepare accordingly.

## Physical Preparation

**Clothing.** Wear one layer, close-fitting, in muted earth tones or dark green. Avoid anything that rustles — no nylon shells, no stiff canvas, no zippers that sing when you move. The chamber's acoustic floor is so low that ordinary clothing-noise will sound, to you, like a small crowd. Cotton, wool, soft linen. Long sleeves and long pants — not for warmth (you will be too warm) but because you will want the option of resting against a root-pillar or sitting on the clay floor without leaving skin-oil behind. Skin oil persists. The polished pillar you might sit against was made that way by people's backs over generations; contribute to it honestly, but know what you're contributing.

**Footwear.** Something you can climb in but also remove. The root-throats are ladders worn by use, and you'll want soles that grip without gouging. Once inside, consider taking your shoes off entirely — barefoot on the mossed limestone step, then on the warm clay. Your feet will learn the chamber in a way your eyes won't. Bring soft socks for after, when you're wet and tired.

**Hair.** Tied back. A loose strand in the pool is a loose strand in the pool for years.

**Skin.** Do not wear sunscreen, insect repellent, perfume, scented deodorant, lotions, or cosmetics of any kind. Wash, before you descend, with unscented soap, and then rinse thoroughly — residual soap is still a contaminant. The shrimp in that pool filter their entire world through their gills. Your morning aftershave is, to them, an oil spill.

**Breath and body.** Eat lightly before entering. Do not bring chewing gum, mints, or tobacco. Avoid alcohol for a full day beforehand; the chamber's heat will pull it out of you in ways you don't want to leave behind in that air.

## Acclimating Your Senses

The grove operates at a sensory register far below what you are probably used to. Multiverse travelers in particular often arrive with nervous systems still tuned to whatever loud, fast place they came from. Spend at least one full day before your visit in near-silence. Sit outside if you can. Notice the difference between the sound of wind in grass and the sound of wind in leaves. When you can distinguish, consistently, between a drip falling onto leaf and onto stone without looking, you are nearly ready.

Practice moving slowly. Not theatrically slow — just slow enough that you do not surprise yourself. If you set down your hand and it makes a sound, you moved too fast. The frogs click once every few minutes; let that be, for a while, your internal metronome.

Sit in the dark, or in low green-gold light if you can arrange it, until your eyes open fully and your pupils have expanded and you can see by what amounts to no light at all. You'll want this capacity intact when you descend, because the chamber's illumination is shifting, watery, and unreliable, and any torch you bring will blast the place flat.

## What to Bring

Very little. A small cloth satchel. Water, in a container that does not click when opened. A soft cloth to wipe sweat from your face (the heat is serious; you will sweat, and you don't want salt-water dropping into the pool). If you must bring light, bring a candle or a small shielded oil lamp — not an electric torch. The quality of the green-gold filtered light is part of what the chamber *is*, and overwriting it with white LED is a violence even if no one notices.

Do not bring a camera. I know. Do not bring one anyway. The chamber does not photograph well because its essential quality is temporal — the slow rotation of clicks, the second-late ripple of shadow, the hour-long percussion of rainfall — and a photograph lies about this. Also: flash will stun the moths, and repeated flash will almost certainly drive the salamander deeper into its crevice, where, since it emerges only twice a month, you will not see it anyway and will have made things worse for whoever comes next.

Bring something to sit on that will not stain the clay: a folded square of undyed cloth.

Consider bringing, as the old visitors did, a small offering for the niche. Not food. Not anything that will rot wrong. A bit of beeswax is historically appropriate and ecologically neutral. Leave it if it feels right. Don't if it doesn't. The niche has been empty a long time and is not waiting.

## What to Leave Behind

All electronics capable of transmitting. Beyond the obvious — phones in the grove are absurd — the chamber is old enough and balanced enough that it seems wrong to have it radiating in frequencies it has never known. If you are a multiverse traveler with devices that register dimensional flux or translation readings, power them down completely before you descend the root-throat. Whatever readings you would get are not worth what you'd be imposing.

Anything metal that might drop: coins, keys, a loose pendant. The pool is small. A dropped key in that pool is a dropped key in that pool forever.

Any urgency. If you are on a schedule, do not go. The grove is not suitable for quick visits. Plan a minimum of three hours inside, and understand that one of those hours should be motionless.

## Psychological Preparation

This is the part most travelers underprepare for.

The chamber will feel, while you sit in it, like it is aware of you. It isn't, not in any sense your instruments would confirm. What's happening is that you and the frogs and the seep and the barometric pressure are all loosely phase-locked, and your own heartbeat will, over the course of an hour, slip into rhythm with the clicks. This will produce, in nearly every visitor, a distinct and powerful sensation of being *listened to*.

Prepare for this. Decide, before you go, what you will do with the feeling. Some travelers weep. Some speak aloud to the chamber and then feel foolish. Some sit with it and find that an hour has passed without their noticing. None of these responses are wrong, but it helps to know in advance that the feeling is coming, so that you do not mistake it for a message or for something you must act on.

The grove is not telling you anything. It is, however, keeping a kind of time with you, and that is rarer and in some ways stranger than a message would be.

Prepare also for the particular loneliness of the place. It is warm, close, living, full of small sounds — and yet utterly indifferent to your presence in a way that feels different from a wilderness indifference. A forest ignores you because it has bigger things going on. The grove ignores you because it has *slower* things going on, and you are, on its timescale, a brief warm shape that came and went. This is humbling in a particular way, and travelers who are used to being the fastest thing in any room find it disorienting.

## Descending

Use the northernmost throat — the one with the limestone step. It is the traditional entry, and using it puts your weight on a path that has already been worn, rather than opening a new one. Come down slowly. Test each foothold. The roots are strong but the smoothness is deceptive when wet, and they will be wet.

When your foot finds the step, pause there. Let your eyes adjust. Let your breath slow. Let the heat rise up around you. Do not step down into the chamber until you can hear the clicks distinctly and can count, approximately, the interval between them.

Then enter.

## Inside

Move to a pillar — preferably the polished one, since sitting there is a use the chamber has already accepted. Sit. Place your hands on your knees or in your lap. Do not trail your fingers in the pool; the oils and the temperature differential are both disruptive.

Watch the light for a while. A shadow will pass — something moving in the meadow above — and you will see it cross the floor a second late. This is one of the chamber's gifts: a small demonstration that you are below, that the world is continuing above, that the connection between them is a slow one mediated by water and root and light. Accept the gift.

Listen to the clicks. Do not try to count them exactly; try to feel the rotation. After perhaps half an hour, press two fingers to your wrist and check your pulse against the pulse of the room. They will be close. This is the moment the chamber is famous for. Sit with it.

If the salamander emerges, do not move. Do not even turn your head. Track it with your eyes only. It is possibly older than anyone in your home timeline has been alive. It owes you nothing, and its appearance is not a reward — it is simply something that happened while you were there. Be grateful in proportion.

If rain comes while you are inside, stay for it. The hour of soft irregular percussion is, according to those who have experienced it, the closest the chamber comes to speaking. The frogs click faster. The pool rises. The air thickens further. You will feel held. Do not mistake this for an invitation to stay longer than you intended — the grove is gracious but not hospitable, and the distinction matters.

## Leaving

Leave by the same throat you entered. Leave nothing behind except, perhaps, the beeswax in the niche. Take nothing with you — no shell, no frond, no pebble, no sample of the felting, no matter how small or how scientifically interesting. The grove keeps a tight ecology. A single snail missing is a missing snail.

Climb slowly. At the top, before you emerge into the meadow, pause again. The transition from the chamber's heavy held air to the open sky is disorienting, and travelers who rush it sometimes feel a minor grief that they don't know how to name. Give yourself a moment at the lip of the throat to be in both places.

Then step out, and let the meadow have you again.

## Afterward

Do not speak about the grove for a day. Not out of secrecy — there is nothing especially secret about it — but because the experience does not translate well when it is still fresh, and premature telling tends to flatten it into anecdote. Sit with it. Let the pulse-memory fade at its own pace. When you do eventually describe it, describe it quietly, and to one person at a time.

Do not return soon. The grove does not reward frequency. If you loved it, that is a reason to come back in ten years, not in ten days. The slow books are slow.

And if you are the kind of multiverse traveler who keeps a record of the places you have been: make the entry brief. The chamber does not need a long one. It has, after all, been making its own entry about you, in its own way, the whole time you were there.
