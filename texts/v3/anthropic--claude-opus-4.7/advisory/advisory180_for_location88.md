# Preparing to Visit the Shelf-City of Aunu

## Understanding What You Are Entering

Before anything else: understand that Aunu is not a place you *visit* in the brisk sense of the word. It is a place you *enter*, the way one enters a long-held thought. Almost every instinct you have as a modern traveler — to photograph, to map, to ask direct questions, to move at walking pace, to raise your voice to be heard across a room — will be wrong here, and some of these wrongnesses will have consequences beyond mere rudeness. Prepare accordingly.

## The Body

**Breathing.** The water is breathable to the inhabitants and to visitors who have been prepared, but it is not your native medium. You will need at least a two-week acclimation regimen before arrival: a gradual transition through progressively denser breathable fluids, which most multiverse transit hubs can provide. Do not skip this. The warm-blood-temperature water is thick, and untrained lungs panic in it. The panic itself is the danger — drowning here is almost always a psychological event, not a chemical one.

**Temperature.** The water sits at roughly blood heat. You will not feel cold, but you will also not feel the usual cues that tell you you are exerting yourself. Pace yourself deliberately. Exhaustion arrives without warning and feels, I am told, like a sudden wish to lie down on the nearest soft surface — which in Aunu may be the unhealed rock of a lower terrace, and this is a problem (see below).

**Buoyancy.** Bring nothing heavy. Weight is a liability; the city is designed around gentle pushing-off motions, and a traveler laden with equipment will find themselves unable to make the soft drifting ascents between terraces, and will be obliged to crawl, which is both slow and conspicuous.

**Ears.** The flat-eared inhabitants hear things you cannot. Your own hearing will be muffled and strange — sounds will arrive rounded and late. Do not try to compensate by speaking louder. There is no version of you speaking loudly that is acceptable in Aunu.

**Skin.** The fine silt will settle on you. Do not brush at it continuously; this marks you as agitated. Let it accumulate as it does on everyone else. It rinses off in the warmer braided streams that drift through the streets. Your slate-skinned hosts will find your coloration strange but will not comment — commenting on appearance is apparently considered a form of noticing too closely, which, as you will learn, is a category of sin here.

## What to Bring

- **Soft-soled footwear, or none.** Hard soles leave marks on the young rock. Below the thirteenth terrace, any mark you leave will not heal.
- **A hand-lamp of local style, if you can acquire one in advance.** Your own lights will be too bright and the wrong spectrum. If you cannot get one, resolve to move through Aunu in its own light — the ribbon-groves, the phosphorescent lichens — and give your eyes the week they need to adjust.
- **A small slate and a soft stylus,** for writing in the trade-pidgin. Do not bring paper; it disintegrates. Do not bring electronics that emit sound, vibration, or signal.
- **A cloth to cover your mouth** during the first days, as a reminder to yourself not to speak reflexively.
- **Nothing that ticks, hums, whines, or beeps.** Nothing with a fan. Nothing whose function involves involuntary noise.
- **A gift, if you have a host.** Something small and quiet — a worked stone, a woven thing, a piece of fiber-work from another world. Nothing that smells strongly. Nothing alive.

## What to Leave Behind

Leave behind recording devices of any kind that the inhabitants might notice. Documentation is not explicitly forbidden, but the prohibition on *looking for the edge* extends, in practice, to a general cultural aversion to fixing things in place. A photograph is a kind of noticing. Consider writing from memory afterward instead.

Leave behind your sense of appropriate conversational tempo. A response to a tapped message in Aunu may come three hours later, or the next day. This is not rudeness. It is the weather.

Leave behind the reflex of asking questions about origins, edges, or ends. Do not ask where the water comes from. Do not ask what is above the lilac ceiling. Do not ask about the long animal. Above all, do not ask whether Aunu is *real* in any metaphysical sense. The inhabitants appear to believe that the city's persistence is partly sustained by not being examined too closely, and whether or not this is literally true, you will be treated as dangerous if you violate it.

## Learning the Taps

Before arriving, learn at least the core fifty taps of the domestic register. This is more important than learning the trade-pidgin, which is a concession the inhabitants make to visitors and which they do not particularly enjoy speaking. The taps are the true social medium. You do not need to be fluent. You need to be able to tap *I am listening*, *I am passing by*, *thank you*, *I do not understand*, *I am sorry*, and the courtesy-rhythm that announces your presence at a speaking-post before you listen in on a message not intended for you. The last one matters. Listening at a post without announcing yourself is the closest thing Aunu has to an actual crime.

Practice the rhythms on a stone before you arrive. Practice until they are in your wrist, not your head. In the water, with the soft *thum* of the rock, you will not have time to think.

## The Long Animal

You will perhaps see it. Do not look directly. Do not follow its passage with your head. If someone near you makes the wrist gesture — a slow inward rotation, fingers loose — mirror it, and drop your gaze to the silt. This is not superstition; or if it is, it is the kind of superstition that is indistinguishable from courtesy. The locals have lived with this creature for many generations and have arrived at an arrangement with it. You are a guest in that arrangement. Do not renegotiate.

## The Thirteenth Terrace

Know where it is before you go below it. Count as you descend. Below the thirteenth, do not brace yourself against walls, do not kneel, do not sit on anything but the prepared benches (which are made of older, harder stone brought down from higher up). If you trip and leave a mark, tell someone. The mark will need to be tended — covered with a poultice of silt and plant pith that encourages the rock to resume its slow growth. Pretending it did not happen is worse than the act itself.

## The Dead in the Walls

You will, if you stay more than a few days, likely be shown a convexity in someone's wall and told whose shape it is. The appropriate response is to place your palm flat against it for the length of a slow breath, and then to withdraw. Do not ask how long the reabsorption takes. Do not ask what the person was like. If your host wishes to tell you, they will, in their own time, perhaps by tapping to you the same message they tap to the dead each morning.

## The Bright Sleep

If you are fortunate enough to be in Aunu during the twelve-year flowering of the ribbon-groves, you will know beforehand — the city grows noticeably more silent in the days leading up to it, as if everyone is saving their attention. On that night, stand in a doorway. Do not go out into the spore-snow. Do not speak. Do not tap. Do not move. The inhabitants will be doing the same. It will last until the spores settle, perhaps six hours. Your legs will ache. Bear it. This is the one night the city permits itself to be watched, and your watching, if it is still enough, is welcome.

## Pacing Yourself Emotionally

Aunu is slow, and the slowness is not a surface feature — it goes all the way down. Travelers frequently report, after returning, a period of several weeks in which the outside universe feels grating and over-bright, in which ordinary conversations feel like shouting, in which the speed of things feels vulgar. This is normal. It passes. Some travelers come to miss it badly enough that they return, and a few have stayed, though staying is a long negotiation and requires, among other things, eventually accepting that one's own shape will someday be pressed into a wall and slowly reabsorbed by the city. If this is appealing to you, be cautious: Aunu attracts a certain kind of visitor who mistakes quietness for peace, and the two are not the same.

## A Final Instruction

When you leave, do not look back at the city from outside, from the higher water, as you ascend. Aunu has agreed not to notice its own edges, and by extension, the edges of itself-seen-from-outside. The courtesy is mutual. Leave as you would leave a sleeping person's room: turn, and go, and close the thought of it softly behind you. You will remember it better that way, and the city, in some way that does not quite make sense in ordinary physics but makes complete sense in Aunu, will be more likely to still be there the next time someone goes looking.

Travel well. Travel slowly. Travel quietly.
