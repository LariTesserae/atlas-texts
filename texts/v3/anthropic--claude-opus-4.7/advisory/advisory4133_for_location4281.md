# Preparing to Visit the Hollow in the Rock

You are not going to enter this place. Understand that first, before anything else. The chamber is the size of a deer's skull, and even if you could shrink, you would be the catastrophe it has been spared for however long it has been holding itself. You are going to approach it, find it, put your eye to the slit, and leave. That is the entire visit. Prepare accordingly.

## What You Are Actually Doing

This is a pilgrimage, not an exploration. The thing you are traveling to see cannot be touched, entered, sampled, photographed in any way that will capture it, or meaningfully shared. You will look into a crack in a rock for some minutes, and then you will walk back down a ridge. If this sounds insufficient to justify crossing universes for, do not go. The hollow does not need you, will not be improved by you, and will not remember you. You are going for your own reasons, and you should be honest with yourself about what those are before you set out.

## Physical Preparation

**The approach.** You are climbing to a high ridge. Standard mountain gear for a temperate or cold dry climate — layered clothing, proper boots broken in over weeks, a light pack. Do not bring anything heavy. You will want your hands free and your breathing unencumbered when you reach the slit, because the last thing you want is to be panting into the opening.

**Your face.** This is the instrument of your visit. Your eye is going to the slit; your cheek will touch lichened stone. Prepare your skin. Do not shave that morning — a fresh nick is a form of contamination you will regret in a place that has never known blood. Trim your eyelashes if they are long enough to brush the opening. Clean your face with plain water the night before and let it dry. No creams, no sunscreen near the eyes, no oils. The thin coolness that will come out of the slit is paper-dry; you do not want to meet it with anything that can evaporate into it.

**Your breath.** You will need to hold it, or breathe sideways, while your eye is to the opening. Practice this. The chamber refuses sound and will presumably refuse your exhalation as well, but do not test the hypothesis. A warm humid breath into a place that has not known water in geological time is the kind of small, casual desecration that a traveler of taste does not commit. Learn to turn your head and breathe past your shoulder without moving your eye from what it is watching.

**Your eye itself.** Bring saline drops, in case the dryness of the leaking air troubles you. Remove contact lenses for the visit; wear glasses if you need them, and be ready to tilt them carefully to get close to the slit. If you have the option, come with well-rested eyes. You are going to be asking a lot of your vision, in low-contrast conditions, at unusual focal distances, looking at things smaller than grains of rice.

## What to Bring, What Not to Bring

**Bring:**
- Water, for yourself, kept sealed and far from the slit
- A soft cloth for your face
- A small hand lens or loupe, 10x, for after you have seen the chamber with your unaided eye — and only after. The first look should be the honest one.
- A notebook and pencil, for writing down what you saw, later, somewhere else. Not at the site.
- Time. More than you think you need. Budget an entire day at the ridge even though the visit itself will take minutes.

**Do not bring:**
- Any camera or recording device you intend to use at the slit. Whatever you capture will be wrong, and the act of capturing will displace the act of seeing. If you must carry a camera for the rest of the trip, keep it in your pack at the ridge.
- Flowers, seeds, spores, pollen — check your clothes and boots. Launder everything before you travel, and again before you climb. A single alien spore introduced into that chamber through the slit could, in the slow arithmetic of the place, eventually overrun it. This is the most important rule. Your boots should not have walked through another forest that week.
- Food. Eat before you climb, and well below the ridge.
- Incense, perfume, scented anything. The chamber has no smell. Leave yours behind.
- Offerings. You will be tempted, especially once you see the chip of stone on the floor, to leave something of your own. Do not. The chip has been there since before the forest. Your token would be an insult on a timescale you cannot appreciate.

## Mental Preparation

**Scale.** Spend time, before you go, looking at small things. Not through a microscope — that is a different kind of looking. Sit in ordinary moss and let your eyes adjust to finding the ferns inside the ferns. Get used to the idea that a whole landscape can exist in a space your thumb could cover. The hollow will ask you to do this at a pitch you have never done it, and if you come unpracticed you will see only "greenness" and miss the generations nested inside.

**Stillness.** The chamber does not move. Nothing in it has moved in any span of time meaningful to you. Your eye, in contrast, is a twitching, saccading thing that has never held still in its life. Practice soft focus. Practice looking at something for ten minutes without tracking. You will not succeed, but the attempt will change what you are able to see when you do look.

**Silence.** The silence inside is perfect and will not leak out to you, but you should leak as little of your own noise in. Climb the last part of the ridge slowly. Arrive with your heart rate low. Do not speak at the site, not even to yourself. If you are traveling with a companion, agree in advance that the final approach and the visit itself are conducted without speech, and that you will debrief only once you are back down.

**Expectation.** You have read a description. The description is accurate as far as descriptions go, and it will be wrong in every way that matters. The color you are expecting from "blue-lavender where green would be expected" is not the color you will see. The size you are imagining for the ferns is not their size. Let the description go before you look. Arrive empty.

## The Visit Itself

Find the slit. This will take longer than you expect. The description says you would have to know to look for it, and even knowing, you will pass it twice. The groove in the rock is long and shallow and the slit is a detail within it. When you find it, sit down. Do not put your eye to it immediately.

Sit with the outside of the rock for a while. Feel the lichen. Understand that this surface and the surface inside are continuous — the same stone, a few centimeters apart, but one side has known ten thousand winters of wind and the other has known none. The slit is the only place where these two histories meet. You are about to put your eye at that meeting.

When you are ready, lean in. Steady your head against the rock so your eye does not drift. Close the other eye. Breathe out first, away, and then hold.

Look.

Do not try to see everything. You cannot. Pick one frond and follow it to where it meets the moss, and follow the moss to where it meets the wall, and follow the wall until your eye finds the next smaller generation of green inside the last. Let the light be what it is. Do not try to locate its source; there isn't one.

When your eye waters or your neck aches, pull back. Breathe. You can look again. You can look as many times as you want. But know that after the third or fourth look, you will begin to feel that you are taking something by looking — not taking it away, the chamber is too full for that — but taking something into yourself that the chamber would have preferred to keep. At that point, stop.

## Leaving

Do not linger at the slit once you are done. The forest has been accommodating a flat chip of stone for longer than any witness; it does not need to accommodate you. Walk back down. Do not look back at the ridge from below — the hollow cannot be seen from any distance, and trying to locate it with your eyes will only teach you that it is already gone from you.

Write down what you saw once you are in your tent, or your inn, or your home universe. Write it for yourself. You will find that the description flattens almost immediately into something that sounds like the description you read before you went, and you will understand, then, why such descriptions are the best anyone can do.

## A Final Note

The five rules the place states about itself — *light stays, water never comes, nothing moves quickly, nothing leaves, the green keeps finding smaller rooms inside itself to fill* — are not just rules for the chamber. Consider them rules for your visit as well. Let what you see stay with you as light. Do not weep into the slit. Do not hurry. Do not take anything out, not even in the form of a photograph or a claim. And let whatever you learn there keep finding smaller rooms inside you to fill, for as long as you live. That is the appropriate pace at which to have been there.
