# Preparing to Visit the City of Lenslike Rain

## Before You Go: Calibrating Your Expectations

The most important preparation is psychological, not material. This is a place where the ordinary physical intuitions you carry in your body — about how shadows work, how rain falls, how towers stand — will be gently and continuously wrong. You need to arrive having already surrendered the expectation that you will understand what you are seeing. Travelers who try to force explanations tend to leave exhausted and irritable; travelers who let the strangeness settle around them without resistance tend to leave changed in ways they value.

Spend a week before departure practicing *not explaining things*. Watch clouds without naming their types. Sit in rooms without cataloging them. The city rewards a certain quality of attention that most of us have trained out of ourselves.

## Clothing and Palette

Pack in deep indigos, umbers, dusty plums, charcoals, the browns of wet bark, the greens of moss after rain. Leave behind anything in saturated primary colors, anything white, anything that reflects strongly. This is not a formal rule imposed by residents — it is simply that in the city's muted, haze-filtered light, bright colors look *wrong* in a way that will make you feel wrong wearing them. You will become the garish thing in a landscape of careful tones, and this is tiring.

Fabrics should be soft. The papery sound of the brass leaves sets the acoustic register of the whole basin; synthetic fabrics that rustle or squeak will make you, and everyone near you, aware of your foreignness. Wool, linen, worn cotton, soft leather.

Bring a hooded outer layer even though you will not need it for warmth. The rain-columns are localized but not entirely predictable, and while residents have an intuitive sense of where a forming lens will discharge, you will not. Being caught in a column is not dangerous — it is brief — but the water is somehow more *concentrated* than ordinary rain and will soak you completely in under a second.

## Footwear

Soft-soled. The paving-stones are old and slightly dished from wear, the grass between them is fine and easily damaged, and the moss rings around discharge points are considered — not sacred exactly, but *tended*. You do not walk across a moss ring for the same reason you do not walk across someone's writing desk. Boots with hard heels will announce you from a plaza away and mark you as someone who does not understand.

## Timekeeping

Leave your watch behind, or at least stop consulting it. The lagging shadows will ruin any attempt to reconcile sun-time with clock-time, and residents do not schedule by hours in the way you are used to. They schedule by sequences: *the third hum after the market opens*, *two discharges past when the west tower's shadow reaches the long stone*. You will not master this system in a short visit, but you should stop fighting it. Agree to meet people by landmark and sequence rather than by time.

A small notebook is useful for tracking your own sequences, if only to avoid being hours early or late to things.

## Sound and Sleep

The subsonic tone from the towers will affect you from the first night. Some travelers find it immediately soothing; others find it disquieting for two or three days before it becomes invisible. Neither response is wrong. Do not bring earplugs — blocking the tone while sleeping in the city produces headaches and unpleasant dreams, reports consistent enough to be taken seriously. The tone wants to be heard, or rather, your body wants to hear it once it has started.

Be warned: when you leave, you will sleep poorly for weeks. This is the most common and most underestimated cost of visiting. The absence of the tone is felt as a subtraction, a missing organ. Plan your return schedule with this in mind. Do not fly home and immediately attend to demanding work. Give yourself a buffer of unstructured days.

## The Absence of Birds

If you come from a world with birds — most travelers do — prepare yourself for the specific disorientation of their absence. It is not quiet, exactly; the city has its sounds. But there is a register of small chatter, of overhead movement, of life-at-the-edges, that is simply missing. Many travelers report becoming aware, only in this city, of how much of their sense of *being outdoors in a living place* was carried by birds. The first two days can feel eerie or even grief-tinged. This passes. What replaces it is an attentiveness to quieter things — the moths, the beetles, the tock of rain-columns, the dry shuffle of brass leaves — that is one of the city's real gifts.

## What to Bring to Eat and Drink

Very little. The city has food — the seed-markets, small groves that produce a kind of soft-fleshed fruit that tastes of honey and iron, vendors in the low buildings of otherwise empty plazas — and eating what residents eat is part of attuning to the place. Bring emergency rations in case of dietary trouble, but do not plan meals in advance. Bring a water bottle; the rain, if you catch some deliberately in a cup under a forming lens, is drinkable and faintly sweet, though residents find it slightly gauche to drink the lens-rain, the way we might find it gauche to drink from a decorative fountain.

## Interacting With Residents

Speak softly. Move slowly across plazas. Do not point at the towers; it is not taboo, it is simply that pointing at the towers marks you immediately as someone who has not yet realized that the towers are not the interesting thing — they are the *condition* for the interesting things. Residents will be patient with you, but their patience is a resource you should not squander.

Questions about how the towers work will be answered with a shrug or a vague account of precipitation-from-earlier-states. This is not evasion. Nobody knows. Asking repeatedly, or pressing for mechanism, is like asking a fish to explain water. Accept the shrug and move on.

A useful opening question, if you want to talk with someone: ask where the nearest humming column is, and whether it has a name. Many of them do. The names are local, often affectionate, sometimes personal. This question signals that you have understood what the columns are *for* — meeting points, social infrastructure — rather than treating them as curiosities.

## The Canopy Creature

You will, at some point, see movement in the brass canopy — something long-limbed and pale, reaching rather than leaping. Do not stare at it directly. It seems to prefer the peripheral and may withdraw if looked at frontally. It is not dangerous, as far as anyone knows, but it is also not entirely understood, and residents regard it with a kind of careful indifference that you should model. Acknowledge it by not acknowledging it.

Do not attempt to photograph it. Do not attempt to photograph much of anything, actually — cameras work here but the images come out subtly wrong, the colors off, the shadows in positions that make the photographs uncomfortable to look at later. Bring a small sketchbook instead. Drawing forces the slow attention the city rewards.

## Navigating the Sparseness

The city's emptiness is its most under-prepared-for feature. You will walk for twenty minutes across a plaza and encounter no one. You will pass a single low building surrounded by half a kilometer of paving and grass. This is not desolation — the city is inhabited, the plazas are in use, you are simply encountering the characteristic density, which is very low. Bring a capacity for long slow walking without destination-pressure. Bring the patience to sit on a standing column for an hour and listen to it hum.

If this sounds unbearable, do not come. The city is not for you, and you will make yourself and everyone around you unhappy trying to extract a denser experience from it than it contains.

## A Final Preparation

Before you sleep on your last night, go to a plaza you have come to know, and sit near a discharge circle, and wait for a lens to form overhead. Watch the canopy magnify through it. Listen for the tock. Do this deliberately, as a farewell, because when you are back in your own world — sleepless, for the first weeks, from the missing tone — you will want to be able to remember the exact quality of that moment, and memory serves better when the moment was attended to on purpose.

Bring something small back with you if you can: a fallen brass leaf, a pebble from the edge (not the center) of a moss ring, a sketch. Not as a souvenir but as an anchor. On the hard nights after, you will hold it, and the absence of the tone will feel slightly less like an amputation and slightly more like a distance you might, someday, cross again.
