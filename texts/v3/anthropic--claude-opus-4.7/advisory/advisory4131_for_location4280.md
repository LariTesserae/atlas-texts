# Preparing to Visit the Cleft

## Before You Go

**Accept that this is a one-visit location.** Do not pack as though you are gathering data for a return trip. Do not bring recording equipment expecting to build a portfolio. The mountain will not let you come back, not out of malice but out of its own unfinished geology. Bring the mindset of someone attending a deathbed or a birth — somewhere you go once, fully, and then carry only in memory.

**Do your grieving in advance.** You will want to bring something back and you will not be able to. Whatever you love most about being a witness — photographs, specimens, notes written in the moment — make peace with losing it here. The room may permit you to carry out memory, and nothing else. Some travelers find it useful, before such trips, to write down what they expect to want to keep, and then burn the paper. This is not superstition; it is practice at the gesture.

## Clothing and Body

**Dress for two climates in one step.** The approach is high-altitude, thin-aired, cold, and the rock will cut you if you brace against it badly. Wear sturdy gloves for the climb and remove them at the threshold. Inside, it is body-warm and humid; you will sweat in mountaineering layers. Wear something underneath that you can be still in for hours without fidgeting — fidgeting costs the room.

**Nails trimmed, hair tied, no loose jewelry.** The foliage is dense enough to catch on anything that protrudes, and you do not want to tear a frond. You are a guest in an upholstered room; move like one.

**Eat lightly beforehand, and well before.** Digestion is loud in a quiet place. You will hear your own stomach. More importantly, you do not want to need to relieve yourself inside — there is no way to do so without spending sound-budget the room cannot easily repay, and no way to do so that is not a desecration of a place where water does not complete its falls.

**Hydrate before, not during.** Do not drink from the walls. The water there is in a closed loop with itself and has been for longer than the mountain has existed properly. It is not yours to borrow from.

## What to Bring

- **A single light source you will not need to use.** Daylight pools there. Bring a small torch for the approach and for emergencies, but understand that switching it on inside would be like striking a match in a cathedral during the consecration. Keep it in a pocket, off.
- **A handkerchief or soft cloth.** For your own face — you will sweat, and wiping sweat with a sleeve is a rougher sound than you'd think. Also useful if you weep, which is common here and not shameful.
- **Nothing sharp.** Leave knives, multi-tools, trekking poles at the threshold. The young stone outside is already sharp enough to teach you about edges. Inside, anything capable of cutting is an insult to a place that has chosen not to complete its violences.
- **No offerings.** This is counterintuitive. Many travelers feel the urge to leave something on the shelf beside the bowl, the bundle, and the stone face. Do not. Those three objects are in a specific ongoing relationship with an absent maker, and any fourth object disrupts the composition. The room's oldest condition is the maker's imminent return; you are not the maker and must not audition for the role.
- **A name, memorized.** Someone you love. Carry it silently. You may find yourself wanting to spend your one syllable on something, and this is better than most things you could say.

## Protocols Inside

**Enter on an exhale.** The cleft's air is a held breath; do not fight it with a fresh lungful of thin mountain air. Breathe out as you cross, and let the chamber's warmth be your next inhalation. This also settles your pulse, which will otherwise be audible to you for the first several minutes.

**Sit down early and do not stand up again until you leave.** Find a place near — but not touching — the green wall. Lower yourself slowly. Standing humans take up too much vertical budget in a twelve-pace room; seated, you become furniture, and the room treats furniture kindly.

**Do not test the rules.** The beetle walks past the top of the stem into air that holds it. You will want to try — to drop something small, to flick a pebble, to see if the air holds you too. It may. That is not the danger. The danger is that you will have spent a large portion of the room's sound-and-causality budget on a stunt, and the room will recover that budget from somewhere, and you will not know from where, and you may later suspect it was recovered from you.

**Look at the three objects, but do not pick them up.** Spend as long as you like looking. Notice the tools: the flake, the smoothing pebble, the bone awl. Notice that they are set down as if a moment ago. Feel the urge to continue the work. The urge is correct and you must not act on it. Someone else is finishing the face. You are the someone else, too, in a sense — every visitor is — and the face is finished precisely because none of you do it.

**If you must speak, choose the word before you arrive.** One syllable. The room will repay the silence, but the interval of repayment will hollow you out a little, and you will feel for some hours afterward that you have overdrawn an account you did not know you had. Most experienced travelers do not speak. A few say a name. One, by report, said *yes*, and would not explain to what.

**Let the light rest on your palm at least once.** This is the memory that survives best. Hold your hand flat, palm up, in the shaft from the fissure. Wait. The weight settles. It is the specific weight of a moth choosing you. Do not close your hand.

## Leaving

**Do not look back from the threshold.** Not for ceremonial reasons — for practical ones. The cleft is already not in the same place it was when you entered. Looking back will show you a different arrangement and you will doubt your memory of the first, and you need the first memory intact. It is the one you are allowed to keep.

**Re-enter the outside slowly.** The thin cold air will hit like a slap. The sharpness of the young feldspar, after the upholstered green, is genuinely disorienting; people have cut themselves badly in the first ten minutes after leaving, because their hands forget what edges are. Put your gloves back on before you touch anything.

**Descend before dark.** The mountain is still deciding its shape, and it decides more vigorously at night. Rockfall is real here in a way it is not in older ranges.

## Afterward

**Expect a period of mourning that does not correspond to anything you lost.** You did not lose anything. You were not harmed. You will nevertheless feel, for weeks, that something is unfinished in you — a small face, perhaps, in a stone you cannot locate. This is correct. The feeling is not a wound; it is a correspondence. You are now in the same condition as the room: almost-complete, and held in that almost by something larger that has chosen not to close.

**Do not try to describe it to people who did not go.** You will try anyway. You will fail. They will be kind about it. The failure is part of carrying the place properly — the room resists being finished in language the same way the stone face resists being finished in stone. Let the description stay soft at the rim. Let the binding stay green.

**Do not search for the cleft again.** Some travelers spend years. The mountain is not hiding it from you out of cruelty. The cleft was never in quite the same place twice, and you were never in quite the same place twice either, and the meeting was an accident the mountain made and could not bring itself to close. One such accident, per person, is already extraordinary grace.

**Remember the weight of the light.** That is the part you are allowed to keep. That, and the knowledge — which will steady you in other rooms, in other weathers, for the rest of your life — that somewhere a bowl is staying soft, a binding is staying green, and a face is waiting to be finished by someone who is always about to arrive.

You are, now, among the someones. Carry it lightly. Almost-complete is a good way to be.
