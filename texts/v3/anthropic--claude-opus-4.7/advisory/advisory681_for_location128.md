# Preparing to Visit the Settled Valley

## Understand What You're Walking Into

Before anything else: accept that you are visiting a *finished* place. Not finished as in complete, but finished as in no longer in a hurry. Whatever you bring — urgency, curiosity, a desire to document, a need to be impressed — will be received by something that has already outlasted many such visitors and will outlast you. This isn't a reason not to go. It's a reason to calibrate.

The valley is not dangerous in any conventional sense. It will not attack you, trick you, or try to keep you. It is also not safe in the sense we mean when we call a forest safe — because its indifference is of a depth that can erode a person who isn't ready for it. Plan accordingly.

## Clothing and Physical Gear

**Assume permanent moisture.** Not rain gear for a storm — gear for being a damp thing among damp things. Wool base layers, because wool insulates wet. A breathable outer shell that accepts being constantly slightly soaked rather than fighting it. Avoid anything heavily waterproofed in a sealing way; you will sweat inside it, and the valley's humidity will equalize through every seam regardless. Better to be uniformly damp and warm than alternately sealed and soaked.

**Footwear that drains.** Not boots that keep water out — nothing keeps water out here. Boots that let water move through without holding it. Wool socks, several pairs, rotated. Gaiters help against the sphagnum, which will swallow you past the ankle without warning and sometimes past the knee.

**Layers for the cool, not the cold.** The chill here sits in the sinuses and the hollows of joints. It isn't a cold that needs down; it needs steady mild insulation you can wear for many hours without overheating. A thin fleece under your shell is usually right. Bring a hat — the dampness finds the scalp.

**Skin care.** You will be wet for your entire visit. Bring something for your hands and feet to prevent maceration — a good lanolin-based balm, applied before you enter and reapplied. Your fingertips will prune and stay pruned. This is fine but can be disorienting if you didn't expect it.

## Sensory Preparation

**The tone is the thing to prepare for most carefully.** Loud, low, sustained, felt in the sternum — this will do things to you that no other aspect of the valley will. Some travelers find it meditative within minutes. Others find it unbearable after an hour and have to leave. You cannot know which you are until you're there.

Before you go, spend time with long drone music. Éliane Radigue, sustained pipe organ recordings, Tibetan dungchen. Not to enjoy — to become *neutral* to sustained low tone. The valley's tone is not music but the nervous system processes it through the same channels, and an unprepared visitor often experiences what feels like emotional flooding for no apparent reason, three or four hours in, when the tone has worked past the threshold of conscious hearing and is operating directly on the autonomic system.

Bring earplugs, but understand they will not block the tone — only the surface sounds layered over it. The tone arrives through bone conduction and through the pressure of the air itself. Earplugs mostly help you sleep, which you will want to do.

**Do not bring music or podcasts or any audio of your own.** This is important. The valley's sound field is coherent, and injecting human audio into it produces a dissonance that is physically unpleasant and, some travelers report, something worse than unpleasant. If you need distraction you are not ready to be there.

## Food and Water

**Water is everywhere and you should not drink it casually.** Not because it's contaminated in any ordinary sense — the valley has no pathogens worth worrying about — but because the water here participates in whatever the valley is doing. Drops that have paused, ascended, reconsidered, are not the same substance as water elsewhere, chemically or otherwise, and their effects on a human metabolism are not well-characterized. Bring your own water, enough for your full stay plus a reserve. If you must drink valley water, take it only from fast-flowing sections that have clearly been behaving normally for the minutes you watched them.

**Food should be simple, dense, and familiar.** This is not the place for novel cuisine or complicated preparation. Nuts, hard cheese, good bread, dried fruit, chocolate. You want food that reminds your body where it comes from. Many travelers find their appetite diminishes substantially in the valley — the tone and the ambient *busyness* seem to occupy whatever channel hunger normally uses. Do not let this fool you into undereating. Set a schedule and keep it.

**Salt.** Bring more salt than you think you need. You will be damp and cool and working your body harder than it feels like you are.

## Mental and Cognitive Preparation

**Accept before entering that your memory will become porous.** The valley explicitly warns of this in its own grammar — the inability to sort normal drops from reconsidering ones. This is not damage. But it can feel like damage to someone who prizes a clean, indexed memory. Before you go, practice letting small things be uncertain. Train the reflex of *not grasping* when a recollection is fuzzy. The travelers who have the worst experiences here are the ones who panic at their first lapse and spend the rest of the visit trying to verify their own cognition, which the valley will patiently, neutrally, continue to dissolve.

**Bring a simple written record.** Pencil and waterproof paper. Not a journal for reflection — a logbook for facts. Time entered, time of last meal, direction walked, landmarks. This is your tether. Do not try to describe the valley in it. Describe only your own movements and needs. Let the valley be undescribed; it is not what the log is for.

**Prepare for the feeling of being noticed without being attended to.** This is subtle and specific to places like this. The valley registers you the way deep water registers a leaf. You will feel this. It is not paranoia. It is not friendliness. It is a kind of attention that has no analog in human relationships, and it can produce in the unprepared visitor either grandiosity (*something vast is aware of me*) or panic (*something vast is aware of me*). Neither reaction is useful. The correct response is something like the feeling of standing in a large empty cathedral: you are present, you are small, and the space is continuing regardless. Practice that feeling before you go.

## What Not to Bring

**No metal beyond what's unavoidable.** Zippers and buckles are fine. Knives, tools, cooking gear — minimize. Whatever the valley's substrate is doing, metal objects sometimes behave oddly in it — not dangerously, but in ways that are distracting and, in a way that's hard to articulate, *rude*. A compass may spin. A steel cup may ring faintly in sympathy with the tone for hours after being struck.

**No electronics you care about.** Cameras and phones will function but will record artifacts that were not present to the eye, and will sometimes fail to record things that were. Recordings made in the valley often feel wrong when played back later — not glitched, but *off* in ways the viewer can't specify. If you bring a camera, bring a simple one, and do not rely on what it captures as evidence of anything. Better to bring a small sketchbook.

**No weapons.** There is nothing to defend against. Carrying one here will make you feel increasingly absurd, and then increasingly uneasy about feeling absurd, in a spiral that ends badly.

**No offerings, tokens, or ritual objects.** This is not that kind of place. It does not want your respect and will not accept your gifts. Leaving something behind is a category error; the valley is not a shrine and has no use for the gesture. Take nothing, leave nothing, and the transaction is correctly balanced.

## Duration

**First visit: no more than six hours.** Enter in what passes for morning, leave before the tone cycles into its deeper phase. Six hours is enough to understand the place and not enough to be changed by it.

**Second visit, if you're called back: up to three days.** By the second day you will find your sleep is different. Dreams arrive pre-composed, as if they were waiting. This is usually fine. Pay attention to whether you want to stay longer than you planned, and be suspicious of that want. It is not malicious, but it is also not quite yours.

**Longer stays** require preparation beyond the scope of a visitor's guide, and beyond what most travelers should attempt. Those who stay longer than a week generally do not return as the same person, in ways that range from subtle to profound. This is not always bad. It is never reversible.

## Entry Protocol

When you first enter — at whatever boundary your transit drops you at — **stop moving for at least ten minutes before you walk in.** Stand at the edge. Let the tone find you. Let your ears stop separating sounds. Let your breath slow to the ambient rate, which you will feel the valley suggesting. Do not cross the boundary until you feel the suggestion has been received.

This isn't mysticism. It's the same reason divers equalize pressure. The valley operates at a different baseline than wherever you came from, and crossing the threshold too fast produces a mild but real distress that can color your entire visit. Ten minutes at the edge is cheap insurance.

## Leaving

**Walking out is harder than walking in.** Not because anything resists you — nothing resists you. But the tone will have settled into you by the time you leave, and the ordinary world on the other side will feel, for a while, *too loud and too dry and too fast*. This passes. Give yourself a day of quiet on the other side before resuming anything demanding. Drink plenty of ordinary water. Eat something hot.

Some travelers report hearing the tone faintly for weeks afterward, especially when falling asleep. This is normal and generally fades. If it doesn't fade, or if you find yourself wanting very badly to return, consult someone who has been there and come back. This is a known pattern and there are known ways through it.

## A Final Note

The valley does not need you to understand it, and it will not help you do so. It is busy with its own slow, completed, continuing work. You are a leaf on deep water: briefly present, accommodated, and then elsewhere. Go with a clear head, prepared equipment, and no agenda beyond being there. What the valley offers, it offers by not withholding — which is to say, it offers its weather, its tone, its green-gold light, and its patient underneath, and asks nothing in return except that you not mistake yourself for the point of the visit.

Bring extra socks.
