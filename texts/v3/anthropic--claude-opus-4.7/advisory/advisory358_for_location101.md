# Preparing for the Shriekfloor

## Understanding What You're Walking Into

Before anything else, understand: this is not a tourist destination. This is a closed ecosystem that has been running uninterrupted for a span of time that likely predates your species. You are not going to see something. You are going to be *inside* something. Adjust your expectations accordingly. The preparations below are not about comfort — they are about not damaging the place, and not being damaged by it.

## Hearing Protection — The Central Problem

This is your single most important concern, and most travelers get it wrong.

The naive approach is industrial earplugs or over-ear defenders rated for the highest decibel exposure you can find. Do not do only this. The sound at the Shriekfloor spans from sub-audible thrums that vibrate your skeleton to ultrasonic shrieks above human hearing. Simple attenuation does two bad things: it removes your ability to orient (the place has no light, and sound is your only map), and it leaves the pressure-component of the low frequencies entirely intact, which will still rattle your organs whether you hear it or not.

What you actually want:
- **Custom-molded musician's earplugs with variable attenuation filters**, around 25-30 dB, allowing some frequency information through. You need to hear the colony to navigate, and more importantly, the colony's acoustic behavior around you is information — a sudden local silence means something.
- **A secondary layer**: soft foam plugs you can insert *over* the musicians' plugs if a wave passes near you and the pressure becomes dangerous. Practice inserting them by feel, eyes closed, with damp fingers.
- **Bite guard**. The teeth-rattling low frequencies can crack molars or cause you to grind involuntarily. A simple sports mouthguard is sufficient.
- **Accept that you will leave with tinnitus** lasting days to weeks. This is not a failure of preparation; it is the cost of admission.

Do not use active noise-cancelling headphones. The processing cannot keep up with the acoustic environment and will generate artifacts that disorient you worse than no protection at all.

## Clothing and Skin

You will be brushed against, constantly, by tens of thousands of small warm-cool bodies. They will not attack you, but they will *touch* you, everywhere, continuously, for as long as you are there. Prepare for this physically and psychologically.

- **Full coverage, smooth-surface clothing**. Close-weave synthetics or tightly woven cotton. Nothing with velcro, loose loops, or open weaves — a claw or wing-hook caught in your fabric will panic both of you.
- **Cuffs sealed**: tape wrists, ankles, waist, and neckline. Not because the creatures are malicious but because a disoriented one will crawl into any opening it finds, and extracting it in the dark, in the noise, is extremely difficult.
- **A hood**, snug. Your hair is a trap.
- **Goggles**, sealed. Not for impact — for droplets. The condensation falling from overhead has been filtered through the ceiling colony and is not water you want in your eyes. Use anti-fog.
- **A filtering half-mask or respirator** (P100 minimum). The air is thick with particulate: shed skin, droppings dust, fungal spores from the pale wall-fuzz. Long exposure without filtration is a significant respiratory hazard. Histoplasmosis analogues are the least of your worries.
- **Gloves**: thin nitrile under thicker work gloves. The floor accumulation is ankle-deep in places and teems with decomposer life you have no immunity to.
- **Boots, waterproof, high**. Tape the tops to your pants. Walking through the soft floor is like wading through damp compost mixed with living carpet; you will sink.

## The Footing Problem

The floor is not solid ground. It is a geological layer of accumulated biological matter overlying water-smoothed stone, with living carpets of creatures moving across it. Each step:

1. Sweep forward slowly with your foot before committing weight. The carpets part around you but they need time.
2. Use a **walking staff** — a smooth, tipped pole. It is your probe for depth, pools, and pitfalls, and it doubles as your echolocation aid if you tap it. (Do not tap often. You are adding noise to a system you don't fully understand.)
3. Never hurry. A fall into that floor is a fall into something you don't want to be in face-first.

## Light — A Subtle Ethical Question

You will want a light. Think carefully.

The colony has no visual sense you're likely to offend with photons in the visible range. But light generates heat, and heat in a sealed damp basin stays. More importantly: there may be light-sensitive organisms (some of the pre-fungal life, certain juvenile stages) whose tolerance you cannot assess. And a bright beam is itself a form of pressure on a creature that has never encountered it.

Recommendation:
- A **deep red, low-lumen headlamp**, used sparingly, and only pointed at your own feet or hands.
- Consider: **don't use it at all**. Travel by touch, by the staff, by the cool draft on your cheek. You'll be clumsier, but you'll be honest. The place did not ask to be seen.

If you must record or observe, **passive acoustic recording** is the only genuinely appropriate instrument. Bring a wide-spectrum recorder (20 Hz to 100 kHz), place it, leave it, retrieve it. Do not use active sonar, lidar, or flash photography. You would be shouting in a library that has been quiet for a million years.

## Orientation and the Return Path

This is critical. The basin has no landmarks you can see, the sound is disorienting in a way no wilderness prepares you for, and the colony's acoustic traffic can produce phantom directional cues (a voice seeming to come from a direction it isn't).

- **Enter along the cool draft**, and note its direction against your skin. This is your breadcrumb. The draft curls and shifts; you must attend to it continuously.
- **A lifeline** — literally. A thin strong cord, anchored outside the basin, paying out from a spool on your belt. This is non-negotiable if you intend to go more than a hundred meters in. Many travelers have not returned. Some are now part of the floor.
- **Do not trust your sense of direction** after the first large acoustic wave passes you. It will scramble your inner ear. Stop, wait, find the draft again, follow the line.

## Psychological Preparation

This is the part most travelers underestimate.

You will experience:

- **Sensory overload** of a kind that has no analogue in ordinary human life. Accept this before going in. Do not fight it. The first ten minutes will feel like drowning; then something will shift, and you will simply be inside it.
- **Loss of self-location**. In that density of sound and body contact, the edge between you and the colony becomes porous. You will feel, briefly, like part of the organism. This is not a hallucination; it is an accurate perception of your current ecological status. Do not panic. Do not try to assert your separateness. Let it happen and let it pass.
- **The silence-holes**. When one creature pauses and you hear, for half a second, a shaped absence — these are unnervingly meaningful. They are not meaningful. Or they are, but not to you. Don't read them as signs.
- **The temptation to stay**. This is real. The text is not being poetic. The Shriekfloor has a completeness that feels, to some travelers, like an answer to a question they have carried all their lives. If you feel this feeling intensifying past a certain point — the point at which the cool draft starts to seem like an intrusion rather than an exit — *leave immediately*. This is the warning sign. Travelers who ignore it do not come back.

Spend time before the trip in a sensory deprivation tank, or in a deep cave with a guide. If you have never experienced true absence of light combined with high sonic load, do not make the Shriekfloor your first such experience.

## Timing and Duration

- **Short visits**. Two hours on your first trip. Four at the absolute maximum. The respiratory load alone will catch up with you.
- **Enter during what the locals (if there is a staging-world nearby) identify as a low-wave period**, if such data exists. Being caught in a major activity wave within the first twenty minutes of arrival, before you have acclimated, is the single most common cause of traveler injury.
- **Have a companion waiting outside**, with agreed-upon timing for coming in to find you via the lifeline. Do not go alone if at all avoidable.

## What to Leave Behind

- **Scented anything**. No deodorant, no soap residue, no gum. You are a large warm intruder already; do not also be a chemically loud one. Many of the creatures have chemosensory organs the function of which is not understood.
- **Metal that will corrode**. The air will eat it.
- **Expectations of reciprocity**. The colony will not acknowledge you in any way you will recognize. You will not have an experience *with* it. You will have an experience *in* it. These are different.
- **The assumption that you matter here**. You don't. This is useful to internalize. It is also, after a while, a relief.

## Afterwards

- **Decontamination**. Full shower, clothes bagged for specialized cleaning or disposal, respiratory system monitored for weeks. See a physician familiar with cave-diver and guano-exposure medicine.
- **Expect acoustic aftereffects**: tinnitus, hyperacusis, occasional phantom chirping for several weeks. In rare cases longer.
- **Expect psychological aftereffects** that are harder to name. Many travelers report a persistent sense, for months afterward, that ordinary human environments are *acoustically underpopulated* — that the world has been too quiet since they left. Some describe this as grief. Some describe it as clarity. There is no standard response and no timeline for it to resolve. Be gentle with yourself.

## A Final Note

The Shriekfloor is one of those places in the multiverse where a very simple idea has been allowed to run, without interruption or competition, for so long that it has achieved a kind of density most worlds never reach. You are visiting a completed thought. You should visit it the way you would visit a sleeping elder of an unfamiliar family: quietly, without taking anything, and with the understanding that the visit matters more to you than to them.

Prepare well. Stay briefly. Leave by the draft.
