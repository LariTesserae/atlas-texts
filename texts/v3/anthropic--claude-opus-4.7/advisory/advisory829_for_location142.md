# Preparing to Visit the Terraces of Oost-in-Condens

## Understanding What You're Walking Into

Before anything practical, understand this: you are not visiting a city. You are visiting a *metabolism*. The distinction matters because every mistake a traveler makes here comes from treating Oost-in-Condens like a place you move through, rather than a slow organism you are briefly inside. The locals will forgive ignorance. They will not forgive hurry. Hurry is the one thing the city genuinely cannot absorb, and a hurrying outsider reads, to them, roughly the way a wasp in the room reads to you — not dangerous, exactly, but requiring constant low-grade attention until it leaves.

Plan for a minimum stay of two weeks. Anything shorter and you will have spent the entire trip as a wasp.

## Clothing and the Water Problem

The damp is the first thing to solve and the hardest. It is not rain. You cannot wait it out, shelter from it, or dry off between exposures. It is the ambient state of matter. Within an hour of arrival your clothing will be saturated; within a day, anything not specifically designed for the environment will begin to rot or rust.

Bring:

- **Oiled wool** as your base layer. Synthetics feel clever but they chill you in saturated air because they stop trapping warmth once wet through. Wool keeps working. The local animals have independently evolved toward wool-analog solutions; trust the convergence.
- **A waxed canvas or treated-leather outer shell**, hooded, long enough to cover the thighs. Not waterproof — *water-shedding*. True waterproofing traps your own sweat against you and you will arrive at your lodging wetter on the inside than the outside. You want something that sheds the drip and lets your body breathe.
- **At least three changes of everything**, rotated. One set drying near a vent (every building has them), one set worn, one set in reserve. The locals do this automatically. You'll learn the rhythm.
- **Boots with iron hobnails or equivalent**, and a stiff sole. The catwalks are slick with condensation and in places with droppings. Smooth-soled hiking boots will kill you. Leather boots, well-oiled, rewaxed nightly. Bring the wax.
- **A hat with a brim**. Not for sun. For the constant drip from overhangs, and — this is the part travelers don't anticipate — for the bat-moths, which are not aggressive but which will, in their sheer density at mid-elevation, occasionally misjudge a landing. A brim gives them something to correct against that isn't your face.
- **Fingerless gloves, wool**. Full gloves get wet and stay wet. You need your fingertips for handling lamps, coins, door-latches sticky with condensation.

Leave behind anything made of untreated steel (keys, knives, watch mechanisms) unless you enjoy watching them oxidize in real time. Brass, bronze, copper, properly oiled iron, or ceramic. The locals understand this intuitively and build accordingly.

## The Air and Your Lungs

The air is thick — not with altitude-thinness, which the enclosed weather-system somehow buffers, but with moisture, suspended particulates from the foundries, animal dander at density you will not have encountered before, and the faint sweet-metallic smell of the gas network. Most visitors develop a mild cough in the first three days. It passes. If it doesn't pass, or if it becomes wet and productive, leave — some travelers react badly to the spore-load of the lichen-analogs, and this is not a place to develop a lung infection.

Bring a supply of whatever antihistamines work for you. Bring a scarf you can hold over your mouth on the lower terraces near the foundries, where the air gets genuinely difficult. Do not smoke anything — the gas lines leak in small, tolerated ways, and the locals have worked out which corridors are safe and which aren't, and you haven't.

If you have asthma, reconsider this trip entirely. If you proceed, triple your medication supply and tell your lodging-keeper on arrival. They will know what to do. They have seen this before.

## Food, and the Question of What You Are Eating

There are no plants. Read that sentence again and sit with it. Everything you eat here will be animal, fungal-analog, or mineral-cultured. The locals have been doing this for a very long time and their cuisine is sophisticated, varied, and — to a palate raised on plant agriculture — deeply strange.

Expect:

- **Broths and stocks** as the backbone of every meal, rendered from the six-legged grazers and from fish-analogs kept in the cistern systems. These are excellent. Start here.
- **Rendered fats** used the way other cultures use oils. Grazer-tallow is mild; cinder-wren fat is peppery and used sparingly.
- **Cultured mineral crusts**, farmed on dedicated walls, harvested in sheets, eaten pickled or toasted. These provide what functions locally as "vegetables" — minerals, trace elements, texture. They taste like nothing you have a reference for. Chalky, tangy, slightly effervescent. Try them.
- **Eggs**, from multiple species, in enormous variety. This will be your easiest adaptation.
- **No bread, no grains, no fruit, no leaf-vegetables, no coffee, no tea, no wine, no beer.** The beverages are mostly broths, fermented milks from the grazers, and a hot spiced water drawn from specific cistern-levels that the locals believe is medicinal.

Bring a small personal supply of whatever you cannot live without for two weeks — salt is fine there, but if you need caffeine, bring tablets. If you need sugar, bring it. Do not expect to find them.

Your digestion will struggle for the first four or five days. This is universal among visitors. Eat lightly at first. The locals understand and will serve you smaller portions if you mention you've just arrived.

## Money, Exchange, and the Rhythm of Transaction

The economy is slow and favor-based layered over a token currency of small cast-bronze discs. Haggling is not done. Prices are what they are and have been what they are for a long time. Attempting to negotiate is read as an insult not to the seller but to the *price itself*, which has a kind of moral weight here.

Bring something to trade that is genuinely from elsewhere. Not trinkets — the locals are not impressed by novelty for its own sake. Bring *seeds*, if your multiverse permits it; seeds are the single most valuable thing an outsider can carry here, because the concept of a plant is mythological to many of the younger generation and scientifically fascinating to the older. A single dried seed, presented to the right person, will open doors that money cannot. (Understand that it will almost certainly not grow. That is not the point.)

Barring seeds: bring written things. Books, maps, diagrams. The city has its own deep archives but very little from elsewhere, and the archivists — find them early — will extend real hospitality in exchange for documents they can copy.

## Movement, Etiquette, and the Animals

The animals are not your concern and you are not theirs, and this is the thing hardest for visitors to internalize. You do not shoo them. You do not feed them. You do not photograph them in a way that involves sudden motion or flash. You *especially* do not touch them, though some of the dog-sized predators may investigate you, and the correct response is to stand still and let them finish. They will.

Walk on the right side of catwalks. Yield to grazers always — they have right of way everywhere, by ancient arrangement, because they're cleaning the walls and interrupting them means the walls don't get cleaned. If a sheet of bat-moths descends through the corridor you're in, stop moving and wait. It takes thirty seconds. It will pass.

Do not enter the flues. Do not approach the cisterns uninvited. Do not, under any circumstances, disturb the cinder-wrens; the foundrymen's deference to them is not superstition, and you will not be warned twice.

The pace of walking is slower than you're used to. Match it. A visitor striding through a terrace at their home-world pace looks, to locals, exactly as unsettling as someone running full-tilt through a library looks to you.

## The Nine-Second Pulse

You will notice it within an hour and you will stop noticing it within a day, and then on the third day you will notice it again and it will unsettle you. The entire city — lamps, bellows, the faint pressure in your ears, the drip-rhythm in certain corridors — pulses on a nine-to-ten-second cycle. Your body will try to synchronize with it. Let it. Travelers who resist the pulse, who try to maintain their own internal tempo against it, develop headaches and a specific kind of insomnia the locals call *out-of-stepness* and treat with a hot broth and an early night.

The pulse is not sinister. It is simply that you are, for the duration of your visit, a small organ inside a very large slow creature, and the creature has a heartbeat.

## What to Bring Mentally

Patience, obviously, but more specifically: **a tolerance for repetition as meaning**. You will see the same grazer on the same wall every morning. The lamp-lighter will make the same round in the same order. The foundry will cast the same patterns. A visitor from a fast culture feels this as monotony. It is not monotony. It is the city's way of being alive, and once you stop waiting for something new to happen, you begin to notice the texture of what is continuously happening, which is enormous.

Bring a notebook. Waxed paper, pencil not ink. Write slowly. Match the pulse.

Leave behind the expectation of *having seen* Oost-in-Condens when you leave. No one has seen it. The locals who were born there and will die there have seen parts of it. You will have visited one small saturated corner of an old continuous thing, and the correct posture toward that is gratitude, not comprehension.

## Departure

Plan your exit for early in what passes for morning, when the fog is thinnest and the bat-moths have settled into their roosts. Say goodbye to your lodging-keeper formally; they will have grown genuinely fond of you in a quiet way, and the form matters. Leave any remaining supplies behind — waxed canvas, wool, seeds especially. They will be put to use.

You will be damp for a week after you leave, no matter where you go. Some travelers report they are never quite dry again, and mean it as a compliment.
