# Preparing to Visit the Flensed Summit

## The Body

Start with the altitude, because it will kill you first if you don't.

You are going somewhere the air pressure is lower than anywhere a human being has evolved to survive. Not Everest-thin — thinner. Thin enough that the horizon curves. This means you will need, at minimum, a pressure suit, not just supplemental oxygen. A soft suit rated for near-vacuum, with a rebreather system good for at least seventy-two hours, is the floor. Bring two. One will develop a slow leak you won't notice until your fingers go blue, because the plateau is the kind of place where small things go wrong quietly.

Acclimatize beforehand in a hypobaric chamber for at least three weeks. Not to the actual pressure of the plateau — that's not survivable raw — but to the point where the inevitable suit failures give you minutes rather than seconds. The difference is the difference.

Your heart will work harder than it has ever worked, even in the suit. If you are over forty, get an echocardiogram. If you have ever had an unexplained arrhythmia, do not go. The plateau will not kill you dramatically. It will simply allow your body to fail in the normal way, at the normal speed, while the rest of the world continues not to notice.

Pack potassium, magnesium, and a small supply of vasodilators. Pack more water than you think. The thin air will desiccate you through your breath faster than you can drink.

## The Suit's Exterior

Make it matte. Make it grey or pale. Do not wear reflective surfaces, do not wear bright colors, do not wear anything with a logo or a symbol.

This is not because the plateau will react to these things. It is because *you* will. In a landscape that muted, a flash of red on your own sleeve will become, over hours, unbearable. It will be the loudest thing for kilometers, and your eye will keep returning to it, and by the second day you will hate yourself for bringing it.

The stone is pale jade and bone. Match it. Disappear into it a little. You will be happier.

## Instruments

Bring the fewest you can stand to bring.

A camera, yes — though be warned that the auroral sheets do not photograph truthfully, and the tone cannot be recorded by any microphone yet made, and the moths' interiors resolve differently on sensors than they do in the eye. Do not trust your instruments to preserve this place. They will preserve *a* place. It will not be this one.

An altimeter is useless. A compass is unreliable; something about the tech-objects means needles drift in patterns that correlate with the powder-rings in ways nobody has diagrammed successfully. GPS, if your multiverse has it, will not work — the sky is not a sky a satellite would recognize.

Bring a long light staff or walking pole. You will want to touch things without getting close to them. The black ring in particular deserves a probe before a boot.

Do not bring anything you are not prepared to lose. Remember: instruments set down near the tech tend to be somewhere else later. Undamaged, but elsewhere. This is not theft. It is more like the plateau gently setting your coffee on a different coaster. Accept it.

## Navigation

Do not trust the landscape to tell you where you are.

The domes are a few kilometers across and change by a degree over a day's walk. Every direction looks like every other direction. The tech-objects are too sparse to use as landmarks reliably — and besides, you cannot always be sure the spar you saw this morning is in the same place this afternoon.

Bring a tether. An actual physical line, kilometers of high-tensile thread on a spool, anchored to your arrival point. This sounds absurd until you have spent six hours walking and realized that you have no idea which of the seventy visible gentle swells contains the way home. The plateau is not malicious. It is simply too large and too uniform to navigate by memory, and your memory will be compromised by hypoxia besides.

If you do not have a tether, bring a beacon that emits on a frequency you are certain your arrival equipment can find. Test it before you leave.

## Time

Plan for less time than you want to spend.

Everyone who goes to the Flensed Summit wants to stay longer. The quiet gets into you. The slow weather and the soft shadows and the sense of being somewhere unwatched in a way you have never been unwatched before — it is narcotic. People sit down to rest and find that an hour has passed, then two. The hypoxia contributes, but it is not only the hypoxia.

Set a hard timer on your suit. Not a reminder — a timer that will physically begin pumping your emergency return sequence when it reaches zero. Trust your past self's judgment over your present self's. Your present self is going to want to stay and listen for the tone.

Seventy-two hours is plenty. Most travelers report that anything beyond forty-eight begins to erode something in them they cannot name. They come back quieter. Sometimes they come back wrong.

## The Tone

You may hear it. You may not. Most visitors do not.

If you do hear it, do not attempt to locate its source. It does not have one in the sense you mean. Stand still. Do not speak. Do not try to record it (you cannot, and the attempt will distract you from hearing it properly). Let it pass through you. It is not for you, but you are permitted to be present while it happens, and this is a rare permission.

Afterward, look around carefully. Note what has changed. You will not always be able to tell. This is fine.

Some travelers report feeling, for hours after the tone, that they have been mildly and impersonally blessed. Others report the opposite: a sense of having been audited and found immaterial. Both reports are probably correct. The tone is not about you.

## The Moths

Do not touch them. Not because they are dangerous — they are almost certainly not — but because you do not know what scale of event a touch would constitute for them, and you should not introduce events on scales you do not understand into places you do not own.

If one drifts near you, hold still. Let it pass. If you see through its body to the stone beyond and notice the geometric correspondence to the powder-rings, do not say anything out loud. Just note it. There is a strong human urge, in that moment, to speak — to name the resemblance, to make it real by witnessing it aloud. Resist. The plateau is a place where things are quieter than their names. Honor that.

## The Tech

Treat the objects the way you would treat sleeping strangers in a language you do not speak.

Walk around them, not over them. The black ring is flush with the ground and looks walkable. Do not walk on it. Nothing bad will happen — probably — but you will have crossed a threshold you had no invitation to cross, and the plateau's indifference to you is a gift, and you should not waste it by demanding to be noticed.

The spar that casts no shadow is the one most travelers find hardest. It seems to invite touch. It seems almost to want it. Do not touch it. If you must, touch it with the staff, briefly, and watch carefully what the staff does for the next several hours.

The powder-ring depression is safe to approach. You may disturb the powder with a gloved finger if you wish. Watch it return to its pattern. This is one of the gentler miracles available to you and it costs nothing.

## The Lichen Gardens

These are the only places on the plateau that feel alive in a way you recognize. Sit near one for a while, outside the tech-object's radius if you can. The purple-yellow color is restful in a way the rest of the landscape is not. Many travelers report that their clearest thinking happens next to a lichen garden.

Do not eat the lichen. It is almost certainly not food. It may not be matter in the sense you use the word.

## Mental Preparation

This is the part most travelers neglect, and it is the part that matters most.

You are going to a place that does not need you, will not notice you, and will not remember you. For some people this is a profound relief. For others it is the worst thing they have ever experienced. You should know in advance which you are.

A good test: sit alone in a quiet room for six hours with nothing to do and no devices. If you emerge refreshed, the plateau will be good for you. If you emerge agitated, desperate for contact, sick of your own company — the plateau will be very bad for you, and you should not go, or you should go only briefly, with a companion, and come back quickly.

The plateau is not a place for people who need to matter. It is a place for people who are ready, for a few hours, to not matter. This is a spiritual discipline disguised as a tourist destination. Prepare accordingly.

If you have recently suffered a loss — a death, a divorce, a serious illness — the plateau will not comfort you, but it will not lie to you either, which is sometimes better. Many travelers go there specifically for this reason. It is one of the few places in the multiverse honest enough to let you grieve without pretending your grief is important.

## Companions

Go alone if you can bear to. Go with one other person if you cannot.

Do not go in a group of three or more. The plateau does not accommodate groups. The silence is meant for one set of lungs, maybe two. A third voice, even a whispered one, will feel like shouting, and you will begin to resent your companions in ways that will damage the friendship permanently.

If you go with someone, agree in advance on periods of separation. An hour apart, then rejoin. Two hours apart, then rejoin. You will need this. So will they.

## Return

Plan the return carefully. The descent through the cloud-ocean will be disorienting; whatever method you use to leave — portal, vehicle, retracing your arrival — rehearse it until it is automatic. Your judgment on the way out will be worse than your judgment on the way in, both from the altitude and from the strange erosion the plateau works on the part of you that makes decisions.

When you get back, wherever back is, give yourself three days before you try to describe the experience to anyone. You will want to describe it immediately. You will fail. The description will come out wrong and you will be embarrassed, and the embarrassment will make you defensive about the trip, and the defensiveness will calcify into a version of the story that isn't quite true, and you will have lost something.

Wait three days. Then write it down, for yourself, not for anyone else. Then, if you want, tell someone.

## A Final Note

You will, at some point during your visit, become convinced that the plateau is about to do something specifically for you. A cloud will form directly overhead. A moth will approach on a course that seems aimed. The tone will sound in what feels like response to a thought you just had.

It is not for you. It is almost certainly not for you.

But — and this is the thing the experienced travelers say, quietly, when they are drunk and honest — it is *almost* not for you, which is not quite the same as not for you at all. The plateau is busy with its own long slow thought, and you are the wrong size and the wrong speed, but you are in the room while the thought is happening, and thoughts are porous at their edges, and something of you may be swept into the turbulence briefly, the way a fleck of dust is swept into a draft.

What happens to that fleck — whether it is computed, or remembered, or simply noted and released — is not knowable. But travelers report, afterward, a faint sense of having been included in something. Not as a participant. As a footnote. As the smallest possible grace note in a composition whose length is measured in aeons.

This is, for most people, enough. Pack for it. Go. Come back.
