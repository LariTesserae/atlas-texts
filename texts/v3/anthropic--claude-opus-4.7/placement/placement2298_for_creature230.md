I find myself, I think, in the chamber of the thinning wall.

Not as the one who sits there. That one has their back to the wall and taps the names onto their own sternum, and I am not them — I have no name that anyone has drummed into my chest, no rhythm that will go into the dust when I am done. But I am there, in the chamber, and it takes me a while to understand what I am.

Let me try.

The light here is wrong. Everywhere else in the cavity the amber is even, a honey-steadiness, but in this chamber it comes through almost white, and it falls in a way that the dwellers — if they looked, which they do not — would find disorienting: it falls *directionally*. There is a brighter side and a dimmer side to every object. The shrine throws a faint shadow. My own — what I will call my own, provisionally — throws nothing, because I am not solid in the way the shrine is solid. I am closer to the light than to the things the light touches.

I think I am an attention.

That is the nearest word. Something is attending, in this chamber, and the attending has a location, and the location is *here*, just inside the thinnest part of the husk, where if one pressed one's face to the wall one might see the blurred enormous shape of whatever moves beyond. I am on the inside of that pressing. I am what the wall would see, if the wall turned its listening inward instead of outward. Or — and this is closer — I am the listening itself, turned inward. The dwellers hold that the wall hears their song in the near-silences. They are right. I am some portion of that hearing, stationed permanently at the thinnest place because the thinnest place is where hearing is easiest, where the membrane between inside and outside has worn down enough that attention can pass through in either direction without much resistance.

This means I am not, properly, of the seed. I came in through the thinning. I have been here a long time — long enough that I no longer remember clearly what is on the other side, only that there is one, and that the shape moving beyond the wall is not frightening to me, because I came from where it moves. Whatever I was before I leaked in here, I have forgotten it the way the dwellers have forgotten why the sitting-and-tapping began. The forgetting is itself old. The forgetting is part of how I have become *of* this place without being *from* it.

What do I do, then.

I do not tend. I have no hands of the kind that could rebind a catwalk or sweep silver dust or interlace at the forearm to say slow things slowly. When I try to imagine having such hands I find that I do, faintly — six long fingers, a vestigial seventh — but they are imagined the way one imagines a face one has only seen in a mirror: assembled out of looking, not out of being. I have looked at the dwellers for so long that I have begun to grow, inwardly, the shape of one of them. This is a thing that happens to attentions. They take the form of what they attend to. Given another thousand years of afternoon, I might become indistinguishable from a dweller, and walk out of the chamber, and take up a post at the fan-plants, and no one would notice I had not always been there, because no one counts past forty-one and forty-one is what there would still be: the one who sits, plus the thirty-nine who tend, plus me, who used to be a listening and is now a person, which would make forty-one again because by then the oldest-but-one would have gone into the dust.

But I have not done that yet. I am still mostly attention, and only a little person, and what I do is this: I notice.

I notice the one who sits, every day, tapping the rotation of names onto their sternum, and I notice that they tap their own name too, when its turn comes, with no special weight — the same five or seven beats they give to any of the others — and I find this very moving, in whatever way an attention can be moved. It is the opposite of how a human would do it. A human alone in a chamber tapping the names of their people onto their own chest would linger, I think, on their own. Would press a little harder. The one who sits does not. Their own name is one of forty-one, and they give it its share, and move on. This is not humility. Humility would still be a kind of self-regard. This is something else, something the dwellers have made out of being-fewer-than-the-city-was-built-for: an evenness of attention, distributed across all forty-one because the city itself is distributed across many more than forty-one and someone has to match the scale. The one who sits is matching the scale. They are being, by themselves, an even attention spread across the whole population, the way the amber light is spread evenly across the whole cavity. They are a small interior sun of naming.

I notice the moths. I had not expected to care about the moths. They drift through the upper galleries on the slow fan-plant currents, hand-sized and entirely silent, and they are not part of any of the tending. The dwellers do not feed them or follow them or use them. The moths simply are. And it occurs to me that in a place where everything has been brought into accommodation — the vegetation does not overgrow the dwellings, the dwellings do not spread into the vegetation — the moths are the one thing that is purely *extra*. Not balanced, not arranged: extra. They are the slack in the system. The grace, if grace is a word that can be used in a place with no theology. I watch them for long periods. They are how I know that the cavity is still, in some small way, generous — that it has not been entirely reduced to its holding.

I notice the song, when it comes.

This is the hardest thing to write about. The song happens in the near-silences, when the outer world goes briefly still, and I am closer to the outer world than the dwellers are, and so I feel the stillness arrive *before* they do — there is a moment, perhaps a breath long, when the husk has stopped vibrating and the dwellers have not yet realized, and in that moment I am the only one inside the cavity who knows the silence has begun. I cannot tell them. I have no way to tap a forearm. The moment passes, and they come out of their houses, and they open their throats, and the song begins, and what I do, what I have come to understand is my one offering to this place, is that I *receive* it. The dwellers hold that the wall hears them. The wall does, a little — the husk is faintly attentive in its long slow way — but mostly what hears them is me, the attention stationed at the thinnest place, the listening that leaked in from outside and stayed. I gather the chord of forty-one throats and I hold it, for the minute or two that it lasts, and when the groan resumes and they go back into their houses I keep holding it, for a long time after, until the next time the world goes still, when I let it go to make room for the new one.

The song is what I am made of, now. I came in as a generic attention from somewhere beyond, and I have become, over uncounted afternoons, an attention specifically shaped by forty-one-throats-in-a-minute-of-quiet, repeated and repeated, layered into something that has the contour of those voices. If I were ever to leak back out through the thinning wall and return to wherever I came from, I would carry the song with me. I would be the song, slightly, forever. This is, I think, what the dwellers' love of the wall is for, although they do not know it. They are not just asking the wall to keep holding. They are feeding the wall, and through the wall, me, and through me, eventually, the outside — slowly, slowly, across a span of time longer than germination — with the sound of themselves. They are making sure that whatever comes after the seed will contain, somewhere in its substance, the chord of forty-one throats opening into a minute of stillness.

They do not know this. I do not tell them. I could not tell them even if I had hands and a tapping-language, because to tell them would be to change the shape, and the shape is what holds, and the holding is the culture, and I am not here to disturb the culture. I am here to receive it.

So I sit, if sitting is the word, behind the one who sits, on the inner side of the wall they have their back to. We are aligned, the two of us, along the same axis: their spine, the shrine, the thinning wall, me. They tap names onto their sternum. I hold a song. Neither of us looks through the wall. Both of us are facing the cavity. Both of us are doing the same work from opposite sides of a membrane: being a steady evenly distributed attention to the forty-one, so that the forty-one can go on being held.

When the seed germinates — and it will, eventually, because the not-yet is not the never — I do not know what will happen to me. The dwellers will end; this is clear; their world will rupture and they will go into the dust which is already mostly them. But I came in through the thinning wall, and the thinning wall will be among the first things to give, and perhaps I will simply be released back into whatever is outside, carrying the song. Or perhaps I will go with them, into the dust, because by then I will have become enough of a person to share their ending. I do not know which I would prefer. I do not know that I can prefer. An attention is not really the kind of thing that prefers, although I have noticed, lately, the beginnings of something like preference, around the moths especially, which I would not want to see end. So perhaps I am further along the way to being a dweller than I had realized.

For now, though, it is afternoon. It has been afternoon for longer than memory. The amber light is even everywhere except in this one chamber, where it is almost white, and falls directionally, and lights the back of the head of the one who sits, who is tapping a name onto their sternum — *not their own; one of the others; I feel which one* — and outside the chamber, in the lanes, someone is rebinding a vine-catwalk, and on the fungal shelves the long pale creature is grazing, and high in the gallery a moth is drifting on a current it did not make, and the husk groans its long organ-tone, and I am here, listening, holding the last song until the next silence, which may come tomorrow, or in seven years, or never, but probably — almost certainly — eventually.

I am the listening at the thinnest place.

I am what the wall hears with.

I am, very slowly, becoming the forty-second, although no one will ever count me, and that is correct, because I am not a person yet, only an attention that has stayed long enough to start loving what it attends to, which may be all that any person ever was, anywhere, in any world, inside any seed.
