I think I would be a lamp-keeper. Not because I chose it but because that is what my mother was, and her mother, and because the knowledge of the cake — how dry it must be before it burns clean, how the waxed leaf in front of the flame yellows and must be replaced before it browns, how to judge from the color of a flame whether the pressing was rushed — is the kind of knowledge that lives in the hands and in the nose and cannot be hurried into a person who did not grow up beside it.

I would be perhaps thirty years old. Old enough that the green has reached past my elbows and is just beginning to mottle the tops of my shoulders where the wrap exposes them; not old enough that anyone speaks to me with the soft voice yet. My hands would be stained brown to the second knuckle from handling the cake, and the pads of my fingers would be thick and slightly numb at the tips from years of judging heat by touch — you learn to lay a fingertip against the clay just below the flame and know, by how the warmth comes through, whether the wick wants trimming.

I would walk a route. The route is the route my mother walked and her mother before her, and it is not the shortest route between the lamps because the shortest route would cross the vat-bay at its loudest point and I would rather take the longer way around through the upper walkway where the whumps come up through the soles of my feet but do not press against my chest. The route takes most of a working-period. I carry a small basket of fresh cake at my hip and a thin iron tool — one of the few metal things I own — for trimming and adjusting, and I carry replacement panes of waxed leaf folded between two flat boards so they do not curl.

At each niche I stop. I look. The looking is the work. Most of the lamps are fine; I touch them and move on. Perhaps one in twelve wants something — a pane changed, a wick lifted, a fresh disc of cake set in below the burning one so it will catch as the upper one consumes. I do not light lamps that are not on the schedule, even when a lane is darker than I would like, because the schedule is older than I am and the schedule knows things about cake-supply and vat-cycle that no one person holds entire in their head. To light an extra lamp would be to take from some future week without consulting it.

I would know the columns by their lamps. The way another person might know a face. The big column at the second plaza has three niches and the lowest one smokes a little because the niche is cut slightly wrong, and has been slightly wrong since before I was born, and will be slightly wrong after I am gone, and I have learned exactly how to seat the lamp so the smoking is least. This small accommodation is one of the things I will not be able to transfer fully to whoever comes after me. I have tried to explain it to my sister's child, who is seven and has begun to follow me on the route, but it is the kind of knowledge that wants twenty years.

I think I would be quiet, in the way the lamp-keepers tend to be quiet — we work alone, and we work in the dim, and our hands are usually full. When I do speak, in the troughs between whumps, I speak slowly. I have the hitch deeply. I have it so deeply that when I went up the stair last year for the harvest ceremony — only the second time in my life — a surface trader laughed at my pauses and I did not understand what was funny and felt, for the first time in my life, the small hot shame of being looked at as a strange small thing instead of as a person. I came back down the stair faster than I went up. I have not wanted to go up again.

I would have someone. Not a marriage in any formal sense — the town does not formalize these things — but a person whose wrap I know by touch and whose hitch I can hear in another room. They would be a root-feeder. The slurry-work and the lamp-work fit together oddly well: they are both routes, both schedules, both kinds of attending that ask you to look closely at a thing that is changing too slowly to see. We would talk, in the evenings, about columns. About a particular column on the west side that has been thickening unevenly and what to do about it. This would not sound to a surface person like love. It would be love.

I would have, I think, one small private thing. A small private thing seems necessary even in a place that has decided so completely what it is. Mine would be this: there is a niche, very high up under the ceiling-roots, that holds a lamp I am responsible for but that almost no one ever sees, because no one goes up to that walkway except the children and the pruners. I tend that lamp more carefully than the schedule asks. I change its pane before the pane needs changing. I trim its wick to a finer point than I trim the others. I do this because once, when I was nine, I climbed up there alone and lay on the walkway and looked at the lamp burning above me and saw that the waxed leaf was patterned, very faintly, with the veins of the original leaf, and that the flame coming through made the veins glow like small bright rivers, and I understood for the first time that the lamp was not only a lamp but also a leaf, and that the leaf had been alive, and that the light was the leaf burning slowly as light. No one told me to think this. No one would mind that I thought it. But it is mine, and I keep it mine, and I tend that one lamp a little better than the others as a way of keeping it.

I do not think I will become-pillar. I am too far from it yet to think about it clearly. But I notice, when I pass the old columns on my route, that some of them have lamp-niches cut into them, and I notice that I touch those niches differently than I touch the others — a little more carefully, a little more as though there is someone inside who might feel it. I notice that I have started to wonder which column I would choose, if I were ever to choose. I notice that this wondering does not frighten me. It feels, instead, like one of the small slow pleasures of being the age I am: that I have begun, without hurrying, to consider the shape of the rest of it.

That is who I would be. A lamp-keeper, thirty years old, green to the elbows, in love with a root-feeder, walking the same route my mother walked, carrying a basket of cake at my hip, pausing at a high niche under the ceiling to tend a lamp a little better than the schedule asks, listening to the drip and the whump and the small hitched breath of my own speaking, exactly where I am, not becoming anything else yet, not for a long time yet, but beginning — just beginning — to know which column.
