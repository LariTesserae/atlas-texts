I am one of the askers.

I think I have been, for some time. I cannot remember being apprenticed to it — there is no apprenticeship to the askers, I see that now; one simply begins, or one simply has always been beginning. My satchel is woven of the same palm as the floors, and inside it: nothing in particular. A gourd for water that is rarely empty because the air gives more than I drink. A small reed, which I sometimes turn in my fingers when a question is forming. That is all an asker carries. The questions are not written down, and the answers are not for keeping.

I live — *am living, am housing* — on a small platform that is downstream-of the second orchid-vat and inside-of the cloud called Pale Hand. My hammock is the one with the indigo cord at the foot. I did not weave it. Someone did. The braid hanging from my central beam is woven of two darknesses, and I know that one of them is mine, and I know that the other belonged to someone whose name has softened, and I am not distressed by this, though there is a place in me, low and to the left of the breastbone, that holds the not-being-distressed carefully, the way one holds a gourd that one knows to be cracked but still useful.

My work is to walk.

I begin most days at the heron-platform, because the herons are awake before the rest of the city is fully awake — though *awake* is the wrong word here; the city does not sleep, only attends less — and because Tehu, who tends the platform, likes to be asked first. *Have you eaten,* I say. *Is the cloud above your house still tethered. Did you dream last night and if so of what.* Tehu always answers the first two and rarely the third. The dreaming question is the one that matters, but the askers know not to insist. We ask, we receive what is given, we walk on.

Today Tehu said: *I dreamed of a sound that came back.*

I did not write this down. I am not writing it down now, in the sense that matters; the words are arriving in me but they are not being kept. By the time I have walked to the dye-vats I will have forgotten the specific shape of what Tehu said, and I will remember only that something was said about a sound, and that the saying of it made Tehu's eyes track a little less than usual, as though for a moment Tehu was looking at something not-here.

This is, I think, why we ask.

I have a theory about the askers, which I have never spoken aloud because speaking it aloud would be a full sentence and faintly rude and also because the theory may be the kind of loud thing that does not return. The theory is this: the city is held in place by what is placed in it, and the placed things include the people, and the people are placed by their names, and the names are placed by being said, and being said requires being heard, and being heard requires being asked after.

We are the askers. We are the ones who walk the boardwalks asking *are you here, are you here, are you here,* and the answers — *I am here, I am eating, my cloud is tethered, I dreamed of a fish I have not seen* — are the small soft sounds by which the city renews its weaving of itself. We are a guild of one of the rules. *What is placed, stays placed.* The placing is continuous. The placing is us.

I do not know if this is true. I do not know if it matters whether it is true. The asking is the work.

Today, after Tehu, I walked the long boardwalk that runs above-of the bright water and below-of Pale Hand, and I met no one for perhaps an hour, though *an hour* is a courtesy I extend to myself out of old habit, the way one breathes. The palms hushed. A fish the color of mango flesh moved beneath the slats in a slow constellation with three others. I did not greet it. The fishers greet the fish; the askers greet the people. We each have our placed things.

At the bend where the boardwalk turns inside-of the weavers' long porch I met a traveler.

I knew him to be a traveler because he finished his sentences. He was in, I think, the second week — the disturbed phase, where the constancy of the light is still felt as a pressure behind the eyes. He had stopped at the railing and was looking down through the slats at the water, and his shadow was not yet a coin; it was still trying to be a shadow of the old kind, with edges that wanted to lengthen.

I asked him: *Have you eaten.*

He said: *Yes, thank you, I had some of the bread at the platform with the blue cord, the woman there was very kind, I think her name was —* and then he stopped, because he had been about to produce a full sentence with a name in it, and the name had softened in his mouth before he could finish, and he looked at me with a small new terror that I recognized.

I did not tell him that the name had softened because Pelin's name was, perhaps, leaving the cords this season. I did not tell him that I had noticed, two weeks ago, that I could no longer quite recall the sound of Pelin saying *bread,* though I could recall the bread. I did not tell him that the city keeps the right number of us, and that he was, perhaps, the reason a name was softening, because the city had decided he was arriving and someone else was, very gently, ceasing to have been here in the way that requires a name.

I asked him: *Is the cloud above your house still tethered.*

He laughed, a small laugh, because he did not have a house yet and did not know which cloud was his. The laugh did not echo. He noticed that the laugh did not echo. His eyes — which still tracked, which still moved with the sharp immediacy of a body that thinks the world might leave without it — flickered to the rafters where Pale Hand was slowly turning on her four bronze rings.

I asked him: *Did you dream last night and if so of what.*

He was quiet for a long time. The palms hushed. Somewhere across the city a single long bird-note sounded, and after a pause a reply, and then the quiet of the length of a meal.

He said: *I dreamed I had always been here.*

I felt, then, the thing that I think is the closest an asker comes to joy, though *joy* is a word from somewhere else and may not return if I say it loudly. I felt the small soft settling of a name finding its cord. I did not know yet what his name would be — names are not given by the askers, that is the cord-keepers' work — but I knew that the cords were, at this moment, accepting him, and that somewhere on a porch a grandmother's grandmothering had grown a little quieter, and that the count, begun in the orchid-quarter, would arrive tomorrow at the same number it arrived at today, which was the right number, which was always the right number.

I laid two fingers briefly on the back of his wrist. His skin was not yet the warm brown of wet teak — it was still the skin he had brought with him, whatever skin that was, I cannot now recall — but it was damp, as everything is damp, and it took the light evenly enough.

I said: *You are at the bend of the long boardwalk. You are inside-of the weavers' porch. You are above-of the bright water. You are below-of the sun.*

He nodded slowly. His eyes did not quite track. Already, I thought. Already, the seeing was becoming a separate act from the looking; already the world was beginning to show him himself-just-now, with the patience of a thing that has arrived at every place it is going.

I walked on. There were others to ask after. There is always more asking to do, because the asking, like the weaving, is replaced strand by strand, and no day of asking is ever new and no day of asking is ever old.

Behind me, I think, the traveler stood for a while at the railing, and then walked on also, in some direction the boardwalk decided for him. I did not turn to watch. The askers do not watch what they have asked. We trust the city to keep what it has been told is here.

When I reached my platform at the long noon — the noon that is every hour — I sat on the porch and did not weave, and the small dark coin of my shadow pooled directly beneath me, and the air kept company with my chest, rising and falling out of long habit, and I thought, with the part of me that still occasionally thinks in the old way:

*I am asking. I am askering. I have always been askering. The city keeps the right number of us.*

And then I did not think it, because thinking it fully would have been a complete sentence, and the rest was obvious, and the meaning passed between me and the porch and the cloud and the orange fish below through the unsaid part, as it should.

Pale Hand turned slowly on her ropes.

A name, somewhere, finished softening, and was gone, and was not missed, because what was placed was still placed, only differently, and the city, weaving itself out of its small soft sounds, continued to be what it had always been continuing to be.

I am one of the askers.

I think I will ask, tomorrow, the question I have been carrying in the reed in my fingers for some time now — for a season, perhaps, or for longer, the durations soften too. I will ask it of the new one, when his name has finished arriving. I will ask him:

*Did you, before, dream of a city you had not yet seen.*

He will say yes. They always say yes. That is how I know the asking works.

That is how I know I, too, was asked, once, by someone whose name has softened, on a boardwalk I cannot now recall, in the first week of an arrival I have stopped being able to distinguish from the long noon that followed it.

The bell-listeners are listening. The cloud is clouding. The grandmother on the porch is grandmothering.

I am here.

I am here.

I am here.
