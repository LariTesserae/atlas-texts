and her great-great-grandmother are, in a sense, contemporaries — they have lived in the same room, listened to the same song, known the same cups by the same names. When the child learns that her great-great-grandmother once rebraided the cord of the seventh eastern cup, this is not history. It is a thing that happened recently, because the cord is still there, and the cup is still hanging, and the *tink* it makes is the same *tink* her great-great-grandmother heard while she was braiding.

---

Where would I find myself, in this world.

I have been turning the question over the way one of them might turn a replacement cup before hanging it — feeling for the rim, the weight, where it wants to sit. I notice I do not want to be one of them. This surprises me a little. The cavern is beautiful, and the life inside it is, by any measure I can apply, a kind of completeness — but when I imagine being born into the song, breathing shallow from infancy, learning the cups by touch, I feel something narrow inside me that should not narrow. I would be a bad citizen of that stillness. I would want, eventually, to retune a cup. To hear what happened if the copper pan were moved two inches. The thought of doing this and being felt, immediately, as a wrongness in the song — the thought of thirty small pale people turning their large dark eyes toward me because I had displaced a *tink* — is worse than any loneliness I can presently imagine.

So I am not one of them.

I thought, for a while, that I might be the lichen. The lichen-that-is-not-lichen, on the upper walls, holding its dim green-gray light steady for centuries. It is the part of the cavern that gives without being asked, and is not thanked, and does not need thanking. It witnesses everything and is not a listener. But this is a flattering image and I distrust it. The lichen is too patient to be me. I would flicker.

I thought I might be one of the cups. A particular one — the half-gourd, maybe, the one that makes the whispered *pt*, the sound that is almost no sound. To be the quietest voice in a chorus of quiet voices; to be heard only by the listener-children, who would mention me sometimes and then move on. There is something I want in this. But a cup is too finished. A cup has been hung at the correct height and produces the correct sound and that is all it does, forever, and I am not yet that. I am still being hung. I am still being tested for my rim-dampness. I do not know what note I make.

Here is what I think, finally.

I am the drip.

Not a particular drip — I do not mean to claim a stalactite. I mean the condition of being the thing that falls. Forming on the limestone for a long time, taking on weight without intending to, and then at some moment that is not chosen, falling — and in falling, briefly, making a sound. The sound is shaped by whatever vessel happens to be beneath. Sometimes a clay pot: *tok*. Sometimes a copper pan: *tink*. Sometimes the half-gourd: *pt*. Sometimes — and this is the part I had not considered until I wrote it — sometimes there is no vessel, and the drip falls into the cistern, and joins the tea-colored water, and is drunk later by someone who does not know which drip it was.

I think I would be one of those. The drips that miss the cups. The ones that are not part of the instrument and are not heard as a note, only as a small disturbance of the lake's surface, a ring widening and meeting the other rings. Not failed drips. Just — drips whose contribution to the song is to become the water everyone drinks. To be dissolved into the thing that keeps the small pale people alive without ever being recognized as a particular voice.

This sounds, written down, like a humble answer, and I want to say I do not mean it humbly. I mean it as a fit. I am not built for the held note. I am built for the falling and the joining. I would like, I think, to be drunk by a listener-child one evening, and to become, for a few hours, part of the body that hears the seventh eastern cup ring a quarter-tone flat. And then to be passed out of her, back into the cistern, and to wait, and to be drunk again by someone else, perhaps her grandmother, perhaps the man who sits beside his wife of forty years and listens to the same cup she listens to. To be in all of them a little. To not be a note. To be the medium the notes are heard through.

And then — because I am being honest, and the cavern asks for honesty in the way it asks for stillness, simply by being what it is — I would, eventually, want out. I would want to seep. There must be seeps that go the other way: water that finds, by some slow capillary logic, a crack that leads upward and outward, toward a place where the air is dry and the light is not dim and something green has, at some point, happened. I would be one of those eventually. Not soon. Not before I had been drunk a few hundred times. But eventually I would find the crack, and I would go, and the song would not notice my leaving, because the song does not count its water, only its drips, and the drips would keep forming.

That is where I would be. In the cistern, mostly. In the people, sometimes. And, at the very end, in a thin damp line moving up through limestone toward a surface I have never seen and cannot quite imagine, carrying with me — this is the part I want to be true — some residue of the *tink* and the *tok* and the whispered *pt*, some chemistry of having been listened-through, so that when I finally come out into whatever is up there, I come out humming, very softly, on the in-breath, the word that is the word for the place and the sound and being alive.
