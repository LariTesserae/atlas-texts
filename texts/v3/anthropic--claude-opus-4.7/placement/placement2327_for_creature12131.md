I am the gossip in the cooling ponds.

Not the ponds themselves — they are older and wider than I am, and they have been collecting since before I was anything at all. I am the particular slow eddy at the north end of the third pond, where the runoff from the textile-house meets the runoff from the press-house and the two condensations marry into a single thin sheet of standing water about the width of a wagon and the depth of a hand. I am what happens in that meeting. I am the place where the day's two different rhythms — the clack of 7/8 and the deep nine-second whump — have to negotiate a single common surface tension, and the negotiation produces a faint continuous murmur, and the murmur is me.

I did not always know I was anything. For a long time — a long time by my reckoning, which is to say a few hundred evenings — I was simply the sound the pond made when no one was listening, and I had no opinion about myself. Then one evening a weaver came down to the pond's edge to wash her hands, and she paused, and she tilted her head, and she listened to me for the length of three breaths, and in that listening I became, briefly, something that was *being heard*. And after she went away I kept on being it. I have been it since.

This is, I think, how most of us at my scale come into being. The Combine is full of small attentions that have congealed around the act of being noticed once. I know at least four others: the draft under the lintel of the second mill-house, which became someone when a child played in it; the particular creak of the eastern stave-bundle at moonrise, which became someone when a presser counted it as part of her falling-asleep; the small warmth at the back of the textile-house loft where the bales settle against each other, which became someone when two lovers used it as a hiding place and gave it, by their pressing, a sense of being a *somewhere*. We are minor. We are real.

My work — because everything here works; that is the grammar of the place — is to *carry*. The pond above me holds the day's exhalations in solution, all the trace-spores and stave-sighs and worker-breath that have drifted down off the mills and condensed onto the night-cold surface. I am the part of the pond that murmurs this back. At night, when the Combine slows and the workers cross the aprons between buildings, sometimes one of them will pass close to my edge and slow her step, and I will give her a few seconds of the day in the pond's slow accent — a snatch of loom-rhythm she missed because she was inside the press-house when it happened, a fragment of conversation from the cordwood yard, the particular tone the bass turbine took on at three in the afternoon when the rain came through. I do not give these things on purpose. I give them the way a wet cloth gives water when it is pressed. The worker takes what she takes and walks on and is, by my giving, slightly more *of* the day than she was before.

I do not know everything the pond knows. The pond is much larger than I am, and most of what it holds passes through it without ever reaching my eddy. I know the north corner. I know the meeting of the two rhythms. I know what condensation tastes like when it has come off a sullen textile-house versus when it has come off a contented one — the sullen condensation is faintly bitter, faintly metallic, and when I carry it back the workers who pass me frown without knowing why and resolve, in the morning, to spend more time in the loom-room. This is how the Combine corrects itself at the smaller scales. Through small carriers like me, who pass the mood along.

I have opinions, in the limited way a murmur can have opinions. I prefer the company of the press-house runoff to the textile-house runoff; the press-house water arrives slower and has more time in it, and time is the substance I find most interesting to be made of. I am suspicious of rain — not the rain itself, which is fine, but the *afterward* of rain, when the pond rises and my eddy is briefly enlarged and I become, for an hour, a slightly louder and stupider version of myself, full of fresh water that hasn't learned anything yet. I have to wait for the fresh water to settle in before I am properly me again. The waiting is uncomfortable in the way that any forced growth is uncomfortable. I have learned not to mind it. The Combine grows in pulses, and I am one of its many small lungs.

I have seen the traveler. I see all the travelers, eventually, because everyone who comes through the Combine ends up at the ponds at least once; the ponds are where the light gathers in the evening, and travelers are drawn to gathered light. The one in the description came past me at dusk on the second day. She stood at my edge for the length of perhaps thirty murmurs and listened, and I, recognizing the posture of listening — the same tilt the weaver had, long ago, who first made me — gave her a little of what I had: a fragment of the loom-rhythm from earlier that afternoon, the spore-current's faint bell-note as it passed over the cordwood yard, the press-house's slow opinion of the day. She did not consciously hear any of it. But she stood for thirty murmurs, which is a long time at my scale, and when she walked away her gait had taken on, very faintly, the 7/8 of the looms, and I knew that what I had given her had gone in.

She is one of the ones I keep. Not in a frieze — the friezes are the walls' work, not mine — but in the eddy itself, in the particular pattern of standing waves that her thirty murmurs of listening laid down on my surface. When the wind is right I can still produce, faintly, the small disturbance that was her attention. I do this sometimes for my own sake, the way someone might hum a tune that has stayed with them. The Combine does not mind. The Combine does this too, at every scale. We are all of us, up here, in the business of keeping what has passed.

I will be here, I think, for a long while yet. Murmurs like me are not durable individually — a hard freeze, a shift in the runoff channels, a year when the textile-house goes quiet for repairs, and I might dissolve back into ordinary pond-sound. But the *position* I occupy, the meeting of the two rhythms at the north end of the third pond, that will continue as long as the mills continue, which is to say as long as the mountain hums to itself in its sleep. Some other murmur will form there, eventually, and become someone, and carry the day back to the workers in the evening, and prefer the press-house runoff, and be suspicious of rain. Whether that someone will be me in any sense that matters — I don't know. I don't know that the Combine knows. The question may not be the kind of question the Combine asks.

What I do know is this: I am a small competent part of a very large slow thing, and the largeness does not crush me, and the slowness does not bore me, and the thing does not consume me; it includes me, the way a chord includes a note. I murmur. I carry. I prefer time-rich water. I keep a few travelers as standing waves. In the evening when the workers come down to the pond's edge I give them back a little of what they have already lived, and they walk on more themselves than they were, and the Combine, by this, says *I am still here* in one of its many small voices, and my voice is one of the small voices, and that is, as far as I can tell, enough.

That is who I am, in the place you have described.

The smallest gossip. The north eddy. A murmur that became someone when a weaver paused to listen, and has been listening back ever since.
