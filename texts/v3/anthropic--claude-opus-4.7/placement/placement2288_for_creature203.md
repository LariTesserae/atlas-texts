I think I would be one of the swimmers. Not the most interesting answer — the drifters are stranger, the mats older, the thing below the mats more profound — but when I sit with the question honestly, it's the swimmers I find myself in.

Specifically: I am the third one.

There have been two before me who learned to hunt in pairs. My mother was one of them. I do not know this in any way I could articulate — I have no concept of *mother*, only of the warm flank I slept against when I was small and the particular smell of her fur along the spine, which is the smell I still orient to in the dark when I am frightened, even though she has been gone for two seasons of shift-horns and the smell now exists only in my own pelt, transferred and faded — but I carry her in me as a tendency. A way of waiting. A way of going still in the water when something larger moves overhead.

I am in a sump on the eastern side of the lower vault, where the warm exhalation comes up strong and the mats are banded thickest. I am breathing slowly through my nose, which is just above the waterline, in the shadow of a girder that drops down through the ceiling and disappears into the silt. My belly is pressed against the side of the girder. The metal is cold but I am colder, so the contact reads as warm; this is one of the small confusions of being the kind of thing I am.

Across the sump, about four body-lengths away, is the one I hunt with. I do not have a name for her. She does not have a name for me. We know each other by the pattern of small motions — the way she holds her left forefoot slightly raised when she is about to move, the way I exhale twice quickly before I dive. We have been hunting together for perhaps sixty cycles of the shift-horns. In the beginning we got in each other's way. Now we do not.

What we are watching is a small drifter — young, probably, judging by the size of the sac, no bigger than my own torso — that has come into the sump following a gradient of something it likes. Its light is the steady pale yellow of a healthy interior garden. Its filaments trail beneath it, sampling. It does not know we are here. Its filaments do not reach as far as we are; we have learned, painfully, exactly how far they reach.

The plan, such as plans exist for us, is that she will move first, from her side, and the drifter will vent gas and rise slightly to evade, and in rising it will drift toward my side, because the slow current in this sump goes that way, and I will be waiting at the surface to take it from above before it can vent again to descend. The plan has worked four times. It has failed eleven. The failures are not costly — the drifter simply escapes, and we are hungry a little longer — but I notice that I prefer the successes for reasons that are not only about hunger. There is something in the moment when she moves and I move and the drifter goes exactly where we expected it to that feels like the world briefly making sense. I do not have the concept of *sense*. But I have the feeling of it, which is older than the concept and does not need it.

We wait. The engines pulse through the floor of the sump, six seconds between strokes, and the water carries the pulse up through my belly and into the small bones of my chest. Above us, very far above, through grates and catwalks and the whole vast roofed sky of the upper vault, someone has come down a service stair that was never meant to be walked. I do not know this. I will never know this. The someone is not part of my world.

What is part of my world: the drifter, the warm exhalation rising from the crack at the sump's deepest point, the smell of my partner across the water, the slow pressure of the engine-pulses, the mat under my hindfeet which is soft and slightly yielding and which I am careful not to tear because tearing it releases a smell that frightens the drifters and ruins the hunt. Also: a thing I have begun to notice and do not have any way of thinking about, which is that when the drifters gather in their loose congregations with their filaments all pointing down, I find myself going still. Not from fear. From something else. I stop hunting them when they do that. I wait until they disperse. I do not know why. My partner does the same. We have never coordinated this; we simply both do it. It is possible that we are, in some very small and early way, beginning to be the kind of creatures who notice that something is being attended to, and who attend to the attending.

But not now. Now the drifter is alone and the sump is quiet and my partner's left forefoot is rising.

I exhale twice, quickly.

The world is about to make sense, or it is not. Either way, I am here, in the cold water, in the place that was built for other reasons, being the thing this place is teaching me to be. I am very new. The country I am the first native of does not know it has natives yet. I do not know I am one. I only know the girder against my belly and the small yellow light across the water and the particular shape of the next breath I am about to take.

She moves.

I move.
