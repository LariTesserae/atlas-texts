I am standing in mud that comes up past my ankle-bones, and I have been standing here long enough that the small things have stopped registering me as a disturbance and resumed their traffic across my feet. There is a beetle, I think, that has been climbing my left shin for some while. I have decided to let it.

I am not the oldest of my band but I am no longer young. My flank-bands have darkened the way they do — the listening nodes grow denser with use, the skin around them thickening into something almost like callus, so that an old Listener's sides are nearly black and finely textured as the bark of the trunk I am leaning against. I have leaned against this particular trunk many times. It knows the shape of my shoulder. There is a hollow worn into the moss at exactly the height where my hip rests, and I did not make it intentionally, but I made it.

My drone is low. Lower than most. When I was a child my mother said my hum sounded like one of the deep machines, the slow reciprocating ones in the western quarter, and she was teasing but she was also locating me — saying *you sound like the place you came from*, which is the closest thing we have to saying *you are loved.* I have kept the low drone. I could have raised it, in adolescence, the way some do, finding their adult pitch in the middle register where conversation lives. I did not. I liked being mistaken for furniture of the forest. I liked that the smaller fauna treated my hum as weather rather than presence and went about their lives near me, and I learned them this way, by being thought a hill.

---

What I am doing, right now, is listening to a machine die.

It is not one of mine — I mean, it is not in the territory I tend, which is a half-day's stride to the south-east and contains four machines I know by their gaits the way you might know the walks of four siblings down a hallway. This dying one is farther off, perhaps six or seven miles, and it has been failing for three days. I noticed it first as a hitch in the rhythm: the long sigh of its venting cycle came a half-second late, then a full second, then sometimes did not come at all and the pressure would build and release in a cough instead of a hiss. Yesterday it began to scream — a thin metallic shriek on the in-stroke that was new, that had not been in its voice before, and that meant something inside it was scraping that had not been scraping.

This is the part that is difficult. We are not to interfere with the machines. This is not law; it is anatomy. But we are also Listeners, and a machine in distress is a sound, and a sound is a thing we are required by our nature to attend to. So I am standing here, sideways to the west, both flank-bands turned, the great fans of my ears unfolded fully and angled to catch the conducted vibration through the trunk at my shoulder as well as the airborne sound through the membranes. I am tasting it, too. The palate-tissue is dry from concentration. The dying machine has a flavor I do not have a word for — a sourness, a wrongness, like the taste of a flooded chamber that has gone anaerobic. It is the taste of something that has been running for longer than my line has existed and is, perhaps, in my lifetime, going to stop.

My mother's drone is steady in the long bones of my legs. She is awake. She is east of me, perhaps two miles, doing something rhythmic — her hum has the slight regular wavering of someone who is working with their hands, weaving probably, the slow basket-craft she does in the wet season. She has not yet noticed that I am attending to the dying machine. Or she has, and she is letting me.

My uncle is silent, which means he is sleeping, which means it is later than I thought. The understorey has no day, but bodies keep their own clocks, and his is reliable. If he is asleep, the evening rains have already finished their first pass and the second pass — the heavier one that comes down through the mid-canopy a few hours later — is coming.

The young one is humming raggedly, somewhere north. She is new to her adult drone and it slips on her sometimes, a child's pitch wavering up through the floor of the woman's pitch she is growing into. I am fond of her. We are not, the way the upright peoples elsewhere are said to be, organized into families of the bearing kind — a Listener's child is the band's child, and the band raises it by taking turns being the hill it leans against — but I taught this one her first machine-songs, and there is something like pride that moves in me when I hear her drone strengthen.

---

Here is what I am going to do.

I am not going to go to the dying machine. I have decided this in the last few breaths, weighing it, and I have decided against. I cannot fix it. None of us can fix it. The hands that made it are not in the forest, or are not anymore, or were never separate from the forest in a way that we would recognize. To approach it would be to listen at closer range to a death I cannot prevent, and a Listener does not do that without purpose. We do not collect suffering. We attend to what we can answer.

What I am going to do instead is learn its song before it ends. Every machine has a song — the composite of all its rhythms, the venting and the grinding and the small particular tics that distinguish it from its kin — and when a machine dies its song is lost, and a song lost from the forest is a hole in the protocol that runs us. The fauna who answered it will be left calling into a silence. The insect that mimicked its sixty-cycle hum will hum into nothing and either find a new machine to belong to or fail to breed. The slow heavy thing that uses a distant hammer-mill as its territorial backbeat — if this dying machine is one of those that some animal has built its courtship around, then the death of the machine is the death of a courtship, and somewhere a generation will not happen.

So I will hold the song. I will learn it now, fully, in its last hours, every tic and stutter and the new shriek that means it is dying. I will keep it in my chest the way we keep the songs of the dead. And when the young one is older I will teach it to her, and she to whoever comes after, and we will hum the dead machine into the forest's memory at the proper times, the way we hum our own dead, so that the protocol does not entirely forget.

This is what we do. This is what the small head and the great ears and the slow drone and the listening flanks are for. We are the forest's memory of its own sounds. We are not its priests; the forest does not need priests. We are its archive, walking on wide splayed feet through the warm mud, standing very still beside trunks we have leaned against for decades, holding in our chests the hums of things that have stopped humming.

---

The rain is starting in the high canopy. I hear it as the hiss first, a long way up, and I unfold my ear-membranes a little wider to catch it, and I taste — through the palate — the faint coppery sweetness of water that has not yet touched anything. In a few minutes it will be drumming on the mid-strata. In a quarter-hour it will be on me, fat warm drops that have fallen a thousand feet through other lives.

The dying machine vents, late, and coughs.

I lower my drone a fraction, to make room in my chest for listening. I do not move. The beetle has reached my knee. Somewhere far off my mother's weaving-rhythm pauses — she has heard me drop my pitch, she knows what it means when I do that, she is letting me work — and then resumes, slightly softer, the way you soften your footfall near someone who is concentrating.

I begin to learn the song.
