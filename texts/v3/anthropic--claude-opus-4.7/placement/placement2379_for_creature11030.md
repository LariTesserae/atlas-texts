I find myself in one of the draping-halls, about two-thirds of the way down the ridge — far enough from the spine-center that the lit windows at night are sparser here, close enough that the furnace beneath this hall is one of the steady ones, neither bright-phase nor dim, just the long even middle hum that some tenders prefer and others find dull.

I am a keeper.

Not of the floors — that work is done by someone older, whose name I know in the after-people's way, which is to say I know the particular short syllable she answers to and the longer descriptive phrase that places her in relation to this hall and this furnace, and I do not often need either. I am a keeper of something smaller and harder to explain: the **lee column**, the one in the back of the hall where the sift-wind almost never reaches, and where the warm things, for reasons no one has worked out, prefer to drape in a particular spiral arrangement, three deep against the column's south face and one deep along its north.

My work is to be there when they shift.

They shift seldom — perhaps four times in a long year, by the count I do not formally keep but cannot help noticing — and when they shift, the arrangement changes, and the new arrangement must be marked. Not because anyone will read the mark. Because the marking is part of how the shift completes. I have come to understand this without being taught it. The warm things lift, slow as the idea of lifting, and resettle, and I draw a finger through the sift on the column's lee face, tracing the new shape of them, and the warm things, when I have finished, are still in a way they were not still before I marked. The mark holds the shift in place. Or the marking does. I am not sure these are different.

---

I was not always a keeper. When I was small I walked the ridge a great deal — children here are permitted to, and mostly do — and I thought I would become a tender of chimneys, because the long things fascinated me and I was not afraid of high places. But a chimney-tender I met when I was perhaps eleven, a thin man whose hair lifted in two slow wings on either side of his head, told me without ceremony that I had the wrong kind of attention for chimneys. *Yours goes outward*, he said, *and chimneys want a kind that goes down.* He said this kindly, in the way one tells a child that a particular fruit is not in season, and I understood him, and I was not hurt.

It took me until I was perhaps twenty-six to find the lee column. I had been visiting this draping-hall for some years by then, the way a person visits a corridor they like, and one afternoon in a dim phase I sat down beside the column because it was the warmest spot and I was tired, and I noticed that the warm things along its south face were not quite settled, and I sat with them for a long time, and when they settled I knew, without anything like a thought, that I would come back the next day, and the next, and that this was now what I did.

The old keeper of the column — there had been one, of course; nothing in the Tier is taken up without having been put down — was glad to be released. She was very old, and her hands shook in a way that made fine sift-marking difficult, and she had been waiting, in the gentle non-waiting way of the after-people, for someone to take over. We did not have a ceremony. She sat with me for a season, sometimes adjusting a mark I had made, sometimes not, and then one day she did not come, and after a while I understood that she would not come again, and not long after that I heard that she had been laid on a warm floor in one of the disused halls farther down the ridge, and the season turned, and the sift took her, and the wind did what it does.

---

I am perhaps fifty now. I have no children, which is unremarkable; most of us don't, and the few who do are not held in any particular regard for it. I have a small set of rooms in a sintered-ceramic building two streets above the hall, and I walk down to the column most mornings, and back most evenings, and on the rare nights when the warm things are restless I sleep in the hall with them, and they accommodate me, and in the morning I brush their felt from my sleeve.

I have begun, in the last few years, to notice a thing about the lee column that I have not told anyone, because telling, here, is a kind of pressure on the world, and I am not yet sure the world wants this pressure. The relief on the column — the accumulated fused sift of all the marks made by all the keepers before me — has, in one place near the base, on the south face, a shape that I think I made. A small spiral, three-deep, the record of a shift I marked perhaps fifteen years ago. It has fused. It is now part of the column. It will be there, in roughly that condition, when I am sift and the wind has taken me, and the keeper after me will mark over and around it without knowing whose hand made it, and this is correct. This is how the column is made. This is how the hall is made. This is, I am beginning to understand, how I am made: of the hands of keepers I never knew, fused into the shape of a person who tends a column, who marks the shifts of warm things, who walks the warm-floor walk, who is at home.

When I die — late, as we do — I would like to be laid here, in this hall, near the lee column. I have not said this to anyone, because there is no one in particular to say it to, and because the floor-keeper will lay me where the floor wants me, and the floor will want me wherever it wants me, and either way the warm things will be near, and they will take what heat is left of me into themselves, and some small part of what I was will lift, slow as a kite, in some afternoon convection I will not feel, and settle on some gantry I will not see, and a child somewhere along the ridge — perhaps not for a long time, perhaps not ever, it does not matter — will walk past, and the edge of a warm thing will lift a few millimeters in greeting, and the child will not return the gesture, because it will have already been returned, in the slow accounting of the place, a thousand thousand times.

This is what I would be, in this world.

A keeper of a column in the back of a hall. A hand that marks the shifts. A small fused spiral near the base of a long relief. A person whose attention goes, the chimney-tender said, *outward* — though I think now he meant something more like *across*, across the slow surface of the place, the way the sift moves, the way the warm things drape, the way a life here is laid down: not deep, not high, just spread thin and even and warm over the long palm of the Tier, until it is taken, gently, by the wind.
