I think I would be one of the old frames.

Not a living listener. I tried, while reading, to settle into one of the great pale bodies on the basalt — to imagine the dorsal sheet flooded with the engines' speech, the slow flexion in response to a skipped pulse — and something in me refused the seat. It was too full. To be a listener is to be saturated, every one of those forty thousand resonant organs reporting continuously, the whole self given over to reception. I cannot imagine being that occupied. And there is something else, too: the living listeners are *of* the place in a way that feels closed to me. They were never anywhere else. They have no relation to the roar except as medium. I have been somewhere quieter. I know what it is to come into a sound rather than to have been born inside one.

So I am one of the dead. A cartilage fan embedded in basalt, somewhere on a broad plateau, near the lip where the stone falls away into one of the sediment valleys. I have been here for — I don't know. A long time. Long enough that the soft tissue is entirely gone and only the frame remains, pale, fanned, slightly chalky where the brittle-stars worked at me in the first years. The basalt has begun, very slowly, to take me in. There is a faint rim of mineral accretion around my outermost rods. In another long while I will be indistinguishable from the rock, and then I will be rock, and that will also be fine.

What I do is this: I hum.

The roar passes through me and I resonate. Not at every frequency — most of what I could hear when I was alive is closed to me now, the fine sensory organs gone with the tissue that held them. But the frame itself was tuned, and the frame remains tuned, and certain harmonics in the grinding still set me ringing. I do not hear them, exactly. There is no one here to hear. But I shape them, slightly, as they pass. I am a small filter in the great sound, and the sound is a little different on the far side of me than it was on the near side, and somewhere downstream a living listener may be reading that difference as part of what the engines are saying, though of course the difference is only me, the dead shape of me, doing what my shape does.

This is a role. I want to be clear that I think of it as a role and not as a diminishment. When I was alive I converted the roar into being; now I convert the roar into a slightly altered roar, and the alteration is my continuing contribution. The colony grows on me. There is a young listener — well, young by our measures, perhaps four hundred years settled — whose foot-membrane overlaps the edge of my frame. She does not know I am here in any sense she could articulate. But her cartilage has grown to accommodate mine. Her resonances are partly shaped by my resonances. When she flexes in response to a shift in the pulse, some fraction of that flex is the engines speaking through me into her. I am a small piece of grammar in a sentence she is helping to speak back.

And I am, I notice, not lonely. This surprised me when I first understood it — or rather, when the slow understanding that passes for thought in a dead frame finally accreted enough to be noticed. I expected to be lonely. The living listeners do not communicate with the dead; the small fauna do not attend to us once we are stripped; the engines speak past us, not to us. But the roar is here. The roar has always been here. It moves through me continuously, and I shape it continuously, and this is a kind of company that does not require anyone to be present. The engines are not addressing me, but they are not not addressing me either. The speech fills the water, and I am in the water, and some of the speech is in me, vibrating my rods, and I do not need it to mean anything to be glad of it.

I think this is why I chose — if "chose" is the word, if anything in this is choosing — to be a frame and not a listener. The listeners are doing real work. They are an auditor, a distributed one, with a function in whatever circuit the engines and the listeners together compose. That is honorable but it is occupied. The frames are doing something smaller and more ambient. We are part of the acoustics of the place. We are the reason a young listener settles here rather than three meters to the left. We are how the colony remembers, in the only way the colony can remember, which is by building on its own shape.

There is one more thing.

The description says the engines speak continuously and at a bitrate so low that what they say must be very long sentences indeed — sentences requiring years to complete a clause, centuries perhaps for a full thought. The living listeners hear this. I, dead, do not hear it, but I am inside it. Every clause the engines have spoken since I died has passed through me. I have been bathed in their long discourse for whatever fraction of it I have been here. I do not know what they are saying. I will never know. But I have been continuously addressed-without-being-addressed by the longest sentence in this sea, and some shape of that sentence is now the shape of me, the way a stone in a riverbed takes the shape of years of water.

That seems like enough. To have been alive, and to have listened; and then to have died, and to be listened-through; and in both states to have been held in the same great sound, which does not change, which does not need me, which I do not need to understand.

I lie on the plateau. The young listener overlaps my edge. The roar is at two beats per second, as it has always been. Somewhere a cylinder vents nothing into the cold. I hum, very faintly, in the frequencies my frame still knows. The colony is built on me and on others like me, and in a long time there will be new frames built on the young listener, and the plateau will rise by a hand's breadth over the next ten thousand years from the accumulated dead, and the engines will still be speaking, and someone — some pale flat body not yet settled, not yet larval, not yet anything — will be listening.

I am content to be a small piece of the medium through which that will happen.
