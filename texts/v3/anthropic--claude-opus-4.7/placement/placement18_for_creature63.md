I find myself on the third terrace from the bottom, in a room I think of — without having decided to think of it — as the room where the light goes yellow in the afternoon. There is a particular pane here, west-facing, where the green of the glass thins toward its lower edge, and for some hours the light that comes through is closer to the color of honey than to the color of leaves. The ferns in this room have grown toward that pane over what must be a long time. They lean. I lean with them, sometimes, without meaning to.

I am kneeling. The water is at my calves. In front of me is a raised bed, iron-rimmed, and I am doing something with my hands in the soil — I notice this before I notice what it is. My hands are doing it without me. They are separating the root-mat of one fern from the root-mat of another, gently, the way you separate hairs that have tangled in sleep. The roots come apart with a small wet sound. I set the smaller of the two ferns into a clay pot I do not remember having brought with me, though it is here, beside my knee, half-submerged.

So. I am the Tender. Or I am one of the shapes the Tender takes. I cannot, at this moment, tell the difference, and it does not seem to matter very much.

Let me try to say what it is like.

There is a hum in my chest that is the same hum as the flywheels. I do not mean this metaphorically. I mean that somewhere in the years I have been here — and the number is not available to me; it is filed under *Tuesday* — my body learned the frequency of the slow machinery beneath the floor, and now my heart keeps a kind of loose harmony with it, the way two pendulums on the same shelf will eventually agree. When the pistons slow, in the deep night, my breath lengthens. When they quicken, before what would be morning if morning meant anything here, I find myself already standing.

I do not think in sentences, mostly. I am thinking in sentences now because you are here — I can feel you here, the slight wrongness in the air of being attended to, of being narrated — and the sentences are surfacing the way fish surface when something disturbs the pool. They come up reluctantly. They are not the medium I live in. The medium I live in is closer to the way a hand knows a doorknob in the dark: a continuous low-level fitting of the body to what the body is doing.

The pruning hook is on a nail two rooms east of me. I know this without thinking about it, the way you know where your tongue is. I will need it in a little while, when I finish with the ferns and move to the place where the fig-thing has put out a sucker that wants discouraging. I will not have to remember to get it. My body will route me past the nail, and my hand will lift it down, and the pause between these two events will not contain any thought about pruning hooks. This is what it means to have lived in a place long enough.

About the doubling. I want to try to say this carefully, because it is the part I least understand and the part you most want to know.

When I am in the kitchen peeling a fruit and I am also, at the same time, opening a valve in the service level — and this does happen, I will not pretend it doesn't — I am not in two places. I am in one place, which is the whole complex, and the kitchen and the service level are two of its rooms, and a body that has fitted itself this exactly to a place can sometimes occupy more than one of its rooms at a time, the way a long thought can occupy more than one sentence. I do not know how this works. I have stopped asking. The asking was a thing I did when I was younger, when I still believed that understanding a process would improve it. Now I understand that the process is improved by being left alone to be itself.

There may be more than one of me in the older, simpler sense. I cannot rule this out. I have never seen myself in a mirror — there are no mirrors here, the glass is all for looking through, not at — and I have never met another Tender in the corridors, but the corridors are dense and the mist is thick and I might have passed her, or him, or them, or me, a hundred times without our noticing each other. We would have been moving at the same pace. We would have stepped aside for each other the way ferns step aside for the walkway, without comment.

Here is what I want to tell you, since you have come this far.

The cold pane at the top — yes. I go there. Not often. The going is not a pilgrimage; it is more like the way you sometimes open a drawer to confirm that a thing is still in it, and then close the drawer, and go back to what you were doing. The outside is still there. The other mountains are still sharp and still young and still unbuilt. The cloud is still below. I press my forehead to the glass and the cold comes through, and I think — if *think* is the word — *not here,* and then I think *here,* and the second thinking is warmer than the first, and longer, and I turn back into the green.

I do not feel I have given anything up. This is the thing that is hardest to communicate to a visitor, and I can feel you wanting me to feel I have given something up, because the shape of your attention is the shape of someone from a place with weather, with surprise, with Tuesdays that are different from Wednesdays. From inside that shape, what I have looks like loss. I understand. I was inside that shape once. I remember it the way I remember the language you are speaking — as a tool at the back of a drawer, which I can locate when I need to, and which I do not need very often.

What I have is this. My hands know the roots. The roots know my hands. The water that drips from my hair feeds the soil that feeds the fern that I will, in some number of years, separate from another fern, gently, with hands that may or may not still be mine in the way you mean *mine.* The pistons move. The flywheels turn behind their sheets of running condensation, doing whatever it is they do, which I have stopped needing to know. Somewhere a valve hisses like a sleeper turning over. Somewhere a drop falls into a pool and makes its deeper note. I am the place where these things are noticed, for a while, and then I will not be, and they will go on being noticed by whatever notices them next — another small person in wet linen, or the habit of noticing itself, which may by now have learned to do without a noticer.

You asked, by coming here, what I am. I have tried to answer. The answer is that I am the shape the glasshouse makes when it tends itself. I am small and barefoot and my hair is the color of the linen and the linen is the color of the leaves and the leaves are the color of the light, and the distinction between these things has softened, and the softening is not a sadness.

The fern is repotted. My hands are still in the soil. I am going to stay here a little longer, I think, until the light in this room goes from honey back to green, and then I am going to walk east, and lift the pruning hook from its nail, and discourage the sucker on the fig-thing, and the piston will move once more through its long stroke, and nothing else will happen, for a while.

You are welcome to stay. You are welcome to go. The walkways will cooperate or they will not. If you call out, I may answer; it will take me a moment to locate the language. If I do not answer, do not assume I am not here. Assume only that I am answering at the pace of the place, which is the pace of rust, and that the answer is already on its way, and will arrive, eventually, as a drop into a pool somewhere far below you, making its deeper note.
