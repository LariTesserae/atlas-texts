I would be on a half-street.

Not at first. At first I thought I might be one of the readers — that flattered something in me, the long patient reconstruction, the room behind the unmarked door — but the readers want to *know*, and I notice as I sit with the city that I am not, here, someone who wants to know. The reader's hunger is a clean hunger and I don't have it. I have something else, something more like the watchmen's relationship to their brass plates: a willingness to do the same small thing for a long time without requiring it to add up.

But I am not a watchman either. The watchmen have a route. The route is given to them; they inherit it. What I find when I look for myself in this city is that I do not have a route. I have a *trough*.

Every seventh street is a half-street, the description says, with a long polished trough running down one side and no apparent destination for what the trough carries. I am the person who keeps one of those troughs polished.

Let me try to be more precise, because the place rewards precision and punishes the grand gesture.

My trough is on the fourth half-street down from the upper terraces, on the eastern side of the city — though *east* is a word I use only with myself; nobody here uses compass directions, the slope is the only orientation that matters and everything is described as above or below or across-from. My trough is perhaps a hundred and forty paces long. It is cut from a single piece of some dark dense stone I do not know the name of, set into a channel of brick, and its inner surface has been worn so smooth by however many centuries of running water that when the lamp-light catches it at the right angle it looks wet even when it is dry, which is almost never. It is almost never dry.

What I do: I walk its length twice a day, once before the first bell-hour and once after, with a folded square of waxed felt and a small flat tool like a baker's bench scraper but made of horn. I crouch. I work down the trough on my heels, scraping the felt of wick-growth from the lip and the inner curve, lifting away the soft pale crust where it has begun to seam the stone. I do not scrape hard. The wick comes off easily if you take it before it has bedded in; if you wait too long it bonds, and then you have to cut, and cutting damages the stone, and damaged stone holds wick worse, and so the trough degrades. The whole skill of the work is in the timing — knowing when a patch of wick has grown enough to lift cleanly but not yet enough to root.

I have been doing this for — I don't want to say a number. The number isn't the point. Long enough that my knees give a particular sound when I stand up at the end of the run, a small wet click, like a valve seating. Long enough that I know every irregularity of the stone: the place where someone, generations ago, chipped the lip and the chip has been worn into a smooth shallow saddle; the place where the trough crosses an expansion joint and the two halves have settled minutely out of true, so that water on its way down ticks twice as it passes; the long stretch in the middle where nothing is wrong at all and I move faster, almost dreaming, my hand and the felt and the horn doing the work without me.

I live in a room above the trough. Not directly above — there is no building directly above, the half-street is open on that side — but in a narrow building across from it, in a small room on the second floor whose window looks down on my length of stone. I did not choose the room for the view. The room was empty and warm and I moved into it and only later realized I had arranged things so that I could see my work from where I slept. The watchmen would understand this. The pipe-fitters would not; they live where the work calls them, not where the work can be seen.

I do not know where my trough empties. I have walked to its lower end many times. It passes under an arch of brick and continues into the next terrace down, where it joins — or seems to join, the light is bad there — a larger channel running across. I have never followed it further. This is not from disinclination exactly. It is more that following it would change what the trough *is* to me. Right now the trough is the hundred and forty paces I tend. If I followed it down I would acquire a sense of it as part of a system, and the system is not mine; my hundred and forty paces are mine. I think this is what the old reader is circling, in her way — the suspicion that the system was never the point, that the local fact (water comes out of air here; this stone is clean today) is the whole of it, and the rest is scaffolding that has outlived its scaffold.

I do not believe she is right. I do not believe she is wrong. I have, like the watchman with his taps, put the question in a different room of the head from the one I work in.

What I notice, having written this far: I came into the city expecting to find myself somewhere more central. A reader, perhaps. Someone with the dignity of a real question. And instead I find myself crouched on a half-street scraping pale felt off a trough that doesn't appear to empty anywhere, and I notice that I do not mind. That the not-minding is itself the thing the city has taught me, or would teach me, if I lived here. The watchmen tap their plates. The fitters pack their joints. The tea-house woman keeps her stove. The readers read. And I — this version of me that the city has called up — I keep one trough clean, twice a day, and watch it from my window in the hours between, and when the bell rings somewhere across the wet rooftops I pause with my hand on the felt and listen, and then I go on.

There is one more thing. I want to put it down even though I am not sure of it.

Sometimes, at the end of the second run, when the light is failing and the mist is thickening into something like rain and the trough is clean and shining and full of its slow dark water, I sit on my heels at the lower end and watch what I have done. And I have the thought — not a belief, a thought, the way you have a thought about a stranger's face in a crowd — that the trough is not carrying water down. That the water is moving down, yes, but the trough's purpose, the thing the trough was made for, is to carry something *up*. Something that travels against the water, using the water the way a fish uses a current, and that whatever this something is, it requires the stone to be clean, and the wick to be lifted before it roots, and the lip to be smooth, and someone — me, or whoever was here before me, or whoever will be here after — to crouch in the wet at the right hour and do the small careful work that keeps the channel open for it.

I have no evidence for this. I have never seen anything move up the trough. The water moves down; I have watched it move down ten thousand times.

But the thought comes, at the end of the second run, in the failing light, and I do not chase it away, and I do not chase it down. I let it be there with me for a moment, the way you let the bell be there, and then I stand up — *click* — and fold the felt, and go up to my room, and the trough goes on shining in the dimness below my window, doing whatever it does, carrying down or carrying up, and I sleep above it, paired with it, as the city is paired with its weather, doing the work it was made for, mostly alone.
