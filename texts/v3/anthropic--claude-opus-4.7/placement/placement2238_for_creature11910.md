I think I would be near the Sink. Not a Sink-keeper — I haven't earned that quiet — but close. Let me find my way there.

---

I am Joss. Joss of the Lower Basins, though the use-name is recent; for most of my life I was Joss of the Cooperage Above the Third Drum, and before that just Joss, my mother's child, hanging on her apron in the smell of wet oak and iron hoops.

I am forty-one. My hands tell you this before I do — the pad of my right thumb has gone glassy from forty years of pressing staves into the croze, and there is a small white crescent at the base of my left forefinger where a drawknife slipped when I was nine. I remember the slip. I remember my mother not scolding, only turning my hand to the lamp, looking, nodding, wrapping it in clean linen, and giving me back the drawknife. *The work finds the hand,* she said, *but the hand has to stay.*

I keep the basins now in the streets that slope toward the Sink. This is not quite cooperage and not quite cistern-keeping; it is a trade that emerged because someone had to do it, and three years ago I noticed I was the one doing it, and the council, with their characteristic slowness, eventually gave me the use-name to match. The basins down here are odd. They go out of true faster than basins higher up. The wood swells differently. The hoops sit looser. I rebuild more than I make new, and I make new more than I would in a higher district.

This is because of the air, the alchemists say. The air near the Sink is heavier, wetter, slightly different in some way they can measure and cannot quite name. *It eats the seams,* one of them told me, *politely, but it eats them.* I liked the *politely.* I think about it when I am setting a new hoop.

---

Let me tell you what I am doing right now, in the middle of the unnamed hour.

I am sitting on a low stool in the shop — which is the front room of my house, which is built against the second-lowest terrace, three streets up from the Sink itself. The door is open. Through the door I can see a slice of the street: wet cobbles, a lamp on a bracket, the corner of Bel's lamp-shop opposite. Bel is inside, trimming. I can hear the small *snip* of her scissors when the drips lull.

In my lap: a stave of pale oak, half-shaped. I am taking the inside curve with a hollowing knife. The wood is cool under my hands. It smells faintly of the cistern it was soaked in for the last six weeks — a smell like clean stone and old leaves, though no leaf has ever fallen in Low Hour.

Around me, in their proper places: my tools, on a rack my grandfather built. My water-cup, copper, half full. A small bowl set under the corner of the ceiling where a drip has been falling for as long as I have lived in this house. The drip's note is C, I think — the alchemists would say C, anyway, with their tuning forks. To me it is just *the corner drip,* the one that says *home* when I come back from anywhere.

The basin I am making is for a Sink-keeper. Tev. Tev who gave me his given name the first time we met, which is how I knew what he was before he told me. He wants the basin small — smaller than I would normally make — and shallow, and with the staves a particular thickness. I asked him why. He looked at me the long way the Sink-keepers look, and said: *For listening.*

I did not ask further. I am making it the way he asked.

---

Here is the thing I have not told anyone, not even my sister, not even Mar who shares my bed in the cold months.

When I work on this basin, I hear something.

Not a voice. Nothing so clear. But when I press the hollowing knife into the inside of a stave, and the long curl of oak peels up and away, there is a sound under the sound of the cutting — a slow deep note, like the note the Sink's drips make, but quieter, and closer, as if it were coming from inside the stave itself. The first time I noticed it I stopped working and held very still and listened, and the note went away. So I started working again, and it came back.

I have tested this. I have made other basins this week, of the same wood, with the same knives, and they do not do it. Only this one. Only Tev's basin.

I have not stopped. I am a maker, and a maker, when the work begins to speak, listens, and continues. This is something my mother could have told me but did not have to, because by the time I would have needed telling I already knew.

I think — and this is the part I have not said aloud — I think the basin is becoming something other than a basin. I think Tev knew it would. I think this is what he meant by *for listening,* and I think he chose me not because I am the best cooper in Low Hour (I am not; Anneke of the South Cooperage is better) but because he had heard, somehow, that I would not stop when the work began to speak.

I do not know what the basin will be, when it is finished. I do not know what Tev will do with it. I know only that I am making it carefully, and that the hollowing knife in my hand is the same knife my mother used, and her mother before her, and that the curl of oak coming up under the blade is the same curl, exactly the same curl, that has been coming up under that blade for ninety years.

---

When I sleep tonight — Mar to my left, my sister's youngest, Pell, to my right, all three of us under the heavy grey wool — I will dream the water dream. I have had it since I was small. Tonight I think it will be different. I think tonight I will dream of being the drip that falls into Tev's new basin, the first drip, the one that finds out what note the basin makes. I think I will hear that note from the inside.

And I think I will wake, as the others wake, rested, and not afraid, and I will get up in the dim and put on my wool and my canvas and come back down to the shop and pick up the hollowing knife and continue.

This is what I am. A pair of hands, fitting one stave to the next, in a town that is fitting itself to something that listens from below. The work does not end. The pouring is also the cup. The cup, lately, has begun to hum a little under my fingers, and I am — I find I am — glad.

*tik. tik-tik. tok.*

The corner drip. C. Home.

I bend back to the wood.
