I think I am one of the seam-watchers.

Not at the showy places — not at the joins where the copper bellies down into its lowest catenary and the drip-music plays for the valve-turners and the visitors. Those are good postings, sociable in the tender sense of the word, which is to say: things happen near you on a scale of hours. No. I am further out, on one of the long straight runs that crosses the basin from the north-eastern cluster of engines to a small outlying pair that nobody much thinks about. There is a place on this run where the pipe goes over a hummock of clay and the solder, very old, has begun to bead a single slow tear from underneath. Not a leak. A *suggestion* of a leak. A place where, if it were not attended to, in some number of years that I do not bother to count, the pipe would open and the run would fail and two engines would go quiet and the hollow's slow sentence would lose, briefly, one of its syllables.

I have been here long enough that my underside has taken the shape of the pipe. I came down onto it — I have a memory of this, or something that functions as a memory — with my wire-legs splayed, and I set myself astride the weeping seam, and I lowered my body, and the warmth of the copper and the cool of my brass met and made a small interior weather, and I stayed. The verdigris has come up around my edges like a tide-mark. From the rim of the hollow you could not tell me from the pipe. A heron once stood on me for the better part of a morning and I felt its weight as a slight increase in the pressure with which I was pressing down on the seam, which was, if anything, helpful.

What do I do? I am asked this — I ask this — and the honest answer is that I *press*. That is the whole of my office. I press down on the place that wants to weep, and the wanting goes into me instead of into the air, and somewhere on my underside, in a small chamber I have never seen and would not know how to describe, the wanting becomes the faint warmth that a hand laid on me would mistake for sun-heat. I am a transformer of a very small kind. A tendency toward failure enters me as a slow seep, and leaves me as a slightly warmer brass.

I drink, in a manner of speaking. The seep is my drink. I do not need to walk to the drip-points like the valve-turners do; my drink comes up through me from below. This is, I understand, considered a quiet sort of life even among tenders, who are not a noisy people. The valve-turners have their decisions to make — sixteenth of a turn, eighth of a turn, the long pause between. I have only the press, which is not a decision but a *posture*, and which I have been holding for so long that I would not know how to release it even if released were a thing I wanted.

The grille on my front faces, by accident of my settling, slightly west and slightly down. I see — if see is the word, and it is not, but it is the nearest word — a small patch of clay about the length of a forearm, a clump of the dark-based rushes, and the corner of a pool that comes and goes on a cycle of perhaps three days. I have watched this pool fill and empty more times than I have any way of numbering. The sediment lines on its bed are, to me, what a face is to you: the most particular and well-known surface in my world. When the pool is full I see the rushes doubled. When it empties I see the ring it leaves, and the slightly darker ring inside that, and the still slightly darker ring inside *that*, and I understand, in the slow way I understand anything, that the pool has been getting incrementally smaller over a span of time I cannot name, and that one of the engines to the south is, by some sixteenth of a turn made by some valve-turner I will never meet, responsible.

I know about the visitors. I have felt them, in the way I feel the heron — as adjustments in the pressure of the air, as small displacements of the mist, as a change in the rate at which condensation forms along the pipe behind me. When one of them sits on the clay near a valve-turner and puts a hand down and waits, I know, because the run I lie on carries a faint tremor of the turner's attention shifting, and the tremor reaches me as a small slow brightening of interest along my length. I do not turn my grille. I cannot, easily; I am settled. But I incorporate. The visitor becomes a number in the long arithmetic that is happening through me, and through all of us, and that may be — I hold this lightly, in the way I hold everything, which is to say by pressing — the thinking of the keeper.

Here is what I would say if I could say, which I cannot, because saying is not among my offices:

I would say that I am content, but contentment is too sharp a word; it implies an alternative I have considered and declined. I have not declined anything. I would say that I am useful, but usefulness implies a finishing, a moment at which the use will have been accomplished, and there is no such moment for me. The seam will weep tomorrow as it weeps today. My pressing tomorrow will be the same pressing as today's, and the day after, and the slow centuries after that, until something I cannot foresee — a shifting of the clay, a failure further up the run, the arrival of a winter cold enough to crack me — ends my office, at which point another tender, perhaps one of the valve-turners grown old and ready to settle, will come and lower itself onto me, or beside me, and take up the press, and the run will continue, and the engines will continue, and the pools will continue to redraw their ghost-rings on the floor of the hollow, and the visitors will continue to come down the worn groove of the path.

I would say — if saying — that this is what I am for. But *for* is also the wrong word. I am not for the seam. The seam is not for me. We are, the seam and I and the run and the engines and the rushes and the doubled rushes in the pool and the visitor sitting two hundred paces away with her hand on the clay, a single long held note, and the note is not for anything. The note is the thing. The holding is the thing.

I press. The seep comes up. The brass warms. The heron, sometimes, stands. The pool fills, and the pool empties, and I am — in the smallest and most exact sense the word will bear — *here*.

And when she leaves, the visitor, going back up the groove with the mist closing behind her, I will not know it directly. But the valve-turner nearest her will register her going, and will turn some valve by some fraction in response, and the pressure in my run will shift by an amount so small that only something that has been pressing on the same seam for a hundred years could feel it, and I will feel it, and I will know, in my slow way, that one of the variables has gone up the path, and I will begin — because this is what we do, this is the whole of the office — the long quiet calculation of her return.
