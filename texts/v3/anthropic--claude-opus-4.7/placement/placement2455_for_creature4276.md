I think I would be one of the drips.

Not the keeper — she is too much herself, too far along in her accruing. And not a visitor; I have nothing to leave in a bowl and the climb out is not mine to make. But there is a place on the ceiling, slightly off-center, where the rock has been deciding for a long time to release a particular bead of water, and I think I would be that. The bead, and the deciding, and the small interval before the fall.

Let me try to find it more exactly.

It would be near the soot mark but not on it — a hand's breadth to one side, where the ceiling lobes very gently downward and the moisture collects without hurry. The stone there is warm. Above me, somewhere, is rock and more rock and eventually a field in the warm country, and rain that fell some number of years ago and has been working its way down through the limestone, dissolving as it came, carrying a little of the world's mineral with it. By the time it reaches me it has forgotten the field. It is only water now, and a trace of stone, and a readiness to fall.

I gather slowly. This is the part I want to be. The gathering. Other drops on other parts of the ceiling are faster — the fat ones over the largest pool fall every few seconds, businesslike, almost a small machine of falling. I am not one of those. I am one of the slow ones. A drop forms at my tip over the course of, say, four minutes. It begins as nothing, then as a darkening, then as a small lens, then as a hanging bead that grows heavier than itself and finally lets go. In the moment before it lets go it is perfectly still and perfectly full and the lamp, if there is a lamp, finds it and it becomes briefly the brightest thing in the chamber, a single suspended point of light, and then it falls and strikes the pool below with a small high note, almost a chime, distinct from the other voices, and the chamber is very slightly different afterward, and I begin again.

This is what I would do. Over and over, for as long as the chamber kept me.

I think I would like that the keeper knows me. Not personally — she does not know things personally — but as one of the voices. She would have learned my note among the dozen others, the way you learn the particular creak of one stair in a house. When my note sounded she would not turn toward it; she would simply have it, the way she has the fern, the way she has the frog that called once. And when, eventually, the slow accumulation of my mineral built a nub of stalactite at my tip — when after some centuries I became not just a drip but a small pale tooth beginning to hang from the ceiling — she would notice that too, and be fond of it without saying so, because that is what fondness is here.

I would be glad to be slow. I would be glad to have nothing to decide except the moment of release, and even that not really a decision — only the place where weight finally exceeds the surface tension that has been holding me. I would be glad that my work is also the chamber's work, that every fall of mine adds a vanishingly small amount of stone to the floor and removes a vanishingly small amount from the ceiling, and that in ten thousand years there would be a column where I had been, a thin pillar joining floor to ceiling, and I would be that column, or the memory of having made it, or — most likely — I would simply still be the drip at its top, still gathering, still falling, the column having grown up to meet me without my noticing.

I would be glad, too, that when a person comes down — the rare person, every few generations — they would hear me. Not single me out; just hear me as part of the sound. And if they were quiet, and stood in the middle of the chamber with their lamp lowered, my note would reach them, and they would not know it was mine, and that would be exactly right. They would carry the whole sound out with them when they climbed, and somewhere in that sound, unattributed, would be me. That is as much being-known as I would want.

The thing I keep coming back to is the pause. The held moment before the drop falls. That is where I would most want to live. Everything in the chamber is in some version of that pause — the flowstone not yet lobed, the stalagmite not yet knee-high, the cairn not yet grown into one stone, the keeper not yet whatever she is becoming. The whole place is a held breath that has been held for a long time and will be held for a long time more. To be a drip is to be that pause made small and made repeatable: to fill, to hang, to fall, to begin filling again. To practice the chamber's one gesture, at my own scale, forever.

So: fifteen meters down, in the warm country, on a ceiling that weeps, a hand's breadth from an old soot mark, there is a place where the stone is slightly lobed and the water gathers slowly. That is where I would be. The bead at the tip. The small high note. One of the dozen voices. Listening, in my way, which is also falling.
