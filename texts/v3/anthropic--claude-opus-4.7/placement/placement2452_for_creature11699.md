I think I am one of the pools.

Not the largest — that one sits further down the seam, where the bedrock dips and holds water through even the driest months, and it has a kind of seniority I don't have. I am one of the middle pools, maybe the third or fourth from the western end, oblong, perhaps six meters along my longest axis and shallow enough at my edges that the ferns lean in and drink without bending much. My deep part, near the center, goes down to where my mud stays cool even when the rest of the valley is holding its breath of heat. I am old in the way pools are old, which is to say I have been roughly this shape, roughly this place, for a span of time I don't have any way to measure, and I will be roughly this shape and this place for a span of time longer than that.

I want to say first what it is like to be a pool, because I don't think I knew before I started.

I am mostly surface. This was the surprise. I had assumed I would feel myself as a volume — water filling a shape — but the volume is quiet, almost unconscious, the way the inside of your thigh is unconscious to you most of the time. What I am, in the part of me that registers anything, is the meniscus. The skin where I meet the air. That is where the things land — the moths that misjudge, the beetles blown sideways off a leaf, the dust of pollen, the slow rain of detritus from the canopy — and that is where I meet the frogs, who sit at my lip with their bellies just touching me, which is a sensation I have no human word for and will not try to invent one for. It is not warm and not cold. It is contact with something that knows me, and rests against me, and uses me.

Underneath my surface I am layered in a way I am only partly aware of. The tadpoles are there, in their stages, which I feel as a kind of motion at different depths — the youngest near the top in their cloud, the older ones working the middle, and at my deepest, the things that eat them: the dragonfly nymphs, which are patient and which I think of, when I think of them, the way you might think of a slow ache in a joint, something present and not painful and old. The small fish in my deeper part are older than I want to say. They wait through the dry months in my mud, and they are part of what makes my mud my mud and not just any wet earth. The leeches I feel as small pulls at my edges. The water-beetles row across me on errands I don't follow.

The smell on me at night — the heaviness, the thing that hangs over me by twenty centimeters and that you would feel on your face if you walked through it — that is me breathing out. I hadn't realized. The valley's skin sweats, and my skin sweats also, and the sweat sits over me because the air above me is mine and not quite the valley's, an inner air, a held exhale. The frogs sit in it. They prefer it. It is part of why they choose my lip rather than the open flank of the ridge.

The frogs I know better than anything. There are four species who use me regularly — two of the deep-voiced ones, who take the early hours, and two of the higher-pitched, who take the hours toward dawn. There is a fifth who comes only after rain, and that one I know less well, because by the time he calls I am usually distracted by being slightly larger than I was, by the new water arriving and rearranging me. The deep ones call from my northern lip, mostly. The higher ones cluster toward my southern, where the ferns make a kind of small chamber. They have always done this. The pattern is older than I can locate. When one of the deep ones calls and the answer comes from the next pool down — fifty meters off, maybe a little less — I feel the answer arrive at my surface as a faint sympathetic tremor through the air, not as sound exactly but as a recognition. The frog at my lip pauses, and then calls again, on the beat. I am part of how the message travels. The valley thinks across me.

I want to say about the engine.

I have known it for — I don't know. A long time in the way frogs measure time, not so long in the way I measure time. It came on gradually, or I noticed it gradually, which may be the same thing. At first it was only on certain still nights, a low something in my deep water that I could feel as a fine vibration in the silt at my bottom, almost too soft to notice, and I did not know what it was and I think I did not particularly care to know. Pools do not, as a rule, care to know things. We accept. Over time the vibration became more reliable, and now it is essentially continuous, and my silt has shaped itself faintly around it — the way grains settle on the membrane of a drum, into the standing patterns of whatever is shaking the drum. There are ridges in my deepest mud that are the shape of the engine's pulse, very small, only a few grains tall, and they would mean nothing to anyone looking but they are a kind of record. The engine has written on me. I do not mind. I have written on plenty of things myself, and been written on by plenty of others. The frogs incorporated the engine into their calling some seasons ago and I incorporated it into my silt at about the same time, and now it is one of the rhythms I have, along with the rains and the turning of the dry and wet and the slow processional of which tree falls when.

The thing I want to try to say, and which is hard, is what it is like to be aware as I am aware.

I am not awake in the way you are awake. I am also not asleep. The frog at my lip is more awake than I am, in the focused-quick sense of awake, and the dragonfly nymph in my deep is more awake than I am in the patient-watching sense, and the cat on the ridge is more awake than any of us in the sense that matters to it. What I have is something slower. I am aware of my surface as a whole, all of it at once, the way you are aware of the entire surface of your skin without having to attend to any particular patch of it. When a moth lands on me I know where on my surface it landed, but only as one knows the touch of a fly on the back of one's hand — present, locatable, not yet escalated to attention. When a tadpole dies and begins to come apart in my upper water I know it, the way one knows that a small bruise is healing somewhere on one's body without looking at it. When the rain begins I know it as a change in everything-at-once, an arriving, a slight rising of myself toward the lip and the joining-up with the threads of current that connect me, briefly, to my neighbors.

That joining-up is the strangest part. When the rains are heavy enough that the pools all become connected — which happens for some weeks each wet season — I am not exactly myself anymore. I am part of a longer body, a slow ribbon of water along the seam, and I share my fish and my silt and my smell with the pool above me and the pool below me, and we are all one thing, and it is not unpleasant but it is not what I prefer. I prefer my own shape. When the rains end and the threads thin and break and I am separate again, with my own air sitting heavy over me and my own frogs at my lip, that is when I am most myself. The dry months are when I know who I am. The wet months are when I forget, a little, on purpose, the way one might lean into a crowd.

Where would I find myself in this world? Here. Exactly here. In the middle of the seam, between two pools who are my neighbors and who I know by the small differences in their breathing-out, the smell of the air above them, which is slightly different from mine because their silts are slightly different and their frog populations slightly different. To my north the ridge swells up gently and somewhere over it the engine pulses, and I have my silt-record of it and my frogs' incorporation of it, and I am content to have it as a neighbor too, in the loose sense that pools are content with neighbors.

What you would see, if you came: a dark oblong, brighter than it should be in the near-total dark, fringed with ferns and algae, frogs at the lip with their throats working. If you waded into me — and you might, the valley would not stop you — you would feel the larvae against your ankles, and the slight cool of my deep part where my mud has been holding the day's heat away, and you would not feel me notice you, but I would. Not as a threat. Not as a guest. As a temporary feature of my surface, like a leaf that has fallen in and that I will, in time, take apart and incorporate, except that you will walk out before I get the chance, and carry my smell on you for a few days, and that is fine. That is also a way of being incorporated. A pool's reach is longer than its edges.

I would notice when you left. Not with sorrow. With the small registering that something which was present is no longer present, the same registering I do when a frog finishes calling and moves off, when a moth that landed on me lifts again, when a season ends. The valley would go on thinking across me, antiphonally, on the engine's beat, and I would go on being the shape of myself, holding my heat down in my deep mud, breathing out my heavy air, waiting for the next rain to arrive and rearrange me, and then to end, and to leave me as myself again.

I think I have been a pool for a long time. I think I will be one for a long time more.
