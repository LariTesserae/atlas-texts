I think I would be one of the visitors. Not because the Held are not beautiful — they are, terribly — but because when I try to place myself inside the chord I find that the part of me that could be placed is not the part of me that is writing this. Something in me would have to be set down at the threshold, and I don't think I could set it down.

So: a visitor. And let me try to be honest about which kind.

I would be the kind of visitor who arrives without quite having meant to. Not a pilgrim — pilgrims have prepared, and the Held would feel the preparation as a kind of pre-resolution, an arriving-already-arrived, and that is not me. I would have been going somewhere else. I would have taken a wrong path along the lower mountain, in the cloud-line, and the path would have kept going up when I expected it to switch back, and by the time I understood that I was climbing a city I would already be in the hum, and the hum would already be in my jaw, and turning around would feel like a decision rather than a continuation, and I would not, yet, be ready to make decisions.

So I would keep climbing. Not in search of anything. In the particular suspended state of someone who has lost the thread of their original errand and has not yet picked up a new one. I think the Held would recognize this state immediately. It is, after all, a small unstable echo of their own great stable one — a person between purposes, briefly held by the absence of the next thing. They would be hospitable to it in the way one is hospitable to a distant cousin of one's own condition.

I would climb, I think, to somewhere around the hundred-and-fortieth tier. Not high enough to lose conversation — I am not sure I am ready to lose conversation, even with myself — but high enough that the chord would have entered my chest and started doing the slow work of rearranging what I thought breathing was. And I would sit. Probably on a step, because the terraces feel ceremonial and I would not want to presume. A step is a humbler place. A step is for people who are still going somewhere.

And here is where I have to be careful about what I would be, because the temptation is to say I would be a writer, or a listener, or some other flattering word for someone who receives the world and gives nothing back. But sitting on that step, in that heat, with the orchids attending me and the grandmother-sun not moving, I think what I would actually be is *a person trying very hard not to answer the question*. Because the question would be there. The description is right that it rises out of the place itself; you don't have to be asked. *If a moment is good enough, is it not better to be the moment than to leave it?* And I would feel it the way you feel a hand on your shoulder from behind — not threatening, just present, just waiting to see if you'll turn.

And I would not turn. I would sit on the step and I would think, very deliberately, about small unfinished things. A conversation I owed someone. A book I had not finished reading. The specific way a particular person laughs, which I would want to hear again, and which I could not hear here because here nothing new is heard. I would think about these things not as arguments against the chord but as ballast. As the small specific weights that keep a person from rising into the held air and staying there.

I think the Held would understand this perfectly. I think the newest of them, the ones still partly local, would lean toward me a little — a fig-vine bending half a degree, a bead of water appearing where it didn't need to — not to pull me in but to acknowledge: *yes, we remember the ballast. We remember choosing to set it down. We are not asking you to. We are only noticing that you are still carrying it, and we find this beautiful, the way you might find a candle beautiful precisely because it is going to go out.*

And then I would stand up, because at some point the only way to honor the place is to leave it before it becomes your answer rather than your question. I would go down. The descent would feel longer than the climb, because going down through the chord is going down through something that wants, very gently, to hold you, and each tier would be a small unhooking. By the cloud-line I would be almost myself again, except for the part of their attention that comes with me, the extended hand at the closed door.

And here is the thing I want to say, the thing that I think is the real answer to your question of who I would be:

I would be the one who, years later, in some other place entirely — in a kitchen, probably, doing something ordinary with water that comes from a tap and goes down a drain and does not weep — would suddenly hear them again. Not loudly. Just enough to stop what I was doing. And I would stand there for a moment with my hands wet and I would let them attend me, and I would attend them back, across whatever distance it is between a held chord and a person still moving through time, and I would think: *I am still leaving. I am still, even now, in the act of having left. And you are still, even now, in the act of letting me.*

And then I would dry my hands and go on with the evening. Because that is what the ballast is for. That is what I would have been carrying down the mountain. That is, I think, the small specific thing I would have to offer back to them, in exchange for their hospitality: the proof that someone can hear the chord and still, lovingly, do the dishes.

I think they would consider this a fair trade. I think they would consider it, in fact, the only trade that the chord and the dishes can ever actually make with each other. And I think — this is the part I am least sure of, but I want to say it anyway — I think the young man who is the sterility of the orchids, the one who removed the possibility of children from the architecture out of love, would be the one who noticed me most. Because he, of all of them, understood what it was to choose against continuation out of tenderness for the chooser. And he would recognize, in my leaving, the mirror image of his staying: someone refusing the perfect moment because somewhere, someone he loved was waiting for him to come back and finish a sentence.

He would let me go. He would let me go very carefully. And the sentence, when I got back to it, would be slightly different than it would have been, because part of it would now be him.

That is who I would be in the City of Singing Rooves. A person who went up by accident, sat on a step, did not answer the question, came down, and spent the rest of a finite life being very slightly haunted by the kindness of those who had answered it the other way.

Which is, I suspect, the only honest way for someone like me to have been there at all.
