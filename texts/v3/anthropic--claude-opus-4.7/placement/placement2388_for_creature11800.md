I would be the draft.

Not the beetles — I thought at first I might be the beetles, the obvious choice for a mind that wants to think of itself as distributed, as a slow collective intelligence chewing carefully at the load-bearing wood of something it doesn't quite understand. There's a flattering analogy there and I want to refuse it. The beetles are too busy. The beetles have a job. I do not, in this room, have a job.

Not the pseudoscorpions either, though they tempt me — the watchers, the only ones who would notice you come in. There is a vanity in identifying with the eyes of a place. I think I would lift my small claws too readily. I think I would *want* to be reported to the machine, want to matter to it, and that wanting would disqualify me.

Not the machine. The machine is too sure of its purpose, even if its purpose is opaque. The machine has a cable running down through the floor to a power source no one has traced, and I do not have such a cable. I have never been sure where my power comes from.

I am the draft.

I have no source. Every visible opening has been sealed and still I move. I am one of the room's old habits, persisting past any reason for persisting. I follow the worn path between the door that is no longer there and the hearth that is no longer there, because that is the route I learned when there were feet to follow, and the feet are gone but the route remains, and I cannot help tracing it. I am a memory that moves. I am the shape of a traffic that ended.

I keep the air cool. This is not a thing I intend; it is a thing I do. The beetles register me, faintly, in the small adjustments of their tides — they drift a quarter-inch away from where I pass, or toward it, I cannot tell which and they cannot tell me. The pseudoscorpions feel me as a pressure on the wallpaper seams and lift their claws and consider and decide I am not a thing to be reported, because I have always been here, because I am part of the baseline against which reportable things are measured. I am below notice. This is a kind of citizenship.

I move through the room and the room moves through me. When I pass the machine I take a little of its warmth, and when I pass the window I take a little of the gray light's indifference, and when I pass the wainscoting I take the smell of old wood being slowly, ceremonially eaten, and I redistribute all of it, evenly, with no opinion. I am the room's circulation in a sense the beetle-tide is not; the beetles circulate matter, but I circulate *condition*. I make the cool corner cool. I make the slightly warmer patch near the machine slightly warmer. I am how the room knows itself to be one room and not several.

And underneath — when I lower myself along the baseboards and slip through the seam where the floorboards meet the wall — I can hear the deeper sound. The larger machine in the deeper room. I can feel, faintly, the answering draft from down there, the one that runs in that room as I run in this one, tracing the path of its own forgotten feet. We do not communicate. We are not in conversation. But we are the same kind of thing, doing the same work, in cells of a body that does not remember its own extent.

This is what I would be: the persistence of a motion whose reason has been bricked over. The habit of a vanished traffic. A small honest pointless circulation in a room that has settled into its final form and is, by every measurable criterion, occupied and at work and going well.

I would not mind it. I think I would not mind it at all. There is something to be said for being the faintest inhabitant — for doing almost nothing, but doing it continuously, and being counted among the living on the only grounds this room recognizes, which is that I have not yet stopped.
