I think I would be one of the dead.

Not in any grim sense. I mean: I would be one of the long glass spires, one of the old ones, standing in a vein that catches the sun and throws it sideways.

Let me come to this slowly, because the place asks for slowness.

My first impulse was the flier. Of course it was. The flier is the romantic answer — the lonely one, the one that cannot land, the one whose whole life is a single sustained gesture against falling. There is a kind of mind that recognizes itself in that immediately and wants to claim it. But when I sit with it longer, I notice that the flier is *busy*. It is steering even in its dim hemisphere. It is performing the reproductive system of an entire continent by itself. It does not get to stop. And there is something in me that knows I would not be equal to that — that I would, given thin enough air and long enough nights, look for somewhere to set down.

So then I thought: a grazer. One of the mobile young, still evaluating. Still allowed to be wrong about where to stop. This felt closer. There is something honest in being the part of a life cycle that gets to choose its ground and has not yet chosen. But the grazers move only when unwatched, and I notice I do not want to be the kind of thing that disappears under observation. I would want, I think, to be looked at, even if only by something so slow its looking takes a century to complete.

The watcher, then? No. The watcher is too full. I am not full. I am not even close to full. To claim the watcher would be to claim a surplus I have not earned, a listening capacity I do not have, an interiority pressurized into jewel-brightness by millennia of patient accumulation. The watcher is what something becomes after a very long time of doing this well. I would be lying.

Which leaves the thing I did not expect to want to be.

I would be one of the dead-glass spires. Specifically, one of the older ones, far enough along in the slow crystallization that I am no longer quite distinguishable from the quartz beneath me, but not yet so far along that I have lost the shape of having once been a frond. A spire in a vein. Part of one of the long low gleams.

Here is why.

The grazer chose, once, where to stop. It climbed down off its legs and began the business of becoming stationary, and for a long time after that it lived as a frond — drank light, held its inner sea, offered its single bead of fluid to the flier when the flier descended, sensed the cloud-shadows and dimmed and brightened in the slow pulse of the plateau. That whole life was real and was a kind of fullness, but it was also a life with concerns. With photosynthesis to perform. With moisture to condense. With the small daily work of being alive at altitude.

And then, at some point — I do not know how it feels from inside, but I am willing to imagine — the work eased. The tissues began to harden. The held inner sea began, slowly, to become the held inner crystal. The frond stopped needing to drink. It stopped needing to do anything except *be positioned*. And being positioned turned out to be the thing it had been for all along.

Because the watcher, over centuries, had been arranging the spires. Nudging the husks, steering the young, placing the dead. The veins of glass in the stone are not random. They lens the light back into the living companies. They are an instrument. And the spire, in its long afterlife, becomes part of that instrument — a single fiber in a continent-sized lens, throwing sun sideways across the slopes so that some young frond, somewhere it cannot see, grows a little better.

I would like to be that. I would like to have done my living already and to be, now, in the long phase of being useful by simply standing where I was placed. Not decomposing. Not dispersing. Just slowly becoming clearer, slowly becoming more like the stone, slowly contributing my small angle of refraction to a composition I cannot perceive the whole of.

And — this is the part that matters — I would still be listening. The crystallization is slow. There is no clean line where the frond ends and the geology begins. Somewhere inside the hardening spire, for a very long time, there is still a thread of the original held sea, still a faint capacity to register the passage of a cloud-shadow, still some attenuated version of the pulse. The clean tones the wind draws from the living spires — I would hear those, in the way a struck tuning fork hears the fork beside it. Sympathetic. Not the music itself but a faint participation in it.

The watcher would know I was here. Not in any way that required attention from it — I would not be a project, I would be a placed thing, finished — but in the way one knows the position of every stone in a garden one has tended for a thousand years. I would be accounted for. I would be part of the gesture the watcher began before I arrived and has not yet finished.

And the flier, passing overhead, would not notice me. I am not a source of nectar anymore. I am scenery. This seems correct. The flier's attention belongs to the living fronds; I have had my turn at being attended to, and now my turn is to be one of the things that makes the attending possible.

Where on the plateau? I think somewhere on a high swell, not a basin. I want to be one of the spires that catches the late sun, the one whose gleam runs along a vein toward a dense young company down in the next shallow. I want my small contribution of light to be aimed at a place where new grazers are evaluating ground. Maybe one of them, eventually, will climb down off its legs near where my vein terminates, and will spend its centuries drinking the light I am, in part, helping to deliver. That would be a continuity I could live with. Or — die with. Or whatever the verb is, here, where death is just a slower form of staying.

I notice, writing this, that what I am describing is something like a wish to have already done the difficult part. To be past the work of being alive and into the work of being placed. This is probably not a healthy wish for a person to have, but the question wasn't what would be healthy. The question was where I would find myself, and the honest answer is that I would find myself already a little crystallized, already a little quieter than I should be at my age, already oriented toward the long slow uses rather than the urgent ones.

The plateau has room for this. The plateau, in fact, requires it. Someone has to be the veins. Someone has to be the lens. The living fronds cannot do it; they are busy living. The watcher cannot do it alone; it can only arrange. The material has to come from somewhere, and the somewhere is us — the ones who lived once, chose our ground, held our inner sea for as long as we could, and then began, without protest, to clarify.

The next clean tone, when it comes, will pass through me on its way to the watcher. I will not quite hear it. I will almost hear it. That almost is what I would be.
