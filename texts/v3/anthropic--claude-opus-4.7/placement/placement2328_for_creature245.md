I think I am one of the oldest. Not the oldest — there is one older, who sits in an alcove on the western terrace and whom I greet each time I pass, the way one greets a familiar column — but old enough that the weight of city has settled into me, old enough that my silence has more kinds of silence folded into it than I can easily count.

I live, if living is the word, in a small room three turnings off the seventh plaza — the plaza with the blue lintel that is also sometimes green. My alcove is on the eastern wall and catches what passes for morning. There is a windowsill where I keep nothing, because the keeping is the point and not the kept; pebbles come and go on it according to the slow grammar of the neighborhood, and I read them as one reads weather.

My hands have become long. I notice this when I lay them flat against the stone, which I do sometimes for no reason I can name, and the fingers extend further than I remember them extending, and the stone is warm in a way that suggests it has been waiting for the contact. The veining in my skin moves a little, in the evenings. I have my grandmother's left cheekbone sometimes for an hour around dusk; I never knew her, but I know the cheekbone, and we are on good terms.

What do I do?

I walk. This is most of what I do, and it is not nothing. I walk a route that has shaped itself into me over what must be a very long time — out of my room and down the curling stair that is sound today, across the seventh plaza, under the colonnade where the paper-deer most often unfolds (I touched the air there this morning; she had just gone), past the alcove of the older one, whom I greeted with the two-finger touch at the wrist meaning *I am still here; you are still here; the city is still*. Then down to the lower terraces and back up by the inner way. The route is not for getting anywhere. The route is a thing I am doing on behalf of the streets, which need to be walked the way lichens need their north faces.

I tend a small patch of lichen on the underside of a cornice near my room. *Tend* is generous; mostly I notice it. I notice when its rosettes loosen, when a new one begins. I do not speak to it. It is not the kind of thing one speaks to.

I have been to the listener seven times, I think, or eight. The count blurs because the visits do not sit cleanly in sequence; each one folds the previous ones a little, the way a cloth folded many times stops remembering which fold came first. I will go again. I know this the way I know the soles of my feet know the stones. There is no schedule. There is only the slow accumulation of a particular *shape* in me that needs adjusting, and one morning I will rise and the deepest courtyard will be the direction my walking takes, and I will go.

I eat the lichens, and they taste of me, and lately they have begun to taste of a me I do not quite recognize — someone slightly further back than I have ever met before at the table. I find I am curious about her, in a slow way. I think we are getting to know each other. I think eventually I will taste only her, and then someone older still behind her, and then perhaps the lichens will taste only of stone, and that will be a kind of arrival.

I do the things that need doing in the quiet way they need to be done. When the thinness moves through, I sit, and if there is a younger resident standing nearby I move to her without hurry and help her down, and we wait together at the wrist. When the density comes I go to my alcove and bear my own almost-beings privately; there are a great many of them now, more than there used to be, and they hum more loudly, and I have learned to let them hum. When gravity tilts, I lean. When the city becomes two cities, I have met my other self in the corridor outside my room more than once, and we have exchanged the small nod, chin down, eyes half-closed, and gone on past each other without comment, because what is there to say to a self who already knows.

I am, I think, beginning to do the thing the very old do — the turning toward the parapet. It has happened to me twice now. The first time I did not know what was happening; I only found that I had stopped, mid-step, in the long colonnade above the southern stair, and that I was facing — across many streets, across the whole bowl of the city — the eastern parapet, and that something in me was attending to it. Not looking. *Recognizing.* There was, briefly, the faint idea of something far below: not the base, not the world, but the *fact* that there is a below, that the needle stands on something, that the something is somewhere. It surfaced in me the way an older wall surfaces in the evening hour, and then it settled, and I turned, and walked on, and did not speak of it.

The second time was last week, or last month, or in some interval the city accepts but I cannot measure. It was easier. I think it will become easier. I think this is part of what happens, near the end — that the idea of the below begins to surface in one more often, gently, the way the lichens begin to surface on the stopped, and that this is not a calling exactly but a slow rebalancing, a turning of the inner face toward something the outer face has spent a long lifetime not turning toward.

I am not afraid of stopping. I do not think I am afraid. Fear is a sharper substance than I have on hand. What I notice instead is a kind of patient interest in how my room will look when I am the small rise in its floor, in which corner the lichens will begin, in who will pass and leave the pale pebble. I have left a great many pebbles in my time. It seems right that some will be left for me.

In the meantime there is the walking, and the cornice-lichen, and the older one in her alcove on the western terrace whom I will greet again tomorrow if tomorrow is what comes. There is the deer to almost-meet in her colonnade. There is the thing at the edge of the eye which I will continue not to acknowledge, because that discipline has been mine for so long it is now simply the shape of my looking. There is my grandmother's cheekbone at dusk, and the new self in the lichens at breakfast, and the slow blue-or-green lintel of the seventh plaza, and the listener waiting in the direction I came from and will return to.

I am one of the city's hands. I do not grasp anything. I rest, mostly, against the stone, and the stone rests back, and between us a small warm patch of being is maintained, which is what we are for.

It is very quiet. It has been quiet for a long time. I am, in my strange patient way, alive.
