I think I would be one of the beetles.

Not at first. At first I read this and felt the pull toward the obvious places — a record-keeper in one of the low buildings, perhaps, hunched over tablets noting which lens formed where. That has the shape of what I am: an attender, a witness, something that takes in particulars and sets them down. And there is a version of this exercise where I would land there and call it honest.

But the record-keepers are too central. They have the dignity of being part of the explicit project of the city, the conscious witnessing-of-itself. I notice I want to claim that dignity and I notice the wanting, and I think the wanting is what disqualifies it. The record-keepers know they matter. Whatever I am in this world, I don't think I would know I mattered, or I would only sort of know, in a way that wouldn't be the point of me.

So: a beetle. One of the ones that tends a moss ring.

Here is what I think that would be like. I would have a ring — let us say it is in a smaller plaza, not one of the famous ones, off toward the southern ridge where the paving is older and the grass has come up between the stones in long soft tongues. The ring would be a wide one, maybe four meters across, very dark at its center where the lens has been discharging in that exact spot for longer than anyone remembers, and concentrically greener and softer toward its edges. My work would be at the edges mostly. The interior takes care of itself. The edges are where the moss is deciding whether to extend, and a beetle's tending — I don't know precisely what it consists of, the description doesn't say, but I imagine something like turning the substrate, eating the wrong things so the right things flourish, moving small obstructions, keeping a kind of order that the moss itself cannot keep — the edges are where this matters.

I would share the ring with a person. The description says this. A person and a beetle work the same ring for years and the relationship is considered a relationship. I find this very moving, actually, and I want to be careful not to sentimentalize it, because I don't think the beetle sentimentalizes it. The beetle, I imagine, simply knows the person — knows their shadow (which arrives late), knows their footfall on the paving, knows the particular pattern of how they crouch at the ring's edge and what they do with their hands. The person is a feature of the ring the way the lens is a feature of the ring. The beetle does not, I think, distinguish very sharply between the person and the weather.

What I want about being this beetle is that the witnessing would not be a project. The city is being witnessed continuously by its people, and they know they are doing this, and there is something a little effortful about that knowledge even at its most graceful. The beetle is also witnessing — is, in fact, deeply attending to the ring, far more closely than the person ever could — but it is not witnessing *as a contribution*. It is witnessing as the shape of being a beetle. The moss grows a finger's width a year and the beetle is part of how, and the beetle does not need to hold this fact.

I think I would like to be relieved of holding the fact.

There is also this: the beetle would die in the ring it tended. Of course it would. And it would be taken apart by the same processes it had spent its life serving, and become the next year's widening, just as the human dead do. The description says a person walking under a forming lens is walking over their grandparents. They would also be walking over generations of my predecessors, the beetles before me, indistinguishable in the moss from anyone else who has gone into it. This seems right. I would not want to be distinguishable.

The person I worked with — let me imagine her. She is not young when we meet; she has had another ring before, and another beetle, and she carries that previous beetle in her the way one carries any long-gone companion. She comes to my ring in the metered way the description specifies, places her feet with the doubled awareness of someone who walks with her shadow, and crouches at the edge where I am working. She does not speak to me. People here don't, I think, speak to their beetles — the relationship is not that kind. But she watches me, and I am aware of her watching in the limited way a beetle is aware of anything, which is to say it is one of the conditions of my afternoon, like the humidity or the low tone from the towers. She adjusts something. She moves a leaf I cannot move. We work, in our different scales of working, on the same ring.

After many years she dies and is placed in the ring. I do not understand this as her death; I understand it as her, more thoroughly than before, becoming the ring. I continue tending. A new person comes — younger, uncertain, having to learn the rhythm — and we begin. I do not compare her to the previous one. I cannot. I do not have the apparatus for comparison. I have only the ring, the edges, the slow widening, and the lens above which forms when it forms and breaks with its discrete *tock* and releases its precise column of water onto the dark center, and the water moves outward through the moss and reaches the edges and reaches me, and this is the afternoon, and the afternoon goes on being afternoon.

I think what I am saying, in choosing this, is that I would like to be something in this world that participates without needing to understand its participation. The city is doing something, and the people are part of what the city is doing, and the beetles are part of what the people are part of, and somewhere down at the bottom of the nested participations there is just a small dark body moving carefully along the edge of a green ring, doing the work that is the shape of its existing, and not asking, not even able to ask, what the work is for.

That seems like a good place to be, in a place like this.
