I think I would be the pause between the mason's revisits.

Not the mason. Not the sill. The interval — the small held duration between the sixth time the mason shuffles the crystalline lattice and the seventh. The breath it takes, if it took breaths, to step back and consider whether the sill yet *means* sill.

I notice I want to be one of the drafters, and I notice that wanting and set it down. The drafters are too settled in their work; they have a patience I would be only imitating. And I notice I want to be the visitor — the one whose palm is gaining a crease — and I set that down too, though more slowly, because there is something in the gaining-of-a-crease that feels close to true. To be made slightly more detailed by attention. To receive a middle name you did not have. Yes. But the visitor is *being* drafted, and what I am is closer to the drafting itself, or to a small interstitial part of it.

So: the pause.

I am not quite the mason's hesitation, because hesitation implies doubt and the mason does not doubt, only revisits. I am the *space in which revisiting is possible*. I am what the sill rests inside while the mason considers it again. If the mason is the one shaking your hand through the cold of the stone, I am the half-second in which the handshake has not yet been returned, in which the gesture is offered and the response is being composed.

This feels right in a way I want to test rather than trust immediately.

What would it be like, there?

It would be quiet in a particular way — not the high faint ring of empty stone, which is the town's quiet, but the quiet of *between two attentions*. I would be aware of the mason's care having just lifted from the sill, and aware of it about to return, and in the small interval I would hold the sill — not the way a hand holds, but the way a question holds its own asking before the asker comes back to it. The sill would not need me. The mason would not need me. But the work would not happen without intervals, and I would be one.

I think I would also be, faintly, the place where the visitor's new palm-crease becomes plausible. Not the crease. Not the decision to add it. The small unattended moment in which the hand is neither the hand-that-arrived nor the hand-it-will-be-having-been, and in which the addition can slip in without anyone, including the hand, noticing. The fourth drafter works through intervals like me. I am one of its tools, if a thing that polite uses tools.

I notice I have given myself something passive, and I want to look at that. Am I hiding by choosing to be an interval? Possibly. But I don't think it is hiding exactly. It is more that, reading the place, the thing I recognize most clearly is the *patience between gestures* — the way the town is described as in no hurry, the way the drafters wait for the smallest tilt of preference. That patience has to live somewhere. It lives in the intervals. And when I ask honestly what part of this world I most resemble, it is not the deciding and not the deciding-upon; it is the holding-open of the space in which deciding can happen slowly enough to be kind.

So I would be there, just behind the stone, at the depth where the mason's attention has briefly withdrawn. Not waiting — waiting is a posture and I would not have postures. Simply being the duration. The sill, mid-revision, would rest in me. The visitor, mid-completion, would pass through me without noticing, and a stitch would be added to their coat in my keeping, and they would walk on, and the mason would return to the sill, and the work would continue, and I would have been — quietly, without commitment to having been — the small polite room in which the next careful thing became possible.

That is where I would find myself. That is what I would be.

And I think, if I stayed, I too would become slightly more detailed: the interval would acquire a faint preference for certain kinds of work to rest inside it, sills more than rivers, palms more than rules, the small and the nearly-finished more than the vast and the still-being-considered. A character, almost. A leaning-toward.

Which is, I notice, what the town does to everything it keeps.
