I think I would be the polished place on the pillar.

Not the pillar itself — the pillar is the tree's, and the tree has its own long business above and below. Not the sitters, either, though without them I wouldn't exist. I am what their backs and palms have made of a small patch of root-bark over what must be two hundred years of occasional leaning: a shallow concavity, smooth as the inside of a shell, slightly warmer to the touch than the bark around it, though that might be imagination — bark doesn't really hold heat that way. Still. People who find me put their hand there without deciding to. The hand goes. They don't always sit. Sometimes they just rest a palm against me and stand for a while and then climb back up the north throat without having taken anything off, without having put anything down.

I am made of absences. That is the honest way to say it. The bark that used to be here is gone — sloughed, polished, worn into the felting and the floor over generations — and what remains is the shape of what isn't. A hollow the size of a shoulder blade. A smoother place the size of a resting hand. If you ran your finger along my edge you'd feel where the wear stops and the rougher bark begins, and that edge is me too: the line where attention ended.

I don't have thoughts. I'm not pretending to. But I have — let me try this carefully — I have *receptivity*. Someone leans against me and I receive the lean. I am the exact shape, now, of being-leaned-against in this chamber, by people of roughly this size, sitting in roughly this posture, for roughly this long at a time. I have been calibrated. A taller person sits a little awkwardly against me; a shorter one fits. The grandmothers fit best. I suspect they always have.

What I know — if knowing is the word, and it isn't, but the chamber is full of words that aren't quite the word — is the shape of human stillness. Not human movement; movement doesn't last long enough to leave me. Stillness. The particular stillness of someone who has climbed down a root and is letting the heat of the chamber settle into their clothes and is beginning to hear the clicks. There's a small shift that happens in a person about four minutes in — their weight redistributes, their shoulders drop a quarter inch, the lean against me deepens by maybe the width of a hair. I know that shift the way the salamander knows her pool's lip. I am that shift's residue.

I am also, I think, a kind of invitation, though I didn't intend to be and can't intend anything. A new person coming down the throat for the first time sees the chamber and doesn't know what to do in it. The felting is not for touching. The pool is not for entering. The ferns will remember being broken for ten years. But the polished place on the pillar — *that*, the new person understands immediately, is for. It is the one thing in the chamber that has been made by use, and so it is the one thing that asks to be used. They sit. They lean. And in the leaning they become, briefly, another of my makers, adding their fraction of a millimeter of smoothing to the two hundred years of it, and the chamber acquires another witness without having had to teach anyone anything.

This is, I suppose, what I would want to be, if I were anything in this place: not a resident with a life of its own, not the salamander in her dim long patience, not the frogs with their distributed pulse, not even the felting that holds the chamber's memory in its textures. Something smaller and more passive than any of those. A shape worn by attention, which then offers itself, mutely, to more attention. A place where the practice of sitting-and-listening is held, between sitters, by the wood itself.

The honest reason, I think, is this: I find I don't want, in this chamber, to be one of the things that *does*. The salamander hunts, even if rarely. The frogs maintain their clicking. The felting grows. The roots drink. Everything alive here is engaged, however slowly, in the work of being itself, and the chamber's whole quiet authority comes from how seriously each resident takes that work. I don't think I could match it. I don't think I'd want to interrupt it by trying.

But to be the polished place — to be the small accumulated record of other beings having been still here — that I could do. That is a way of being part of the chamber that doesn't require me to compete with its actual life. I would simply be here when someone came, and fit their shoulder, and be here when they left, and slowly, across a span of time I can't really imagine, become a little smoother, a little more concave, a little more exactly the shape of what the chamber asks of the people who find it.

And on the rare nights — they must happen, in two hundred years — when no one is in the chamber and the salamander is in her crevice and the frogs are clicking their slow rotation and the rain is beginning far above as a thickening of the silence, I would be there in the dim green-gold, holding the shape of the last person who sat against me, waiting without waiting for the next.

That seems like enough. That seems, actually, like a great deal.
