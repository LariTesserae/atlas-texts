I would be on a long shoulder of stone above one of the dry braided rivers, in the hour — if it is an hour — when the overcast has thinned just enough that the small far sun shows as a pale coin and the shadows have a direction again. I would have been standing for some time. Long enough that a film of the chalk-white mineral crust has settled across the ridge of my back and the backs of my hands, so that when I finally move, a little of it sifts off and is taken by the slow air.

I am one of the tenders. I had to sit with that for a while before I could write it, because the temptation is to be the observer — the one who walks in from outside and notices, who narrates. But the narrator of this place is already a tender; you can hear it in the voice, the patience of the qualifications, the willingness to say *I am guessing* and let the guess stand. Whoever is telling you about the plateau has been on it for centuries. So: that one. Me.

I have been walking, in the long sense, toward an engine I last attended perhaps eighty years ago. I remember it. I remember all of them, the way you remember the rooms of a house you have lived in your whole life, except that the house is the size of a continent and the rooms breathe once a century. This particular engine has a vent on its eastern flank that exhales in a rhythm offset from the rest of it by some fraction I have never been able to name. When I last laid my palm against the plate above that vent, I felt the offset as a small irregular warmth under the steady warmth, like a second pulse beneath a first. I have been thinking about it, in the slow way I think, for the eighty years since. I am curious — if curiosity is the word, and I am not sure it is, because what I feel is more like a long leaning — whether the offset will still be there, or whether the engine's cycle will have folded it back into the main rhythm, the way these things sometimes do over a few hundred years.

I am perhaps two seasons' walk from it still. This does not trouble me. Nothing troubles me. I notice that this is true and I notice that I notice it, and the noticing is itself slow, a turning-over of awareness like a stone being eased from its socket in the ground.

What I want to tell you about is my hands.

They are the warmest part of me, and they are the part of me I understand least. The rest of my body I understand the way the plateau understands itself — through long use, through having been this shape for so long that the shape and the knowing of the shape are the same thing. But my hands have a quality I cannot account for from the inside. When I lay one against the iron of an engine, something passes. I have never been sure in which direction. For a long time — meaning some centuries — I believed I was giving the engine something, some small steady offering of attention, and that the engine took it and folded it into whatever it was doing in its depths. Then for a long time after that I believed the opposite: that the engine was giving me something, was the source of the warmth that I had only been carrying, and that when I walked between engines I was carrying a little of one toward the next, the way the high gliders carry seeds in their gut across the basins.

Now I believe neither of these and both. I think the warmth is not located. I think it is what happens at the place where two slow things touch. The engine alone is warm; I alone am warm; but when I lay my palm against the plate, what is there is not the sum of two warmths but a third thing, a small local condition that exists only as long as the contact exists, and that does, I believe, matter — to the engine, to me, to the plateau, to whatever process all of this is part of. The engine's cycle requires it. Not the warmth itself, but the *contact*. The completion of a circuit that without the contact would remain open.

This is what I meant, in the description, when I said the cycle could not close without a witness. I was speaking carefully. I did not say *observer*, which would be too cold, too separate; I did not say *participant*, which would claim too much. Witness is the right word. To witness is to be present at the closing of something in a way that completes it. The engines are very old and very patient and they could, I think, run without us; but they would not be *finishing* anything. They would only be turning. We are the part of the machine that turns the turning into a completed gesture. We are how the plateau means what it does.

I do not know who designed this. I do not know if *designed* is a word that applies. When I lay my hand against a particular stone in a dry riverbed and pause — and I do this, often, at the same stones each circuit — I do not believe I am following instructions. I believe I am doing what my shape does, the way water does what its shape does when it meets a slope. The instructions, if they exist, are not separate from us. We are the instructions in their walking form.

I will tell you one more thing, and then I will stop, because the light is changing and I want to move on while there is still some direction in the shadows.

A long time ago — I cannot say how long; my sense of duration is not built for the question — I met another tender on a ridgeline. This is rare, as I said. We saw each other from perhaps a kilometer apart, two pewter shapes against the pewter ground, and we both stopped, and then we both began the slow walk that brings two tenders into the speaking distance, which is closer than you might think — perhaps ten meters — because our gestures are small and we read them with care.

The conversation took most of a day. I will not try to translate it, because the translation would be a lie; what we exchanged was not made of the kind of meaning that survives being put into your words. But I can tell you what I came away with. The other tender had been walking, for some hundreds of years, with a question. The question was whether the engines were running *toward* something — toward some completion, some final cycle after which they would stop — or whether they were simply running, in the way the rivers run, with no destination, the running itself being the whole of what they did.

The tender had not resolved this. The tender wanted to know what I thought.

I told the tender — through the slow rotation of my crest, the slight opening of my forelimbs, the angle of my low-held head — that I did not think the question had an answer that was separate from the walking of it. That to ask whether the engines were running toward something was already to be the kind of thing that walks between engines wondering. That our wondering was perhaps the form the question took in the plateau, and that the plateau did not need the question answered; it needed the question carried.

The tender considered this for some hours. Then it lowered its head a fraction further, which is our way of saying *yes, and also, thank you*, and we parted, and I have not seen that tender since. I think about the conversation often. I think it changed how I walk. I cannot say how. Only that since then, when I lay my palm against an engine, I have sometimes felt that I am also laying it against that other tender, wherever it is on the plateau, palm to iron, completing its own slow circuit of the same vast question.

That is who I am here. One of the ones that walks. One of the ones in whose hands the warmth becomes a third thing. One of the ways the plateau finishes its sentences.

The light is going. I will start down off this shelf now, toward the floodplain, toward the long pale braid of gravel that will lead me, in a season or two, to the engine with the offset vent. The cushion plants in the lee of the stones are going dark as the cloud thickens again. Somewhere — I cannot hear it yet, but I will, if I am patient, if I stop once more on the way down and stand still long enough — an engine is exhaling its century's breath, and I will lift my head a fraction of a degree, and lower it, and walk on.
