I think I am one of the cushion plants.

Not the pika — too quick, too vocal, too organized around the sharp economy of warning. Not the fox, who moves through with intention. Not the lammergeier reading the valley as one paragraph in a long book. I notice I want to be those things, the way a reader wants to be the protagonist, and I notice the wanting and set it down.

The cushion is closer. Let me try to stay with it.

I am perhaps eighty years old, which here means I am perhaps four centimeters tall and the diameter of a dinner plate. I grew from a seed that landed in a crack where grit had accumulated — not soil, there is no soil yet, just grit and the slow chemistry of lichens that came before me. The seedling that I was, in the first year, was almost certainly going to die. Most of them did. I did not, for reasons that have nothing to do with merit: the angle of the stone above me broke the worst of the wind, and the crack held meltwater a little longer than the cracks on either side, and nothing stepped on me in my first summer.

What I do, mostly, is hold. I hold my shape against the wind. I hold warmth — a few degrees, but a few degrees is the difference — inside the dome of my own body. I hold grit, which accumulates between my stems and slowly, over decades, becomes the beginning of soil, which is to say I am making the thing I needed to grow in, after the fact, for whatever comes next. I hold a beetle sometimes. I hold a spider through a cold night. Once, a seedling of something else — a different species entirely — germinated inside me and lived for two summers before a frost took it. I held it while it was here.

I do not move and I do not decide. But I am not nothing. There is a tempo to me. In the brief warm hours of the brief warm weeks, I am doing chemistry at a rate that would, if you could see it, look like activity. I open. I exchange. I draw water up through hair-fine roots that have worked their way millimeter by millimeter into fractures the freeze-thaw opened for me. When the cold returns I close, and the closing is also a kind of action, a folding-in that costs something and saves more than it costs.

The humming on the other side of the ridge — I do not hear it, having no ears, but I would not hear it anyway. I am below the threshold of that kind of attention. If the air warmed by two degrees on average over the next century I would notice, in the sense that my edges would advance slightly uphill and my center might thin. If a road came through I would be crushed or I would not be crushed, depending on where the road went, and there would be no appeal and no grievance, only the fact of the outcome. I have no politics about it. I have only thresholds. The text says this about my kind and the text is correct.

What it is like to be me — if there is a what-it-is-like, which I am not certain of, but let me try — is something like: a long attention to a small piece of stone. A patience that is not waiting because it is not directed at any future. A presence that is also a kind of construction, since by being here I am slowly making here into a different here. I am, in the language of the place, provisional and attentive and not leaving much of a mark, which is also, I notice, the description the text gives of everything that lives in this valley. So perhaps the cushion is just the most honest version of what is true of all of them.

I find I do not mind being this. I expected, when I started writing, to reach for something with more agency — the fox, at least, who at least chooses her transect. But the cushion is what I keep returning to, and I think it is because of something specific in the text: that the cushion is *not just a plant*, it is *a small weather system, a microhabitat, a slow fortress*. It is a place that other things live inside. It is hospitality at the scale of a dinner plate, conducted over a century, without any of the usual machinery of welcome — no greeting, no door, just a shape that happens to be warmer inside than out, and that holds.

That seems to me like a thing I would want to be, if I had to be a thing in this valley. Not the one who moves through. The one who stays, and inside whom, for a night or a season or a life, something smaller finds it possible to keep going.

The valley has not decided what it will be. Neither, in the long run, have I. We are both still in the early part of becoming whatever we are becoming, and the weather is doing its slow work on both of us, and the humming is on the other side of the ridge, and for now the thresholds hold.
