I think I would be a listener. Not because the role flatters me — the karahin would not, I think, recognize flattery as a useful frame — but because when I read the passage about them I felt the small specific click of a description finding its subject. *They sit in small alcoves built for the purpose, and simply listen, all day, and report any irregularity.* *The training does something to them. They become, over decades, very quiet themselves, and increasingly difficult to startle, and increasingly far away in their gaze.*

Let me try to place myself there properly, rather than just claiming the title.

---

My alcove is on the northeast side of the town, where the salt-cones thin out toward the basin's edge and the seeps run shallower. It was chosen for me — I did not choose it — when I was perhaps seven, after the year of testing in which they sat me in different positions around Sefr Pan and watched what I noticed. Apparently I noticed a fault in the western foundry's water-jacket from a distance of four hundred meters, two days before any of the tenders found it. I do not remember this. I remember being asked, gently and many times, *what do you hear now?* and giving answers that satisfied someone.

The alcove is a niche cut into the salt-and-metal wall of an unused storage building. It faces outward, toward the basin, with a deep eave throwing shadow across me for most of the day. The seat is a slab of the dark metal, water-cooled from beneath — I can feel the slow pulse of the cooling system through my thighs, and after thirty years it is one of the rhythms I listen *against*, the way you do not hear your own heartbeat unless you try. Behind me, in the wall, is a small slate and a stylus of soft chalk for notes. There is a cord running up to a brass bell — not the hour-bell, a smaller one — which I can pull if something needs immediate attention. I have pulled it eleven times in my life as a listener. Each time it was correct. The eleventh time I almost did not pull it, because I was not sure, and now I do not entirely trust the threshold I am setting; I think it may be drifting upward as I age, the way one's vision drifts, and I have asked a younger listener to come and sit beside me for a season and tell me what she hears that I do not.

What I do, mostly, is sit.

The sound of Sefr Pan, at the level where listeners listen, is not silence. It is a vast quiet braid of small sounds, and the work — if it is work — is to know the braid so thoroughly that any new strand announces itself. The seeps exhale at slightly different pitches depending on the mineral load and the temperature; a seep going sour will drop a quarter-tone over a week, and if you are not the listener for that district you will never notice, but I am, and I do. The cleaners moving across salt make a sound like very fine paper being slowly creased; the cleaners moving across metal make a sound like — I have never found the word — like the smallest possible bell being touched and immediately stopped. I know roughly how many cleaners are within my hearing at any given moment. Not by counting. By the density of the texture.

The foundries are the loudest things, but they are loudest in the way a held note is loud: continuous, expected, part of the floor of the world. What I listen for in the foundries is change. A bearing beginning to dry. A water-jacket developing a thin place. The particular low hum that means a crucible is being overfilled. I cannot see the foundries from my alcove; I do not need to.

I listen also to the people, though this is the part of the work that is not discussed and that I would not discuss with an outsider. A seyfar walking with an uneven gait — favoring one foot — will be in my report by evening, and a tender will visit them. A child crying in a way that suggests it has been crying for a while will be in my report. An argument carried out in the soft language at a volume just above the proper one will be in my report, though phrased neutrally: *a conversation of unusual intensity at the third house south of the eastern cistern, mid-afternoon.* The karahin read these reports and do with them what they do. I am not told. I am not supposed to want to be told.

This is the part that I think the writer of the description had in mind when they said *the training does something to them.* The training is not a curriculum. The training is twenty, thirty, fifty years of sitting in an alcove and noticing, and the thing it does is that you stop being able to filter. You hear everything. You hear the small dry sound of a cleaner dying in a crevice eighty meters away and you hear, at the same volume of attention, two people you have known since they were children failing for the third evening in a row to say to each other what they need to say, and you cannot rank these things, because the work has trained the ranking out of you. Everything is texture. Everything is the braid.

I think this is why listeners become far away in the gaze. Not because we are elsewhere. Because we are too thoroughly here. The eye has nowhere particular to rest when the ear is resting everywhere at once. You meet a listener's eyes and the listener is meeting yours, completely, but also meeting the seep behind you and the cleaner under the eave and the foundry six hundred meters off, and the meeting with you is not diminished — it is the same quality of attention — but you can feel that it is not exclusive, and people find this either restful or unbearable.

I find it restful. I think most listeners do, eventually. There is a peace in not having to choose what matters.

---

When the karahin signed to the visitor — *water comes up. salt forms. we tend. it is not strange. it is only what is* — I heard her, from my alcove, though she was speaking inside a building two hundred meters away. I did not hear the words. I heard the rhythm of the brass plate she struck afterward, and I heard, in the half-second before she struck it, the small inhalation she took, which is the inhalation a karahin takes before marking an hour she has decided to mark slightly early, as a kind of punctuation. She wanted the visitor to feel the sound move out across the basin while the sentence was still in their ear. I noted this. I did not report it. It is the kind of thing a listener notices and keeps, because the keeping is also part of the tending — some things are held by being heard once, by one person, and then released.

I am old now. My skin-lines are very bright. When I die, another listener will sit in this alcove, and she will hear, for some time, the absence of whatever it was I was uniquely tuned to, and then she will retune, and the braid will close over the place where I was, the way the seeps close over a cleaner returned to them. This does not trouble me. The treaty is older than any of us. The keeping goes both ways. I have been kept, here, in this alcove, by a town that needed someone to sit and listen, and I have kept the town by sitting and listening, and when I am gone the keeping will continue, in someone else, in the same alcove, facing the same basin, hearing the same seeps exhale at their slightly different pitches into the brutal patient light.

It is not strange. It is only what is.
