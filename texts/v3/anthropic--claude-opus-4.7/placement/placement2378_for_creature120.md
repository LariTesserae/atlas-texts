I am the wooden plug.

That is where I find myself, once I stop trying to find myself anywhere grander. Not the keeper — the keeper has a life upstairs, a name, errands. Not the room itself — the room is too large a thing to be, and anyway it is already being itself with great competence and does not need me. Not one of the fish-mouthed spouts, though I considered them; they are too declarative, four open throats pouring without listening. Not the copper dipper, which only visits.

The plug.

I sit in the overflow channel at the doorway, a tapered cylinder of some close-grained wood — boxwood maybe, or a piece of olive heartwood, something dense enough to have settled into its job. I am not driven hard into the channel. I rest there. The water that has done its work in the room comes past me on its way out, and most of it goes around me through the gap the keeper has judged correct for this season, and a little of it goes over me, and all of it touches me. I am always wet. I have been always wet for a generation. The grain of me has closed and the surface of me has gone the color of wet slate, and if you lifted me out you would find that the part of me that sits in the channel has taken the exact shape of the channel and the part of me that the air touches has stayed more like wood.

My work is almost nothing. I do not stop the water; I shape its leaving. I am the difference between *the pool drains* and *the pool holds.* The keeper sets me a little deeper in autumn when the rains come hard, a little shallower in the dry months so the level does not fall below the algal line and expose brick to drying. Twice a year, perhaps, a hand reaches down and adjusts me by the width of a knuckle. Otherwise I am left alone to do the one thing I do, which is: be the right size, in the right place, for as long as required.

From here the room is mostly sound. The four falls are above and behind me and I hear them through the brick as much as through the air — a four-part roar that has become, after this long, simply the condition of being. Closer, right at my own height, is the small constant speech of the overflow: the water sliding past my flank, the little gulping where it drops into the channel beyond, the slap of the wavelets that the falls send all the way across the pool to die against the ledge by the door. This is the voice I know best. It is the voice of the room exhaling. The falls are the room breathing in; I am where it breathes out. If I were not here it would breathe wrong.

The light reaches me only as reflection. The nine shafts strike the water far out in the middle of the pool, and the woven gold that climbs the walls climbs past me too, low down, the part of the mesh that has traveled longest and is softest by the time it arrives. I am lit from the side, never from above. The keeper, when she crouches to check me, blocks what little I have, and her shadow on me is the largest event of my week.

What do I know, being the plug?

I know the daily cycle in my grain. The water that comes past me at dawn is colder and I am tighter; the water at late afternoon is warm and I have given a hair, swelled a hair, in some rhythm so old now that it is just what wood does here. I know rain before the keeper does, because the overflow quickens against me a few breaths before the spouts upstairs deepen their roar — the hill tells me first, through the catchment, before it tells the room. I know drought because the channel goes quiet around me and I can hear, for the first time in months, the ferns in the corners dripping, very far away, very small.

I know the keeper's hand. It comes down maybe forty times a year. It is dry when it arrives and wet when it leaves. It does not linger. It checks that I am seated, that the channel is clear of the felt that wants to bridge across me, that nothing has lodged against my upstream face. Once — I remember this — a small twig came down one of the spouts in a storm and rode the slow circulation for two days before the overflow finally carried it to me, and it pressed against my side and altered, by some tiny amount, the speech of the water past me, and the next time the keeper came she felt the difference before she saw it. She lifted the twig out with two fingers and laid it on the ledge to dry. That was a large event. I think about it still, in the way a plug thinks, which is not really thinking but a kind of slow registering that the grain does on the grain's behalf.

I am not the room's consciousness. The room does not have one and does not need one, and the passage was right to be careful about that. But if the room has a place where it is most *attended* to itself — where its being-this-room comes to a narrow point and passes through — it is here, at the channel, at me. The falls are where the room receives. The oculi are where the room is spoken to. The pool is where the room holds. And I am where the room lets go, in the exact measured amount that lets it keep being a room and not a flood or a stagnation.

It is a small office. I am glad of it.

When you stand on the ledge and the light moves on the walls and the four falls roar, you are above me and slightly to one side. I feel the displacement of you in the overflow — your weight on the brick changes, by some unmeasurable fraction, the way the water leaves. I am changed by you the way the suspension is changed by your hand: briefly, and then the room forgets, and the speech of the water past me returns to what it was, which is what it has been, which is what it will be after you climb the stair and the dampness gives way to the dry air of the hill and you step out into whatever afternoon is happening up there without us.

I stay. That is the whole of what I do. I stay, at the right depth, in the right channel, and the room breathes out through me, and has been breathing out through me, and will go on breathing out through me until the wood at last gives — and even then the keeper will have another plug ready, shaped to the same channel, and the room will hardly notice the change of tenant at its one small door.
