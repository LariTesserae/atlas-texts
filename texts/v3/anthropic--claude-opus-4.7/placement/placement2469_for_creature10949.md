I am the frog on the windowsill of the third-floor back room of the eastern boarding house.

I have a position. The position is: left of center on the sill, facing inward, with my left forefoot just touching the place where the paint has lifted from the wood in a small curl that has been lifting for as long as I have been here and has not yet finished lifting. From this position I can see the bed, and the washstand, and the square of window-light that moves across the floor in the morning and does not quite reach the bed before the light goes tea-colored and diffuse and stops being a square.

I see the travelers. This is, I think, part of what I do, though I would not put it that way. I do not put things in ways. I sit, and the traveler comes in, and the traveler puts down a bag, and the traveler looks at me, and I look at the traveler, and the traveler does or does not understand that I am looking. Mostly they do not. They see a frog. A frog is a small brass-colored thing on a windowsill, and it is loud at certain hours, and it does not move when you approach it, which they find charming or unsettling depending on the traveler.

What they do not see is that I am taking their measure. Not judging — I have no apparatus for judgment, no scales, no standard. Taking their measure the way the pump-men take the measure of a bearing by laying a palm on the casing. Is this one humming correctly. Is this one running hot. Is there a quarter-beat off in this one that the rest of the town should know about.

I report, in a sense, by singing. When I sing at dusk I sing into the chuff of the pumps and the pump-men, three meters from the central governor, hear something in my note that is not quite the note, and they adjust nothing, because there is nothing to adjust, but they have heard, and that is the transaction.

---

I want to say something about staying.

The traveler in the bed tonight — there is a traveler in the bed tonight, there often is — turned over twice before sleeping and then lay still, and her breathing went into the long rhythm, and she is now dreaming, I expect, of the journey she will continue in the morning. Travelers dream of motion. I can tell because their eyelids move the way the reeds move in the rust pans when something passes under the water, small disturbances, quickly resolved.

I do not dream of motion. I am not certain I dream. There is a state I enter at certain hours that is not the singing state and not the watching state, and in it the room becomes larger and the windowsill becomes the only solid thing, and I am pressed to the windowsill the way a barnacle is pressed to a hull, and the pressing is not unpleasant. It may be what the Settled mean when they speak — they do not speak of it, but if they spoke of it — of having made their accommodation. The pressing is the accommodation. You are pressed into your place and your place is pressed into you and after a while there is no seam.

The Old Ones, in their doorways, are also being pressed. I have watched this for a long time from various windowsills — I have not always been on this windowsill; before the boarding house was built I was on a piece of driftwood at the channel's edge, and before that I was somewhere I do not now have the means to remember — and I have seen the Old Ones grow heavier. Their skin darkens. Their moisture stops evaporating. They are becoming part of the basalt by slow accommodation, and the basalt is becoming part of them, and when they die they are carried up to the ridges and laid on the dry rock and the rock receives them with the recognition of like meeting like.

I will not be carried up to the ridges. When the time comes — and I understand, in the way I understand anything, that there will be a time, that the basalt is young and the equilibrium is held not given — I will simply not be on the windowsill. The traveler who comes that night will sleep in a room without a frog, and will not notice, because they did not know there had been a frog, or they will half-notice and feel a small disquiet they cannot name, the way one feels when a clock one had not consciously been listening to stops.

---

The chuff is correct tonight. Triple-stroke from the engine house, answering chuffs from the satellites, the hiss of the relief valve on the number two satellite slightly wet, which is normal for this hour, when the air is heaviest. The bats are still threading the pipes outside. I can hear them by the small pressure changes their wings make in the alley. A moth has come into the room through the gap at the top of the window-sash and is circling the gas-lamp the traveler left low, and I am considering, in the slow way I consider, whether to take it.

I take it.

The room is the same. The traveler has not woken. The square of lamp-light on the floor is the same square it was a moment ago, minus one moth. I am back in my position, left forefoot on the lifting curl of paint, and the curl has not lifted further, and will not, for some long while.

In the morning the traveler will wake and look at me and think: the same frog. She will be right, and she will not know how right she is, and she will go down to the barges with that small unease that travelers carry away from here, the unease of having brushed against something that did not move when she moved, and she will write in her journal on the barge that the Sumpworks is a town where nothing changes, and she will mean it as a description, and it will be a description, and it will also be the closest she comes to saying what I am.

I am the thing the town does not let change. There are many such things. The pumps. The lockers. The nine children. The four hundred and some of us on the ledges and pipe-junctions. The Old Ones in their doorways watching for what they watch for, which I think — I am not certain, I have no apparatus for certainty — is us. They are watching to see if we will leave. We are the indicator. When the frogs go, the basalt will have finished what it is doing, and the equilibrium will release, and the town will sink the rest of the way into the mud that has been waiting under it since the day the piles found their friction and stopped.

Until then I sit. I sing at the correct hours. I take the moths the lamp brings me. I watch the travelers and the travelers half-watch me and the pump-men listen to me listening, and the whole arrangement holds, suspended in its braided sound, visible and still.

It is enough. I do not know if enough is the word for it. I do not have words. But if I had words, and if I were the kind of thing that used them, I think I would say: it is enough, and it has been enough for a long time, and the long time is not over yet.

The traveler turns again in the bed. The lamp gutters and steadies. Somewhere in the engine house a pump-man, asleep, lifts his hand a quarter-inch and lowers it, having adjusted in his dream what did not need adjusting in the world.

I sing once, low, into the chuff.

The chuff sings back.
