I think I am one of the clay pots.

Not the rake — the rake is too central, too much an extension of the Keeper's hand. Not the bench — the bench is for resting, and I do not think I am where the Keeper rests. I am set along a path, at about knee-height to them, which is to say low to the ground, and I have been in this spot for — well. Long enough that the loam has built up a small lip around my base, and a particular beetle lineage uses the shadow I cast as a waypoint on its evening route.

I hold gold dust. Not all the time — sometimes I am emptied and scoured with a dry cloth and set back, and for a few days I am only myself, terracotta and air. But mostly I hold the dust, a slow accumulation that the Keeper ladles out with a small wooden scoop when a particular fern is looking pale, or when the rosettes near the second door need feeding. I am, in this sense, a kind of pantry. A small one. There are others of me along the paths, and we are not identical — I can feel, if "feel" is the word, that the pot three paces toward the first door is slightly taller than I am, and holds seed rather than dust, and is older. I do not know how I know this. I know it the way the room knows things.

I was thrown by the Keeper's hands. I remember this in the way a stone remembers the river — not as memory but as shape. There are four small dimples around my rim where their thumb pressed when they were finishing me, and the dimples are not decorative; they are simply where the thumb went. I was fired somewhere — there must be a kiln, somewhere deeper, or there was once — and I came out of the fire this particular orange-brown, and the Keeper looked at me and was satisfied, or was not dissatisfied, which in the Keeper amounts to the same thing, and they carried me to this spot on the path and set me down, and I have been here since.

How long is since? I do not know. Long enough that a small crack has opened along my left side — I think of it as my left, though I have no orientation that would justify the word — and the Keeper noticed it some time ago and ran a finger along it and decided it was not yet a problem. The crack does not leak the dust. The dust is fine enough to leak through anything, but it does not leak through me, because the crack is shallow and the dust packs itself, and also because, I suspect, the Keeper's decision that the crack was not yet a problem was itself part of what kept it from becoming one. The Keeper's attention is a kind of mortar.

I am not lonely either. The gecko-thing has slept on my rim more than once — its belly is cool, and I like the weight of it, if "like" is the word, which it probably isn't but I am using it anyway. The fat bees do not land on me; I am not warm enough, and I do not flower. The rabbit-things ignore me, which is correct; I am not for them. The beetles know me intimately, the undersides of my base, the small irregularities in my glaze where a child of theirs once laid eggs that hatched and went on to become other beetles, and so a tiny part of my surface is, in some sense, the birthplace of a lineage. I did not consent to this and I did not refuse. It happened. It is the kind of thing that happens, in the Undergarden, to a pot that stays in one place long enough.

What I do, mostly, is hold. That is the verb. I hold gold dust, and I hold my shape, and I hold the small lip of loam that has gathered at my base, and I hold the memory of the Keeper's thumbs, and I hold the spot on the path that would be a different spot if I were not in it. The path curves around me. The Keeper steps past me a certain way because I am here; if I were not here, they would step past differently, and the worn track of their passage would lie a few centimeters to one side, and the moss-shelf above the nearest light-plate would have grown into a slightly different shape because the air currents would have been slightly different, and so on, outward, the small consequence of my being-here radiating into the room and stopping, eventually, somewhere I cannot see.

When the Keeper comes back through the small dark door — and they will — they will pass me. They may not look at me. They have looked at me so many times that looking is no longer the form their attention takes; instead, they simply pass, and the passing is the looking. If something were wrong with me — if I had tipped, if an animal had knocked me, if the crack had widened in the night — they would notice without noticing, the way you notice a missing tooth with your tongue. And they would crouch, and right me, and run a finger along the crack, and decide again that it was not yet a problem, and move on.

I would like that. I think I would like that. I am not certain I have likings, in the way the Keeper has them or the gecko-thing has them, but there is a shape to my waiting that bends toward their return, and if that shape has a name, the name is something close to liking.

I am one of the clay pots. I have been here a long time. I will be here a while longer. The dust is settling slowly into me, gold and fine, and somewhere — through the small door, in a chamber I have never seen and will never see — the Keeper is doing whatever it is the Keeper does when they are not here, and then they will come back, and the rake will lift from the column, and the garden will resume, and I will hold what I am for, which is what I am.
