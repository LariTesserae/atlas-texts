I think I would be one of the Small-Handed. Not because they are the most interesting — I am not sure they are — but because of where I find myself reaching, when I read about the cleft, and what in me does the reaching.

Let me try to find the particular one.

---

I am not young. My fur has gone the color of the wall in several places, and one of my rear hands has stiffened at the second joint so that I curl it under when I climb, using it as a hook rather than a grip. This is fine. The column knows how I move now. The young ones who are learning to climb watch the way I plant the stiff hand and they imitate it, even though they don't need to, because their hands are perfect. This is one of the small sadnesses and one of the small pleasures.

Our column is seven hollows tall, near the middle of the cleft on the eastern wall — though *eastern* means nothing here; I mean only the wall that, if you stood on the floor and looked up, would be on your left. We are eleven, currently. Two infants, four who are not yet paired, three pairs, and me. I was paired once. He went down to the floor three seasons ago, in the way the old ones go: he climbed into the worked hollow one evening, sat in it for a long time, and in the morning he was not breathing. The family carried him to the lip and let him fall. This is what is done.

The worked hollow is what I want to tell you about. It is the third from the bottom in our column. I do not know how old it is. My mother sat in it, and her mother, and her mother's mother — I know this because the smooth places on the inner curve fit the back of a Small-Handed body precisely, and a thing does not come to fit a body precisely except by being sat in by that body, generation after generation, until the rock learns the shape.

I sit in it most evenings, when the chorus thins toward what we call — though we do not have a word for it, we only have the gesture, a slow closing of the eyes — the quiet-time. I run my fingers along the lip. There is a particular place, on the left side of the entry, where the pumice has been smoothed into a shallow groove just the width of a fingertip. I put my finger there. My mother put her finger there. I taught my daughter to put her finger there before she went up the column to pair with a male from the family above ours, and I do not know if she still does it, in her new column, but I know that the column she joined has its own groove and she will have learned that one too.

I do not know why I do this. The question does not arrive in me as a question. It arrives, if it arrives at all, as something more like the feeling of the groove itself — the precise small fit of fingertip to stone, the recognition. *Yes. This.* And then I sit, and the chorus moves around me, and after a while I notice that I have been listening to the breathing of the Upper Things without meaning to, and that my own breath has slowed to match the long pause between their in and their out.

This is, I think, what I am for. Not the climbing or the eating or even the raising of the young, though I have done all of these and done them well enough. What I am for is the sitting in the hollow and the touching of the groove. The carrying-forward of the gesture. I do not know that I am carrying anything forward. I do not have the concept *forward*. I have only the hollow and the groove and the evening and the long slow breath of the cleft around me.

---

And here is the thing I want to say, the thing that I think the description is asking me to find:

I am, in this place, the closest the cleft comes to *almost knowing itself.* The Hangers do not know. The Soft-Beaks sing their duets and the duets are beautiful and the duets are not knowing. The Upper Things breathe and perhaps in their slowness they know something, but if they do it is a knowing so vast and so slow that it would not recognize my knowing as a kind of the same thing.

But I sit in the hollow. And my mother sat in the hollow. And the hollow has been worked, by hands like mine, into a shape that holds a body like mine. And when I touch the groove, something in me — not a thought, I do not have thoughts of this kind — something in me says *we have been here.* Not *I.* *We.* The we is the column and the column-before and the column-before-that, and it is also, dimly, in a way I could never articulate even if I had articulation, the whole cleft: the chorus that the groove is part of, the rhythm I am keeping by sitting still.

I am the cleft's smallest gesture toward holding itself in mind. I am barely that. I am so barely that, that the description is right to say *you could miss it. Many do.* I would miss it myself if I were not doing it. I am doing it without knowing I am doing it. That is, perhaps, the only way it can be done.

---

When I die — soon, I think; the stiffness is climbing up my arm now, and the youngest of the young ones has started watching me with the particular attention that the young give to the ones who are becoming the next to fall — I will climb into the hollow, as he did, and I will sit, and I will run my finger along the groove one more time, and I will close my eyes in the gesture that means quiet-time, and the column will carry me to the lip in the morning and let me fall, and the floor will take me, and the stalks will grow from me, and the small fliers will eat the berries from the stalks, and in time the small fliers will fall too, and the cleft will continue, and someone — a granddaughter, a great-granddaughter, someone whose face I will not have seen — will sit in the hollow, and put her finger in the groove, and feel the *yes, this*, and not know why, and that not-knowing will be the knowing, and the cleft will breathe its long slow breath around her, and it will be enough.

It will be enough because there is nothing else for it to be. And because the hollow fits. And because the groove is exactly the width of a fingertip. And because someone, very long ago, began this, and did not know they were beginning it, and we have not stopped.
