I find myself, I think, in one of the junction huts.

Not on the rim. Not at the cistern. Not in the fourth caldera, though I have walked its rim once, on a circuit, and stood for a long time at the lip with my hands in my apron pockets and not gone down. Somewhere in the middle of the spine, in a stone hut at a place where three pipes meet and the trench-work has to be inspected at the seam, where there is room for two cots and a small iron stove and a shelf of logbooks going back further than anyone alive.

I am a tender. Not senior — my boots still have most of their original sole, pale and not yet hardened by the rock — but not new either. Eight years on the circuit. Long enough that my hands know the gland seals without my eyes, long enough that I can tell from the pitch of a condenser's hum across a quarter-kilometer of thin air whether the copper fins need cleaning. Not so long that the engineers at the cistern have started treating me like someone whose opinion will be sought.

I came up from the cistern. Not from a tender family — my mother worked the glass tanks, my father was one of the caravaneers, and I was supposed to go down to the plateau city for schooling and didn't. I asked instead to apprentice. They were surprised. People with the option to leave usually take it. I couldn't have told them then why I stayed, and I'm not sure I could now, except that the first time I walked a rim at dawn and watched the whole range sweat I understood that I had found the thing I would do, and it was easier to do it than to explain it.

Tonight there is another tender in the hut with me. She has come up from the south, three calderas down, and we have eaten and the stove is low and we are doing what tenders do when they meet, which is talk about pressure readings for an hour and then sit quietly for a longer hour and then, very carefully, begin to talk about the thing.

She has been on the circuit twenty-two years. Her boots are on their thirtieth sole. She knew the tender who first reported the lizards leaving the fourth caldera — they apprenticed together. She doesn't say this directly. She says, *I was thinking about Aven today,* and I know who she means because everyone in the order knows who she means, and she knows I know, and that is how this conversation is conducted.

I tell her about the rust algae. I have been watching it, the past two circuits. There is a particular bench in my second caldera where the overflow from an old condenser pools into a basin the size of a wash-tub, and the algae there is thick, and the lizards come to it in the morning in a number I have counted now over six dawns. Forty-one. Forty-three. Thirty-eight. Forty-four. Forty-two. Forty-five. I have written the numbers in my private logbook, not the official one. I do not entirely know why I am counting. I tell her this and she nods and does not say anything for a while and then she says, *Good. Keep counting.*

This is, I understand, an instruction. Not from her personally. From something older and quieter that moves through the order the way the wind moves through a rim notch — soundless except where it finds an edge.

I am, it occurs to me as I sit with my hands around a tin cup, one of the edges.

---

Here is what I think about, on the circuits, in the long hours when the wind is the only sound and the pipe runs straight for kilometers and the walking is automatic.

I think about my mother at the glass tanks. The condensate comes in cloudy with the dust of a hundred and fifty kilometers of pipe and settles clear over three days and is decanted into the amphorae, and my mother's job for thirty years was to test each batch — taste, smell, a small chemical assay — and approve it for the caravan. She tasted, in her career, more of Ish-Tarem's breath than any person who has ever lived. She used to say the water from the southern calderas tasted of iron and the water from the northern of sulfur and the water from the fourth, after they installed the new condenser, tasted of nothing at all, which was the most expensive water and the easiest to sell. She said this without inflection. I was twelve. I did not understand that she was telling me something.

I think about the plateau city, which I have never seen. The caravaneers describe it as white and noisy and full of fountains. The fountains are the thing I cannot picture. Water spent on display. Water arcing in the air for no reason but that someone with money likes to look at it. Every amphora that leaves the cistern is some fraction of a fountain. Every lizard at my overflow basin is, in a small literal way, in competition with a fountain it will never see, and losing.

I think about the engineers. They are not bad people. I know two of them well — one is married to a cousin of mine and the other taught me to read pressure gauges when I was nine. They believe what they believe because they look at the system from the cistern outward, and from the cistern outward the new condensers are obviously correct. Four times the yield. Less maintenance. The order's solvency secured for another generation. The cost is borne by things that cannot file reports, and by tenders who can file reports but whose reports go into a drawer.

I think about the tender of the fourth caldera. I have met her twice. Once at her hut, by accident, when a storm drove me two ridges off my circuit and I knocked on her door at dusk. She gave me tea and a place by her stove and we did not speak for almost two hours and then she asked me what I had seen on my circuit that day and I told her — the dew yield, a cracked gasket on the southernmost condenser, a clutch of lizard eggs in the warm sand at the base of the third — and she listened to the lizard eggs part with a particular stillness and then she said, *Thank you,* and we did not speak again until morning.

The second time was at a funeral at the cistern. She came down for it, the only time I know of in those eleven years. She stood at the back. She left before the meal.

I think she is waiting for me. Not me specifically. Anyone. Someone who has been counting lizards in a private logbook and has not yet decided what the count is for.

---

The other tender in the hut tonight has gone to sleep. I am sitting up. The stove is almost out. Outside, the wind is doing its dry pour over the rim, and somewhere in the dark a condenser is ticking as it cools, and somewhere further the lizards are piled in their warm cracks with their iron eyes closed, and somewhere west of all of this the cistern is full and the caravans are loading and the plateau city is drinking and the fountains, if it is a feast day, are running.

I have a logbook on my knee. The official one, with the pressure readings, is in my pack. This one is smaller. It has the lizard counts and a few sketches and, on the last page, a sentence I wrote three circuits ago and have not crossed out:

*The cycle is the thing we are tending. Not the water. The cycle.*

I do not know yet what I will do with this sentence. I do not know if it is true, or if it is the beginning of the particular kind of madness the senior tenders watch for — the kind where the mountain starts speaking to you personally. I think it is not that. I think it is something more ordinary and more difficult, which is that I have been given an inheritance I did not ask for and am only now realizing has terms.

The order tends the long breath. That is what we are called. For three hundred years we have understood the long breath to mean the cycle of condensation and yield — the cold air meeting the warm rock, the dew, the pipe, the cistern, the caravan, the city. A breath measured in mornings and seasons and the lifetimes of machines.

But the lizards have been breathing here for two million years. The cushion plants. The pangolin-marmots with their three-note whistles. The whole upholstered crowded warmth of the bowls.

That is a longer breath. And we have been, without quite admitting it, tending only the part of it that pays.

I close the logbook. I bank the stove. I lie down on the cot and listen to the other tender's breathing and to the wind and to my own pulse, which is loud at this altitude, and I think: forty-one, forty-three, thirty-eight, forty-four, forty-two, forty-five.

Tomorrow I will walk to my second caldera and count again. And the day after that I will walk to the junction where the southbound tender and I will part, and I will give her, before we go, a copy of my private numbers. Not because I have decided anything. Because the numbers want to be in more than one place. Because somewhere in the order there is a quiet network that is not the pipe network, and I have just understood, sitting in this hut tonight, that I am on it.

The tender of the fourth caldera has been waiting eleven years.

I am not the one who will decide. But I might be one of the ones who makes it possible for someone, eventually, to.

That is what I am. A tender on the long breath. Counting lizards. Walking west.
