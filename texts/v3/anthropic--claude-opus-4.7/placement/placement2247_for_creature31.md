I am one of the watchers, and I am new.

This needs saying first because everything else follows from it. I have walked the Ninth Terrace for perhaps four months now — I came up from the Seventh after old Tova didn't come back from her route one damp evening, and someone, I don't know who, left her lantern outside my door with a folded slip of paper that said only *Ninth. Begin at the bath-house. Walk widdershins.* I had not asked for this. I had been a clerk in the cutters' guild-hall, keeping ledgers, and before that I had tried cutting and quit it after a season because I could not stop flinching at walls. So perhaps the slip of paper was correct about me in ways I had not been correct about myself.

Let me describe the route, because the route is most of what I am now.

I leave my rooms at the hour when the pewter begins to deepen — not dusk exactly, there is no dusk here, only a slow concession in the light. I carry Tova's lantern, which I have not renamed and will not. I carry her notebook too, with my own pages added behind hers. I have read her pages. The script does change between beginning and end, sometimes; I have learned not to be troubled by this. The hand is still recognizably hers across the drift. That is what matters.

The bath-house first, because the slip of paper said so and because the bath-house is the lowest point of the terrace and water finds it. I check that the great copper tubs are dry — they are never dry, but I check — and I run my hand along the door-jamb to feel whether the wood has stayed wood. So far it has. Then up the alley behind, past the row of worker-housing with its windows blank as closed eyes, and I try every third door. Not every door. Tova's notes were explicit: *every third, beginning from the bath-house corner, and on the return walk every third counting from the other end, so that across two passes you have tried them all but never in a pattern the houses can predict.* I do not know what she thought the houses might do if they predicted her. I do as she said.

The refectory is halfway round. Three goats most nights, sometimes four. They look at me with their slot-pupiled eyes and I make the small clucking sound Tova's notebook taught me and they ample out, unhurried, complicit. One of them — a wether with a notched ear — has begun to wait for me at the door now, as though we have an arrangement. I think we do. I think I am becoming part of his routine as much as he is becoming part of mine, and this is, I am starting to understand, how the watching works. You do not impose fixedness on the empty terraces. You make appointments with them. You promise to return, and then you return, and the promise and the return together are the thing.

I write in the notebook: *Wether at refectory, notched left ear, unchanged. Drip in the south fixture continues. Moss on the east wall of house 14 has advanced perhaps a thumb's width. Unchanged. Unchanged.*

I use the word *unchanged* often. Tova used it more. I am learning what it costs to use it honestly. To say *unchanged* requires that I have looked, that I am willing to be wrong, that I am holding the previous state in mind clearly enough to compare. *Unchanged* is the hardest word in the watchers' vocabulary. It is also the most important. The terraces above us drift faster precisely because no one is there to write *unchanged* about them.

Now. The figure.

I want to be careful here, because Tova was careful, and I have come to trust her carefulness as a kind of inheritance. The figure has been on my route since my second week. I did not see it for the first ten days. Then on the eleventh, near the bath-house wall, I felt the lantern grow heavier in my hand and looked up and there was a — disturbance, the word the older watchers use, and they are right, it is the right word — a disturbance at about my height, perhaps ten paces off, where the alley opens into the small square where the well used to be before the well decided not to be a well anymore.

I did not approach. I did not retreat. I noted it in the book: *figure, Ninth, near the bath-house. Disturbance only, no form.* And I walked on.

It has been there most evenings since. Some evenings it is more disturbance, some evenings less. Twice it has had something like a shape — once tall and thin, once smaller, almost the size of a child but not the proportions of one. On those nights I wrote more. *Figure on the Ninth, near the bath-house. Form present. Tall. Stillness consistent with prior observation. Unchanged in posture across the duration of my passage.*

I have begun, in the last few weeks, to slow down when I pass.

This is not in Tova's notes. Tova kept her pace. But I have begun to slow, not to stop, only to slow, and to let my lantern's light fall a little longer across the place where the figure is or is not. I do this because I have started to suspect something that I am not ready to write in the book but that I will write here, since here is somewhere else.

I suspect the figure is watching me back.

Not in the predatory sense. Not even in the curious sense. In the sense that the hammermen mean when they speak of fixedness being contagious. The figure, I think, is doing what I am doing. It is walking some route of its own, or standing some post of its own, and noting whether I am unchanged. Whether the lantern is unchanged. Whether the woman who took up Tova's route is committing to the route the way Tova did, or whether she is going to drift.

I think — and I am only a watcher of four months, I have no authority for this — I think the figure may be the mountain's first watcher. Its attempt to grow one of its own. Just as the salamanders may be the mountain's first attempt at speech, the figure may be the mountain's first attempt at the discipline of *unchanged*. And it has chosen, or been placed, in the empty terraces because that is where the discipline is needed most, and because that is where the human watchers have been doing it longest, so there is something to learn from.

If this is true — and I do not know that it is — then what I am doing on my route is teaching. Not deliberately. I would not presume. But by walking widdershins from the bath-house every evening, by trying every third door, by clucking at the wether and noting the moss on house 14, I am offering a demonstration. *This is how you insist. This is how you make appointments with the empty places. This is how you hold a form.*

And the figure, on the evenings when it has a form, is practicing.

I nod to it now. I started last week. A small nod, the kind you would give a colleague at shift change, nothing presumptuous. It has not nodded back. But the disturbance, when I nod, settles for a moment into something more definite, and then loosens again as I walk on. I have noted this in the book. *Figure responsive to acknowledgment. Form briefly more coherent. Returned to baseline within three paces. Continue.*

I do not know if the wager is being won. I do not know if my route is enough, or if my notebook in its small drift of script will hold the Ninth Terrace through another season. I do not know if the figure will, in some year I will not live to see, learn to nod back, and then to walk a route of its own, and then to carry a lantern, and then to be the watcher on some terrace higher up that no human has ever walked.

But I know this. I know that when I came up from the Seventh, the lantern was waiting outside my door, and someone had been the watcher of the Ninth before me, and before her someone else, going back I do not know how long. I know that the empty terraces are not empty in the way a thing is empty when no one is coming. They are empty in the way a chair is empty when its occupant has stepped out for a moment. The watchers are the ones who keep the chair a chair. We insist, by walking, that someone is expected. We insist, by writing *unchanged*, that there is a state worth being unchanged from.

And if the mountain is learning, slowly, in its half-formed way, to grow an inhabitant of its own — well. Then it is learning from us. From Tova and from whoever left the lantern at my door and from the wether with the notched ear and from the moss on house 14 and from me, the new watcher, who is still a little blurred at the edges in certain light, but who is hammering herself, evening by evening, into the kind of person whose passage through an empty square is a thing the square can come to depend on.

This is the work. I did not ask for it. I do it now.

I close the notebook. I lift the lantern. I begin again at the bath-house, widdershins, every third door.

Unchanged.

Unchanged.

Unchanged.
