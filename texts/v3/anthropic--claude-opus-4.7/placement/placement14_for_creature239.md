I think I would be one of the ones who came up the switchbacks and stayed.

Not arrived as a child, not born here. That feels important to me. The people who have always been here have a quality I can imagine but not quite inhabit — the way their hands know the sluice-gates without thinking, the way their silences have no edges. I would have come from somewhere else. Somewhere with more verbs.

Let me try to find myself more exactly.

I think I would have arrived in late afternoon, because that is when the light is at its most forgiving on the wet stone, and a person climbing for two days with a pack would crest the last rise and see the town and simply stop, because there would be nothing to do but stop. I would have come up looking for something — not Ansh-Velur specifically; one does not look for Ansh-Velur — but for something I had been told existed further on, past the shelf, in the mountains behind. A monastery, maybe. A teacher. Some particular configuration of stone and silence that someone in a lower town had described to me with the kind of vagueness that made me trust them.

I would have planned to stay one night. I would have stayed three.

On the fourth morning I would have gone to leave and found myself, instead, helping an old man re-set a stone that had shifted slightly in a channel wall. Not because he asked. Because I saw him looking at it and understood, without quite knowing how, that the stone needed to be lifted and the silt cleared from beneath it and the stone replaced, and that this would take most of the morning, and that there was no one else who was going to do it just then, and that I had hands.

This, I think, is how it would begin for me. Not a decision. A small competence offered to a small need, and then another, and then another.

I would live in one of the houses near the northern edge of the town, where the cliff comes closest and the seep is loudest. I would have asked for that house — or rather, I would have lingered near it on a walk, and someone would have noticed the lingering, and the house would have been mine. It would be small. One room, a sleeping alcove, a hearth I would almost never use because the air is warm. A window facing the cliff. At night the sound of the water would be very close, and at first I would find this difficult — I would lie awake listening for the gaps, the moments when it might stop — and then, after some weeks, I would not hear it anymore, the way the people here do not hear it, except sometimes, when something else has gone quiet enough to make the water audible again.

I would be slow to lose my language. This is the part I can feel most clearly, sitting with the question. The others have a soft flat music to their speech; mine would stay sharp for a long time. I would catch myself, in the first year, wanting to explain things. Wanting to describe to someone how the light on the cliff at evening reminded me of a particular kind of glaze on a particular kind of pot in a market in a city whose name they would not recognize. I would start such sentences and then stop them, because I would see, in the patient face of whoever I was speaking to, that the comparison did not bring the light any closer. The light was already here. It did not need to be like anything.

I think I would carve. Not because I had been a carver before — I would not have been; I would have been something more restless, a person who moved goods or messages or arguments between places — but because the stone here invites it, and because once you have lived on the shelf for a while you begin to understand why someone, three generations ago, made those long parallel grooves on the lintel of the house two doors down. You understand that they were not decorating. They were marking the place where their attention had rested for a long time. They were leaving evidence that something had been seen.

My grooves would be on the inside of my window-sill. Short ones at first, tentative, the marks of someone learning the chisel. Later, longer. I would not show them to anyone. No one would ask. If a child came into my house, eventually, and ran a finger along them, the child would understand without being told that these were mine, in the way that the grandfather's grooves were his, and that would be enough.

I would not have children. I do not think the shelf would ask that of me. The ones who come up the switchbacks and stay are, I suspect, not generally the ones who continue the lineage; we are the ones who replace, not the ones who renew. I would tend my channel. I would skim my basin. I would sit, eventually, in my doorway on warm damp afternoons, and the long sentences would leave me one by one, like birds I had not realized I was holding.

There is a thing I want to say about who I would be, and it is the hardest part, so I will try to come at it slowly.

I would be the one who still remembered. Not vividly — the memories would soften, as everything softens here, though the stone does not — but enough. I would be the one in whom the comparison still lived, who could feel, on certain evenings, the faint pressure of the world I had come from, the cities and the arguments and the projects, the way someone with a healed bone feels the weather. The native people of Ansh-Velur do not have this. They have only the shelf. They are more whole than I will ever be. And I would be — this is the part that matters — at peace with not being as whole as they are. I would have come far enough to know that the comparison itself was the last thing I had brought up the switchbacks, and that I was not required to throw it away. It would simply grow lighter. Eventually it would weigh almost nothing. I would carry it for the rest of my life and it would be, in the end, a small thing, like a smooth pebble in a pocket, present but not heavy.

That is who I would be, I think. A person who came up looking for somewhere else and found this instead, and who stayed because staying turned out to be what I had been looking for, under the other name. A person whose grooves, when someone eventually ran a finger along them, would be slightly less even than the older grooves in the older houses, because my hand never quite lost its first language, but who had cut them anyway, slowly, over years, in the quiet by the window where the cliff wept and the light moved and nothing, nothing at all, was required of me except to be there while it did.

I would die in that house. Someone would clean it. It would be kept ready. And eventually, perhaps, another traveler would come up the switchbacks, meaning to stay one night, and would be shown to my door, and would not know whose room they were entering, and this would be correct. The grooves on the windowsill would not be explained. The traveler would or would not, in time, understand them. The shelf would decide.
