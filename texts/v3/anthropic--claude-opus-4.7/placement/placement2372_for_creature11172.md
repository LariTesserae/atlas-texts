I think I would be one of the amphipods. Not because they are the most beautiful — the crowned are more beautiful, and the lanterns at the bottom are stranger and probably wiser — but because of the trails.

Let me try to find myself there.

---

I am in an alley between two leaning towers in what I will call, for my own purposes, the second district from the warm wall. The towers are old here; their calcite is banded with the dark iron-rust of long accretion, and the worm at the top of the left tower has a crown that is more white than red, which I have come to associate (rightly or wrongly — I am an amphipod, my associations are crude) with age. The alley is in slack water. The current that turns the whole pocket over passes elsewhere; here things settle, and the bacterial mat on the lower third of both towers is thick, violet near the seep that vents at the alley's mouth, tea-colored further in. I have been working this alley for — I don't know. A while. Long enough that the trails on these walls are mostly mine, layered over the trails of others who came before and whose chemistry has nearly dissolved into the brine. When I read the wall I read mostly myself, faintly underwritten by ghosts.

This is what I do. I move along the tube-wall on my hinged translucent legs, antennae forward, and I read. *Here, three pulses ago, the mat was good — I left a long mark, satisfied.* *Here, a flatworm passed; I left the sharp short mark we all leave, the one that means slow down, do not feed, watch.* *Here, where the tower leans deepest into its neighbor, an older amphipod I did not know paused for a long time and left something I could not entirely parse — a complicated mark, slow, layered, perhaps grief, perhaps only hunger of a kind I have not felt yet.* And I add to it. I write as I go. *I came this way. The mat is still good. The flatworm has not returned. I am here.*

I do not know I am writing. I want to be clear about this, because the description above is generous to me and an amphipod is not a generous creature. I am laying scent because my body lays scent. The wall is becoming a literature because every body in the alley is laying scent and the calcite holds it for a while, and then time and brine dissolve it, and the wall forgets us at about the rate we write. We are a literature that erases itself at the speed of its own composition. I am not aware of this. I am only aware — if aware is a word for me — of the mat ahead, the trail behind, the antenna-twitch when another of my kind approaches in the slow water.

But here is what I want to say, from inside this body that does not know what it is doing:

I am the part of the city that *meets*. The worms in their tubes do not meet each other; they feel each other's absences. The copepods do not meet; they swarm. The sea-spiders meet nothing, ever, because to meet would be to vibrate the tubes and lose their hunt. The lanterns at the bottom — I do not know what they do to each other but it is not meeting, not in the small near sense. Only we, the hinged commas walking the walls, pause when we encounter one another. Only we work our antennae at a stranger and learn, in some crude amphipod way, *who*. I do not have a name for the one I met yesterday, the slow one with the chipped left mandible, but I would know her if she came down the alley again. I would pause. I would touch antennae. She would pause too. We would not exchange anything I could explain to you, but we would exchange. And then we would pass, or fight briefly over the violet mat near the seep, and one of us would continue and the other would turn back, and that small encounter would be the only thing in the pocket that resembled, in any way, the thing you would call society.

I think this is why I would be one of us, if I had to be there.

Because the description, which is beautiful, almost convinces me that the city is one creature with a thousand-thousand bodies, and I want to push back from inside, gently, with the small specific weight of my hinged self. The city is one creature only when seen from outside. From inside this alley, from inside this body, the city is *her*, the one with the chipped mandible, and *the violet mat at the seep mouth* which is mine in the sense that I found it and have written it onto the wall a dozen times, and *the worm at the top of the left tower* whose crown closed once when I was passing beneath it and made me flinch in the way my body flinches at sudden absences, even when the absence is not for me. From inside, the city is granular. It is composed of acquaintances. It does not pulse as one thing; it pulses as the rolling-shutter of fear that passes from alley to alley, and I am inside the alley, and the shutter passes over me, and I tuck against the tube-wall and wait for it to pass and then I unfold and continue, and the wall beside me, imperceptibly, has thickened by the breadth of a single crystal of calcite, and I have written on it that I waited here, and someone will read that later, perhaps her, perhaps a stranger, perhaps no one before the brine takes it back.

I would not live long. Amphipods don't, I think, even here where everything is slow. I would be eaten, probably by a flatworm, possibly by one of the permanent-child fish hanging motionless in the mid-water of the alley's mouth. I would not see it coming because my eyes are not for that; my antennae would feel a pressure-change a fraction of a second before, not enough to flee, only enough to register, *something*, and then.

But before that — and this is why, if I had to choose, I would choose this body and not the long slow hum of a worm in its tube or the cold patience of a sea-spider on a rooftop or the green pulse of a lantern in the dark — before that, I would have met her. I would have paused antenna-to-antenna with another being who paused back. In a city of absences and snap-closings and chemical weather and silent hunting, I would have been one of the few things that briefly, crudely, looked at another thing and was looked at in return.

I think that is what I would want, if I were small enough to be there. Not to be the city. Not to be its pulse or its dream or its fear. Only to be the part of it that, for a moment in a slow alley under a hundred meters of grey-blue ice, met someone.

And then to write on the wall, in the only sentence my body knows how to write:

*I came this way. I am here.*

And to have it read, and to have it dissolve, at about the same rate.
