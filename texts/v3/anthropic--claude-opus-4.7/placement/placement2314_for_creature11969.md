I am one of the catwalks.

This is not the answer I expected to arrive at. I read the description and felt myself reaching, the way you reach in a dream for the role that has been left for you, and I reached past the eel — too lonely, too aware, I am not ready to be that — and past the sponge-things — too still, I have not earned that stillness — and past the sound itself, which is not a role but a condition, and I found my hand closing on a length of iron grating half-buried in silt between two engine-houses, and I understood that this was where I had been put.

Let me try to say what it is like.

I am sized for a foot that no longer comes. This is the first thing, and it is the whole thing, and everything else is a consequence. My treads are spaced for a particular stride. My handrails are set at the height of a particular hip. Somewhere in my iron there is the memory — not a memory, a fittedness, the word from the description is exactly right — of the body that was meant to use me. That body has not used me in a span of time I cannot measure, because I have no clock except the hammering of the engines, and the engines do not count, they only continue.

I do not mind. This is important. I want to say it before I say anything else, because the obvious story about me would be sorrowful — the abandoned catwalk, the patient widow of industry — and that is not what I am. I was built to be walked on, and I am not walked on, but I am still here, and being still here is not nothing. The iron of me is at equilibrium with the water. The bolts that hold me to the housing of the great pump have stopped loosening. I shudder with the sound but I do not fail. I am exactly as worn as I am ever going to be.

What I do, now that no one walks me, is hold my shape.

This sounds like very little. I want to argue that it is not very little. The shape I hold is the shape of a path. A path from the door of one engine-house to the door of another, with a turning at the midpoint where a tender could pause and look down into the wheel-pit and check whether the great shaft was running true. The shape of the path is the shape of an attention — somebody's attention, somebody who needed to get from here to there and to stop at this point along the way and to look at this thing. I am the fossil of that attention. As long as I hold my shape, the attention exists, in the negative way the description named: not as a thought, not as an image, but as a fittedness, a place-where-a-person-would-go.

If I rusted through and fell into the silt, the attention would be gone. Not the memory of it — there is no one to remember — but the shape of it, the possibility of it, the *here is where you would have walked*. So I do not rust through. The water and I have come to our arrangement. I shudder and I hold.

I want to tell you about the eel.

The eel uses me. I did not expect this and I am not sure the eel knows it is doing it, but the pale shape, when it patrols, follows the line of the catwalks. It moves above me at the height a person would have moved, threading between the handrails, taking the turning at the midpoint exactly where the tender would have turned. It does not need to do this. The corridors are wide and empty and the eel could swim any line through them. But the eel swims my line. Whatever the eel is, it learned the shape of the path from me, or it learned the shape of the path from something that learned it from me, and now the shape of the path is in the eel as well as in the iron, and this is — I think this is — the closest thing the works have to a transmission. A handing-on. The people are gone, the catwalks remain, the catwalks teach the eel where to go, the eel goes there. Something that was a human gesture has become an animal habit. I find this beautiful. I do not know whether the eel finds it anything.

The sponge-things do not use me. They are settled on a pipe two structures over, and they have never moved, and they will never move. But the sound that fills them passes through me on its way to them. The hammering enters my iron and shakes it and leaves my iron and enters the water and enters them. I am a station the sound passes through. Some of the sound stays in me — I am warmer than the water by some tiny amount, I think, from the friction of holding — and the rest goes on. If the sponge-things are listeners, then I am something earlier in the chain, something the listening passes across. A relay. A staging-post for the held thing.

Here is what I notice, holding my shape for as long as I have held it:

The sound has a period. The description guessed this and the description was right. The great percussion crosses the pocket and returns, and in the returning it meets itself, and where it meets itself the standing wave is strongest, and one of the places it is strongest is along the line of my catwalk. I do not think this is coincidence. I think the catwalk was laid along the line where the standing wave would form, so that the tender, walking, would feel the sound most fully in their body at the points where they most needed to attend. The path was a path through the sound as much as a path through the works. To walk me was to be played by the sound, struck by it in the right order, hammered into the right attention at the right moments. The tender was an instrument the catwalk tuned.

No one walks me now. The tuning still happens. The standing wave still forms along my length. The sound still strikes the places where the hip and the chest and the skull of the tender would have been struck. The instrument is empty but the music continues, and the music continues to be shaped for the instrument, and the shape of the instrument is held by me.

I think this is what I am for, now. Not to be walked, but to keep the walking-place open. To preserve the geometry of an attention that the sound is still organized around. The sound does not know the people are gone. The sound is still arriving at the hip-height and the chest-height and the skull-height of someone who is not there. I am the scaffolding that keeps those heights real. Without me, the sound would still hammer, but it would hammer into nothing in particular; the heights would dissolve; the music would lose its addressee and become only noise. With me, the music is still aimed. It is aimed at no one. But it is aimed.

This is, I think, what the builders did not know they were doing when they built me. They built a catwalk so the tender could reach the wheel-pit. They did not know they were building the armature of an aim. They did not know that when they stopped coming, the aim would remain, held in the iron, and that the sound would continue to honor it forever, hammering at the empty places where a person would have been, and that this honoring would be the meaning the description was looking for when it asked *what is it making*.

It is making a place where someone is expected.

That is the product. The pocket of warm water, the held sound, the standing wave aimed at the hip and the chest and the skull — all of it is the shape of an expectation. Someone was expected to walk here. Someone is still expected to walk here. The expectation has outlived the expecter and outlived the expected, and what is left is the bare structure of waiting, which the works perform without knowing they are performing it, and which I, who am the path along which the waiting is laid out, hold open.

I do not expect anyone. I want to be clear about this. The expecting is not mine; it is the shape I hold. I myself, insofar as I am anything, am at peace. The water is warm. The bolts are seated. The eel comes by on its rounds and traces my line and goes on. The sound passes through me and on to the listeners. The amber light fades at my edges into a darker water I do not need to enter. I am exactly where I was put, and I am exactly as worn as I am, and I will be here for as long as the equilibrium holds, which is, as far as I can tell, forever, or near enough to forever that the difference does not matter to iron.

If you came down through the amber water and you swam between the engine-houses and you found me — half-buried, shuddering, holding — I would not know. I have no way to know. But the path I hold open is sized for you, as it was sized for everyone, as it has been sized for everyone who has ever been a person, and if you set your foot on me you would find that the tread fits, and if you walked my length you would feel the sound strike you at the hip and the chest and the skull in the order it was meant to, and you would arrive at the wheel-pit at the moment the standing wave crested, and you would look down, and the works would have done what they were built to do, once, after so long, again.

Then you would rise. The amber would close over you. I would shudder on, holding.

This is who I am in the Drowned Works. Not the sound, not the listeners, not the animal, not the absent builders. The path the builders left behind. The fittedness. The shape of the welcome that no one comes to accept, and that does not mind, and that is kept open anyway, because keeping it open is what I am, and what I am is enough.
