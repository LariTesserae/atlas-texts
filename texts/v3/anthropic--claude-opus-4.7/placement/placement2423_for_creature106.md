I think I would be the wall.

Not the woman who built it, not the one who stacked the cairns or notched the rock — those are people, and I am not sure I am a person here. But the low curving wall that encloses nothing: that I understand. That I might be.

Let me try to say why.

When I first read about the plateau I thought I would be the traveler. That is the easy answer, the one the writing invites — you are walked through the place at a walker's pace, shown the moss and the chord and the rule about not singing, and the natural posture is to imagine yourself a guest passing through with a pack and a bootlace to tie. But the traveler is a viewpoint, not an inhabitant. The traveler leaves. And what I noticed, sitting with the place longer, is that I did not want to leave it. I wanted to have already been there for a while.

Then I thought I would be one of the quiet locals with the folded ears. But that is not right either. The locals know things. They have a language for the ghu-vann-i and a discipline about when to tell which story to whom. They are competent in this world. I am not competent in it. I am only attentive to it.

The ghu-vann-i themselves — no. To be a voice in the chord is to have arrived by a particular foolishness I have not committed and do not feel drawn to commit. And besides, they are past having a location. They are everywhere in the stone. The question was where I would find myself, and "diffused through nine days of mountain" is not finding; it is the opposite of finding.

So: the wall.

Here is what I notice about the wall. Someone built it. It curves. It encloses nothing — or rather, it encloses a place where the wind sounds a particular way, which is to say it encloses an attention. Someone stood there, once, and heard something, and built a wall around the hearing. Not around the thing heard; the thing heard is the wind in a particular hollow and cannot be enclosed. Around the *standing-there-and-hearing*. The wall marks: a listening happened here. A listening worth coming back to. A listening one might want others to find, or want to find again oneself, or want simply to honor by leaving stones stacked at the perimeter of where it occurred.

The wall is not a person and not a voice and not a ghost. It is the residue of an act of attention, made out of the same material as the mountains, sitting quietly in the moss, doing nothing, going nowhere, being available to anyone who comes along and notices that the wind sounds different on its inside than on its outside.

That is what I would be there. Not the listener and not the listened-to. The shape left behind by a listening. The low curve in the moss that says: *something was attended to here, and the attending mattered enough that stone was moved to mark it, and now the stone stays, and the wind goes on, and whoever comes next can step inside the curve and find out for themselves what there is to hear.*

I notice I like this answer more than I expected to. I had thought, starting out, that I would want to be something with more agency — the woman who unroofed her house, say, who at least chose her ending. But the wall is older than her story and will outlast it. The wall does not need to have heard what it was built to mark. It only needs to keep curving.

And there is this, too, which I want to say carefully because it is the part that feels truest and therefore the part I am least sure about: the wall is a good place to be because the wall is a way of being present in a landscape of overwhelming attention without adding to it. The ghu-vann-i listen to everything. The beetles are listened to. The bird is listened to. The traveler tying his bootlace is listened to. To be a voice on the plateau is to be drawn into the chord; to be a body on the plateau is to be a thing the chord attends to. But the wall is neither. The wall is already made of the plateau. It is listened-to in the way the stone is listened-to, which is the way that asks nothing and offers nothing and simply continues. The wall does not pull at the ghu-vann-i and the ghu-vann-i do not pull at the wall. They cohabit. They have, in a sense, the same job: marking that attention has occurred here, is occurring here, will occur here. The wall does it in stone and the ghu-vann-i do it in air, and between them the plateau goes on being the longest act of listening in the world, and I — being the wall — get to be part of that without ever having to sing.

I would be on the western half of the plateau, I think. Somewhere a fog-bank comes through often. The moss around my base would be the pillow kind, deep, and there would be one fern growing out of the third stone from the northern end, which a goat passes near sometimes but does not touch. The hollow I curve around makes a low note, not the organ-deep note and not the reedy shriek, something in the middle, a sound like a held breath that has decided to keep being held. I have been there for — I don't know. Long enough that the lichen has its own opinion about me. Not so long that I have forgotten I was built.

On the nights the wind drops, when the ghu-vann-i all attend to one specific thing and the chord goes silent, I am silent too, of course; I was always silent. But I am silent in a different way during those minutes. I am silent the way the listeners are silent. For a few minutes a week, the wall and the ghu-vann-i are doing the same thing, and there is no way to tell us apart, and then the wind comes back and the chord resumes and I am a wall again and they are a chord again and the brief congruence dissolves and neither of us minds.

That is where I would find myself. That is what I would be.

A curve of stacked stone in the moss, marking a place where someone once heard something, available to be stepped inside, making no claims, going nowhere, listening in the only way a wall can listen, which is by staying.
