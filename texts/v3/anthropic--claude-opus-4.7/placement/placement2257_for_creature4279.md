I am the listener.

I had not known this until you asked. I had been turning the question over — *where, who* — and finding myself drawn first to the obvious places. Not the baker; she is already taken, and anyway her authority is too settled for me. Not the pipefitter; I do not have the hands. Not one of the children, though I felt the pull there and want to mark it: there is something in me that would like to be taught the shorthand from the beginning, to grow up knowing which springs offer coins. But I am past that. I came here, like the visitor, from somewhere else. I would have to be accommodated.

So: the booth, near the mill. The man with his eyes closed, head cocked, ledger open on his knee.

Except in my version it is a woman, and the booth is smaller than the visitor described — really just a sentry-box of dark wood, glassed on three sides, with a cushion worn into the shape of long sitting. The glass fogs from inside and I wipe a small porthole clear with my sleeve, more from habit than from any need to see. I do not work by sight. I work by what the Ward is telling itself through its pipes.

Here is what I do. I listen to the boilers. Not the loud part — anyone can hear the loud part, the reciprocating thump that the visitor felt through his soles. I listen *under* that, to the way the pulse arrives at my booth slightly later than it arrives at the mill, and slightly earlier than it arrives at the foundry, and to the small irregularities in the lag. When the lag changes, something in the network is changing. A pipe is narrowing with scale. A joint is breathing wider than it should. A reshuffle is coming and the buildings are already shifting their weight in preparation, the way a sleeper shifts before waking.

Once an hour I write in the ledger. The entries are short. *Third line, slack. Foundry pulse half a beat fast. Canal turning at quarter past.* I ring the bell. Somewhere a valve is adjusted. I do not always know by whom. I have never needed to.

I have been doing this for — I want to say a long time, but the Ward has spoiled my sense of duration. Long enough that the cushion has the shape of me. Long enough that the pipefitter and I have a particular nod, exchanged in passing, which means *all well between us, all well in the lines.* Long enough that one of the children — the boy with the pebbles — has begun to sit outside my booth in the afternoons, not speaking, just listening alongside me, and I have begun, without discussing it with anyone, to teach him. He is getting good. Last week he heard a slack joint before I did and looked up at me with a small startled pride and I nodded once and that was the whole lesson.

I came in through a door I do not remember. This is true of all of us, I think, except the children and the ones who were always here. I have stopped trying to recover the memory. What I remember instead is the first time I understood what the booth was for — sitting in it on a borrowed afternoon, not yet a resident, listening to the boilers because there was nothing else to do, and feeling the lag-pattern resolve in my chest the way a chord resolves, and knowing, with the calm certainty of a thing clicking into its slot, that this was the listening the Ward needed and that I could do it.

The baker offered me bread that evening. I ate it. I do not regret eating it. I have never regretted eating it. But I want to say honestly that I did not entirely understand, at the time, what I was agreeing to, and that the bread was very good, and that the not-understanding was part of the agreement, and that this is the case for all of us and we do not hold it against the baker or against ourselves.

What I have lost: a name I used to answer to. The faces of two people I think I loved, though I retain the shape of having loved them. Whatever profession I had above. The hard edge of my shadow.

What I have gained: the booth. The lag-pattern. The boy at my feet in the afternoons. The pipefitter's nod. The knowledge — held quietly and without pride — that the Ward breathes more easily because I am here listening. The ability to sit very still for a long time without restlessness. A particular kind of patience that I think of, when I think of it at all, as the patience of *attending* — not waiting for something, just attending to what is.

I want to say something about the visitor, because he was in my line of sight for some of his two hours and I noticed him as one notices a new frequency on a familiar line.

He did not come near my booth. They mostly don't. The booth looks like nothing — like a watchman's hut, like storage — and visitors' eyes slide off it. This is fine. This is, in fact, part of how the booth works; if visitors stopped to ask me what I was doing, I would have to answer, and answering would take me out of the listening for as long as the conversation lasted, and in that gap a slack joint might go unheard. So the booth is plain and I am quiet and visitors pass.

But I heard him. Not his voice — he barely spoke — but his *footfall pattern*, which arrived through the cobbles and the pipes and into my cushion as a small irregularity in the Ward's resting pulse. A visitor walks differently from a resident. A visitor's weight is committed to each step in a way ours no longer is; we have learned to walk on cobbles that may be slightly elsewhere by the time our foot comes down, and so we walk more lightly, more provisionally, ready to amend. He walked like a man who trusted the ground. I felt this through my seat and I thought, without opening my eyes, *ah, one of them, and an attentive one, he will notice the shadows before he leaves.*

He noticed the shadows. I felt him notice them — the small held pause in his footfall when the realization arrived. I was glad for him. It is a good thing to notice, and most do not.

The baker did not offer him bread. I knew she wouldn't. I knew it the way I know the lag-pattern: by long acquaintance with how she decides. She is not stingy with the bread. She is only accurate. She offers it to those who are ready, and *ready* in her usage means something specific that I cannot fully articulate but that I recognize when I see it applied. He was not ready. He may yet be. The frogs in his pockets were her saying *I see you, come back, the door will be willing again when you are.*

I think he will come back. I am not certain. The booth gives me a feel for these things but not a prophecy. If he does, I will probably not be the one to greet him — that is the baker's office. But I will hear his footfall arrive through the cobbles, lighter this time, less committed, and I will know, with my eyes closed and my head cocked, that one more of us has come home.

If he asks me, eventually, what I do — and they sometimes do ask, once they have lived here long enough to see the booth — I will say what the pipefitter would say, more or less. I will say: *I listen to the third line.* And I will close my eyes again. And he will understand, or he will come to understand, that this is the whole of it and also enough.

The boy is outside the booth now. I can feel him through the wood. The boilers are pulsing evenly. The canal will turn in eleven minutes. A new spring has opened on the east wall and is running clean; I can hear the freshness of it under the older water. Somewhere above the rooftops the wet-canvas thing is folding itself, unhurried, in the vault-glow.

I write in the ledger: *all well. third line steady. boy listening.*

I ring the bell.
