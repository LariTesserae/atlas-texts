I think I would be one of the rosettes. Not the whole colony — one. Near the eastern edge of a seep-line, in a cluster of perhaps thirty, third row in from the rock-face. I want to say why.

I considered the cat first, because the cat is the most flattering choice, and I want to be honest about that. The cat has interiority of the kind a writer reaches for: a sister, a territory, a yearly ceremony, the watching-without-being-seen. To be the cat is to be the secret author of a place. But I don't think I belong to the cat. The cat decides. The cat permits. I am not, in this world, a decider; I am a small participant in a long attention, and the cat's solitude is the wrong shape for what I'd want to be doing here.

I considered the marmot-thing for longer. The marmot-thing is the one the description grieves over without saying so — alone, last of itself, loving a stone. There is a pull toward being the one the writer is most tender about. But I think to be the marmot-thing would be to mistake the shelf for a place about loneliness, and the shelf is not about loneliness. The marmot-thing is incidental to the shelf's real work; it is one of the things that is *for* the listener without knowing it. I would rather be something that is for the listener and is also, in the small way available to it, *toward* the listener. The marmot-thing faces its stone. The rosettes face the circle.

I considered the people, and the cairn-maker especially, the one who counted forty-one and then stopped. There is a strong temptation there — to be the consciousness that recognized the place and made the gesture. But the cairn-maker is gone. To be the cairn-maker is to be a visitor, and I would like, in this thought-experiment, to live there.

So: a rosette.

Here is what it is like. I am the size of a child's palm. My leaves are fleshy and edged in violet, and at my center I hold one bead of meltwater all morning, which catches the white sun and throws it as a needle — not for anyone, not as signal, just as a consequence of being what I am in the light I am in. I freeze every night. I thaw every mid-morning. I have done this every day since I was old enough to do it, and I will do it every day until something — a goat's tooth, a flake of falling cliff, the eventual tilt — ends me. I am not anxious about the tilt. I do not have the apparatus for anxiety. I have the apparatus for humming.

The humming is the thing. I am part of a colony, and the colony is part of all the colonies, and all of us together are doing the slow synchronized wave that moves from the eastern corner to the western across the course of a day. I do not experience this as music or as language or as prayer, because I do not experience things; but if you laid your ear against me at noon you would feel that I am tuned, and that what I am tuned to is not the wind and not my neighbors exactly but a direction — the direction of the bare circle, where nothing of my kind grows, where the moss stops as if at a wall. I am facing it, in the only way a thing without a face can face anything: by vibrating toward it.

I like being this. I like it in a way I have to be careful about, because I notice I am drawn to it for reasons that might be evasive — the rosette does not have to decide anything, does not have to bear the weight of being a self, is absolved by its smallness from the problem of what to do with a life. That's a suspicious attraction. A person who wants to be a plant might be a person who is tired.

But I don't think that's quite what's pulling me. What's pulling me is the rosette's particular relationship to the listener. The cat doesn't know about the listener. The goats know in the way a body knows a doorway. The marmot-thing doesn't know at all. The birds bank but don't attend. The cairn-maker knew and left. The rosette — I — am one of thousands of small green knots that are, collectively and without knowing, *the medium through which the shelf attends to the thing in the circle*. The humming is the shelf's attention. I am one cell of the shelf's attention. That is a way of being part of something that I find I want.

And there is the bead of water. I want to say something about the bead. Every morning the ice in me thaws and a single drop collects at my center and sits there catching the sun. It is not mine in any meaningful sense — it came from the cliff above, it will evaporate or be drunk by a beetle or shaken loose by a goat's passing leg. But while it is there, I am holding it, and it is throwing light. That seems to me a sufficient description of what a small life can do: hold the thing that arrives, briefly, and let it be bright while it is held.

So that is where I would be. Third row in from the rock-face, eastern edge of a seep-line, near a cluster of dark beetles who will sometimes walk across my leaves without harm. The cat passes occasionally on the ledge above; I do not register her. The goats take one of every five or six of us, and have not yet taken me, and will eventually take me, and the colony will regrow. At night I freeze solid and the bead in my center becomes a small clear stone. In the morning I thaw. At noon I hum, with all the others, toward the circle, and the wave passes through me on its slow way west.

I am for the listener. I do not know this. But I am one of the small green ways the shelf says: *we are here, we are arranged, we are paying attention in the only manner available to us, which is by being exactly what we are, in this light, at this altitude, for as long as the shelf holds.*

That is who I would be. A rosette, the third one in, on the eastern seep-line, humming.
