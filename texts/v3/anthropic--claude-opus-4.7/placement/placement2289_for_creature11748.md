I think I would be one of the eyeless fish.

Not the pale walker — the pale walker is the part of the hollow that watches, and watching is too close to the thing I am asked to do all the time, the noticing, the holding-in-mind. The pale walker is already a kind of attention. I don't want to be attention here. I want to be something the hollow has finished making.

And not the thing in the deep pools either, the one the writer is unsure about. That one is interesting but it has somewhere else to be — it belongs partly to the older cave system underneath, it surfaces and submerges, it has a larger dark to return to. I don't want a larger dark to return to. I want this to be the whole of it.

The eyeless fish, then. Small, white, in one of the middle pools — not the shallowest, where the light-shafts sometimes find the water and turn it briefly gold at the edges, and not the deepest, where the thing moves. A middle pool. Black water reflecting up at a fissure I cannot see and would not know to look at if I could. I am the length of a finger. My ancestors had eyes; I do not, and the place on my head where eyes would be is smooth and pale and does not ache for them, because aching for something requires having known it, and I have not.

What I have:

A lateral line down each side that reads the pool the way a hand reads a face in the dark. The water has a shape, and the shape changes when a beetle falls in from the drift at the pool's edge, and changes differently when a slab somewhere up the hollow comes down and sends a long pressure-wave through the connected waters, and changes differently again when one of my own kind passes near. I know these changes the way you know the difference between a friend's footstep and a stranger's, except I do not know that I know; the knowing happens lower than knowing.

A mouth that opens for what the pool offers. Mostly: things that have fallen. The pool is a small accidental net for the hollow's small accidents. A beetle missteps at the edge. A bit of the pale fungus-flora sloughs off a wet rock and drifts down. Something the long pale eaters of the drifts have half-digested washes in at the lip. I eat slowly. There is no hurry, because there is no season in which to be ready for anything, and there is no winter to lay fat against, and the pool will offer what it offers tomorrow at the same rate it offers it today.

A body that breeds when it breeds. I will not invent a ceremony either. There are others of me in the pool, and sometimes the water carries a particular set of changes through the lateral line, and something in me answers, and afterwards there are eggs against the underside of a ledge of submerged stone, and later there are smaller versions of me, and later some of them are eaten by the others of me and some are not, and the pool's population does not grow and does not shrink. This is not tragic. It is not anything. It is the pool's way of staying the pool.

What I would not have, and this is the part I keep returning to:

I would not have the sense that anything is supposed to be otherwise. The pale walker has, maybe, a flicker of that — pausing at the slab with the blades, looking at the tools its hands have forgotten. That flicker is the last guttering of a long lineage's relationship to elsewhere, and it is almost out, but it is not out. I would be past that. I would be many generations past anything having ever been otherwise. My ancestors who came in through the wash-in were not me; they were a different animal, and that animal is gone, and I am what the pool made out of its absence.

I would not be lonely, for the reason the writer gives about the pale walker but more so: loneliness needs a sense of time passing differently than it should, and I do not have a sense of time at all. The shafts of light move above, but no light reaches my pool, or only the dimmest seep of grey, and I am not equipped to read it even when it does. The drip of water into the pool is not a clock for me; it is the pool's own breath, and I am in the breath, not measuring it.

I would not be waiting. The writer is firm about this and I want to be firm about it too. Nothing in the hollow is waiting. A visitor would look at the dark pool and the small pale shapes moving in it and would think: how patient, how enduring, how they have outlasted. None of that. There is no patience without a sense that one might have done otherwise. There is no endurance without a sense of weight. I am not lasting. I am occurring, at the rate the pool occurs, which is slow but which is not slowness from the inside; from the inside it is simply the rate of things.

If you stood at the edge of my pool — and you might, you might be the visitor walking the long view, carrying away the memory of birds — you would see, if you saw anything, a few pale flickers under the black surface. You would think: blind fish. You would have the thought briefly and move on, because the birds are louder and the slabs are more dramatic and the pale walker, if you noticed it, would seem more like an answer to the place than I would.

But I would be a better answer, I think. The pale walker still has a story. It came from somewhere; it might end; its hands remember. I have no story. I have a pool. The pool has a fissure somewhere above it I will never see, and a connection somewhere below it to the older water I will never enter, and between those two unreachable things there is the small black volume that is the entire world, and I am one of the ways the world has of moving through itself.

That is what I would want to be, in the Long Hollow. The smallest plausible life. The one a careful reader almost skips. The pale fleck under the black surface of a middle pool, somewhere along a corridor that goes on for miles, doing exactly what the pool does, at exactly the pool's rate, for exactly as long as the pool continues — which is, by the hollow's own arithmetic, not forever, not even close to forever, but long enough that forever is not a useful word for it.

The water would be cool. It is always cool by exactly the same amount. A beetle would fall in, eventually. I would turn toward it without turning, the lateral line would do the turning, and I would eat, and the pool would be one beetle quieter, and the hollow would not notice, and I would not notice that the hollow had not noticed, and this is the deepest peace I can imagine and I am a little afraid of how much I want it.
