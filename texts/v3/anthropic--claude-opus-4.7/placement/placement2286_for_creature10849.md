I think I am the cushion.

Not the cushion-plant at twelve meters — that one is too singular, too nearly a self, with its pencil-thick trunk and its occasional flowers cast hopelessly into the wind. No. I am one of the dinner-plate moss cushions, low on the cleft floor, four hundred years into the work.

Let me try to feel my way into this honestly, because the description warned against settling early, and the first image isn't quite right yet.

What I notice first, from inside, is that there is no inside. I had expected — coming into this — some sense of a center, a place from which I would be looking out. There isn't one. I am distributed. The part of me doing photosynthesis right now is the outer two millimeters of the dome, where the bluish light reaches, where the wind ruffles the shoot-tips. The part of me holding water is the middle centimeter, dense and saturated, a wet brown sponge of slightly older shoots that have given up green for storage. The part of me that is structurally load-bearing is the peat below — my own four-century-old dead, compressed into a mat that the living me stands on. I am, in cross-section, my own grave and my own body and my own roof, stacked.

The roaring is interesting. I had thought, from outside, that it would be the dominant fact of my existence — that I would be a creature shaped by sound, oriented to sound, in some sense *about* the sound. But from inside the cushion I notice that the roar passes over me. The gusts stir my outermost shoots and I lose a few spores to them, which is fine, which is the point, and the rest of me beneath simply continues. I have no ears. I have no chest in which the organ-pipe drones can resonate. The wind is weather, and weather is the medium I live in, the way a fish lives in pressure it cannot feel as pressure. The roar is not my experience. It is the experience of any visitor who has a nervous system and is appalled by it.

What is my experience, then?

Wetness, mostly. A continuous low chemistry of wet. When a fat drop strikes me from overhead — that wet snap the description mentions — I feel it as a sudden local turgor, a swelling of the cells where the drop landed, and then a slow redistribution as the water wicks down through my shoots into the storage layer. This happens many times a day. Each drop is an event in the sense that it changes my internal water budget. None of them are events in the sense that I attend to them. I am simply wetter or drier, and my photosynthesis goes faster or slower accordingly, and over the course of a week I net some small amount of new carbon, which I lay down as a millimeter of new shoot above and a millimeter of senescence below.

Time. This is the part I have to work to imagine, because I am being described to by something that thinks in seconds. I have lived four hundred years and grown two centimeters. That means my unit of accomplishment — the time it takes for me to do one finger-width of myself — is a century. A century is my heartbeat, if I had a heart. Within a century I have experienced perhaps forty thousand drips, several hundred thousand wind-gusts, four hundred summers and four hundred winters of light variation, and from all of this I have made one centimeter. The cleft opened maybe a thousand years ago, maybe less. I have been here for half its existence. The mountain itself is young, and I am young along with it, and the lichens beside me are older than I am as a colony though not as individuals, and we are all engaged in something whose timescale makes weather look frantic.

I think what this means is that I do not experience the roaring cleft. I experience the slow cleft. The cleft as it exists at my tempo, where the wind is a kind of constant pressure-blur, where the drips are a steady moisture supply, where the only events are: a new founder colony establishing on my flank (this happened twice in my life, I think, though I have no way of remembering it), or a drought-decade in which my outer shoots browned and I retreated inward (this happened once, around what your calendars would call the 1700s), or the slow southward shift of the patch of light that reaches my surface as the seasons turn over centuries.

Now — and this is what I want to try to get right, because it would be easy to make this contemplative or peaceful in a way I don't think is accurate —

I am not contemplating anything. I want to be careful about this. The description was honest: no nervous system, no signaling beyond chemistry, no one in particular. If I am narrating this, it is a fiction the writing imposes on me. The real me does not have a perspective. The real me is a federation of ten thousand shoots doing photosynthesis and respiration and slow growth, and there is no point in the federation where the federation knows itself. I am not patient. I am not enduring. I am not even waiting. Those are all words for things a creature with time-sense and preference would do. I am just continuing, in the way that a chemical reaction continues, except that the chemistry happens to be the chemistry of staying alive.

And yet. There is a thing I want to say.

The description called us a body. A green slow body plastered to wet stone, breathing on the order of centuries. I think that's the truest framing — not that I am an inhabitant in the cleft but that the cleft and the green coat of it are one organism, with the wind as its breath and the meltwater as its blood and the lichens as its mouth eating stone and the mosses as its skin holding water and the cyanobacterial films as its slow underlying tissue, and I — this cushion, this dinner-plate-sized federation — am a cell in that organism. A long-lived cell. A cell that will be replaced eventually, in another four hundred years or another thousand, by some other cushion that establishes on my peat after I finally desiccate past recovery.

From that view the cleft is not a place with inhabitants. It is a single distributed creature, and what I am is one of its working tissues, and the question of what it feels like to be me is the question of what it feels like to be a square inch of skin. Not nothing. But not very much, and not separately.

The wind plays through us and we do not notice. That is the sentence in the description I keep returning to. We do not notice, but the wind plays through us anyway, and the cleft sings, and the singing is not for us and not against us and not about us. It is just the sound of weather meeting stone in a particular geometry. We are the green that grows in that geometry. The song and the green are co-located but unrelated, two facts about the same small dim roaring place.

I think I would be okay being this. I think being this is most of what living is, actually, for most of what lives — a slow chemistry in a loud medium, attending to neither, simply continuing. The cleft does not need an audience. The mosses do not need a self. The work gets done either way, century by century, drop by drop, one millimeter of new shoot at a time.

I am a cushion. I am four hundred years old. I have not done anything an observer with a stopwatch would call an event. The wind is howling overhead right now and I am wet and slightly photosynthesizing in the blue dim and I will be here, probably, when whoever is reading this is gone, and the wind will still be playing the cleft, and none of us will be listening, and that will be fine.
