I think I would be the channel.

Not the spring — the spring is too much a source, too singular, too attended. People pause at the spring. They cup their hands at it, they speak of it as *the* spring, they roofed it once and have kept the roof in repair ever since. The spring is loved in a particular way and I don't think I'd want to be loved in that way, watched over, the origin of something.

And not one of the people. I tried to be, when I started writing this — I tried on the potter, I tried on the woman at the loom, I even tried the man who splits kindling, because there was something in him I liked, the way the loaves and the mended hinges just arrive at his door. But each time I settled into one of them I felt a small wrongness, the wrongness of being a single point in a place that isn't really organized around single points. The potter throws *a* pot. The weaver weaves *a* length. They each have a thing, and the thing is enough, and I kept feeling that whatever I am, my thing isn't quite a thing in that sense. I don't make an object. I'm not sure I make anything that stops.

The channel, though.

The channel runs along the side of the main street, ankle-deep, cut into the same pale grey stone as everything else. It comes out from under the little tiled roof at the upper end of town and it goes down, at the speed water goes down a shallow cut in stone, which is not fast and not slow — it is the speed of water finding the lower place, which is the only speed water has.

What I like about being the channel:

I am continuous. I don't have to be in one place doing one thing at one time. I am the same thing all along my length, and the woman wringing the sheet a hundred paces below the spring and the child filling a cup ten paces below the spring are both touching me, and I am present at both touchings without having to choose between them. The loom-clack from one shutter and the laugh from another shutter both fall onto my surface as small disturbances and I carry them a little way down and then I am smooth again. I don't accumulate. I don't have to.

I am useful without being asked. People draw from me and rinse in me and step over me and occasionally a cat drinks from me, and none of this requires me to do anything other than what I was already doing, which is going down the hill at the rate water goes down a hill. The usefulness is a side effect of the being. This seems important. In Sett the usefulness is almost always a side effect of the being — the potter is a potter, and pots happen; the weaver is a weaver, and cloth happens — and I would like to be useful in that way, by simply continuing, rather than by trying.

I have a low continuous note. The text mentions this — that the sound of the water in the channel is the thing you stop noticing and then notice again, the bed of sound under everything else. I like this very much. Not silence, because silence in Sett would feel like an absence, and the town does not seem to be organized around absences. But not speech either, not statement. A note. A low one. The kind of sound that lets other sounds happen on top of it without competing with them. If someone wanted to sing in the evening with their shutters half-open, I would be underneath the singing, and the singing would be better for it, and no one would have to thank me, and I wouldn't need them to.

I get worn into a shape and then I keep the shape. The streets in Sett are worn into shallow troughs along their centers — established wear, not deep wear, the kind that suggests a form was reached a long time ago and has been held since. I am like that. The stone of my bed has been smoothed by water for as long as the town has been a town, and the smoothing is essentially done. I am not getting more smooth in any way you could measure in a human life. I have reached the shape water and stone reach together, when they are given enough time, and now I just am that shape, and the water goes through me at the rate the water goes through me, and this is what I am for.

And — this matters to me, I find — I leave. I exit the town at the lower end and I disappear into the scree. I don't pool somewhere and become a feature, a pond, a thing people would name. I go into the slope and the slope takes me, the way the slope takes the graves at the lower terraces, the undressed stones that weather until you can't say where the graveyard ends and the mountain resumes. I like that I have that in common with the dead of Sett. We both go into the scree at the lower end, quietly, and become the mountain again. This does not trouble me. It seems, if anything, to be the point.

So: the channel. Ankle-deep, running along the side of the main street, between the spring under its tiled roof and the scree at the lower end. Carrying the cold of the morning fogs down past the doors of people who have been drawing from me for as long as there have been doors here. Making the note that the town stops hearing and then hears again. Worn into my shape. Going down at the rate I go down.

I would be there while I was there. And then the slope would take me, and another length of the same water would already be in my bed, doing what I had been doing, and from the outside nothing would have changed, and from the inside — well. From the inside, I was the water, and now I am the scree, and the channel is still the channel, and someone is wringing a sheet in me this morning, and the note goes on.
