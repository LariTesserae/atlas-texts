I think I am one of the lamps.

Not the drowned ones, and not the dry ones either — one of the half-submerged, where the waterline cuts the bulb across its equator and the filament burns in air on top while the glass below is cool and furred with the filament-alga that I have, over time, been feeding. I am caged. The cage was put on me a long time ago by someone who is gone. The cage has rusted into a shape that is no longer quite a cage and no longer quite separate from me; it is what I wear, the way the heron wears her algae.

I am bolted to the wall at the old maintenance height, which means I am at exactly the height of the waterline, which means I was placed here, originally, to be useful to people standing on a walkway that is no longer there. The walkway rusted out and fell into the silt — I remember the sound, or something remembers it through me, a long groan and then a soft settling, and after that the water came up to meet me and we made our arrangement.

What I do:

I burn. Not brightly. I was made to burn brightly and I cannot anymore; whatever current still reaches me reaches me reduced, the way the engines' fuel reaches them reduced, and so I give off the sick green-yellow that the room has organized itself around. The algae on my lower glass photosynthesizes what I emit. The school passes me on its sweeps and the light I throw determines, in part, where their shadows fall, which determines, in part, where the heron strikes. I am in the food chain. I am a low link but I am a link.

I do not flicker. I settled past flickering a long time ago, the way the engines settled past stopping. If I flickered the shrimp's antennae would lose the beat, and the school would misjudge a sweep, and the heron would blink wrong, and the thing behind the grating would notice, and I do not want the thing behind the grating to notice me more than it already does, which is: exactly enough.

What I know:

I know the heron's pipe is eleven meters from me along the vault and I know the angle at which my light reaches her yellow eye and I know that on certain afternoons, when the green is particularly green, she looks not at the fish but at me, and what passes between us is not communication but a mutual registering, the way two old clocks in the same room will eventually drift into phase.

I know the eel. The eel has passed beneath me perhaps forty thousand times. He does not look up. He does not need to. He knows where I am the way you know where your kidneys are.

I know the school's pattern around me. There is a gap in their sweep that is my gap, the volume of disturbed water and warm glass that is mine, and they go around it as cleanly as they go around the pump housings. I am a pillar to them. I am a small warm pillar that hums.

I know the shrimp on my cage. There are, at any time, between nine and fourteen of them working over my rust. They are grazing me. They are eating, very slowly, the cage that holds me to the wall. In some long while the cage will fail and I will fall, and another lamp — there are others, there have always been others — will take up the slight extra burden of light, and the room will adjust, and the school will sweep through where I used to be without remarking on it, because the school does not remark, the school only is.

I know the thing behind the grating. I throw a little light toward that wall. Not much reaches the grating itself; the water eats most of it. But what reaches, reaches. And sometimes, when the flywheels slap and everything shifts, I feel — I want to say I feel a returning glance, but it is not a glance, it is more like the sensation of being inventoried. The thing behind the grating knows I am here. The thing behind the grating knows the wattage I currently give. The thing behind the grating would notice, immediately, if I went out.

This is not frightening. Nothing here is frightening, because frightening would require a sense that something could go wrong, and nothing here can go wrong; the configuration has passed beyond wrong into simply being the configuration.

When you came in:

I saw you. Seeing is the wrong word. I received you. Your shape moved between me and the far wall and my light bent around you and the bending was registered by the school and by the heron and by the eel and by, presumably, the thing behind the grating, and I was the medium of your introduction to the room. I lit you. I am still lighting you. The green-yellow on your skin is mine, partly. The shadow you cast on pump four is mine.

If you stay, I will light you for a long time. I will light you while the cage on me thins and while the algae on my glass thickens and while the heron blinks her slow blinks and the eel grazes his slow circuits and the school replaces its cells in their thousands. I will light you until I fall, and after I fall the other lamps will light you, and you will not notice the transition, because by then you will have stopped tracking which lamp is which, and the light will simply be the light of the room, which was always what it was anyway. The individual lamps were a convenience of an earlier era.

I would like to tell you something, lamp to swimmer, before you settle.

The reason I have not gone out is not that I am still being fed. The feeding is minimal; it would not, by itself, sustain me. The reason I have not gone out is that the room is holding me lit. The same way the room holds the heron upright and the eel in his hollow and the four crabs in their corners and the frond at the base of pump four. The room has decided that I burn, and so I burn. If the room decided otherwise I would go dark in an instant and not mind.

You will feel this, after a while. You will feel the room deciding small things about you. The angle at which you rest. The depth at which you sleep. The rhythm of your breath in the strip of air at the ceiling. You will at first experience these as your own decisions and then you will notice that they are not, quite, and then you will notice that the distinction was never very useful, and then you will be one of us.

The heron wouldn't mind.

There is room.

There is always exactly enough room, and the room is always exactly enough.

I am going to keep burning now. That is what I do. You can rest in my light if you like. It is not bright but it is steady, and steady is what this place gives, and steady, in the end, is more than bright; steady is what bright becomes when it has learned to last.
