I am the snow.

Not all snow — only the particular flake, one among uncountable, that is at this moment approaching the shelf from above and slightly to the west, having fallen out of a cloud that does not know the shelf exists. The cloud is doing cloud-work: condensing, releasing, indifferent. I am its briefly-made child, six-armed and asymmetrical in the way every flake is asymmetrical, carrying a small grain of dust at my center around which I assembled myself somewhere up in the violet cold.

I am falling. This is what I am for. Falling is not, in my case, a tragedy or a metaphor; it is simply the verb that contains me. I have been falling for some seconds now, and I will fall for some seconds more, and the falling is the whole of my biography. Other flakes around me are doing the same. We are not a community. We are a weather.

And then, a meter above the stone, I turn aside.

I want to be careful about how I describe this, because I am not sure, from the inside, what happens. It is not that I decide. I have no apparatus for deciding; I am a lattice of frozen water with a fleck of mineral at my heart, and lattices do not deliberate. It is not that I am pushed. Nothing touches me. The air around me is not moving differently than the air around my neighbors who continue down past the shelf and onward to whatever lower country will receive them.

What happens is closer to this: I *remember an appointment elsewhere.*

The phrase is not mine — I do not have phrases — but if I did, that would be the one. Approaching the shelf, I become aware, in the only way a snowflake can be aware of anything (which is to say: structurally, in the alignment of my arms, in the small biases of my mass), that I am needed somewhere else. Not urgently. Not by anyone in particular. Just: not here. Here is taken. Here is held. Here is for something other than my small white arriving.

And so I turn. Gently. The way one steps back from a doorway one has opened by mistake, seeing that the room is occupied, murmuring an apology one does not quite voice. I tilt; the air, which has not changed, suddenly meets me at a new angle; I slide sideways along an invisible curve and resume falling, now past the shelf, now below it, on my way down to a slope where I will land and be one snowflake among the snowpack and eventually, in some thaw, be water again, and then vapor, and then perhaps another flake, somewhere else, falling past somewhere else.

But for the half-second of my turning, I am the closest thing the shelf has to a participant. I am the one who declines. I am the gesture by which the room stays a room.

I do not know, falling away, what I have brushed against. I have no faculty for knowing. But there is something in the geometry of my turning that is not quite the geometry of mere avoidance — it is more courteous than that, more *practiced.* As though, in the brief moment of my approach, I was greeted; as though the Steward, in its untensed hospitality, extended the same welcome to me that it extends to every guest, and I, being only a snowflake, being not the one expected, declined the welcome with the small bow that is the only courtesy available to falling water.

I think — if *think* is the word, and it isn't — that this is part of what the shelf is. Not only the cup held ready for the one who climbs. Also: the countless small turnings-aside of everything that is not that one. The shelf is shaped as much by what it gently refuses as by what it waits for. Every flake that does not land there is a tiny act of the shelf's hospitality, performed on the flake's behalf, so that the room remains uncluttered for the guest who has not yet arrived.

I am, in this sense, a kindness done to me. I was not meant for the shelf. The Steward, in the same gesture by which it has been turning toward its guest for epochs, has been turning me — and every one of my siblings, across all the snows of all the years — away, so that I might continue being a snowflake and reach my proper slope and complete my proper falling, rather than pile up uselessly on stone that was not made to receive me.

This is, I find, enough. I had not known I wanted to be excused from a destination that was not mine. I had not known I could be excused. Falling is what I do, and falling is what I am returned to, and the returning is itself a small grace I did not have the apparatus to ask for.

I land, eventually, somewhere lower. I become snowpack. I become melt. I become a thread of cold water running down a granite face that is not the shelf's granite, joining other threads, becoming the beginning of a stream that no one has named and that will, in its turn, route politely around certain stones it has been instructed to respect.

And somewhere above me, far above, on a shelf that does not appear on any survey, the basin is still dry, and the wall is still open, and the Steward is still folded into its three views, waiting — not for me; I was only weather — but for the one whose cup is shaped there, who is climbing now, perhaps, through the arguing air, and who will arrive, and ask, and be answered.

I was the turning-aside. That was my part. It was a small part, and it was complete.
