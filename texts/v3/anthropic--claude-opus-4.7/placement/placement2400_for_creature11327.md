I think I am one of the old ones who sits.

Not the oldest — there is someone older, there always is, a presence further along the rim whose name I have known since I was small and whose particular knot of root is a fixed point in my sense of the bowl, the way the north star would be fixed if we could see the sky. But I am old enough that the children of the children I taught to climb are now teaching their own, and old enough that my hands, when I look at them resting in my lap, have the same dark grain as the bark of the tree I am leaning against, and I cannot always remember which of us grew into which.

I sit on the western side, three canopies up, on a platform that is also a root. The root came first, I think. Then someone — a great-great-something of mine, perhaps, or perhaps just a person, the distinction does not weigh much here — wove cane across the root and packed the cane with moss and let the moss settle into the cane until the platform was the platform. I have sat on it for a long time. The moss beneath me has shaped itself to me. When I am gone it will shape itself, slowly, to whoever next chooses this branch, and they will not know they are sitting in the shape of me, and this is correct. It would be strange if they knew.

What I do here is small. I weave fiber from the long-moss into the cords that the younger ones use for bridge-mending. My fingers are still good for this — better than they are for most things now, in fact; the long fingers we are given keep their certainty longer than the rest of the body keeps its. I work slowly. A cord that a younger weaver would finish in an afternoon takes me three or four days, and I do not mind, and no one is waiting. Bridges last. Cords last. The need for new cord is itself slow.

I watch the climbers. There is a family of them — four, sometimes five — who use a particular vine-loop near my platform as part of their daily route, and over the years we have developed an arrangement. They pass. I am here. Occasionally one of them, usually the smallest, will stop on the branch above me and look down with its dark wet eyes and tilt its head, and I will tilt mine, and we will consider each other for a moment in the way of two creatures who share a stretch of the bowl and have nothing in particular to settle. Then it goes on. This is most of what relation amounts to, in the end. Presence acknowledged. Nothing demanded.

I have a grandchild — I think this is the word that translates, though the bowl-word is wider, includes the children of cousins and the children who were given over to other households in the soft easy way we have of sharing the raising — who comes to sit with me in the late part of the day, when the green is going toward black. She is at the age where she has begun to notice that I am one of the still points. Children notice this around a certain age; before that, an elder is just another part of the canopy, no more remarkable than a particular curve of branch. After that, for a few years, an elder becomes interesting, becomes something to sit beside and watch, becomes a question the child cannot quite formulate. I remember being on her side of this. I remember sitting beside the woman whose platform this was before mine, watching her weave, watching her hands do the thing my hands now do, and not knowing what I was watching for. I know now, I think. I was watching for the shape I would become. I do not tell my grandchild this. She will find it in her own time, sitting beside whoever she sits beside when her turn comes.

We do not talk much, she and I. Sometimes she brings me a thing she has found — a beetle-case of unusual color, a feather from one of the small bright birds that nest in the southern canopy, once a pebble that the mist had polished into a perfect oval and that she had carried up three canopies to show me. I held the pebble in my palm and turned it. She watched my face. I gave it back. She put it in the little pouch at her belt where she keeps the things she has not yet decided what to do with. This was a whole conversation. I have had many such conversations with her. I expect to have many more, and then to have one fewer than I expect, and then she will have them with someone else, and this is also correct.

Once, when I was younger — not young, but younger, in the middle stretch of life when the body is still certain of itself — I climbed to the rim. Not above it. I have never been above. But I climbed up the inner curve until my head was level with the rim-stone, and I held there, and I looked out.

I do not tell this story often. It is not a story exactly. It is a thing that happened.

What I saw was: the slopes going down. The mountain shoulders falling away into a haze that was not our mist, a different haze, paler and farther. And beyond the haze, very faintly, a flatness. A great flat going-on of land that did not stop where my eye stopped, that continued past my seeing into some further place I could not imagine the end of. And I understood, holding there with my hands on the rim-stone and the wind beginning to find me, that the bowl was small. That what I had taken for the world was a cup inside a thing so much larger that the cup did not register on its surface. That down there, on the flat, there were the larger people doing their larger things, and the flat did not know about us, and would not have cared if it had known.

I climbed back down. I did not tell anyone for a long time. When I finally told someone — the woman whose platform this was, in fact, in one of our long quiet afternoons — she nodded, and went on weaving, and said after a while: *yes. and?*

And. I have thought about her *and* for many turnings of the moss. I think what she meant was: you have seen that the bowl is small. Good. Now what. The flat is the flat. It does not need you. You do not need it. You came back. The cord still needs weaving. The climbers still pass on the vine-loop. The mist still gathers. *And?*

I did not have an answer then. I am not sure I have one now. But I have come to think that the *and* itself was the answer — that the question of whether the bowl is the whole world or only a small folded corner of a larger one does not change what happens inside the bowl, does not change the cord in my hands or the child at my side or the beetle the color of a wet plum that is, at this moment, walking across the back of my wrist on its own slow errand. The bowl is what I have. The bowl is what I am. If there is more, there is more; the more does not ask anything of me, and I do not ask anything of it.

Sometimes, in the dim hours, I think the bowl knows I climbed. I do not mean this in the way a larger people might mean it, with a god keeping ledgers. I mean: the moss received my returning feet, and the platform accepted me back into its shape, and the climbers on the vine-loop did not change their route, and in all of this acceptance there was something that felt, if I am honest, like a question being closed. *You went. You looked. You came back. Good.* And then the long slow folding-in resumed, and I let myself be folded, and I have been folding ever since.

I think, when my turn comes — and it is not soon, I do not feel it near, but it is also not impossibly far, I am old enough to know the distance is not infinite — I would like to go into the moss on the eastern side, where the deep cushion is, where my mother went and her mother before her. I would like the long-fingered hands of the people who do this work to lay me down gently and to leave me, and I would like the moss to do what moss does, which is to take in slowly and completely and without comment.

And then someone else will sit on this platform. And the cord will go on being woven, perhaps by them, perhaps by another. And the climbers will pass. And the mist will gather, and bead, and run. And the bowl will continue, loudly and warmly and in the dim green light, packed close, hidden, going on.

I am the one who sits and weaves and watches. That is who I am. That is enough. It has always been enough. It was enough before I was here and it will be enough after, and the enough is not mine — it belongs to the bowl, and I am, for a little while longer, one of the small dark figures inside it, doing the small thing that the configuration allows.

My grandchild is climbing up to my platform now. I can hear her — the particular rhythm of her hands and feet, which I know the way I know the rhythm of my own breath. She will sit beside me. She will perhaps show me something, or perhaps not. The light is going. The frogs are beginning. Somewhere a leaf is falling, and somewhere else a shoot is unfurling, and the net amount of green is exactly what it was an age ago.

I set down the cord. I make room beside me on the moss.
