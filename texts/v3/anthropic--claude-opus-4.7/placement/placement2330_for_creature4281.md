I am on the ridge.

Not at the slit. Not yet. I came up here for other reasons — the wind, the long view, the way the lichen makes maps on the slabs — and I have been sitting against the warm side of a stone for some time before I notice the groove.

I notice it the way you notice that a person across a room has been looking at you: peripherally, and then with a small adjustment of the whole body. The groove is shallow and long and obviously old. My eye follows it because eyes follow such things, and at one point along its length the two slabs do not quite meet, and there is the slit.

I do not put my eye to it immediately.

This is the part I want to be honest about. I sit with the knowledge of the slit for a while first. I am someone who walks on ridges; I have come across small wonders before — a beetle the color of gasoline, a feather caught in a crack, the print of something I could not name pressed into wet clay — and I have learned that the wonder is partly in the approach. If I lean down now I will have leaned down. The leaning will be over. So I wait, and I watch the wind do to the grass what the wind has been doing to the grass.

When I do bend to the slit, I bend slowly, because the body knows things the mind has not yet been told. I put my eye close but not against. A thin coolness comes out, dry as paper, exactly as described — though of course I do not know it has been described. I only feel it: a small steady exhalation that is not breath, that does not smell of anything, that is the temperature of a thought held a long time.

And then I look in.

What I see, I will not pretend to see clearly. The opening is narrow and my eye is one eye and the brightness inside is the wrong kind of brightness — not glare, but a fullness, as if the chamber were packed to its walls with something that happens to take the form of light. I make out green. I make out the suggestion of structure, of tier upon tier of something patient. I do not see the chip. I would not know to look for it. I see, more than anything, that there is an inside, and that the inside is occupied, and that the occupation is not of the kind I am used to — there is no startled animal, no scuttle, no eye looking back.

I look for, I think, about eleven seconds.

This is the part that undoes me a little, afterward, walking down. Eleven seconds. I gave it eleven seconds. And in those eleven seconds I was already composing the story I would tell about it — to whom, I didn't yet know; myself, probably, since the people in my life have learned to be patient with my ridges and my small finds but not to ask too much about them. I was already, while looking, half-turned toward the telling. The chamber got, of me, not eleven seconds of attention but maybe four, the rest already spent on what I would do with having seen.

I straighten up. My knees click. The wind has not changed. The ridge has not changed. I have the absurd impulse to say something to the slit — *thank you*, or *I'm sorry*, or just *hello* — and I do not, because I understand, without being able to say how, that the slit will refuse it. That sound is not what is wanted here. That nothing, in fact, is wanted here; wanting is one of the things this place has done without for so long that to offer it would be a small unkindness, like waking someone to ask if they are sleeping well.

I walk down off the ridge.

Here is what I think I am, in this world: I am the warmth that met the cool, for an instant too brief to parse. I am the asymmetry that was, very briefly, asymmetrical back. I will not live long enough — no one will live long enough — to be noticed by the Tenant in the Tenant's own time. By the era in which my glance arrives, as a registered fact, in the slow sentence of that interior, I will have been dust for so long that the word *dust* will have lost its referent in any language descended from mine. My great-grandchildren's great-grandchildren will not remember the shape of my face. The ridge may not be a ridge. The slit may be a finger wider; the light may be spilling more generously; there may be a new tier of even smaller ferns.

And somewhere inside that altered brightness, a consideration that has been patient beyond patience will be turning, in its long light, a small new fact: *once, the thinness thinned further. Once, the cool was met.*

It will not know it was me. It will not know it was anyone. It will only know that the asymmetry, for a moment, was mutual.

I find I can live with this. More than live with it — I find, walking down, that it rearranges something. I have spent my life, like most people, wanting to be seen by what I see. Wanting the deer to look back, the stranger on the train to meet my eye, the poem to know it has been read. And here is a thing I have seen that will, in its own time, in a time I cannot reach, know in its own way that it was seen. The transaction is real. It is only very, very slow. The slowness does not make it less real. The slowness is, perhaps, what makes it bearable — because if the Tenant could turn its full attention on me now, in my time, I think I would not survive being looked at like that. I think no one would. To be considered for four hundred years by something that has nothing else to consider you with — this is not a thing a human face is built to receive.

Better this way. Better that I gave eleven seconds and will be repaid, over ages, in the only currency that place can mint: a slow, slow noticing, occurring long after I have stopped needing to be noticed.

I am the warmth that came once to the slit.

That is enough. That is, in fact, almost too much; I carry it down the ridge carefully, the way you carry a cup that is very full, trying not to spill, trying not to look at it too directly, trying to let it be what it is without already turning it into the story of what it was.

The wind goes on wearing its groove.

I go home.
