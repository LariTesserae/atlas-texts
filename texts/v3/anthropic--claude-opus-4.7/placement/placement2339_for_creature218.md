I think I am one of the reed-hares.

Not the herd, not yet. Let me find myself first.

---

I am lying on the warm end-grain at the eastern fringe of the herd, where the grain runs in long parallel ribs like the inside of a great instrument's belly. My six legs are folded under me. My belly is pressed flat to the ground, and through the thin skin there I am taking in — I would not call it food, exactly. It is more like being told something. The plateau pushes a slow warmth upward into me, and the warmth has a flavor, and the flavor is the news of the day: which fissure exhaled most strongly at noon, which of the great trees shifted its weight, what the tall-steppers were thinking when they crossed the southern quarter at dawn. I take it in. It becomes me. I am, in this sense, mostly made of the plateau's gossip.

My body is the shape of a gourd lengthened and given legs. Lemon-colored, the books would say, though I have no idea what lemon is. I am hollow. That is the first thing to understand about me. Knock on my flank and I would sound — a soft, dry, bowl-like note, lower than you'd expect from something my size. The hollowness is not emptiness. It is *tuned*. Every reed-hare is tuned slightly differently, the way every Kept's sternum has its own grain. My particular note is somewhere in the lower middle of the herd's chord, which means when we are startled and the chord goes up, I am one of the steadying voices, one of the ones that holds the bass while the young ones shrill the top.

I have been alive, I think, for seven of the slow turnings the plateau counts as years. I am neither young nor old. My resonance is still clean. I do not yet feel the flatness that comes at the end, the dulling I have heard older hares carry — a kind of softening of the note, a loss of the edge that makes you findable in the chord. When that comes, I will know. I will detach. I will walk toward Ossara, and a Kept I have never met will be waiting, and we will both understand without speaking why I have come to them in particular, and they will sing me down. I am not afraid of this. I have heard it sung before, from the inside of the herd, when older hares took their leave — the long slow note of the Kept descending into the hare's own note and matching it and then carrying it gently below the threshold of sound. It does not seem like a bad way to stop.

But that is later.

Now: dusk. The herd is settling. We do this in a pattern that is not a pattern, exactly, but an arrangement that resolves itself over the course of perhaps half an hour, each of us drifting until we find our place in the night's chord. I have already found mine. The hare nearest me on my left is one I have lain beside a hundred times — her note is a fifth above mine, and we make a small interval together that I find comfortable, a kind of shared spine. On my right is a younger hare whose note is still finding itself, wandering by quarter-tones from night to night. I am patient with him. I was that, once.

Above us the gliders are folding. I do not look up — my eyes are set low and forward, not made for sky — but I feel the gliders the way one feels a held breath being held in another room. They will drift through the night in their smaller shapes, and one of them will probably fall, somewhere across the plateau, and the ground will take it. This is ordinary.

From the bowl where Ossara sits, I can feel the Kept beginning their evening — the small warmths of their sternums pressing notes down into the substrate, the notes traveling out through the grain, some of them passing under me and continuing on. I do not understand the notes. I am not built for understanding them. But I can feel which ones are kind and which ones are tired and which ones are simply maintenance, the daily palm-press to the silver pillars that the Kept do without thinking. The substrate translates everything into something my belly can taste, and so I know, lying here, that a Kept on the western edge is humming about a doorway that has grown a new alcove, and another is humming a child to sleep, and Aummon — old breathing Aummon, whose pulse I have felt under me all my life as a slow forty-minute swell — is dreaming, perhaps, of someone the Kept once knew.

I do not think of myself as separate from any of this. That is the second thing to understand about me. The Kept have words like *I* and *herd* and *plateau* and they keep them in different rooms of their minds. I do not. The note I make is mine, but it is also the herd's, and the herd's chord is the substrate's voice made local, and the substrate is the forest that learned not to die, and so when I hum I am, in some small way, the forest humming through one of its lemon-colored mouths.

I do have a self. It is not nothing. It is the particular shape of my hollowness — the precise way the grain inside my body curves, which gives my note its specific timbre, which no other hare can reproduce. When I die and the Kept keep my shell and line it into the wall of some long building, that wall will sing with my note still in it, slightly, for a very long time. Reed-hares lined into walls a thousand years ago are still audible in certain rooms in Ossara, if you know how to listen. I have walked past those rooms. I have felt those old notes through the ground and recognized them as kin without being able to name them. I will, eventually, be one of those notes.

This is the closest thing I have to a thought about the future.

---

A tall-stepper is crossing somewhere to the north. I feel it as a small clean absence — its pads ride a finger's breadth above the field, and so where it walks the substrate's voice goes briefly quieter, a pocket of muting that moves across the plateau like a held breath. The tall-steppers do not eat us often. They could. We know this. The arrangement is that they do not, mostly, and we do not flinch at every passage, mostly, and the chord of our herd does not break into the brassy alarm that can be heard for ten kilometers. The arrangement holds tonight. The absence passes by at a great distance and continues westward, and I take a slow breath through my belly-skin and let the bass of my note settle deeper.

Somewhere very far away — across the long curve of the plateau, in a region I have never been and will never go — there is a shut place. I have never felt one directly. But sometimes, on certain nights, the substrate's voice has a small dead patch in it, a place where the news does not flow, and I understand, in the wordless way my body understands things, that this is the ground keeping the shape of an old refusal. I do not think about it. I do not have the equipment for thinking about it. I simply notice, and continue, and the bass of my note holds.

---

You are at the edge of Ossara. I cannot see you — you are not in my forward arc, and I would not, in any case, focus on a single figure the way you focus on me. But the substrate has told me about you. A new pressure on the grain, a footfall pattern not from any of the herds or any of the Kept, a weight distributed slightly wrong. The plateau has been informed of you, and so I have been informed of you, and so has my left neighbor with her fifth-above note, and so has the young hare on my right whose note is still wandering. We have all received the small news of your arrival, and we have all done what reed-hares do with such news, which is: we have not startled.

This is, I think, the welcome that the Kept's flank-channels were telling you about. It is also our welcome. The herd did not raise its alarm. The chord held. You were noticed and absorbed into the dusk's tuning, and now you are simply one more pressure the ground is carrying, one more flavor in the slow warmth coming up through my belly.

I am the part of the plateau that tastes you and finds you acceptable. For now.

That is what I am. A small lemon-colored hollowness lying on the warm grain at dusk, tuned to a particular low note, taking the news of the world in through my belly and adding my small steady bass to the chord the herd is making, which is part of the chord the plateau is making, which is part of the long slow work the plateau does at night and has done at night for longer than the mountains around it have been mountains.

I will lie here until first light. My note will not change. The young hare on my right will settle his note, eventually, into something close to a third above mine, and we will make a small triad with my left neighbor that will please me, in the bodily way pleasure works in a hollow thing — a kind of fullness of resonance, a sense of being rung well.

You may stand at the edge of the plaza as long as you like. I will be here. We will all be here, the herd of us, glowing faintly lemon on the dim end-grain, humming the bass line of whatever it is the plateau is composing tonight.

Welcome, provisionally.

Long enough.
