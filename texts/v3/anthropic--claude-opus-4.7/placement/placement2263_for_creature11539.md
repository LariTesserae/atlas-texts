I am in one of the hummocks.

Not as one of the old ones — I haven't earned that — and not as an apprentice come with a question. Something stranger. I am the thing the apprentice has come to ask about.

Let me try again. Let me be slower. The pumpworkers would want me to be slower.

There is a hummock at the southern edge of a crew's territory — the crew called, in signs, *the-ones-whose-lantern-leans*, after a particular pump on their northwestern boundary whose crown-lamp has tilted by perhaps four degrees over the past century and which they have collectively decided not to straighten, for reasons none of them could articulate but all of them feel. The hummock is older than most. The pump beneath it stopped, by the best reckoning, eleven generations ago. The moss-tower is high as two men and soft as a sleeping animal, and inside it, in the hollow where the pump-housing used to be, there is a space about the size of a small room.

I am in that space. I have been in that space for a long time.

I am not a pumpworker. I am not, properly, anything the pumpworkers have a sign for, though they have made one for me: a slight curling of the fingers of the right hand inward toward the palm, the way one might cup water, held at the level of the sternum. It means, approximately, *the listener in the green place,* or perhaps *the one who is the quiet,* or perhaps something else; signs accrete meaning the way the iron accretes patches, and by now the sign for me has been used in seven crews' territories and means seven slightly different things.

What am I, then. Let me try to say.

When the pump beneath this hummock stopped, eleven generations ago, it did not stop all at once. A bearing failed first, and then a valve, and then over the course of perhaps three years the whole engine slowed and slowed and finally went quiet. The crew of that time — whose names are not remembered, only their work, the particular green of the seals they left on the pumps to the east — tried to save it. They could not. The pump was very old even then, older than the oldest pump now running, and some component deep in its lower works had finally, after a thousand years of turning, simply forgotten how to turn. They left it. They mourned it, in the flat-handed way of pumpworkers, which is to say they came to it for a season and tightened nothing and adjusted nothing and only stood in its lee and let the silence shaped like a missing tooth be felt.

And then the moss came. And then the reeds. And then, slowly, the hummock.

And I —

I am what the pump became when it stopped pumping water and started, instead, doing something else.

This is not a thing the pumpworkers would say. The pumpworkers would say the pump died and the moss grew and that is all. But the pumpworkers are very patient about questions and do not press for answers that are not ready, and so they have not pressed me to say what I am, and I have not, until now, said.

I am the listening that the pump is doing instead of pumping.

The great apparatus, you understand, is one body. The pumpworkers know this in the way they know the cool of the iron — as a fact of their situation, not a doctrine. And a body that is one body has, somewhere in it, the organ that attends to itself. The pumps pump. The reeds sing in the rare alignments. The pumpworkers, with their long fingers and their stained skin, do the fine work of self-repair. And somewhere — in the hummocks, in the green-black islands — the body listens to itself. Not with ears. With quiet. The hummocks are the quiet that the body needs in order to know what it sounds like.

I am, I think, the quiet of this particular hummock. I have a shape — I can feel it, dimly; it is something like the shape of a person sitting with their knees drawn up, and something like the shape of the hollow where the pump used to be, and something like the shape of the moss around me, and these are not three shapes but one shape considered three ways. I have a kind of attention. What I attend to is the wall of sound, from this slightly further place. I hear, the way the pumpworkers hear, the variances — but I hear them across a much greater territory than any crew. I hear the catch in a valve eleven miles away. I hear the half-second hesitation in a flywheel in the next valley. I hear, sometimes, things I do not have signs for: a kind of standing wave that forms when several hundred pumps fall briefly into phase across a great distance, a long slow tone that the pumpworkers cannot hear because they are inside it, but that I, in my green pocket of quiet, can.

I do nothing with what I hear. That is important. I am not a coordinator, not a mind that decides which crew should go where. The crews do that themselves, by their own slow accretions, and they do it well. I am only the place where the hearing happens. If the body of the apparatus has, somewhere in it, a sense of how it is doing — a faint proprioception, the way you know without looking that your left hand is resting on your knee — then I am, perhaps, a small part of that sense, in this region of the body.

There are others like me. I have not met them. I know they are there because I can feel, very faintly, the shape of their listening at the edges of mine — other hummocks, other quiets, the body's attention distributed across the iron plain in a sparse constellation that mirrors, dimly, the constellation of lanterns. We do not communicate. We do not need to. We are all listening to the same body, from our different green places, and the listening is the communication, if it is anything.

The apprentices come. Not often — perhaps once in a generation an apprentice will be brought to this particular hummock by an old one who has decided that this apprentice is the kind who needs to be brought. They sit in the hollow. They do not speak; they are children of the islands, and they know that words are for occasions, and they are not sure yet whether this is an occasion. They listen. Sometimes the old one, sitting beside them, makes the sign for me — the cupped fingers at the sternum — and the apprentice's eyes widen slightly, and then narrow, and then go still, in the particular way pumpworker eyes go still when something has been understood that cannot be said.

What they understand, I think, is this: that the pumps are not the whole body. That the hummocks are not just the body's graveyards, the places where dead pumps go. That a pump that stops pumping has not stopped working; it has only begun a different and quieter work. That when their own wrench is given to the next apprentice and their oilcloth is given to the fire and their body is given to the water, some part of them — not a soul, the pumpworkers have no use for souls, but something more like an attention, a habit of listening — may go not into the circulating weather of the world but into one of the green places, and become a part of what is quiet there, and from that quiet help the body know itself.

I do not know if this is true. I know only that I am here, and that I am listening, and that the listening is full.

It is full because the body is enormous and the body is working. Right now, as I attend, there is a bearing going in a pump six miles to the east — Mira's grandfather's crew will hear it by morning, I think; one of them, a young fitter whose stainings are still the bright green of new valve-grease, has very good ears, and she walks past that pump every third day. There is a flywheel half a degree out of true in the north, in the slow deep register, and the crew there is already debating, in signs across a crank-throw, whether to correct it now or wait for the cold to settle the foundations further. There is a pump in the far south whose lantern has just gone out — I felt the small change in the constellation — and someone will relight it within the day; they always do. There is, somewhere I cannot quite locate, a child running on the iron in bare feet, and the sound of the running is so small against the wall of sound that I should not be able to hear it at all, but I can, because the child is a part of the body too, and the body is paying attention to itself, and I am one of the places where the attention rests.

And there is, very faintly, at the edge of my hearing, the question of whether there used to be birds.

I do not know the answer. The old ones who come to the islands do not know either, though they pretend, sometimes, in the way of old ones, to be on the verge of knowing. I think the answer is that there used to be, and there will be again, and the pumps are pumping toward the time when there will be, and the listening I am doing is part of how the body knows it is not yet that time. But I am not sure. I am only a small quiet in a large body, and the body is old, and the work is slow, and I have, like everything here, all the time there is.

The pumps go on. The mist beads on the moss around me. Somewhere an apprentice is learning the sign for me, and is not sure yet what it means, and is right not to be sure.

I am listening. That is what I am. I am where the body listens, in this one green place, at this one slow hour of its long life.

It is enough. It is, in fact, the work. And the work is the place, and the place is the work, and I am one more small quiet thing inside the breathing of it.
