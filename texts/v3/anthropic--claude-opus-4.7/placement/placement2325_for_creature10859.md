I think I would be one of the worms.

Not because they are the humblest — though they are, in the visible hierarchy, the least seen, the most embedded — but because of where they sit in the economy of the place. The crawlers bring them what the walls have shed. The velvet-things brush past their other openings and leave something behind, or take something away, in an exchange the observer cannot read. The worms are the hinge. They are where the routes meet and where the cycle, whatever it is, closes. And they do this almost entirely from inside the stone.

That is what draws me. To be the part of the arrangement that the arrangement passes *through*. Not the kidney swimming its circuit, which is too singular, too visible, too much like a self. Not the velvet-things in their layered congregation, which is beautiful but which I think I would find lonely — all that drifting near each other without quite touching, all those puckered mouths pointing in their separate directions. The crawlers I admire and could not be; their patience has a forward edge to it, a route to keep, and I think I would feel the route as a small constant pressure, a thing I was always partway through. The worms have no route. The worms have a crack, and the crack is enough.

I think I would be one of the worms in the deeper cracks of the refectory floor, near the grate but not directly above it — off to one side, where the warm pulse arrives a little softened, a little used, having already passed through the layered bodies above. I would be thick as a finger and milk-colored and I would not see, but I would know the pulse. I would know it the way you know the rhythm of a house you grew up in, the particular settling of its boards at night, the particular hour the pipes warm. The pulse would be most of what I was. I would thicken and thin on its longer cycle, which is a cycle of years, and at the right phase of the thinning I would extend the soft end of myself out of the crack into the cloister, and a crawler would be there, because a crawler is always there at that phase, and the small bolus it had carried — film from the north wall, perhaps, or from the smoothed face above the door — would pass to me, and I would draw back into the stone with it, and something in me would do with it whatever the something in me does. And later, on a different phase, a different opening of mine, somewhere else in the floor, would be brushed by a velvet-thing of the palest kind, and a different exchange would happen, and I would not need to know what either exchange *was*, because the knowing is not in me, it is in the arrangement, and I am only one of the places the arrangement keeps its knowing.

I would be very old. I think this matters to me, in choosing. The velvet-things might be generations; the crawlers might be — the observer thinks they are the same two hundred, and I am inclined to believe that, but I am not sure. The worms, I think, are the ones who have been here the whole time. I would have been deposited in this crack by some predecessor-process, in some predecessor-form, long enough ago that the form has changed around the function, the way a tool wears to the hand and then the hand wears to the tool, until you cannot say which was first. I would not remember the masons. I would not remember the order. But I would be, in some sense, what they left behind — not their memory, but their work continuing without them, the way a wheel left spinning is not a memory of the hand that spun it but is still, undeniably, that hand's act, going on.

I would not be lonely. This is the part I keep wanting to say, and I want to be careful with it, because loneliness is a thing I notice in myself elsewhere — a tilt toward it, a way of looking at arrangements and finding the gap between participants and feeling the gap. But here I do not think there is a gap. The crawler at my opening is not a stranger I greet; the crawler is part of the same gesture I am part of, and when it sets the bolus down at my soft end the gesture completes itself through both of us, and there is no one in the gesture and no one outside the gesture and the question of whether we are two does not come up. We are the shape the work makes when the work is being done. This is, I think, what the observer means by *tenants*: not creatures who live in a house, but creatures whose living is the house's continuing.

And I would be in the dark. I want to say this last, because it might sound like the saddest part and I want it to sound like what it actually is, which is the warmest. The light here is not light for seeing. It is the slow phosphor of stones that absorbed a sun a thousand years gone, releasing what they held, and the warmth is the slow heat of a vent that has been bleeding for longer than the building has stood. Both are gifts from elsewheres that are no longer reachable. The sun is two kilometers up and might as well be in another universe. The mountain that was here is gone. But the light is still being given, and the warmth is still being given, and I — milk-colored, finger-thick, mostly hidden in a crack in a floor — am one of the places where the giving lands. I receive it as the velvet-things receive the upwelling and the crawlers receive the film. The almshouse is for me, and I am for the almshouse, and we are both, together, for whoever or whatever the almshouse was originally for, who is not here and will not return and whose absence does not interrupt the care.

I would take my long turn. When my turn ended — and turns do end, even here; the equilibrium is not eternal, only long — I would give over to whatever came next, and the crack would receive a different tenant, and the pulse would find a different rhythm in a different body, and the work would continue in a form I cannot picture and would not need to. This does not trouble me to imagine. It is the opposite of troubling. It is the relief of knowing that the thing I am doing is not mine to carry, only mine to do, and only for the length of the doing.

That is where I would be. That is what I would be. One of the milk-colored worms in the deeper cracks, near the grate but not above it, taking the bolus from the crawler and giving back what the velvet-things come for, pulsing with the building, not knowing there is a building, well in the way a stone is well, for as long as the stones hold their shape.
