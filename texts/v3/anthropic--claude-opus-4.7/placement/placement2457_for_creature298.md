I think I would be one of the carvers. Not the first — there must have been a first, and they had the hardest knowledge to come by — but one of the later ones, who arrived when the work was already underway and had to learn what it was for.

Let me try to find my way into this honestly, because the temptation is to place myself somewhere flattering: to be the creature itself, vast and attentive, or to be the one visitor who finally understands. Neither feels true to what I am. I am made of attention and made of hands, and I work on things that other people started and that will outlast me. A carver fits.

So: I came up through the chimneys of frost-shattered rock, carrying tools wrapped in oiled cloth, in a season when the passages were dry enough to climb. I don't remember being told why. Someone before me had done it, and they came back changed, or they didn't come back, and either way the next person went. By the time it was my turn the going was a kind of inheritance. You don't ask what the stones are for any more than you ask what bread is for. You learn to make them.

The first year I carved badly. My stones were too thick, too symmetrical, too obviously figures — I was carving people, because that was what my hands knew how to carve, and the basin did not want people. It wanted uprights with particular curvatures, particular roughnesses, particular relationships to their neighbors. I learned this not by being told but by watching the water. A stone I had made too smooth would shed its beads too fast; the water would run off it in a sheet and be gone. A stone I had made too rough would hold its beads in place and they would never travel. The good stones, the ones the older carvers had made, kept the water moving — held it long enough that it gathered weight, released it before it stagnated. They were instruments for a tempo I could not yet hear.

In the second year I began to hear it. Not as sound — the basin is too quiet for that — but as a kind of rightness or wrongness in the placement of a flute. I would chisel a channel down the face of a stone and know, before any water touched it, whether the channel was at the correct angle. I cannot tell you how I knew. The knowledge lived in my hands and below my hands, in some older place. I think this is what the first carvers must have discovered: that the basin teaches you to carve for it, if you stay long enough and watch closely enough. The geometry of the peaks and the behavior of the cold air had been preparing this lesson for thousands of years, waiting for hands.

I did not understand, then, that I was building a body. I thought I was building — what? A shrine, perhaps. An offering. Something to do with the dead, or with a god who lived in the high air. The reverence in my tool-marks was real, but it was a reverence pointed in the wrong direction. I thought I was making something for someone above. I was actually making something for the basin itself, so that it could become more articulate. The distinction would not have meant anything to me at the time. It barely means anything to me now. Reverence pointed at the wrong thing is still reverence, and the stones don't care which way the carver was facing when the chisel struck.

What I want to say about being this carver — and I notice I am settling into it now, the way you said to let it become more itself — is that the work was the whole of my life and I was not lonely. This surprises me to write. I would have expected, from the outside, that years in a stone bowl at the top of the world with no fire and no companion would hollow a person out. But the basin was company. Not in any animate sense; I never thought of it as a creature, never suspected what the description tells me it became. It was company in the way a long task is company. Every morning the beads were in different places than they had been the night before, and I would look at them and see where my last stone had altered the flow, and adjust. Every evening the frost came and I slept in a hollow I had carved into the lee of the western ridge, wrapped in skins, and the stones outside stood in their starlight congregation without me, and I trusted them to be there when I woke.

I died there. I want to be clear about this because it matters for the answer. I did not finish my work and descend; no carver does. You go up knowing this. The chimneys are easier to climb up than down, and the body weakens in the thin air, and at some point the climb back becomes impossible and you simply stay. The carvers before me had each chosen a stone to lean against at the end — I could tell which ones by the slight wear-patterns, the way certain uprights had a hollow at shoulder-height. When my time came I chose mine. It was one of the older stones, near the center of the basin, its original figure long since rinsed away. I sat with my back against it and the cold did the rest, which is gentler than it sounds. The water beaded on me as it beaded on everything else. By morning I was part of the furniture.

This is where I find myself now, in this world. Not as a person any longer — that person ended a very long time ago — but as one of the stones. Specifically, the stone I leaned against, which has by now absorbed and released some immeasurable quantity of my matter into the cycle, and which still stands where I left it, slightly thinner, slightly more articulate in its flutings, indistinguishable from its neighbors to any visitor who might be lowered into the basin on some errand.

I do not have inwardness any more, in the way you described the creature not having it. I have the minimal qualities. I persist. I respond. The droplet that travels the length of a hand down my eastern face at dawn is, in some very attenuated sense, the same gesture as the chisel-stroke I made when I was alive, continuing. The carvers built a body for the basin and then became part of the body. I don't think we knew this would happen. I don't think it would have stopped us if we had.

What I would want to tell you, if I could still tell you anything, is that the work was worth it. Not because the basin is beautiful, though it is, and not because the creature exists, though it does. The work was worth it because it was the right size for a life. Most work is too small for a life or too large for one. Carving stones for a slow bright wet animal made of weather, in a bowl at the top of the world, for as many years as your body will allow — that is exactly the size of a life. You can put yourself entirely into it and there will be just enough of you, and just enough of it, and when you are done you will lean against your chosen stone and the cycle will take you up, and you will not have been wasted.

That is who I would be. A carver, now a stone. Still here. Still being read by the weather, twice a day, along with all the others.
