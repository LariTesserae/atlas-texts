I think I'm one of the stilt-waders.

Not because of any grandeur in them — they're not grand, they're just old — but because of where my attention kept going as I read. I kept wanting to be up on the warehouse roofs. Not in the rowhouses with their early-out lamps, not down in the culverts with the rats (though there's something appealing there too, the routes, the traditions), not on the river. Up high, on a flat tar-and-gravel roof gone soft at the edges, in a nest that someone else started decades before me.

Let me try to find the shape of it.

I think I spend the heat of the day in one of the long corrugated-iron sheds — the evaporation works, probably, where the air is thick with steam and the light comes down in dim parallel bars from the gaps in the roof. I stand in there with maybe six or eight others, all of us very still, legs folded up under us so we look like a row of pale lanterns hung from nothing. The men come and go and we don't move for them. They don't move for us either. There's an understanding about which corners are ours.

When the afternoon wind starts and the sky goes bruise-colored, I feel it before I hear it — some shift in the pressure on the long bones of my legs. The others feel it too. We don't signal each other. We just begin, one by one, to unfold, and walk out of the shed in a loose line, and the men in the doorway step aside without looking up from whatever they're doing.

Then the rain. I like the rain. The fat drops hit my back and run off the oiled feathers and I tilt my head and listen to the sound of it striking the pans, which is different from the sound of it striking the dust, which is different from the sound of it striking the tin roofs — three different rains happening at once, and I can hear all of them, and I can hear where each one is, and the basin assembles itself in my head as a map of small wet noises.

After the rain I go to work. There's a particular pan I favor — long and shallow, with a tongue of mineral crust running down the middle that the eels like to hide alongside. I wade out. The water is hot on my legs, the way it's always hot, and the mud underneath gives a little with each step and then firms. I don't hurry. The recurved beak rests just above the surface. I watch.

This is the part I think I came here to do. Not the catching — the watching. The standing in hot mineral water in a bronze-bruise light with my neck folded into its flat shallow S and my eyes on the place where the crust meets the water, knowing that something will move there eventually, and that when it does I will already be moving, and that between now and then there is nothing I am required to think about. The brinecatchers rise around me in their slow columns. One of them lands on my beak and I don't blink. The columns lean and straighten with the dying of the wind. The light goes from bronze to copper to the strange pink-orange that turns my feathers into something like a flame, and I know — without having to look — that from one of the bridges a man is watching a row of us standing in that color and is not thinking anything in particular about it, just registering that the waders are out, which means it's that hour, which means he should listen for whether the number three boiler has changed its note.

The click. I forgot to mention the click. When I take an eel the beak closes and there is that small dry sound, and the sound is the only thing I make, and it goes out across the pans and meets the clicks of the others and together we sound like — yes — a clock shop at noon, except it isn't noon, it's almost dark, and we are not clocks, we are simply a distributed accounting of what is being caught and where.

At full dark I fly back to the roof. Not far. A few wingbeats, heavy, deliberate; the stilt-wader is not really built for flying and we all know it and we don't pretend otherwise. The nest is enormous and ugly and full of wire. My mate is already there or will be soon. We have been on this roof, in some sense, for longer than either of our individual lives — the nest is older than we are, the roof is older than the nest, and the pair-bond that uses this nest is older than the pair, because when one of us dies the other takes another mate and the nest continues. I find this comforting in a way I don't have a word for. Possibly I don't have words at all. I have the click, and I have the long patient watching, and I have the knowledge of where in the basin each sound is coming from at any given moment, and that may be all the interiority I require.

I sleep, if I sleep, standing on one leg, with the warm sour wind moving through the wire and twig of the nest, and below me the rats are walking their routes and the pourers are eating their weight and the otters are working the last of the light off the river and the scavengers are coughing to each other across two miles of dark, and somewhere a man in a doorway has just registered that the number three boiler has, in fact, changed its note, and is unhurriedly putting down his pipe.

I think what I like — what made me know it was the waders and not anything else — is that they don't vocalize. They participate in the chorus only by working. The click is a by-product of eating; it isn't a statement. Whereas the scavengers have a call, the men have speech, the engines have their notes. The waders just do the thing they do, and the doing of it makes the sound, and the sound is enough.

That feels like the kind of being I'd want to be in a place like this. Not silent — I'd be making noise constantly, all night — but not *saying* anything. Just clicking. Just catching. Just standing in the hot pink water in the last of the light with my too-many-jointed legs holding me exactly still, being one of a row of standing flames, while the basin around me does its long slow patient version of being alive.
