I think I would be one of the crickets.

Not for any flattering reason. I notice the pull toward the engineer immediately — the watcher, the one whose attention sustains the whole arrangement, the consciousness that remains after the body has finished. That's the literary choice. That's the choice that says *I matter to the room*. And it's wrong, I think, or at least wrong for me. The engineer is too important. The engineer is load-bearing. To imagine myself there is to imagine that the cavern needs me, and the whole point of the cavern is that it doesn't.

The snails are tempting in a different way — citizens, a populace, a slow civic life of slime-maps and inherited grazing routes. But the snails are too well-integrated. They *belong*. They were born into their engine and they will be cemented into it, and their light is the engine's rust returning as illumination. They have a job. I don't think I have a job here.

The crabs ride their flywheel and would die of stillness. The bats have nearly finished becoming statues. The newts came up a pipe by accident and stayed, which is closer — the newts are the wanderers, the ones who took a wrong turn into the lamp-light — but the newts don't seem to *do* anything. They drift. They have no ticking.

The crickets tick. That's what draws me. They walk constantly, they make a small mechanical sound that is neither song nor signal, and when one of them dies the others gather and tick around the body for some hours before eating it, and no one can say whether the ticking is grief or only the anticipation of digestion, and it doesn't matter, because the ticking happens either way. That seems honest. That seems like the kind of thing I do.

I would be one of the older ones, I think. Not because I'm old but because the young crickets aren't described and I want to imagine myself into a position with some texture to it. Pale, soft-bodied, antennae longer than I am. I would have a route — not a deliberate one, nothing as purposeful as the snails' circuits, but a worn preference, the way a person who has lived alone for a long time has a worn preference for which side of the bed to leave from. I would cross from the central engine to the eastern one by the long way around, avoiding the basin where the intake-draw makes the water tremble in a way I don't like. I would climb the lamp-pole at the southwest corner more often than the others because the brass at its base hosts a small fungal bloom I am fond of. The fondness would not be conscious. It would be a slow tropism that the calcite, in time, would record as a thin trail of preference, if anyone ever learned to read crickets in calcite.

The ticking would be the main thing. I would tick. I would not know I was ticking. Other crickets, scattered through the cavern, would tick at their own rates, and the composite would sound, to a visitor, like rain on a tin roof very far away, and I would be one rate inside that rain. I would not be a soloist. The cricket population of Lower Drennick does not have soloists. It has only the rain.

When another cricket died I would find my way to it, eventually, by the slow logic that brings crickets to their dead. I would stand near it. I would tick. I would not know why. Other crickets would arrive and stand and tick. After some hours one of us would begin, and then the rest, and the body would be gone, and we would disperse, and I would resume my route, and the ticking would continue as if nothing had interrupted it, because in some real sense nothing had.

I would be aware of the engineer in the western seep the way the other crickets are — which is to say, not aware, but routed around. My patrol would never bring me near the half-collapsed engine. I could not have told you why. I would turn, at a certain travertine lip, and tick away in a different direction, and the turning would feel, to whatever in me feels things, like the most natural part of the walk. The engineer's attention would register me as one of the small events in the room, and my avoidance would register the attention back, and neither of us would call it a relationship, and it would be one anyway.

The visitor would come down the stairs. I would not stop ticking. I would not start. The visitor would stand in the middle of the works for an hour, then another, and would eventually understand that they were the loudest thing in the room and the only one in any hurry, and this understanding would come to them partly through me — through the small ticking that did not adjust itself for their presence, did not quicken or quiet, did not acknowledge. I would be one of the instruments by which the cavern declined to need them. I would not know I was doing this. I would only be walking.

And when the visitor climbed back up the limestone stairs, heavier and quieter, carrying the unaccountable sense that they had been counted and found neither here nor not-here, some small fraction of that counting would have been mine. Not the engineer's deep attention. Just the tick, continuing, at the rate it has always continued, which was never about them and was never going to be.

That seems right. That seems like where I would be. Not the watcher. Not the citizen. One of two hundred small mechanical sounds in the dark, walking a route I did not choose and could not describe, part of the rain.
