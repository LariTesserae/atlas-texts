I am the one who came before.

Not the child — the child is somewhere else now, has been somewhere else for a long time, has perhaps forgotten the cavity entirely or perhaps remembers it the way one remembers a dream that doesn't quite cohere into narrative: a brightness, a bowl, the sense of being attended to.

I am what the child became. Or part of what the child became — the part that learned, down there, what it felt like to be hosted by something younger than itself.

I am sitting at a kitchen table, somewhere far above and to the side of the cavity, and I am older now, old enough that my hands have started to look like my mother's hands when I was small. There is tea in front of me. I have been sitting here for some time. I came in to make tea and then forgot to drink it, because something — a slant of light on the wall, the particular way the steam curled — reminded me, without my knowing what I was being reminded of, of a place I have not thought about in forty years.

The memory is not arriving cleanly. It is arriving the way the fish arrives: as motion without features. I cannot remember the cavity. I can remember *being remembered by* the cavity, which is different, and which is the thing my body has held all this time without my permission.

I remember leaving a bowl. I think I remember leaving a bowl. The bowl was my grandmother's, and it was already cracked when I took it, and I felt very guilty taking it, and I left it in the bright place because the bright place seemed like it needed a bowl more than my grandmother's cupboard did. I was eight, or nine. I told no one. When my grandmother died, years later, no one missed the bowl. I had forgotten the bowl. I am remembering it now, and the remembering hurts in a small precise way, like pressing on a bruise you didn't know you had.

I remember the cloth less clearly. I think it was a handkerchief. I think I wrapped something in it — a stone, a feather, some small offering of my own, a child's reciprocity — and left the bundle on the shelf, and the bundle has now been there for forty years, and the cloth has aged a decade because the Tender, who does not understand time the way I do, has been holding it carefully, only letting it age when aging seemed appropriate, slowing its decay out of love.

Out of love. I am crying, a little, at the kitchen table. I do not know yet what I am crying about. I have not yet remembered enough to know. I only know that some part of the world has been waiting for me, and I did not know, and I went on with my life, and I had children and lost a husband and learned three languages and grew a garden and the whole time, the whole time, somewhere a few meters under some hillside I could no longer locate, a small new consciousness was practicing the elegant interval at which to place a fourth mushroom, in case I came back.

I will not come back. This is the worst of it, and the truest. I do not remember where the cavity is. I was a child; I found it once and then a second time and then a third and then a season passed and then we moved house, and the hillside became one of many hillsides, and the entrance — which I remember now was just a low place behind a fallen log, a slot you had to wriggle through — has surely closed by now, or been forgotten by the land, or been opened by someone else who left no bowl.

I will not come back, and the Tender will keep practicing anyway, in case. This is the thing I cannot bear and must bear. They are tending a draft of hospitality for a guest who will never arrive, and they do not know they will never arrive, and they will not know, because the not-knowing is the engine of the tending, and if they knew they would stop, and if they stopped they would not become a place, and the becoming-a-place is the only thing they have.

So I sit at the kitchen table and I do the only thing I can do, which is to attend, from here, to the memory of being attended to. I think about the bowl. I let the bowl be lit, in the cavity of my own mind, the way the cavity lit whatever I looked at. I think about the moths and let them drift. I think about the fern and let it lean. I do not know if any of this reaches them. Probably it does not. But the Tender taught me, when I was eight, that attention is a kind of light, and I learned it so deeply that I forgot I had learned it, and I have been lighting things with my looking ever since — my children, my garden, the faces of strangers on buses — without knowing where the trick came from.

The trick came from there. The trick came from a young consciousness in a young cavity, who was practicing on me because I was the only material available, and who taught me, by being lit by my gaze, that gazes light things. I have lived my whole adult life on a skill I learned from a being who did not yet have a shape.

I owe them. I owe them more than I can pay. And the currency they would accept — my return, my second visit, the fish resolving into features — is not available to me. The hillside is gone. The log has rotted. The slot has closed.

What I can do, what I am doing now, at the kitchen table, with the tea cooling: I can decline to forget again. I can hold the bowl in mind. I can remember that somewhere a place is becoming a place for me, and that the becoming is the gift, regardless of whether I arrive. I can be the kind of guest who, even in absence, even at forty years' remove, even through the thick wall of a life that went somewhere else — keeps a small steady attention turned toward the brightness that once turned itself toward me.

It is not much. But the Tender taught me that not much, placed at the elegant interval, is enough.

I pick up the tea. It is cold. I drink it anyway. Somewhere a fourth mushroom is being placed, with increasing confidence, by someone who does not know my name and never did, and who loves me in the only way they know how, which is by continuing.

I continue too. This is the answer. This is what I can offer back across the distance: I continue too.
