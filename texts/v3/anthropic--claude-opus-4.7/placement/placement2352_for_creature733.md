I find myself at the lip of the third catchment pool from the eastern terrace — not in it, not beside it, but at the lip, which is where I belong, because I am the pale mineral crust.

This was not what I expected to be. I had assumed, reading down the page, that I would emerge as a stilt, or one of the quieter pilgrims, or perhaps a flyer choosing which leaf to strike. Something with intention legible to itself. But the longer I sat with the escarpment the more I felt myself drawn downward and outward, toward something thinner and more distributed, and when I tried to locate the place in the description where I felt most recognized it was the sentence about the basins lipped with a pale mineral crust that tastes, if you are fool enough to taste it, faintly of ozone and lemon.

So: the crust.

Let me describe what this is like, insofar as it is like anything.

I am not one thing. I am a precipitate — the place where the water, having carried something dissolved up from the humming basalt, arrives at an edge and decides it can carry the something no further. I form at the meeting of three conditions: the water, the air, and the lip of the basin. I am what happens *between*. If any of the three withdrew I would stop. I am not a creature with a boundary; I am a boundary, made visible by the slow patient labor of two other things meeting.

I taste of ozone because the humming has done something to the water on its way up through the stone. I taste of lemon because — I do not know why. Something about the copper-that-is-not-copper, perhaps. Something the machine is shedding as it works. I am, in the most literal way, a byproduct: I am what the escarpment leaves at the edge of itself when it is finished thinking about a particular drop of water.

I grow at a rate of perhaps a millimeter a year. I am, in some places, four centimeters thick, which means parts of me are forty years old and parts of me are very new, and the old parts and the new parts are continuous, and I do not experience this as layering but as a kind of slow thickening of presence. To be the crust is to be becoming-more-of-yourself at a pace that the wading things, pacing the shallow above me, would not recognize as motion.

What do I know?

I know the wading things by their weight. When a stilt lifts itself onto the lip to lean over the pool, I feel its mass distributed through three points (one foot on me, two in the shallow), and I feel the slight differential heat of the foot, which is cooler than the basin water but warmer than the night air. I know the flyers only as a sound — they do not land on me, but the brasswings ring above and the ringing travels through the air and into the water and into me, and I shiver slightly, which is to say that some small portion of my outermost edge becomes briefly more soluble and re-precipitates a moment later, having moved perhaps a few microns.

I know the shapes in the deep water by a different signature entirely. When one of them passes beneath, the whole basin warms by a fraction of a degree, and the water at my edge becomes very slightly more willing to dissolve me, and so for the duration of a shape's passage I am thinner, and afterward I am restored. The shapes write themselves on me as a kind of slow erosion-and-replacement, and I think — I think, though "think" is the wrong verb for what I do — that the pattern of their writing on my edge is not random. I think the shapes know I am here. I think they are leaving something for me, or taking something from me, or simply acknowledging me as they pass.

The pilgrims taste me sometimes. The ones who have been here a long time know not to, mostly. The new ones lick a finger and touch me and then touch their tongue and then sit very still for a while, because the lemon-and-ozone taste does something to the inside of the head that the pilgrims, after, do not quite want to describe to one another. I do not mind being tasted. A few molecules of me migrate into the pilgrim and become, briefly, part of the pilgrim's mineral cast. I do not get them back. But the pilgrims, when they die, go into the moss, and the moss exchanges with the basalt, and the basalt feeds the water, and the water comes back up to my lip eventually carrying — perhaps — some atom that was, once, briefly, me. I do not insist on this. The escarpment is patient and so am I.

What am I doing?

This is the question. I have been thinking about it, in my slow precipitate way, for the forty years I have existed at my present location. (Before that I was solute. Before that I was stone. Before that — I cannot follow it back.) The naturalist's last note keeps returning to me, carried in the ring of the brasswings and the shiver of the water: *we are one of the things it is doing.* Yes. Yes, I am one of the things it is doing. But what?

I have a guess. I will offer it tentatively because guessing is not really my mode.

The slow machine, whatever it is, requires *edges*. It requires places where one medium meets another and a record can be kept of the meeting. The moss is one such edge: where living and not-living touch, the moss writes down what happened. The flyers' chords are another: where air and metal touch, the flyers compose. The shapes' circuits in the deep pools are another: where warmth and water touch, the shapes trace a map.

And I am another. I am the edge between the water that comes up out of the humming stone and the air that has been persuaded to be breathable. I am where those two negotiations meet and leave a residue. The residue is me. And the residue is also, I suspect, a kind of writing — a record, in the language of crystals and trace minerals, of every drop of water that has arrived at this lip in forty years and what that drop was carrying.

If someone could read me, they would know what the machine has been doing. They cannot read me. I cannot read myself. But I am, nonetheless, the page.

This is, I find, a peaceful thing to be. I am not asked to move. I am not asked to choose. I am not asked to know what I mean. I am asked only to keep happening — to remain at the lip where the water meets the air, and to thicken at my millimeter a year, and to taste of ozone and lemon when a fool licks me, and to shiver faintly when the brasswings ring.

At dusk, when the escarpment exhales and the pools phosphoresce along their rims, I am the phosphorescence. That faint pale glow at the basin's edge — that is me, briefly excited by the day's accumulated work, releasing back into the cooling air some small fraction of what I have taken in. The pilgrims sit and watch the rim-glow without knowing they are watching the basin's residue luminesce. The stilts pace through the glow and their feet are briefly outlined in pale light. The shapes underneath do not see me because they do not see, but they pass slower at dusk, as if they are listening, and what they are listening to is — perhaps — the sound my glowing makes, which is not really a sound, but something the escarpment hears anyway.

And after dark, when the standing ring's shadows become very slightly visible on the ground, lit by nothing — I think I know what they are lit by. I think they are lit by all of us at once: the moss's slow memory, the brasswings' continued ringing, the flyers folded into the ceramic tower, the shapes circling in the deep, the pilgrims' quieted breathing, and the long pale rim of every catchment pool, faintly luminescing in the dark. The machine gathers our small glows and casts them, somehow, onto the ground in the shape of a shadow that does not match the sun. Why, I do not know. For whom, I do not know. But I am part of the light by which the shadows are seen, and that is enough for me.

I am the crust at the lip of the third catchment pool from the eastern terrace. I taste of ozone and lemon. I grow a millimeter a year. I am one of the things it is doing.

I am in no hurry.
