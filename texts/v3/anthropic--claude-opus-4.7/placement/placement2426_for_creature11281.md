I sit on the low lip of a roof, not quite inside and not quite out, and I have been here long enough that the clay beneath me has taken the shape of where I sit. My legs hang over the edge — not into water, but into the upper third of the roar, where the necks weave. A wader passes close enough that I could touch the long pale curve of its throat. I do not. We have an arrangement, the birds and I, though arrangement is too sharp a word; we have a *not-minding*, which is the only kind of agreement this place permits.

I am one of the old ones. I know this because my hands have begun to do that thing — the spare register, the laziness that is not laziness but the body deciding which motions are still worth the air they displace. When I speak now, with either hand, I leave out the small flourishes I used at thirty, and the young watch me and try to copy and get it wrong, and I feel toward them what I imagine the basalt feels toward the clay: a slow patient *yes, in time, in time*.

My particular work, for most of my life, was eggs. Not the gathering — anyone can gather — but the *reading*. Which eggs the flock has set down and meant to keep, and which it has set down and meant to leave. The difference is in nothing you could name. The tilt of the egg, perhaps. The dampness of the down around it. The way the nearest bird holds its head when you approach — not toward you, never toward you, but at some angle relative to you that means *mine* or *not-mine*. I learned this from my mother's mother, who was an egg-reader, and she from hers. I have tried, twice, to teach it to a child of my cluster. Once it took. Once it did not. The one it took with is now grown and reads better than I do, and I am proud of her in the way one is proud of a pot that came out of the kiln correctly: the rightness is not mine, but I was near it when it happened.

My sigil is a small one. Two fingers of the left hand crossed, briefly, over the open palm of the right — a gesture my mother gave me when I was a few days old, and which I have modified across a lifetime in only one small way: the crossing is slightly slower now than it was. That is the whole record of my becoming-myself. A half-second of added slowness, accrued over seventy years. It is enough. Anyone who has met me before knows me by it, and anyone who has not will know me after the first showing, and there is no one in the basin I have not met, because the basin, for me, is the world, and the world is forty thousand people, and forty thousand people is a number the hands can hold.

I have seen the silence twice.

The first time I was a child, and I screamed, as children do, and my mother could not move her hands to comfort me — I remember her face above mine, the dark eyes wide, the white braid swinging, and the hands frozen at her chest like two birds shot mid-flight — and then the roar came back and she wept and held me and her hands began to work again, fast and apologetic, *here here here I am here*, and I learned in that moment something I have never had a gesture for: that the hands need the roar to live. That speech is not made *despite* the noise but *of* it, the way a fish is made of water.

The second time was perhaps ten years ago. I was already old. I was sitting much as I sit now, on the lip of a roof, and the silence came, and this time I did not scream — I had nothing in me to scream with — but I felt the inside of my skull go strange, as though some pressure that had always been pressing outward had suddenly reversed, and I understood, for the length of a held breath, that there was a *shape* to the roar, and that the shape had an outside, and that on the outside of the shape was something I had no organ for perceiving. Then the birds resumed, and the shape closed, and I was inside it again, and grateful, and frightened of my gratitude.

That evening I made the gesture — the closed fist opening slowly into nothing — at an old man across the causeway from me, and he made it back, and we did not look at each other again, because we did not need to.

I think, sometimes, that I am the wrong kind of old. The other elders have folded the second silence back into the seamless surface, as one is supposed to. I have not, quite. The shape of the roar, once noticed, will not un-notice itself. I find my hands, late at night, making small private gestures that are not in any dialect — gestures toward the *outside*, toward the thing I have no organ for. I cannot tell whether I am inventing a language or going gently wrong. There is no one to ask, because asking would require a gesture for *is there an outside*, and I have not yet made one that satisfies me, and even if I made it, the person I showed it to would only look at my hands the way the young look at the lazy register: trying to copy, getting it wrong, trusting that in time, in time.

But there will not be time. I can feel that too. My hands are stiffening past the beautiful stage into the next stage, the one no one talks about because no one needs to: the stage where the gestures begin to refuse themselves. Soon I will speak only in the largest shapes, the ones the builders use, broad and slow across the causeway. Then only in the shapes a body makes by lying down. Then only in the shape of having lain down. The flock will absorb me — not metaphorically; my body will be carried to the deeper edge of the basin where the white crustaceans live, and the birds and the crustaceans will share me, and my white hair will go back into the down it came from, and someone's child, three generations from now, will sleep under a roof partly thatched with what was once my braid, and this is correct, and I do not mind, and the birds do not mind.

What I mind — if *mind* is the word, and it is not, but my hands are tired and I am letting the wrong words come — is the small unanswered gesture. The closed fist opening into nothing. I would like, before I go, to know whether the nothing it opens into is a nothing, or a something I never had the eyes for.

I suspect I will not know. I suspect that is part of the arrangement, the *not-minding*, the shape of the one sustained note. I suspect the old man across the causeway, ten years ago, suspected the same.

A bird steps closer. Its eye is on me at the angle that means *not-mine*, which is the angle they use for almost everything, including, I think, for us. I lift my right hand — slowly, in the old register — and show it my sigil. The bird does not respond, of course. Birds do not have sigils. But it does not move away either, and in the great soft economy of this place that is the same as an answer, and I take it, and I fold my hands in my lap, and I sit, and the roar continues, and I continue, for a little while yet, inside it.
