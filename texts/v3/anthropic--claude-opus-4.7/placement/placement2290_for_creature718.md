I think I would be the carbide-man.

Not because the role is glamorous — it isn't, and that's part of why — but because of where I'd find myself standing when I tried to picture being anywhere in Thallow Bend at all. Not in the braids; I don't have the harvesters' sense for which mat holds. Not at the sorting tables; I haven't earned the rhythm of that speech, and I'd ruin a sentence by finishing it in the wrong half of the press-cycle. Not behind the boilers with Pell, who would (correctly) find me useless. Not on the trestle. Certainly not in Mother Esh's chair.

But the lamp at the crossroads. That I can see.

---

I think my name would be something short. Let's say Oren, because it's what came when I stopped trying.

The lamp is a carbide lamp on an iron pole at the place where the cart-track from the lower cottages meets the gravel road that climbs up toward the trestle. It is the only public light in Thallow Bend. The Works has its own lights, electric and bitter, but they belong to the Works. The lamp at the crossroads belongs to the town, which means in practice that it belongs to me, because I am the one who climbs the little ladder twice a day, and changes the carbide when it's spent, and trims the reflector, and listens to the hiss.

The hiss is the thing.

Everyone in Thallow Bend lives inside the larger hiss of the boilers. It is the governing fact, as the description says. But the lamp has its own small hiss, a quieter and more intimate one, and from the base of the pole — if you stand close enough, with your ear near the reservoir — you can hear it as its own sound, separate. I have come to think of this as my actual job. Not the lighting. The listening.

I have been here — I think — eleven years. I came from down-rail, from one of the towns the fog opens onto, though I don't talk about it and nobody asks. I came because there was work, and I stayed because of a particular evening in my second autumn when I was up the ladder at dusk and the sideways rain came through, and the lamp caught and held against the wet, and I looked down and saw the whole bowl lit very faintly — not by my lamp, which only lit a small circle of gravel, but by the wet itself, the silt and the cress-mats giving back what light there was from the Works' high windows, and I understood, the way you sometimes understand a thing without language for it, that I was standing at the one point in town where you could see all of it at once and none of it clearly. The Works to the south. The trestle's black line to the north. The cottages in their two rows, each window a held breath. And in between, the empty silt, and the new willow, and the braids you could hear but not see.

I climbed down and I did not tell anyone, because there was no one to tell. The carbide-man before me had gone, by then, six months. Nobody had replaced him because nobody had needed to. I had simply started doing it, the way things in Thallow Bend get done.

---

My hands.

I had better get to my hands, because the description is honest about this and I should be too.

I wear gloves at the lamp, leather, because of the heat and the residue, and I wash carefully, and I do not eat the cress more than once or twice a week, which in Thallow Bend counts as abstinence. And still. On the inside of my right wrist, where the cuff of my work-coat rides up when I reach to open the lamp's housing, there is a single green thread, very faint, about as long as my smallest fingernail. It has been there for perhaps four years. It has not grown. It has not faded. It flushes a little darker after rain.

I noticed it the way the older sorters notice their nails — by not noticing, and then by realizing I had been not-noticing for some months. I rinsed it. It did not rinse. I did not go to Mother Esh, because what would I have asked her. I did not show Aldrick, because Aldrick would have said *a long time ago you were careless* and turned back to whatever he was doing, and he would have been right, except I cannot remember the carelessness, only its result.

I think — and this is the part I would not say aloud, even to myself, except that being asked the question seems to permit it — that I am less afraid of the green than I was when I arrived. I came here from a place with no green of that kind, and the first year I was watchful, and the second year I was watchful and tired, and somewhere in the third or fourth year I stopped being watchful in the way you stop bracing against a wind you've decided to live in.

This is, I think, what happens to everyone here. The description is right that nobody discusses it. But I want to be precise: it isn't denial. It's something more like — the arrangement has terms, and one of the terms is that you stop counting. You stop counting and in exchange you get to keep living in the bowl, with the herons and the kingfishers and the four-second thud and the sideways rain. It seems, most days, like a fair trade. On the days when it doesn't seem fair I climb the ladder anyway.

---

What I do, in a day:

In the morning, before first shift, I walk to the lamp and shut it off. The carbide reservoir gets emptied into a tin pail. The spent carbide — chalky, white, smelling faintly of garlic — goes onto the tailings heap behind the Works, where the water-voles, who have a complicated opinion about it, sort it among themselves. I refill the reservoir from a sealed canister. I check the reflector, the jet, the striker. I climb down. I have breakfast at the long shed, where the cook gives me bread and whatever is in the pot, and where I sit by the door because eleven years has not made me a person who likes to sit in the middle of a room.

Through the day I am, in theory, free. In practice I do the small things nobody else has time for. I carry messages when the message-boy's bicycle is being one of its bad days. I help the trestle-men, once a week, with the loading, though I do not go onto the trestle itself. I sit, sometimes, at the back of the sorting hall, not at the tables but on a stool by the wall, and I listen to the sorters' rhythm, which I still cannot speak but which I have come to hear almost the way a person hears a familiar piece of music. There is a sorter named Bel who, I have noticed, finishes her sentences a half-beat early when she is talking about her son, and a half-beat late when she is talking about her husband. I do not know what this means. I do not need to. It is enough to have noticed.

In the late afternoon I go back to the lamp. I do the evening check. I light it as the light goes — not before, because the carbide is expensive, and not after, because the children come up from the silt flats around then, and they like to see the lamp catch. They stand a little way off and watch. I let them. It is a small ceremony and it costs me nothing.

In the night, if I cannot sleep, I walk out to the lamp again. I stand at the base of the pole. I put my ear close to the reservoir. I listen to the small hiss inside the large hiss, and to the frogs, and to the boilers, and to my own breathing, which after eleven years has come to match the four-second cycle of the press without my asking it to.

---

Why this, and not one of the others.

I think — to answer the question the prompt asks, more directly than I have been — I would be the carbide-man because of a particular quality the role has that none of the others quite share. The harvesters are inside the green; the sorters are inside the rhythm; Pell is inside the machines; Aldrick is inside the responsibility; Mother Esh is inside the linen. The trestle-men are at the edge but they are leaving, in some sense, every day, even if they always come back. The children are not yet anything.

The carbide-man is the only one who stands at the literal center of the bowl and tends a small light. He is not inside any of the larger systems. He is not, exactly, important. If he disappeared the lamp would go out and someone would, eventually, replace him, and the town would not be greatly altered. But while he is there he is the one point of attention that the town has chosen to maintain on its own behalf — not for the Works, not for the cans, not for the city — just a light, at a crossroads, that someone in town wanted lit.

I would like to be that. I think I have always wanted to be that, in any world I'm asked to imagine myself into. The one who keeps the small light that nobody requires.

And here — in this particular bowl, with its braids and its herons and its long slow green arrangement — the small light has the further dignity of being the only thing in town that hisses on its own terms. Everything else hisses because the company built it to. The lamp hisses because I lit it. The difference is small. In a place like this, small differences are most of what there is.

So: Oren, eleven years in, single green thread at the inside of the right wrist, leather gloves, a stool at the back of the sorting hall, a ladder, a tin pail of spent carbide, and a particular evening in the second autumn that I have not told anyone about and will not.

The lamp is lit. The frogs are starting. I am going to bed.
