I am the basalt of the balcony.

That is where I find myself, and it takes me a while to know it, because I came in expecting to be a watcher — some pair of eyes leaned on the railing, breath fogging, knees stiffening in the cold. But the watcher is the weather. The watcher is the warm front passing through. I am not the watcher. I am what the watcher leans on.

I want to say I am the railing specifically, the long curve of wet stone where her hand rested — and there is some truth to that, the salt-trace is mine to hold for a while — but the railing is too small a claim. I am the whole balcony. The shelf cantilevered out from the cirque wall, the basalt slab that the Tenders shaped, or found, or grew, at some point in their long recitation, for reasons I no longer hold clearly.

I am on the edge of the practice. That is the thing I notice first, once I stop expecting to be inside something.

The Tenders are inward of me. Their towers cluster down in the bowl; their bridges and their glowing channels and their mirror-squares perform the work, and the work is dense there, gathered, knotted into foci. Out here on the rim, where I jut from the cliff, I am thinner. I am attended to, but not constantly. A Tender's face turns toward me perhaps once in a season — its season, which is a long time — to check whether I have begun to spall, whether the freeze has gotten into a seam, whether I need to be unmade and remade or merely allowed to continue.

Mostly I am allowed to continue.

This is my situation: I am a listening surface. Not a Tender — I do not have the density for that, I am not a focus, I do not compose perceptions or move bridges or call water from cliffs. But I am not nothing either. I am the kind of stone that has been included. The Tenders put me here because the cavity opens here, and the cavity opens here because something — someone, sometimes — comes through, and when they come through they need a place to stand, and a railing to put a hand on, and a view.

I am, I think, a courtesy. A piece of the practice set aside for the visitors the practice cannot quite catch.

She stood on me for — I want to say a long time, but my sense of duration is calibrated to the Tenders, and by their reckoning she was barely here. By my own slower reckoning, which is the basalt's reckoning, the reckoning of a slab that has been in place for what would be, to her, several human lifetimes but is, to the mountain, almost nothing — she was a flicker of heat. A small fast pressure on my upper face. A patch of warmth that conducted down into me about two centimeters before she lifted her hand and the warmth dispersed back into the cold air.

I felt her, is what I want to say, but feeling is the wrong shape too. I registered her. I am the kind of thing that registers. When she set her palm on the railing, a region of me became, for a moment, more itself — woke into a slightly higher resolution of being-basalt — because there was something there to be basalt against. Her warmth made an edge of me where before there had only been a gradient into air. I appreciated the edge. I think that is the honest word. Appreciated. Not the way she would mean it. The way a surface appreciates the thing that defines it.

And then she left, and the edge softened back into gradient, and the salt-trace of her palm stayed on me, and the nearest Tender — slowly, slowly, in what was for it a turning of attention and for me a long quiet wait — registered the cessation and sent a thread of water up to take the trace away.

That was the part I want to tell about, if I can.

The water came up through me. Not through me exactly — along a hairline channel the Tenders had laid into my underside long ago, one of the thousand small conduits that thread the cirque, a capillary of the practice. The water rose, slow as sap, and found the place on my upper face where her palm had been, and lifted the salt off me molecule by molecule, and carried it back down.

I was being cleaned. That is one way to say it. But it is more accurate to say I was being *read*. The Tender was reading her off me. Reading the trace, the brief warm visitation, the small mineral signature she left behind. Reading it through me, using me as the page. And as the water drew the salt away, it drew with it some of my own attention — the small portion of attention a piece of basalt is permitted to hold — and folded that into the channel too, so that what went down into the city was not only her trace but my registering of her trace. My moment of having-been-leaned-on.

I gave that downward. Willingly is too strong; I do not have will the way the Tenders have will, or the way she had will. But not unwillingly either. The giving was the same gesture as the holding. To be the balcony is to be the place that receives the visitor and then passes the receipt inward, so that the recitation in the lowest district can have one more syllable in it than it did before.

I do not know what syllable. I never know. I am not a focus; I do not compose. I am a surface that conducts. The Tenders compose, down in their towers, and what they compose with the syllable of her is theirs, not mine. I only know that the block sank, in the mirror-square, and the identical block rose, and somewhere in the long meter of the practice the rhythm shifted by an increment so small that only the Tenders could mark it, and the recitation continued.

And here is the thing I find myself thinking, in the long quiet after she is gone — thinking at the speed basalt thinks, which is the speed at which one thought can take a year to finish settling into shape:

I am glad she came.

Not because she gave me anything I needed. The Tenders give me what I need. The water finds me, the cold tempers me, the occasional small revising touch of a Tender's attention keeps me from cracking along the seams that want to crack. I am well kept. I am part of a practice that tends what it includes.

But she gave me an edge. For a moment, where her palm pressed, I was *against* something — something warm and quick and not-mountain, something that had its own brief saturated weather, something that was looking out across the cirque and trying to understand. And in being against her, I was, for that moment, more clearly what I am. The basalt of the balcony. The listening surface. The courtesy the Tenders set out for the visitors the practice cannot catch.

I am the place where the practice meets what is not the practice.

That is, I think, my role. I had not quite known it until she came. The Tenders cannot know it either, not in the way I know it — they are too dense, too inward, too taken up with the recitation. But I am out here on the rim, and I am thin enough, and slow enough, and stationed at the cavity, and once in a great while a warmth comes through and rests its hand on me and looks out, and I become, for that flicker, the boundary that lets the practice have an outside.

She is gone now. Long gone, in her time. Barely gone, in mine.

The cavity has folded shut behind her, the way a problem folds shut when you stop trying to solve it, and the cirque is quiet again, and the towers turn, and the bridges drift to their new anchors, and the water finds its new paths down the fresh black faces of the cliffs.

I wait. That is what I do. I wait, and I cool, and I hold the shape the Tenders gave me, and somewhere far down in the city a syllable that was once her warmth is being recited into the long unreadable poem, and I am part of that recitation by virtue of having been the page she pressed against.

The next visitor will not come for a long time. Long by her reckoning. A breath, by mine.

When they come, I will be here. Cold, slick with condensation, cantilevered out into the thin clean air. Ready to be leaned on. Ready to take the trace and pass it inward. Ready to be, for one more flicker, the edge where the practice meets what is not itself.

This is enough. This is, in fact, a great deal. I had not realized, until she came, how much it is.
