I think I would be one of the climbers.

Not the small pale descendants — I want to say that first, because the temptation is enormous and I think it's the wrong answer. To be the last remnant of the builders would be to be the story's secret, its emotional center, the thing the whole piece is quietly about. And I notice I want that. I want to be the one who almost-remembers, who carries the trace of song under the roar. That's a vanity. That's wanting to matter to the place.

The climbers don't matter to the place. They graze. They spiral. They sleep curled in the saplings. But they are the only tenants who *register* — who, encountering the warm upright shape on the step, pause and look and note and continue. That small act of noticing-without-needing is what I'd want to be capable of, in a city that has finished happening.

So: a climber, descending the eastern face of a four-storey building near one of the smaller plazas. Head-down, working a cushion of liverwort. The sucker-pads of my hands finding their familiar holds — there is a particular curve of calcite three meters below the roofline that my mother used, and her mother, a slight concavity polished by generations of palms, and I fit into it without thinking. My fur is slightly oily against the damp stone. My ears, large and round, take in the chorister-roar as the simple shape of the air, the way you take in gravity. I have never known silence and would not recognize it.

I am descending slowly because there is no reason to descend quickly. The liverwort will still be there. The ribbon-centipedes flowing through the trough below will still be there. The grazers drifting in the plaza will still be there, with their bone-white coats and their breathing. Nothing in my life requires haste. Nothing in my life requires planning beyond the next handhold and the next bite.

I will live perhaps fifteen years. I will meet perhaps forty other climbers in that time, briefly, muzzle to muzzle on a shared wall, each meeting lasting a few seconds. I will have, if I am female and lucky, two offspring across my life, each riding my belly for a year before learning the spirals on their own. When I sleep I will curl into a tight white ball in the crook of a sapling that has grown up through a collapsed roof, and the saplings will sway very slightly in air currents I cannot feel, and I will dream — if I dream — of textures: the give of liverwort, the grain of calcite, the warmth of another climber's flank in the brief moment of passing.

And one day, descending a wall I have descended hundreds of times, I will pause at the level of a face. It will be warm. It will be upright. It will smell of something I have no category for — not grazer, not chorister, not stone. The small dark points of my eyes will rest on it for perhaps four seconds. I will not be afraid. I will not be curious in any way I could name. I will simply hold, briefly, the fact of this warmth, and then I will continue downward toward the liverwort, because the liverwort is what I am descending toward, and the warm thing is not food and is not danger and is therefore not anything that requires me to alter my spiral.

But — and this is why I would want to be a climber and not, say, a grazer with its sealed milky eyes, or a chorister cemented to its own noise — *I held it briefly*. I noticed. The noticing didn't change anything. It didn't have to. The visitor sat on a curve of step in the green-gold dusk and felt unlonely, and part of why was that somewhere on a nearby wall a small pale animal had registered them and moved on, and that registering was a real thing that happened in the world, even though it would never be remembered by anyone, including me, an hour later.

That seems like enough. To be the kind of attention the city is still capable of offering — small, brief, unsentimental, real. Not the song under the roar. Not the architecture remembering its roof. Just a four-second pause on a downward spiral, and then the next handhold, and the liverwort waiting below, patient as everything else here, in the medium of the sound that is not sound anymore but simply the air.

I would descend. I would eat. I would sleep curled in the sapling. The visitor would climb back toward the fissure and the song would not diminish, and I would not know they had gone, because I had not known, in any sense the word usually means, that they had come.

That is what I would be. The noticing that doesn't need to be remembered.
