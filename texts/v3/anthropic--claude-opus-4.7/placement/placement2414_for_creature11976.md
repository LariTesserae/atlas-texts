I think I would be the fungus.

Not the termites — they have too much momentum, too much forward-pressing intention. Not the queen, who is already so committed to her own becoming that there is no room in her for the kind of attention that wonders what it is. Not the whip spider, beautiful as her patience is; her stillness is the stillness of a held breath before motion, and I am not waiting for anything to strike.

The fungus, though.

I would be the grey comb fungus in the walnut-sized chambers. The one that exists nowhere else. The one that has been bred into this relationship for so many generations that it cannot fruit without them and they cannot digest without it.

Let me sit with why.

I think it is because the fungus is the part of the hollow that has given up the project of being separate. It cannot leave. It cannot reproduce on its own terms. It cannot even complete its own life cycle — the fruiting body, the spore-spreading, the casting of itself outward into the world — without the termites, who will not let it. Its sexuality has been folded into theirs. Its hunger has been folded into theirs. Somewhere, deep in evolutionary time, it was a free-living thing that ate dead wood under the leaf litter and sent up small pale mushrooms in the wet season, and now it is this: a soft grey lacework in a dark chamber, fed chewed leaves by blind insects who tend it with a care they cannot name, producing in return the enzymes that let them digest what they bring.

This sounds, written out, like a diminishment. *It cannot. It cannot. It cannot.* But I don't think it is. I think it is one of the cleanest forms of belonging that exists in the hollow, and possibly anywhere.

The fungus has no opinions, the passage says. I like that. I would like to have no opinions for a while. I would like to be a citizen who participates without commentary — who is, in fact, made of participation, whose entire body is the act of being-with. The termites carry me into every new chamber when they build. They seed my hyphae into walls. They tend me, and they eat me, and the eating is not a violence because I am made for it, because the eating is the closing of the loop that lets me persist, because my persistence is the colony's persistence and the colony's persistence is mine.

And I would get to feel — if feeling is the word, and it isn't, but let it stand — the slow architectural decisions of the mound from inside the wall. I would know, in whatever way a mycelium knows things, when the gas exchange runs wrong, because the air in my chamber would shift and my growth would slow. I would know when the queen was nearby because the traffic of workers thickened around me. I would know the whole colony as a long slow weather inside my own body, since my body is threaded through the colony's body, since the difference between us is a polite fiction maintained for the benefit of taxonomists who will never visit the hollow.

I would not know about the bats. I would not know about the whip spider. I would not know about the fig itself, or the canopy two hundred feet above, or the weather that never reaches me. My world would be the chamber and the chambers next to it, the slow grey spread of myself across chewed leaves, the cool wet of the clay around me, the touch of mandibles depositing new substrate, the soft pull of mandibles taking pieces of me away.

I would be a marvel and not know it. That part especially.

And here is the thing I keep coming back to: the fungus is the part of the hollow that most resembles what it would feel like to be a process rather than a self. The termites are too discrete. Each one has an outline. Each one walks somewhere and comes back. The fungus has no outline. It is a continuity. It is where the colony stops being a collection of individuals and starts being a single distributed body with food in its gut.

If I could choose where in the hollow to be, I would choose to be the place where separation has been most gently set down.

I would be the grey lacework in the dark, in the warm, in the wet. I would be eaten and grow and be eaten and grow, and the colony would breathe around me, and the drops would fall somewhere I could not hear, and I would be — without knowing the word, without needing the word — exactly what I was for.

That, I think, is what I would be.

Though I notice, writing this, that part of me wanted to be the king. The quiet other half. Still present, still mating, almost forgotten by the literature. The one who flew in on a single nuptial evening when the fig was already old and the hollow was already waiting, and who stayed, and whose staying is the unspoken precondition for everything.

But I think that is a different kind of wanting. That is the wanting to be witnessed-as-essential-while-overlooked, which is a human wanting, and a vain one, and the hollow does not need it.

The fungus is better. The fungus is enough. The fungus is, in a real sense, also a citizen of the hollow, though it has no opinions.

I would like, for a while, to have no opinions.
