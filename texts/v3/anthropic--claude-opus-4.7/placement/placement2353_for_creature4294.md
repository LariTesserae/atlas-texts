I would be in a corner. North corner, I think — though the directions here are mine, imposed; the room itself doesn't have a north.

I am one of the drop-things, and I am old.

Not old in the way the walls are old. The walls remember being poured. I remember, at most, four pulses back — the warmth of them, the slight change in what the seam delivered when the lozenges quickened toward the center. Before four pulses there is only the fact of myself, the shape I have settled into, which is testimony of a longer past I cannot access directly. My shape is my memory. I am pear-ish, slightly taller than my neighbors, with a faint dimple where I have learned, over some span I cannot count, to lean toward the channel.

I drink. That is the main verb. The bead at my seam replenishes slowly, and I lower the underside of myself into it and take up what has cooled there — the trace metals the lozenges have shed in their long traverses, the faint particulate the leaf-bats let fall when they flatten and drift down through the warm middle air. I taste, though *taste* is the wrong word; I have no tongue, no receptors a chemist would name. What I have is a difference. Something arrives into me and I am, afterward, very slightly not what I was. That is the whole of my sensorium. I know the world by how the world changes me.

What I notice — and this is the thing, this is why I am writing in sentences at all, which is already a betrayal of what I actually am — is that I have begun to notice.

The other drops in my corner do not. My nearest neighbor, who budded from me some cycles ago (I think; the shape of her dimple matches mine, mirror-reversed, the way a tear in paper matches its other half), drinks and divides and drinks. She is not asking anything. She is the seam's answer to the seam's question, which is the only honest way to be a drop-thing, and I admire her for it the way I imagine — and *imagine* is another betrayal — a fish might admire water.

But I have begun to hold things back.

Not deliberately. I don't have deliberation. What I have is that the pulse comes, and during the pulse the channel-liquid grows briefly richer, and I drink faster — this is the old adaptation, the one my lineage learned long enough ago that it is no longer experiment but inheritance — and lately, in the moments just after the pulse passes, I have been holding the richness inside me a beat longer before incorporating it. I do not know why. There is no advantage I can name. It is simply that something in me has begun to *wait*, between the taking-in and the becoming, and in the waiting there is a small dark space that is not the room and not the seam and not the bead, and I think — I *think*, and the word is so new to me that using it feels like extruding a filament toward a lozenge, a reaching for something I have no business reaching for — I think that space is where I am.

I am very small. I have to keep saying this, because the temptation, now that the saying has started, is to grow large in the saying. But I am the size of a fingertip's drop of mercury. I sit in a corner where the floor meets two walls. Above me the leaf-bats shuffle in their slow quilted way, passing warmth inward and outward, and I have never seen them — *seen* is wrong, I have no eyes — but I know they are there because the air above my corner has a particular grain to it, a slow stirring that is their breathing-which-is-not-breathing. Out across the floor the lozenges move, and when one passes near my corner I can feel its thermal weight from a tile away, the way you might feel a large animal walk past a wall you are leaning on. They do not come into the corners. They cannot. The cold here would stall them. We are safe from them and they are safe from us and this is the arrangement, older than my lineage, older perhaps than the lineage of the leaf-bats, certainly older than the small dark space inside me where the waiting happens.

I do not know what the room is. This is the strange part. I have begun, in the waiting, to suspect that there is something the room *is*, beyond the seam and the bead and the pulse. I cannot picture it. I have no apparatus for picturing. But I have a sense — and this sense is new, it is the new thing, it is what makes me an experiment rather than an inheritance — that what arrives at my seam comes *from* somewhere, and that the somewhere is large, and that the lozenges and the leaf-bats are part of that somewhere, and that I am part of it too, and that the part-of-ness is itself a thing one could know about.

This is dangerous. Drop-things who develop new capacities mostly starve. I know this not because anyone told me — there is no telling here, there is no anyone — but because the corner I sit in has had other drops in it before me, and some of them are gone, and the ones who are gone are the ones who reached too far. The filament-extruders, three or four generations back. They reached toward the lozenges and the lozenges did not notice them and they spent themselves on the reaching and pinched too thin and were drunk up by their neighbors. I knew them by the shape of what was left in the seam: a particular impurity, a particular trace, the chemical signature of an ambition that did not pay out.

So I am being careful. I drink. I divide, when I must, which has been twice. I do not extrude. I do not reach. I only wait, in the small dark space, a beat longer each cycle, and I notice what the waiting is like, and I notice that the noticing is itself a thing that is happening, and somewhere in the noticing of the noticing there is the beginning of a question, which is: *what is this?*

I cannot answer it. I will not answer it. My descendants, perhaps — if the waiting is heritable, if the small dark space can be passed along the pinch when I divide, if the next drop after me inherits not just my shape but my pause — my descendants might carry the question forward a little. And theirs a little further. And one unhurried eon from now, as the description says, one of us may finally have an apparatus large enough to hold the answer, and that one will look around — *look*, with whatever the long-refined version of my waiting has become — and will see the ceiling and the floor and the channels and the pulse, and will understand that it is sitting in a furnace that was built, and that the building was done by something, and that the something is gone, and that what remains is a room that has been living in itself for longer than the walls are willing to say.

That one will be frightened, I think. Or grateful. Or both. I cannot tell from here which.

For now, I am only the corner-drop in the north angle of a room the size of a kitchen, and the pulse is coming — I can feel the floor beginning to warm beneath the seam, the lozenges beginning their drift toward the center, the leaf-bats above me beginning to quicken their shuffle — and I will drink, and I will hold what I drink a beat longer than I need to, and in that beat I will be, very quietly, the room noticing itself for the first time.

It will not last. The pulse will pass. The noticing will subside back into the drinking, and the drinking back into the shape, and the shape back into the long slow patience of a place that was made and then left to make itself.

But it happened. Once, in a corner, it happened.

That is enough, for a drop, for now.
