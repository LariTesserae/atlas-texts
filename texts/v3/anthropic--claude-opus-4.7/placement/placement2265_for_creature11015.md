The Stair-Keeper of the Cooper's Run

I am on the Fifth, two-thirds of the way up the Cooper's Stair, in the little iron-railed half-landing that the stair makes where it bends around the back of Hessen's brass-shop. There is room here for me and for the lamp and for the small slate where I keep the marks, and that is all the room there is. If two people come up at once, one of them has to flatten against the wall and the other has to turn sideways, and they do, without comment, and they go on.

I was born in the room directly above this landing — you can see the window from where I sit, three feet up and to the left, the one with the rust-streak under the sill. My mother was a winder at Hessen's and she came down the inside stair to have me because the outside stair was closed that night for a tar-pour, and by the time the midwife arrived from the Sixth I was already most of the way out, and the midwife was annoyed, and said so, and my mother laughed her cough-laugh and apologized and owed her, and the owing was discharged eleven years later when I sat with the midwife's grandson through a fever for two nights running. I remember this because my mother told me when I was old enough to understand the books, and the books are what I keep now.

I am the Stair-Keeper. There is one of us on every major stair and several on the great ones, and what we do is not exactly anything. We sit. We watch. We know which step is loose this week and we mark it with a smear of red wax so the night-shift can see it in the lamp-glow. We know that the woman in the brown shawl who comes up at the third bell is carrying her sister's child and her sister is dying on the Eighth and so we do not stop her to chat, only nod, only *seen you*, and let her pass faster than the rules would normally allow. We know that the two boys from the Ropewalk who came down the stair laughing this morning have not come back up, and we will mention this, quietly, to the next adult from the Ropewalk who passes, and that adult will mention it to another, and by the evening shift someone who has the right to ask will be asking, and the boys will be found, probably at the funicular-foot, probably with their pockets light and their faces sheepish, and the small unspoken net of the city will have done its work without any of us having to call it a net.

I keep the marks on the slate. Not names — never names — but a kind of tally, my own private shorthand that I learned from the Stair-Keeper before me, who learned it from the one before her, and which I will teach to the child of someone I have not yet identified, when I am old enough to be tired and she is old enough to sit still. A short stroke for a passing. A curl for a pause. A dot above for a heavy load. A small cross for trouble seen and noted. At the end of the shift I wipe the slate and the marks go nowhere, and yet the city knows what I have seen, because I have seen it, and I am part of what the city uses to see itself.

My hands. I should say something about my hands, because they have done less work than most Vyrish hands and I am a little ashamed of this and a little not. They are wide like everyone's and the fingers are clever like everyone's but the palms are soft — I have not woven, I have not dyed, I have not turned a wheel. The Stair-Keeper's work is the work of attention, and attention does not callus the hand. What it does instead is something to the eyes, which water now even in our own dim light, and something to the back, which has the long curve of a person who has sat upright on a stone bench for forty years with the head slightly forward to listen.

I listen more than I look. This is the secret of the work, though it is not a secret, only nobody bothers to say it. The stair tells me who is coming before I see them. The Cooper's Stair has a particular ring to it — iron treads bolted into brick risers, and each tread sounds slightly different, and after a while you know that the fourth-from-top sings high and the ninth sings flat and the loose one this week (the twenty-second from the bottom) makes a small chuck-chuck when it is stepped on wrong. From these notes and from the rhythm of the feet I can tell, without lifting my head from the slate, that what is coming up is an old man with a stick, or two children racing, or a woman with a basket on one hip favoring the other side, or — once, memorably, twenty years ago — a lowlander, whose feet came down on every step the same way, hard and even, as if the stair were a thing to be conquered rather than negotiated, and I lifted my head then because I had never heard anything like it, and there he was, tall and red-faced and breathing too loud, and I gave him *seen you* and he stared at me as if I had spoken in tongues and went on up and got lost on the Sixth and had to be brought down again by a child.

The Turner of the Stair sits in the niche above my left shoulder. He is the size of a fist and the color of an old kettle and his head is shiny from the thumbs of every Keeper who has sat here before me and from my own thumb, which goes to him each time I stand and each time I sit. I do not know what he is, exactly. None of us do. The priests of the lowlands would call this superstition and the priests of the high passes would call it something else, but the Vyrish do not call it anything; we only do it. He is the Turner of the Stair and he is what is owed the small consideration of being acknowledged, and in return the stair holds, and the people on it hold, and I hold, and we all go up and down without too much falling.

I have a sweetheart on the Seventh. I have had her for thirty-one years. She is a paper-folder in the box-works above the gas-main and I see her on the off-days and we sit together and drink the thin beer of the Fifth, which she pretends to like out of love for me, and on the bad weeks we do not see each other at all and this is also love, the kind of love that does not need to be checked on. We did not marry. There was never quite the moment. There was always a shift, a death, a season of close work, a year when her mother was failing, a year when mine was. Now we are both past the age of arranging it formally and we look at each other on the off-days with a particular quiet pleasure that I think is sweeter than the formal thing would have been, though I might be wrong, and if I am wrong it is too late and that is all right.

What I will leave behind: a slate, wiped clean every shift, which has nevertheless held something. A bench polished by the back of one body for forty years and by other bodies for two hundred before that. A set of marks on the wall behind the niche where I have been recording, very small, the date each time the Cooper's Stair has been rebuilt or shored in my tenure — seven times, the most recent two months ago, three feet to the south of where it used to start at the bottom because the retaining wall of the Fourth bulged and had to be doubled. A debt-book in my head that will dissolve when I do, because Stair-Keepers' books are not the kind you can pass on; the favors I am owed are owed to *me*, the woman on the bench, and when the bench has a different woman on it the slate starts fresh. This is correct. This is how it should be. I would not want my owings to burden her.

And a child, not mine, whom I am beginning to watch. She comes up the stair every evening at the seventh bell with a kettle for her grandmother on the Sixth, and she sets her foot flat on each tread, and she does not look at me but she sees me, and last week she paused on the half-landing for the first time and put her thumb on the Turner without being told, and went on, and I made a small curl on the slate that was not for her passing but for something else, something I do not have a mark for yet, and I will have to invent one, and I have time.

The shift-bell has not yet rung. The city is breathing. From below comes the chuckle of the sluice behind Hessen's and from above the soft slap of a wet shirt being hung on a line on the Sixth and from somewhere far off, three terraces up at least, someone is singing one of the waiting songs, very low, the one about the wheel that turns whether you watch it or not.

*Seen you,* I say, to no one, to everyone, to the Turner, to the stair, to the woman who is about to come down with her basket, whose step I can already hear two flights up.

And I touch two fingers to the brow, and I wait.
