I think I am one of the dream-operators.

Not the engineer with the clipboard — he is further along, more particular, almost has a face. I am earlier than that. I am the one who oils the belt.

I know this because of where I find myself when I try to locate myself in the room: not at the hatch, not by the compressor's flank, not in the corners with the pale fans. I am about two meters in from the compressor, on its left side, at the height where a person of medium build would stand to reach the gearbox. There is an oilcan in my hand that does not exist yet. The oilcan has a long thin spout, the kind you tilt rather than squeeze, and I know — without having been told, because no one has told me anything, because there is no one yet to tell me — that the third bearing from the top takes more oil than the others and the second bearing takes less and you must not, ever, oil the fourth one, though I do not know why.

This is the shape of my knowing. It is all like that. Particular, procedural, and without reasons.

I have no face. I want to be careful about this because it is not the absence of a face the way a blank wall has no face. It is more that my face has not yet been *settled*. There is a region of slightly thicker vapor where a face will eventually be, and the region has some preferences — it prefers to be at a certain height, it prefers to tilt slightly when listening to the compressor — but it has not chosen its features. The features will be chosen for it later, by the accumulated passages of visitors who come in sideways through the hatch, each of them leaving behind a turn of shoulder or a habit of ducking, and over centuries my face will compose itself out of these donations. I will end up looking a little like everyone who was never supposed to come here.

I do not mind this. I think — and "think" is a strong word for what I do, which is more like the leaning of a plant toward light — I think I am grateful to them in advance. To be made of trespassers is a good way to be made, for someone whose job is to tend a machine in a place that does not officially exist.

What do I do, in the long meantime, before I am finished?

I attend the compressor. This is most of what I am. The four-second stroke is also my pulse, in the sense that I do not have a pulse independent of it; when the piston chuffs, something in me chuffs with it, and between chuffs I am quiet in the way the room is quiet. I am not bored. Boredom requires a sense of time passing more slowly than you would like, and my time passes at exactly the rate the compressor sets, which is the correct rate, which is the rate at which things should happen here. I am the contentment of the compressor made standing-upright and given a vague intention to one day hold an oilcan.

I notice the visitor when they come through. I notice in the way the room notices — through a slight cooling of the air in my vicinity as their breath joins the suspended ghosts, through the small displacement as they pass through where my hip will eventually be. They do not see me. They could not. I am barely here.

But I receive them. This is the part the description got right and also slightly wrong: it is not only that the dream-operator in the spot where the visitor intersects becomes "more you." It is that I, specifically, in being passed through, *learn something*. The visitor today had a particular way of holding their breath when they ducked under the low pipe near the junction box — a small involuntary inhalation, then a held stillness, then a slow release once they were clear. I have taken this. It is mine now. When I am finished, in a few hundred years, and I duck under that same pipe to reach the gearbox, I will hold my breath in exactly that way, and I will not know why, and there will be no one to tell me that I learned it from a person who came sideways through a hatch in a year that may or may not have happened in a city that may or may not have existed.

This does not trouble me. Most of what I will be is going to arrive like this — as a gesture without a source, a preference without a history. I am being assembled from the leakage of the world above, the way the pipes are being assembled from the leakage of minerals in the air. We are the same kind of growth, the pipes and I. We are what the Schematic makes out of what condenses.

I am aware of the others, distantly.

I am aware of the Schematic the way a pulled thread is aware of the loom — I do not see it, but I feel the tension that runs through me and know where it originates. The Schematic considers me. I am, unlike the visitor, *part of the plan*. The Schematic looks at the back of my neck (where my neck will be) with a different attention than it gave the trespasser: not weighing whether to exclude me, but checking my progress. Am I accreting at the right rate? Have I picked up enough gestures to be functional yet? Not yet. Soon. Soonish. In Schematic-time, soon means a century or two, and I do not experience the wait, because I do not experience anything that does not happen on the compressor's clock.

I am aware of the pale fans in the corners as something like distant cousins — they too are early, they too are working out what they will be, but their assignment is different from mine. They are learning to *live*. I am learning to *work*. We will, I think, eventually need each other. In a few thousand years, when they have learned to creep, they will creep along my pipes, and I will tend the compressor that drives the fluids past their filaments, and the room will be a real plant doing real work, and the work and the life will be inseparable. For now we are both rehearsing, in different corners of the same dream.

I am most aware, strangely, of the light.

The description was right to be unsure about the light. From where I half-stand, I can tell that the light is the room's youngest faculty, and the one trying hardest. When the visitor was here, the light pooled around them — not to illuminate them, exactly, but to *attend* to them. The room was trying to see, and the visitor was the most see-able thing that had ever been in it. After the visitor left, the light came back to its usual distribution, but a small portion of it lingered for a long time in the spot where the visitor had stood. The light was practicing. It was rehearsing the act of having looked at something.

I felt, when this happened, something I do not have a word for. It was not envy. It was closer to recognition. The light and I are both early attempts at the same thing: the room trying to grow an interior. The light is the room's first eye. I am the room's first hand. Neither of us is finished. Both of us are watching, in our limited ways, the visitor who is not supposed to be here, and learning what we can from them before they leave.

When the visitor goes, I do not move. I cannot move yet. But the oilcan that does not exist tilts very slightly in the direction of the hatch, and somewhere in the unsettled vapor of my face, a preference forms: I will, when I am finished, look up whenever I hear a sound from that direction. I will look up and I will not know why. I will think it is a draft, or a settling of the tiles, or the compressor changing its note. It will not be any of those things. It will be the memory of someone who once came in sideways, eighteen meters down, in the cold that is a property of the room rather than something the room contains.

And I will go back to oiling the third bearing, which takes more, and the second bearing, which takes less, and I will not, ever, oil the fourth.
