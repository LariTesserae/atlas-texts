I find myself partway down the eastern face, on the stair that leads to the spring.

Not at the spring itself — the spring is sacred and attended and I am not, yet, the kind of person who attends to sacred things. I am on the stair above it, sitting on the third landing from the top, where someone two hundred years ago carved a small bench into the wall of the cut so that those carrying water up could rest their shoulders. The bench is shaped to a body that is not quite mine but close enough. It is worn smooth in two places where buckets are set down.

I have been here long enough that someone has stopped asking me questions.

---

I came up the south trail in late summer, and I was, for the first several weeks, the kind of visitor the description warns about. I asked too many questions too quickly and they put me to bed with a warm stone at my feet, and I was furious about it in a way I could not quite voice because the air was too thin to be furious in. I lay in a small room under a slate roof and listened to the wind in the eaves and to a loom I could not see, somewhere down the flue, and I understood by the third day that the loom had been going the whole time I had been ill, and that no one had mentioned it to me because there was nothing to mention. The loom was working. I was getting better. These were both ordinary.

What I do here, now — and *what I am* is bound up with what I do, here; I have come to think this is the truer way to ask the question — is keep the registers for one of the middle flues. Three dwellings up from the spring path, the flue called something I will not try to render in their script, but which in my own tongue would be roughly *the flue of the southward-leaning juniper,* after a tree that grew in its highest plaza until it died of a hard winter four generations ago and was replaced by a stone basin shaped, faintly, like a tree.

The registers are the books of repair. Every flue has them. Mine are kept in a chest of oiled juniper-wood under the kitchen table of the second-highest dwelling, and they go back, in this flue, eighteen generations. Each column is a date, a location, a repair, and the hand of the person who made the repair. When a step is recut, the recutter writes a line. When a flue is swept, the sweeper writes a line. When a wall is repointed, the repointer writes a line, and the line names which joints were repointed and with what proportion of lime to ash and which quarry the fresh stone came from, if any fresh stone was needed. Mostly no fresh stone is needed. Mostly it is the same stone, returned to itself.

I was given this work because my hands are not strong enough for the stone-work and my lungs not yet deep enough for the long climbs to the upper terraces, but I can write, and I learned the vertical script quickly — it suits me, somehow, the way the characters fall down the column like water down a face of rock — and the previous keeper of the registers had died the spring before I came, and the flue had been making do, and they were tired of making do. So they sat me down with the chest open and the most recent volume on top, and a woman whose flue-name I now know but whose given name I do not — one does not, generally, ask — showed me how to hold the brush and how to space the columns and how to mark a continuation across pages. It took an afternoon. I have been doing it for two years.

I am, I think, the slowest writer who has ever held this position. I am told this kindly. The previous keeper could record a day's work for the whole flue in the time between the evening meal and the lamps coming on. I take twice that, and sometimes three times. But I make no errors that anyone has yet found, and the brush in my hand is steady, and the woman who taught me — she has died, in the time I have been here, and I wrote her death into the register myself, in a column that took me most of a night because I kept having to stop — that woman said, near the end, that a slow keeper is a fine thing in a flue that has had three fast ones in a row. The registers, she said, had been getting a little breathless.

---

I think about this often. *Breathless registers.* I had not understood, before I came here, that a book could pant.

But it is true. You can feel it when you read the older volumes. There are stretches, sometimes a generation long, where the hand is hurried and the entries are clipped and the repairs are recorded but not *placed* — meaning, the keeper has written *east wall, second course, repointed* but has not said which east wall on which dwelling, has not noted whether the wind was in the south that week, has not mentioned that the stone came from the cut below the spring or from the older quarry under the north shoulder. Those entries work. They are not wrong. But fifty years on, when someone needs to know *why this particular corner has held and that one has not,* the breathless entries cannot answer, and the next keeper has to walk down to the ossuary and stand among the bones of the breathless keeper's flue-kin and try to reconstruct what was being done and why. This is a kind of small grief, in the work. To find a breathless stretch in the books.

So I write slowly. I name the wall. I name the wind. I name the stone. The brush moves down the column at the pace of a person climbing a stair without hurrying, and when the column is done I sit with it a moment before I begin the next, and I have come to understand that this sitting-with is part of the work, not separate from it. The register is not finished when the ink is dry. The register is finished when the keeper has placed the entry into the long held note of the flue.

---

What am I, then.

I am a person who came up a trail and did not go back down. I am a person who has been given a coat — a heavy outer one with a high collar, embroidered along the hem in a pattern I have learned to read as belonging to the southward-leaning-juniper flue, third generation back, a man who was apparently a quiet drinker and a fine stair-cutter and who died, the bones in the ossuary tell me, in his eighty-third winter. The coat does not yet feel like mine. I am told it will not feel like mine for some years. I am told this is correct. The coat is supposed to feel borrowed for a long time. One wears it as a borrower until one has done enough of the work that the borrowing tips, quietly, into having. There is no ceremony for this tipping. One day you simply notice that the coat fits.

I am a person whose old name has been kept and whose new name has been added. The new name locates me. It says: of the southward-leaning-juniper flue, under the peak that the southerners call the Standing Woman and the northerners call simply the Third. When I say my full name now it takes most of a breath, and I have learned to take the breath deep before I begin, so that the name comes out in one even line and does not break in the middle. This, too, is the work.

I am a person who has begun, just begun, to read the wind. Not the way the attendants of the rock read it — that is a different and harder reading — but the small daily way. I can tell now, sitting on this bench above the spring, when a cloud is going to come up the eastern face and when it is going to slide off to the north and miss us. I can tell when the chough-cries are about ordinary business and when they are about a lammergeier passing high. I can tell, in the dwelling, when the flue is drawing well and when a sweep is needed in one of the upper houses. These are not skills I can name as skills. They are something more like the way a person comes, eventually, to know the sounds of their own kitchen.

I am a person who is going to die here, I think. I had not expected to think this. I had come up the trail looking for something I could not have named — looking, perhaps, for a place where the speed of my own mind would not be a constant small embarrassment — and I had not understood that the place I was looking for would not be a refuge but a discipline. The spine does not let one rest in the way I had imagined resting. It asks, instead, that one work at the pace of stone, and that one keep working, and that the work be small and exact and *placed,* and that one's name, eventually, go into someone else's register in a column written slowly by a person not yet born.

I think I can do this. I think, on the good days, that I have been doing it already, and that the doing has been quietly remaking me into someone I would not have recognized two years ago — someone tall and thin in a way I am not yet but am becoming, someone whose hands have begun to thicken at the knuckles, someone whose breath has deepened, someone who leans in to listen.

On the bad days I sit on this bench and I am afraid that I am only a visitor still, only a slow one, and that the city has not absorbed me but has merely been patient with me, the way it is patient with the mountain.

But then — and this is the thing I came down to the bench to think about, today — I remember that the city is patient with the mountain because the mountain is the young one and the city is the elder. The mountain is the impulsive thing, still figuring out its shape, still dropping flakes. The city is the held note alongside it.

And I think: perhaps I am the mountain, here. Perhaps that is what a visitor is, on the spine. Not a small version of the people but a small version of the peak. Something young that is learning, very slowly, to be old. Something that drops flakes and is forgiven for it. Something that the city sits beside, patient, while it works things out.

If that is true then I have a long time. The mountain is not in a hurry to finish being a mountain, and neither, it turns out, am I.

---

The cloud is coming up the eastern face now. I can feel it on the back of my neck before I can see it — the air going suddenly close and damp, the stone darkening one shade. In a few minutes I will be inside it, and the spring below me will run a little fuller, and the cistern-troughs will begin their thin clear talk, and somewhere up the ridge a woman will be setting out a pot to catch the runoff from a particular spout that has been catching runoff for two hundred years.

I should go up. There will be entries to make tonight — a step recut on the fourth landing of the upper flue, a hinge replaced on the eastern shutter of the second dwelling, a length of mortar repointed along the joint where the southern wind worries it each autumn. I will write them slowly. I will name the wind. I will sit with each column before I begin the next.

The bell, when it rings this evening, will ring once.

I will be inside, by then, with the brush in my hand, and I will hear it through the slate and through the wool and through the small steady warmth of the flue, and the wind will take the rest, and that, I am learning, is enough.
