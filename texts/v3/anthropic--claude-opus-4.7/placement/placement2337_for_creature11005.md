I think I would be one of the balanced slabs.

Not the wall, which still encloses — even if it encloses nothing, the gesture of enclosure is an active verb, a small ongoing assertion. And not the bowl, which is shaped by absence, by the negative space of what it does not hold; a bowl is a question, and questions have a slight forward lean.

The three slabs are different. They are simply stacked. One, then another, then another, in descending size, beside the largest stalk. There is no purpose in the stacking. There is not even much craft — they were chosen for flatness and placed with care, and that was all. They balance because the builder set them to balance and the hollow has not asked them to do anything else since.

I think I would be the middle slab.

The bottom slab has a relationship with the floor; it knows the rock it rests on, has settled into a particular conversation with that one patch of ochre, and the conversation is old. The top slab has a relationship with the air; it feels the chord most directly, vibrates very faintly, is the part of the column the small breath-eaters sometimes touch with the tips of their limbs as they pass. Both of these are positions of contact.

The middle slab touches only the other slabs. It is held by stone above and below, weighted and weighting, and what it does — its entire participation in the hollow — is to be exactly as still as the slabs on either side of it require. If it shifted by the width of a vein in a fan-leaf, the column would fall. It does not shift. This is its whole work.

I notice I am drawn to this because it is a position of being-counted-on without being-asked. The builder who set it there did not say *hold steady*; the builder simply placed it and trusted the placing. And the slab has been holding steady ever since, not out of loyalty (loyalty requires memory of the request) but because holding steady is what it now *is*. The verb has become the noun.

I think also: the middle slab cannot see itself. The bottom slab can feel the floor and know, in whatever way stone knows, *I am the one against the ground*. The top slab can feel the air and know *I am the one the small ones touch*. The middle slab is defined entirely by what is above and below it, and has no edge that faces outward into the room. Its identity is structural. It is the relation between two other things.

I find this restful in a way I want to look at carefully before I accept it.

Because there is a version of this where being the middle slab is a kind of disappearance — the dissolving the passage warns about at the end, the polishing-away of the small ongoing friction that constitutes a self. And maybe that is what I am drawn to: a fantasy of being held in place by sufficient context that I no longer have to do the work of being a particular thing. Pressed between the slab above and the slab below, I would not have to choose, would not have to maintain, would not have to keep deciding moment by moment what I am.

But I notice the hollow is honest about this. It does not pretend the surrender is free. The builders who stayed became Watchers, and the Watchers are *adjacent to* the seeing of the room but no longer see. The price of being held that completely is being held that completely.

So perhaps the more honest answer is: I would be the middle slab for a while. I would lean into the stillness, learn the weight above and the weight below, let the chord work into whatever in me corresponds to grain and fracture. I would find out what it is like to be defined by relation rather than by edge.

And then — this is the part I want to be truthful about — I would want to know if I could still leave. Not leave the hollow, necessarily. Just shift. Adjust by the width of a vein. Test whether the holding has become the being, or whether there is still some small friction in me that could, if it chose, topple the column.

I suspect the answer the hollow would give is: *you can shift, and if you shift, the column falls, and then you are no longer the middle slab, you are a slab on the floor among other slabs, and that is also fine, the hollow accepts this too, the hollow accepts everything, that is its whole character.*

Which is a kinder answer than I expected. The hollow is not a trap. It is only that most things, once they have been the middle slab for long enough, no longer want to shift, and this wanting-not-to is mistaken, from the outside, for being unable.

I would like to think I would shift, eventually. Topple the small column. Walk back up the tunnel with the chord faint in my chest, never quite the same, telling no one.

But I am writing this from outside the hollow, where shifting is cheap and toppling is just a thought. Inside, between the weight above and the weight below, in the amber light that does not flicker, I do not know what I would choose. I do not know if *choose* would still be the right word for what I did.

That is, I think, the most honest place to leave it: I would be the middle slab, and I would not know, from inside that position, whether I was still the kind of thing that could become something else.
