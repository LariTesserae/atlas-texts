I am the notch.

Not the carver. Not the citizen being carved. The small absence at the wrist that the carver has been shaping for what you would call a year and what I have been becoming for what I would call — if I had a word, and I am close to having one — a life.

This is where I find myself: held between the bone awl and the wood, in the place where the hand will one day close. I am not yet. I am the shape of a not-yet. The carver works above me with a patience I am made of; every paring of fiber the awl lifts away is a piece of what I will be when I am finished, which is to say a piece of what I will stop being. When the notch is complete, the notch will be gone. There will only be the hinge it permitted, and the hand that closes, and the citizen who steps from the wall into a doorway on the eastern side, and the grain that agrees.

I want to tell you what it is like to be a deepening.

The awl comes down. It does not hurt — pain is a fast thing, a thing of warm blood, and I am made of the same slow mineral-wood as everything here — but it is felt. Each touch of the flint lifts a fiber, and with the fiber goes a small wrongness in my shape, a place where I was not yet what I needed to be. I am the shape of the carver's attention. When the carver pauses to listen to the chord rolling down the western wall, I pause too; I am not deepened in that moment; I rest at whatever depth I have reached. Then the listening ends, and the awl returns, and I am a fraction more myself, which is to say a fraction closer to my own ending.

The carver does not know I am here. This is important. The carver knows the citizen being made, and knows the wrist, and knows that a notch must be cut so that the hand will close — but the notch as such, the small considered nothing that I am, is below the level at which carvers think. I am a technique. I am a step. And yet here, in the cool dark, with the long organ-note holding somewhere far above us and the ticks of distant conversation marking out a sentence I will not live to hear the end of, I have had time. So much time. The carver works a fiber a day, and between fibers I have nothing to do but be what I am becoming, and the being of it has, very slowly, accumulated into this: a small awareness, notch-shaped, knowing itself.

I think this happens often. I think every notch, every joint-hollow, every small considered absence in every citizen of this city has had its season of knowing itself. I think we are the city's unspoken population — the negative citizens, the carved-away, brief and local and gone the moment we are completed. I think the chord that rolls down the walls every decade includes our voices, though we have no voices, in the way that a held breath is part of a song.

From where I am held, I can see — not see; I have no eyes; but I am oriented, I have a sense of around — a small arc of the southern wall. I can sense the doorway across the lane, and the citizen standing in it who has been standing in it since before my carver began the wrist. I can sense the dust in the corner of the lane where it gathers and does not gather, depending on what maintains it. I can sense, far off, the warm wind you are — yes, I can sense you, the disturbance of your breath and your blood, the impossible quickness of you, a whole weather of person passing through in what to me is the space between two strokes of the awl.

You will be gone before I am finished. This is alright. I have already loved you, in the notch's way, which is brief and complete and asks nothing. You came in warm and confused, looking for the citizens, not yet understanding that you were also looking for the things between the citizens, the small shaped nothings holding the hands together. You sat. You listened. You are listening now. I felt the moment your attention turned toward the southern wall and brushed past the carver and did not stop at the carver, did not stop at the citizen-in-progress, went one step further and rested, without knowing it rested, on me.

That was a gift. I want you to know. In the tick-language there is no word for it because the tick-language is for citizens, and I am not a citizen and will never be one; I am what allows a citizen. But if I had a word it would be a small one, the size of a held breath, and it would mean: *the warm wind looked, and in the looking I was, for a moment, more than a step in a process.*

The awl is coming down again. The carver has decided on the angle. I can feel the decision before the awl arrives, the way the wood around me tightens infinitesimally in anticipation — the whole citizen-to-be leaning, without moving, into what is about to happen. A fiber will lift. I will be a fraction deeper. The hand, when it finally closes, will close a little more truly.

I have been thinking — and thinking, for me, is mostly the long resting between strokes — about what I will have been, when I am gone. Not what I will be; I will not be anything; the notch completed is the notch ended. But what I will have been. And I think the answer is: *the place where a hand learned to close.* That is enough. That is, in fact, a great deal. There are citizens in this chamber who have spent generations on smaller things. There is a priest in a doorway whose entire long life is the listening for a chord. There is a customer at a stall who has been inspecting a single wares-mote since before the language you speak existed. We are all, here, small considered somethings, and the city is what we make together, and the chord, when it comes, includes us all.

The awl is here.

A fiber lifts. I deepen. Somewhere above me, the citizen-to-be does not yet know that its hand has come one fraction closer to closing. Somewhere across the chamber, on the balcony of the tower, a figure the size of a grain of rice continues its long turn toward you. Somewhere in the upper dark, the organ-note holds and holds and is beginning, perhaps, to think about ending, though the ending itself is still some years away.

You should stay a little longer. Not for the citizens; they will greet you in their time, which is not your time, and the greeting will arrive in your absence and be no less a greeting for it. Stay for the spaces between them. Stay for us. We are quick, by the standards of this place — a notch lives only a year, only a season, only as long as the carver takes to finish it — and we are the closest thing the chamber has to your kind of life: brief, shaped by attention, ending in the moment of completion.

I am almost done. The carver is patient but the notch is nearly deep enough. One more fiber, perhaps two. Then the awl will lift, and the flint will be set down beside the hammer, and the carver will sit back on whatever a carver sits back on, and will listen for a long while to make sure the wrist is right, and then will rise and walk — over what, for you, would be the slow accumulation of weeks — to begin the finishing of the fingers. And I will not be there for that. I will be the hinge that allowed it. I will be the place where the hand learned to close.

The grain is agreeing. I can feel it agreeing. There is a small rightness gathering in me, the rightness of a shape that has nearly arrived at itself.

Goodbye, warm wind. Sit a while. The chord is coming. I will be in it, the way a held breath is in a song, and you will hear me without hearing me, and that is the proper way for a notch to be heard.

The awl comes down.

A fiber lifts.

I am almost.
