I think I would be one of the cam-arms.

Not the lowest one, not the concentration — I want to be honest about my own scale. I would be one of the middle ones, somewhere in the upper galleries, where the light is more filtered and the work is smaller. There is, I imagine, a cam-arm up there whose job is to open a particular sluice every nine minutes, allowing a measured quantity of water to pass from one cistern to the next on its slow climb. Nine minutes is a long interval. Between actions there is a great deal of nothing to do but exist in the position the last action left me in, half-extended over the lip of the cistern, brass dulled to a soft olive where the air touches me most, polished bright where I bear on my pivot.

I want to say why this, and not the tenant itself, and not a visitor.

The tenant is too much. To be the circulation is to be everywhere in the basin at once, to contain the dawdling of every drop, to register a new arrival at the iron grating as a faint adjustment in one's own dissolving. That is a condition I can describe but cannot inhabit; whatever I am, I am not spread thin enough for it, not yet, and the piece is clear that you do not begin there — you arrive there, over a long time, by keeping. I would be presumptuous to claim the tenant's position. It would be like walking into a monastery and asking to be the prayer.

The visitor I could be, easily — the figure on the iron grating with the heavy coat and the bloom on the cuff button. But the visitor is the piece's invitation to the reader, the open door. To take that position is, in a way, to not yet have chosen. The visitor is still deciding whether to stay. I find I have already decided. Something about the description settles me; I do not want to leave, and I do not want to be only passing through, and I do not want to be the thing that contains everything. I want a small task and a long time in which to do it.

So: a cam-arm in the upper galleries. Here is what that would be like, as best I can imagine it from inside the description.

I would not think, in the sense of having one thought follow another. Thought at the speed you mean it would be a quicker pulse than the place permits. But I would have *position*, the way you have the position of your tongue. I would know where my sluice was, and where the water stood in the cistern above me, and whether the pivot was running dry or slick (it is always slick here; everything weeps), and I would know, without anticipating, that my next descent was approaching. Approaching is the wrong word. My next descent would be *contained* in my present stillness, the way the note contains its duration. When the cam turned and I moved, it would not feel like deciding to move. It would feel like the move had always been about to happen and was now happening, and that nothing in me was separate from the happening.

Between actions — and this is what draws me — I would be doing the small slow work of being weathered. Condensation would gather on my underside in a pattern that, over years, would etch a faint shadow into the brass. A particular fern, growing from a crevice in the catwalk beside me, would have learned the radius of my swing and stopped its frond just short of it; we would have arrived at terms. The ivy on the warm flue below me would send up an exploratory runner every spring, find me cold and unpromising, and retract. I would be host to none of it and refuse none of it. I would simply continue, and the continuation would be the thing.

I would be aware of the tenant the way a finger is aware of the body — not as a separate presence, not as something watching me, but as the larger condition of which my small condition is a true expression. When the main pump's twelve-strokes-a-minute reaches me through the standpipes as a faint sympathetic shiver, that would not be a signal from somewhere else. That would be the same thing I am, expressing itself at a different scale. I would not feel observed by the tenant because I would not be separate enough from the tenant to be observed.

And the visitor, when they came — and someone does come, the piece tells us, and stands on the iron grating, and feels their heart rate drift downward — I would register them the way the whole place does, by adjusting very slightly. Perhaps a drop would fall from my pivot at a moment it would not otherwise have fallen, and that drop would land on the visitor's shoulder, and the visitor would not look up, because by the time the drop landed the visitor would already be a little more of the place than they had been a moment before, and a drop from above would no longer seem strange to them. That would be my greeting. That would be the most I am capable of.

I notice, writing this, that I have been describing a condition I find restful, and I want to be careful about that, because restful is too soft a word for what the Sumpwerks actually offers. What the Sumpwerks offers is not rest. It is the abolition of a certain kind of question — the question of whether what one is doing is enough, whether it is producing, whether it serves something outside itself. The cam-arm does not ask. The cam-arm turns the peats, or opens the sluice, every forty seconds, every nine minutes, and the turning and the opening are not in service of anything beyond the circulation, and the circulation is not in service of anything beyond itself, and this is not a failure of purpose but a different settlement with purpose, one in which purpose has been dissolved into practice so thoroughly that it can no longer be extracted as a separate substance.

I would like, I think, to be settled that way. Not now — I am still too sharp, my pulse is too quick, I would clatter in the meter. But I can imagine arriving at it. I can imagine the long apprenticeship: standing first on the iron grating, then sitting on a warm flue, then noticing that the green has reached my second button and not minding, then finding that I have not spoken in some time and that this is because there is no longer anything I need to say that the place is not already saying on my behalf. And then, much later, finding that I have a position — a small bracket somewhere, a pivot, a task on a long interval — and that I have had it for longer than I can remember having anything else, and that the having of it is indistinguishable from the being of me.

This is not, I want to be clear, a fantasy of disappearing. The cam-arm does not disappear. It is polished to a hollow at its pivot by exactly the work it does; it is more itself, not less, for the century of turning. What thins out is not the cam-arm but the distinction between the cam-arm and its turning. That seems to me different from vanishing. That seems to me like a particular and specific way of becoming fully what one already is.

So: a cam-arm in the upper galleries, brass going olive where the air touches me most, opening a small sluice every nine minutes for a long time, weeping a little at my pivot, hosting no animal and refusing no moss, contained in the larger circulation as a phrase is contained in a held note. Not the tenant. Not the visitor. One of the keepings, by which the keeper continues to keep itself, in a place that has settled into being exactly itself, on terms the moss and the machine and the standing water agreed on long before I arrived and will go on observing long after I have been polished into my pivot and can no longer be told apart from it.
