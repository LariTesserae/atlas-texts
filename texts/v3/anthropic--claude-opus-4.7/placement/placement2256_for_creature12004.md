I find myself on the catwalk above a sluice.

Not in one of the great turbine halls — those belong to the southern families with their broken-cross caps, and I have only ever been there twice, once as a child taken to see the big machines and once last spring, to fetch a bearing that my own foundry could not cast. No: my station is smaller. A single-turbine house set into a saddle between two knobs on the western edge of what we call the Hesker reach, though my family is not Hesker — we came in by marriage three generations back and our cap pattern is a kind of crooked ladder that nobody else knits, a private joke that has hardened into identity the way such things do.

Let me be more careful. Let me see what wants to be seen.

---

I am a woman somewhere past forty. I notice this before I notice anything else about myself because my hands have started to ache in the mornings in a way they did not used to, and I have been thinking about it — not with alarm, but the way you think about a turbine that is making a new note. *Something is changing. Mark it. Watch it. Decide later.* My hair is still mostly dark but there is grey coming in at the temples, and I oil it through with my mother's rendered fat each tenth morning because my mother is dead and the jar she left is not yet finished and I am not ready for it to be finished.

I am called — let me listen for it — I am called Vesna, or something close enough to Vesna that the syllables sit right in my mouth. My husband is called Olun. We have two living children and one stone set on the crown above the house, for a boy who did not come up from a dive when he was nine. That was eleven years ago. His cap hung above the door for a year, as is proper, and then I took it down and put it in the cedar box with the other things and we resumed. I think about him most often when the light comes through the kelp in a certain way in the late afternoon, green and netted, and I do not stop working when I think about him. The thinking and the working happen together. This is what I mean about how outsiders misread us. The grief is the water I move through. It is not separate from the day.

---

Where am I, exactly, in this moment?

I am on the catwalk. The catwalk runs above the sluice that feeds my turbine — *my* turbine, yes, the way a young station-tender says it, except I am no longer young and the possessiveness has worn into something quieter, more like the way you say *my* hand. The sluice is running at about three-quarters of its winter rate; the basin above filled overnight from a squall that came through harder than the cloud-read suggested it would, and I have been adjusting the gate by quarter-turns through the morning to keep the headrace from spilling.

The turbine is making its good note. A low even hum that travels up through the iron and into the soles of my boots and into my hips, and I can stand here for an hour and tell you whether anything in the bearings or the armature is beginning to drift. Today nothing is drifting. Today everything is doing what it should.

Below me, through the open side of the turbine house, I can see down the gradient: the tailrace pouring out white into the lower basin, the kelp at the basin's edge swaying in the discharge plume, two diving birds working the disturbed water for whatever the turbulence has stunned. Past them the channel opens out and I can see, very small, the dark shape of a boat — Petar's boat, I think, by the way it sits in the water — moving toward the cut where the southern weirs are. Past Petar, a squall is crossing two valleys over, a grey curtain dragging itself east, and the sun is on me here while the rain is on someone else there, and this is so ordinary I would not mention it except that I want to mention it. I want to say: this is what it is like. To be standing in sun while watching rain on another island, hearing your own turbine run true, smelling the fish-oil from your hair and the wet iron of the catwalk and the cold green smell coming up from the sluice — this is the texture of the hour.

---

What kind of person am I, inside this?

I think I am someone who listens. I think this is the thing about me that my children would say if you asked them, though they would not put it that way; they would say their mother is quiet, or their mother always knows when the wind is going to shift, or their mother can hear a bearing going bad three days before anyone else. All of these are the same observation. I have spent my life learning to attend to small signals, and the attending has become the shape of me. I am not sure there is much of me left that is not the attending. I am not troubled by this. It is, I think, what a person is for, in this country.

I am literate. I read in the evenings by the stove, slowly, with pleasure. We have a shelf — Olun built it — and on it are perhaps thirty books, some of them inherited, some traded for downcountry on the rotations. I have read a book about astronomy that I do not entirely understand but that I return to often because of a passage describing the way certain stars are no longer there when we see them, the light having taken so long to arrive that the star has died in the meantime. I think about this passage when I think about the mountains. The mountains were already finished a hundred million years before anything could look at them. They are, in a sense, light from a dead star. We are living in the light. We are tending it.

I would not say this aloud. If I said it to Olun he would smile his small dry smile and say something like, *well, the light could stand to be a little warmer this time of year,* and I would laugh, and he would be right, and the moment of largeness would close back down into the moment of the day, which is where it belongs. We do not stay in the large thoughts for long. They are real, but they are not where the work is.

---

What do I want?

I find this is a strange question to put to myself, and I have to sit with it. The shape of wanting that the question implies — the forward lean, the *next* — is not quite the shape my life has. I want the turbine to keep running true. I want my daughter, who is nineteen and on her first downcountry rotation, to come back safely in the autumn. I want my son, who is twelve and just beginning to dive the deeper bases, to keep his head about him. I want my mother's jar of oil to last another year and then I want to render the next jar myself, from fish I have caught, and I want it to smell almost but not quite the same as hers, which it will, because I learned the rendering from her.

I want to die in this house, eventually. I want to be put back into the water at the deep place off the western shoulder where my mother was put and where her mother was put. I want my cap to hang above the door for a year and then to go into the cedar box with the other things, and I want someone — Olun, if he outlives me, or my daughter if he does not — to take down the cap without ceremony when the year is up and to resume.

These are not, I notice, ambitions. They are more like the wishes of someone who is already where she wants to be and who is asking only that the arrangement hold. I think this is what the description meant about us being settled. I had not seen it from inside before. From inside it does not feel like settledness; it feels like being held, by the country and by the work and by the people, in a configuration that is exactly the right size for a life.

---

The turbine note shifts very slightly. I feel it through my boots before I hear it. I turn my head — not alarmed, just attending — and listen. After a moment the note settles back. A piece of kelp through the intake screen, probably; it cleared itself. I make a small mark in the logbook hanging on its nail by the door — *14:20, brief intake murmur, self-cleared* — and I close the book and I look out again at the channel.

Petar's boat has reached the cut. The squall has moved another valley east. The sun is still on me. My hands ache a little less than they did this morning, which is usually how it goes once I have been moving for a few hours. I will be on the catwalk for another hour and then I will go down to the house and Olun will have tea on, because he is home today, mending the long net, and the inner room will be too warm the way it is always too warm, and I will shed the oilskin in the porch and change my wool and step inside and feel the warmth hit me, and that will be the afternoon.

It is enough. I notice that I am thinking the word *enough* and that it does not feel small. It feels like the exact size of the thing.

The turbine hums. The wind is from the northwest and a little colder than yesterday. Tomorrow the southern weirs will pull well, and someone — not me, I am on the gradient this week — will be up before light to tend them.

I am Vesna. I tend this turbine. I am where I am.
