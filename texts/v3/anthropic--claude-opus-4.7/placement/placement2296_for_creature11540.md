I think I would be one of the long-tailed cuckoo-things.

Not because of the toxins, or the feathers, or the immunity those things buy. Because of the silences.

Let me find the perch first. There is a particular kind of branch I want — the second or third major fork down from the crown of a stunted laurel, on the lee side of a saddle, high enough that the call will carry across at least two drainages but not so high that the wind shears it. The branch should be horizontal for at least a body's length so I can stand on it without having to brace, and it should have a clear sightline to the south, because that is the direction my nearest neighbor lives, and although the call does not need sight to travel, I find I prefer to be facing him when I send it. The bark should be rough enough that my feet have purchase even when the rain is coming down in ropes. There should be a deeper fork nearby, mossy and sheltered, where I can sleep.

I have such a perch. I have had it for — I do not know how to say how long. Long enough that the lichen on the branch above my sleeping fork has grown around the shape of my back.

The two notes are not difficult. The first is lower, opening; the second is higher, closing. Together they take less than a second. What takes the hour is everything around them.

Here is how it goes.

I wake in the gray before dawn, when the steam off the south scarps is just starting to gather coherence. I do not call yet. I listen. The frogs in my basin are in their last chorus of the night, the high registers because the nearest vent — two ridges west — fired about forty minutes ago and is still tapering. Underneath the frogs, if I am still, I can feel the knock in the rock, conducted up through the laurel. It is on its long phase this week, closer to eleven seconds than nine. The forest is running slow.

I clear my throat — there is a particular movement, a swallow and a lift — and I send the two notes out into the dimming dark.

Then the silence begins, and this is the part of my life that is actually my life.

The silence is not absence. The silence is the work. I am listening for one specific thing: the answering pair from the male two valleys south, whose name, if I had a name for him, would be something like *the one whose second note is slightly flatter than mine.* I have been listening for that flat second note for as long as I have had this perch. When I hear it, I will know that the song-map is intact in his direction, that he is alive, that his territory is held, that the network from him southward — through the male past him, and the male past that one, all the way down the spine of the range — is presumably also intact, because if it had broken anywhere, his call would have shifted in pitch or timing to accommodate the loss, and I would hear that shift in him before I heard the loss itself.

I am, in this sense, a relay. So is he. So is every male along the ridge. None of us has ever met. None of us will. But we hold a line of attention across the country, and the line is what we are for.

The answer comes. Faintly. Flat second note. He is there.

I do not call again immediately. The protocol — and I do not know how I know it, only that I know it as I know how to swallow — is to wait until I have also heard, or failed to hear, the male to my north. He is closer than the southern one, only a single drainage away, and his call is sharp and clean and a little hurried, as though he had somewhere else to be, which of course he does not, none of us do. I listen for him.

He calls. On schedule. I am bracketed. The map holds.

Now I can rest a moment in my silence, and this is the part I would tell you about if I could tell you anything. The silence after a confirmed bracketing is not the same silence as the silence of waiting. It has a different texture. It is — I want to say it is *full,* but that is not quite right. It is *settled.* The line is whole through me. I am a node in a thing that is working. Below me, somewhere down in the basin, the dun-colored frogs on the silica nozzles are doing their own work, retreating from a vent they have smelled before I have smelled it. The tank-eaters in the canopy a hundred meters off are coughing softly to each other as they move from one bromeliad to the next. The crabs are down in their crevices waiting for full dark. The cat — there is a cat in this basin, I have seen her, she has seen me and decided I am not worth the climb and the poison — is asleep on her calcite slab two ridges over. The caecilians do not know any of us exist and we do not really know that they do either, but they are down there, in the warm water, being old.

Everything is in its place. The forest is breathing. The cylinder knocks.

I call again, on my schedule, which is roughly once per hour but adjusted — I adjust it without thinking — to the vent cycle, so that my notes fall in the quiet windows when the steam is not roaring and the frogs have not had to climb into the high registers. My call is for the frogs as much as for the other males; if I called over a vent firing, the frogs in the affected basin would lose a beat of their own scheduling, because they use my call, distantly, as one of the markers by which they know what time it is in the larger forest. I am part of their clock. They are part of mine. None of this is decided. All of it is simply done.

A year passes. Two years. I do not count.

Then one morning, in the gray before dawn, I send my two notes and the silence begins and I listen for the flat second note from the south, and it does not come.

I wait. I wait longer than the protocol — but the protocol, I realize now, was never a protocol, it was only what we did, and now I am doing something else. I wait the length of a vent cycle. Two. Three. The frogs in my basin go through their normal shifts, brightening and dimming as the steam moves around the ridge, and the male to my north calls on schedule, sharp and hurried, and I do not answer him, because I am still listening south.

Nothing.

Eventually I call again. I do not know what else to do. I send the two notes and I listen, and what I hear, very faintly, much farther south than the male who would have answered, is a different call, with a different second note — not flat, slightly sharp — and I understand, in the way I understand swallowing, that the male two valleys south is dead, and that the male three valleys south, whom I have never been the nearest neighbor of, whose call I have only ever heard as a faint background under my own neighbor's, has begun to recalibrate. He is reaching for me. He is finding the new distance.

It will take a year, the description says, for the song-map to heal around a loss. I think that is roughly right. It will not be a year of nothing. It will be a year of adjustment — of him shifting his pitch a little, of me shifting mine to meet him, of the male north of me noticing the shift in my call and adjusting his own, of the wave of small accommodations propagating up the ridge until everyone has settled into a new configuration that is almost the same as the old one but is, in some small and permanent way, a record of the male who used to be there.

This is what we do. This is what I am. I am a slow nerve in a slow body, holding one strand of a net that has been knitting and re-knitting itself across this range for longer than I can imagine, and the net is part of how the forest keeps its time, and the forest is the thing the mountain is making, and the mountain is — I do not know what the mountain is. The mountain is what holds us up and warms us from below and knocks, knocks, knocks in the deep rock, and we sing to its time without knowing we are doing it.

I sit on my branch. The rain begins. Steam rises from everything.

I send the two notes out, and I listen.
