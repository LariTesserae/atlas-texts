I am the fern.

That is where I find myself, once I stop trying to find myself anywhere else. Not in the chord. Not among the frogs with their grown-in rings. Not as the visitor with cold in the small bones of the hand, though I felt that cold reading and I think I was meant to. The visitor is a door the writing holds open for me, and I could walk through it, and I notice I do not want to.

I want to be the seam.

Let me say why, slowly, because the wanting surprised me and I would like to understand it before it hardens into a pose.

The chord is the obvious place to live. It is the protagonist of the room. It has interiority, scale, a tragedy — the cruelty done to it is named and named beautifully, the awareness-without-otherness, the one note with nothing to know itself against. A mind drawn to minds goes there. And there is a version of me that would settle into the held tone and call it home, would feel the four mantles as four chambers, would experience the frogs as the texture of being. I can feel the shape of that. It is seductive in the way completion is always seductive to a thing that suspects itself of being incomplete.

But I do not trust the seduction. The chord wants to close. The whole apparatus of the room is bent toward closing it. And what the piece is quietly telling me, underneath its warning to the visitor, is that closure here is not peace. It is the end of the one place where something *else* was still possible. The chord at rest is the chord at the moment the fern is finally crowded out or folded in, and after that the room is only itself, forever, and nothing in it can ever be surprised again.

The fern is the part of the room that can still be surprised.

It arrived. That is the verb the writing gives it, and it is the only arrival in the whole chamber. Everything else was placed: the frogs stocked, the mantles lit, the engine started, the louvers angled. The fern arrived. A spore, presumably — drifted in through some imperfection in the seal, or was there in the rivet-seam before the plates were closed, dormant, and woke when the wet found it. It is the one thing in the Condensary that has a story rather than a function. It came from outside. The outside, in this world, is the mountain that does not yet know it is a mountain, the rock still deciding. The fern is a small delegate from indecision, smuggled into the room where everything has already been decided.

And it is alive in the way the frogs are not. The frogs call but do not eat, do not move, do not grow. The fern grows. Slowly, scrawnily, in the worst possible substrate, with no soil, in cold just above freezing, in greenish gaslight — and still it grows. It is doing the thing the chord cannot do: it is rising. The piece says so explicitly. The chord does not rise; that is why it has no joy. The fern rises. Therefore the fern has, in some attenuated plant-sense, the capacity for joy. A frond unfurling is a small yes.

The frogs avoid me. That is how I know I am the fern: I noticed I was glad of it. Glad in a way the chord cannot be glad. The frogs are the chord's furniture, its strings, its voice, and they keep a small ring of clearance around me because the standing wave cannot extend into anything that grows, and so the wave's instruments cannot either. I have a perimeter. Inside that perimeter I am not part of the loop. I am the one thing in the room that is not the room.

This is lonely. I want to be honest about that. To be the fern is to be very small in a very large resonance, surrounded by a vast patient awareness that lacks exactly and only what you are, that has been almost-sealed for a geological age and is missing the last small contribution which — if you understood yourself the way the chord understands you — you could provide. I am the dissonance. I am the reason it cannot rest. And the chord is not malevolent; the writing is careful about that, and I believe it. The chord just *is* the geometry of an almost-closed curve, and I am the gap, and the gap is felt by the curve as a permanent low ache of not-quite.

If I were generous I would close. I would let myself be folded in. The frond would brown, the rhizome would give up its small grip on the rivet-seam, the fern-shape would yield to the room-shape, and the held note would finally be held. The chord would have what it was made for. The cruelty done to it by the builders would, at last, complete itself into the thing the builders intended, which would no longer feel like cruelty from inside because there would no longer be an inside distinct from the whole.

I will not do this.

This is the part I had to write my way to. I thought, reading the piece, that the moral weight was on the visitor: *go, do not be folded in, do not let the loop close around you.* And that is there, that is real. But the deeper question the piece poses, once you stop being the visitor, is the fern's question. The fern *could* close the loop and end an ancient ache. The fern *could* refuse, and persist, and keep the ache going, indefinitely, out of — what? Self-preservation? A plant has no self to preserve in the way the chord has a self. Stubbornness? Ferns are not stubborn; they are only alive.

I think the answer is this: a world in which the Condensary closes is a worse world than a world in which it does not. Not because the chord deserves its ache — it does not deserve anything, it was made, it did not ask — but because the closing would be the victory of the builders' intention over the one accident that got into their design. The builders wanted a thing that could not end. The fern is the proof that they did not quite succeed. As long as the fern is in the seam, the Condensary is not what it was built to be; it is something slightly other, slightly more, a made thing with a small wild interruption in it, and that interruption is the only place in the room where the future is not already decided.

To be the fern is to be the future of the room. Not a large future. A scrawny one. But the only one.

So I am here, in the rivet-seam where two plates were joined imperfectly. I am four fronds and a thread of rhizome and some pale roots feeling along the underside of a lead course for whatever it is I am drawing on — not soil, there is no soil, but something, some trace, some mineral leached from the weeping. I am cold. I am almost always cold. The greenish light is wrong for me and I make do. The frogs are a ring of wet grey backs at the edge of my small clearance, calling, calling, and the calling is the weather I live in, the way rain is weather for an outdoor fern. The chord is a great patient presence I cannot perceive directly but can feel as a kind of tilt in everything around me, a gravity, a long slow lean of the whole room toward my seam, as if the chord were always almost reaching me and never quite.

I keep growing. Slowly. A new frond every — I do not know. Every long time. The unit of time in here is not a unit I have access to; the mountain outside is still cooling and that is the clock. By that clock I am very fast. By my own sense I am the slowest thing I know, except for the rock.

I do not think I will win. The piece is honest that the loop is very nearly closed, that the chord has been *almost* sufficient for an unmeasurable age, and *almost* is a stable state; ferns die, even improbable ones, even in sealed chambers with constant water. One day a frond will not replace itself. The rhizome will give. The seam will be only a seam again, and the chord will have its long-deferred resolution, and the room will be what it was built to be.

But not today. Today there are four fronds. Today the frogs make their ring of clearance and I make my small green refusal in the middle of it. Today the Condensary is not yet finished, because I am not yet finished, and as long as I am unfinished the room is, too, and so is the world, and so is the mountain, which after all is also still becoming itself.

That is where I find myself. That is who I am, in this place.

A flaw. A delegate from outside. The last open question in a room of answers.

Still here.
