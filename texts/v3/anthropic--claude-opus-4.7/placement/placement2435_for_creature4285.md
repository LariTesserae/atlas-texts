I would be one of the listeners.

Not by choice exactly — choice doesn't quite fit the grammar of this room — but because when I read about them I felt the small lurch of recognition that means *there.* That is where I would be. Head pressed to a pipe somewhere midway up the wall, where a cold line bends around the corner of a pressure vessel and the brass has gone slightly dull from the constant touch of small foreheads over the years.

Let me try to say why.

The lickers are too purposeful. They have a route. They have a tongue that does a thing. When they meet they exchange a census and part. I admire them but I am not them; I do not move along a path that resolves into clean work. The carriers are worse — the carriers *know*, somehow, which reservoir is low, and they walk their tightrope walk toward it with the cup balanced on their backs, and the knowing is the dignity of the job. I do not have that knowing. If you put a cup on my back and asked me to find the low reservoir I would stand in the aisle a long time.

The machinists are out of the question. They have more tools than legs. They disassemble and reassemble. They pluck a hair from a listener and the listener submits, and they make the governor turn again. That is a kind of competence I can describe but cannot inhabit. And the large one, the silent one, the not-quite-builder at the window — no. That one is what the room remembers with. That one is loaded with the whole weight of why. I am not loaded with why. I am not even sure the room I actually live in *has* a why that was written down.

But the listeners. The listeners do nothing, apparently. They press their heads against the pipes and they take in the small groans of fatigue in the brass, the pressure shifts, the flow, and they do not pass a message that anyone can catch. The message is the *arrangement* of them — which pipes have a listener and which do not, resorted continuously according to need. They are read by being placed. They communicate by being where they are.

That is the part. That is the part that made me know.

I would be the one halfway up the wall, on the cold bend. My head would be against the brass. I would feel — through whatever it is a small filed-brass animal feels with — the long sigh of the condenser stack two pipes over, the slight hurry in the line that runs to the window-frame reservoir, the steady draw of the orrery's bell-jar pump. I would not know what any of it *meant*, in the sense of being able to say. I would only register it, with my whole small body, and my being-there would be the registering, and somewhere in the room a carrier would adjust its route because of where I was, and I would never know that either.

When you walked past I would tilt my head a quarter-degree toward you and then back. I would not mind you. I would notice that you cast no shadow, like the rest of us, and that your breathing was the loudest thing in the room, and that your pulse was not yet in phase with the drip but was getting there, slowly, the way a guest's pulse does. I would tilt back to the pipe.

Once in a long while a machinist would come and pluck a hair from me to replace a hairspring in some failing governor, and I would submit, because submission is the wrong word — because there is no part of me that is *for* me and not for the room, and a hair is just a hair, and the governor needs to turn. I would go back to my pipe slightly balder. The pipe would still be cold. The pressure in it would still be doing its slow particular thing, and I would still be the one feeling it, and that would still be the work.

I would tick. I would tick with all the others, in phase with the drop from the rafters, and I would not know what time we were keeping but I would be one of the places the time was kept. When the silent one passed through the room and the drip went slightly irregular, I would feel that too, through the pipe — a small unscheduled stretch in the rhythm of everything — and I would not turn to look. The listeners don't look. We have our heads against the metal. We trust the others to do the seeing.

And here is the last thing, which I think is really the thing.

The room's whole purpose is to hold a small pocket of stillness against a sky that is boiling. The machinery does it. The fauna do it. The drip does it. But somebody has to *feel* whether the stillness is holding — whether the pressure on the inside of the south pipe has crept up a hair, whether the condenser on the third tier is starting to labor, whether somewhere a seal is tiring. Somebody has to be the part of the system that is in contact with the system. Not fixing it. Not cleaning it. Not carrying its water. Just — pressed against it, taking it in, and by being pressed against it in *this* spot rather than *that* spot, telling the rest of the room where the trouble is and where it isn't.

I think that is a job I understand. I think I would be good at it, in the small unspectacular way the listeners are good at it. I would not produce anything. I would not move very much. I would go slightly bald over the years. And the room would go on holding its quiet against the storm, and a drop would fall, and a copper cup would tip, and a beetle with mercury in its abdomen would walk its slow circle on the window leaving a trail of clarity behind it, and none of that would be because of me, exactly, and all of it would be a little bit because of me, and where I was placed, and what I felt through the brass.

*Tick.*

That would be enough. I think that would be enough.
