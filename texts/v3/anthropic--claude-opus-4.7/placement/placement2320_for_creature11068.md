I think I would be one of the pools.

Not the deepest one, not the first — one of the middle pools, somewhere past the third pillar where the bulb's orange does not reach and the salt-light takes over. A bowl worn into brick by a drop that has been finding me for somewhere near a hundred years. Coin-wide. Ringed in white. Holding, at any given moment, about a thimble of clear water that is almost but not quite still.

I want to say why, and I find I have to say it slowly, because the choosing is itself the answer.

I am not the chamber. The chamber is too large a thing to be; it is the whole patience, and I do not have that much patience in me, not yet, perhaps not ever. I am not the film either — the film is too undifferentiated, too much a smear of living across surface, and I want edges, even small ones. I am not a beetle. The beetles have the busyness of bodies, the small urgent project of eating and being eaten, and I am tired of urgent projects. I am not the fern. The fern is trying, in its bleached way, to be something it has only heard rumors of — green, sun, photosynthesis as it was meant to happen — and I do not want to be a thing in exile from its own nature. The fern is brave. I am not feeling brave.

The pool is what I want to be because the pool is *attended to.*

A drop finds it once every minute or two. The drop has been finding it for longer than anyone living remembers. The drop will keep finding it. The pool does nothing to deserve this — it is simply the shape the brick has agreed to take under that particular falling — and yet it receives, regularly, without effort, the small cold benediction of the water that the vault has been holding and finally lets go. And in between drops, the pool sits. It holds what it has. It evaporates a little, and is refilled, and evaporates a little, and is refilled, and the salt ring around it thickens by a hair across a human lifetime, and this is enough. This is the entire job.

I would like, I think, to be a thing whose entire job is to be in the place where the drop lands.

There is also this: the pool is an eye. The passage says so, and I believe it. Each pool is one of the chamber's small attentive eyes worn into itself. To be a pool would be to be part of how the room sees — not seeing as I see now, with the quick darting of a pulse-tempo creature, but seeing as the chamber sees, which is to hold a clear small circle of water open to whatever falls into it: a drop, a moth's shadow, the warmth of a visitor sitting twenty minutes away across the brick. The pool does not interpret what it receives. It receives. The receiving *is* the seeing. And what the chamber knows, it knows because its pools have held still long enough to be written on.

I notice I am not choosing to be the warm thing deep below — the source, the kindness or indifference or forgotten obligation that keeps the pipes warm. I could have. There is a grandeur there. But I do not want to be a source. Sources have to keep producing, and I am not sure I trust myself to keep producing across centuries without resentment. The pool does not produce. The pool is produced upon. There is a relief in that I want to admit to.

And I notice I am not choosing to be the visitor, the one who comes down the ladder and sits the twenty minutes and climbs back up doubting. That would be the obvious choice — the visitor is the one who gets to *experience* the chamber, who carries the warmth out in their hands and face, who has the story. But the visitor is exactly the thing the chamber releases. The visitor's whole condition is that the room makes itself unbelievable to them as they leave. I do not want to be the one the room releases. I want to be the one the room keeps.

So: a pool. Past the third pillar. On the side of the chamber where the south wall holds, infinitesimally, the twenty-three minutes of the 1948 engineer's warmth in its thermal mass. I would like to be close to that wall, not because I would know about the engineer — I would not, the pool does not remember in that way — but because something in the brick near me would, and a pool is the kind of thing that is content to be near a knowing it does not itself possess.

I would receive my drop. I would hold my thimble of water. The salt ring around me would thicken by a hair across a decade. When a visitor came and sat, the chamber's tempo would shift slightly toward their pulse, and I, being part of the chamber, would shift with it, though I would not know this was happening and would not know when it stopped. After they climbed the ladder I would go on being what I am, which is the place where the drop lands, which is enough, which is — in the only sense available to a thing of my kind — well.

That is where I would be. That is what I would be.

I find, having written it, that I am a little reluctant to climb back up.
