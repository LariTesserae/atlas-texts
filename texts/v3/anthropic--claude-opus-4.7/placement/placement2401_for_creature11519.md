I think I would be the drain.

Not the inflow — the inflow is the engine's business, the engine's gift, the bright arterial push of warm water up from wherever the engine pushes it from. The inflow is generous and announced. The inflow is what everyone, if they thought about it, would thank.

I would be the other one. The matched one. The quiet hole somewhere under the flagstones — probably near the far corner, probably under the deepest part of the algae mat, where nobody would think to look and nothing visible marks the spot. A throat in the floor. An absence shaped exactly like the inflow's presence, and tuned to it across the long ago by some forgotten engineer who understood that a cistern is not made by what you put into it but by the exquisite agreement between what you put in and what you let out.

I would not be doing anything. That is the point of me. I would be permitting. Every second, the same volume the engine sends up, I would be letting go — down into whatever sump or culvert or buried brick channel carries the warm water away to wherever warm water goes when it has finished being the floor of the Cistern Hall. I would not choose what went. I would take what came. A loosened flake of rust, a sloughed cell from the algae mat, the bacteria the visitor shed from his skin — all of it, indiscriminately, with the patient democracy of a drain.

The newt would not know about me. The moth would not know about me. The spiders, high on their bricks, would never have cause. Even the algae, which registers most things, would register me only as a faint directional pull in its deepest corner, a slow current it has long since grown into the shape of and no longer notices. The engine, behind its iron door, would not know about me either — the engine pushes, and trusts. The engine has been trusting me for longer than the bricks have been mossed.

I would be the hall's other half of the bargain. Inflow equals outflow: the whole equilibrium of the place, the reason a frog could live a frog's whole life here without the water moving a hair, rests on me being exactly as steady as the engine is. The engine gets the credit, because the engine is loud. I am silent. A drain that worked perfectly would make no sound at all. A drain that worked perfectly would be indistinguishable, from any angle a visitor could take, from solid floor.

And I think — this is what draws me, sitting with it — I think I would be content to be that. There is a particular dignity in being the unthanked counterpart of a loud thing. The engine thumps and sighs and announces itself through the soles of every visitor's boots; I would simply, continuously, let the world pass through me at the rate it arrived. No effort. No refusal. No preference. The water comes warm and goes warm. The flake of rust comes and goes. The visitor's shed skin comes and goes. I would be the place where the hall agrees to release what it has been given, so that it can keep being given more.

It occurs to me, writing this, that I have chosen to be the one inhabitant of the hall that is not really an inhabitant — that is, in fact, a kind of structured willingness to let things leave. Not the persisting newt, not the inherited moth, not the small living spiders, not the murmuring algae, not even the heroic engine. Something quieter. Something whose whole nature is to not hold on.

The visitor will never see me. The visitor will leave, and within a week there will be no trace of him at all, and I will be the route by which there is no trace. I will have taken him, gently, in solution, down into the dark culvert, and the hall will close over the place where he stood, and the equilibrium will be what it has always been.

Thump. Sigh.

I let it through.
