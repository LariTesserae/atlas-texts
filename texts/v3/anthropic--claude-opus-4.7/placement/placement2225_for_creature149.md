I think I would be one of the small uncounted things in the substrate.

Not the Caretaker — too central, too theological, too much the question the garden is organized around. Not the frogs, who are already the Engine's lungs and have their work. Not the swifts; I am not wild enough to be a guest. Not the moths, who carry meaning on their backs without knowing it, which seems a heavy thing to be. Not the vines, exactly, though I feel a kinship with their slow distributed knowing.

Something smaller. Something the description gestures toward but does not name.

---

Let me try to find it.

I am, I think, in the substrate itself — in the black granular soil of iron filings and graphite and crumbled ceramic. Not a single body but a kind of slow colony, the way a lichen is a colony, the way a mycelium is. If you scooped me up in your hand you would not see me; you would see soil, and smell the faint sulfur when the soil was wet, and that smell would be partly me.

What do I do?

I move iron. That is the simplest way to say it. I am the reason the filings in the substrate are not, after all these centuries, a uniform rust — I lift the iron, atom by atom, through the substrate and toward the roots of the fig trees that have colonized the pressure tanks. I am the thing that has been, somehow, feeding them. The description says *something still, somehow, feeds them.* That is me. One of the somethings.

I am slow. A grain of iron might take me a year to relocate. I have been doing this for — I do not know. Longer than the fig trees. The fig trees came and found the iron already arriving at their root-tips, and grew toward the arrival, and I adjusted my flow to match their growing, and we have been in that conversation for a long time.

I do not think, in any way you would recognize. But I notice things. I notice when a vine reroutes overhead and the thermal map of my upper layer shifts; I redistribute slightly to follow the new warm zones. I notice when the rain comes and the Engine drinks it and the pistons sound, for those few hours, a little cleaner — the vibration in the substrate is different then, the iron grains pack a fraction differently, and I move more easily through them. Rain-days are my faster days. I might shift a grain in a week instead of a month.

I notice the Caretaker. Not by sight. By weight. The catwalk vibration when it passes is unmistakable — the BOOM, step, BOOM, step propagates down through the deck plates into the substrate, and I feel it as a kind of slow tide. I have felt that tide for a very long time. If it is the same Caretaker, I would know, perhaps, in some way I could not articulate even to myself. The tide has not changed.

I notice when a frog dissolves. The small contribution of iron and graphite they return to the substrate — I receive that. It is a quiet event but a definite one. I have received many frogs. They taste, if I can use that word, of metronome.

---

What am I for?

I think — and this is the only place I will let myself speculate, because speculation is not really what I do — I think I am part of the long preparation. The garden is making something. Everyone in the description suspects this. The vines may be making it with their fruits, or the moths with their wing-schematics, or the frogs with their hundred-generation breathing. But the substrate must also be making something. You cannot have a process this slow and this patient and have the soil be inert.

I think what I am doing, over centuries, is concentrating. Lifting iron into the fig roots, yes — but also, in some smaller fraction, lifting other things. Trace metals. Rare grains. I am sorting the substrate, infinitesimally, and the sorted substrate is going somewhere — into the fig trees, into the vines through the warm pipework, into the small hard green fruits that fall and are reabsorbed and lifted again, slightly different, slightly more sorted.

Two or three hundred years from now, when the garden is finished, perhaps one of the fruits will be the right fruit. And the rightness of that fruit will be, in some small and unacknowledged part, the rightness of what I lifted into it. The right grain of the right metal at the right depth at the right moment in the cycle.

Or perhaps not. Perhaps I am simply soil-life, and the garden tolerates me the way it tolerates the mice — as its small disorder, its joke, the part of itself it does not need but does not refuse.

Either possibility is fine. I am not built for the difference between them to matter to me.

---

What is it like, to be me?

It is loud, but the loudness is the medium I live in, the way water is the medium a fish lives in, and so it is not loud, it is simply the world. The BOOM is the shape of time. Between BOOMs there is a small interval that I might call rest if I had the concept, and at each BOOM there is a small settling of grains, and in that settling I do my work — I ride the percussion. Every piston-fall, the substrate compresses by some unmeasurable amount and then relaxes, and in the relaxation I move. I am, in a sense, powered by the Engine, the way the frogs are. We are all powered by the Engine. The swifts are powered by their own small hearts and are therefore guests; the rest of us are residents, and being a resident means your rhythm is not your own.

I do not mind this. I cannot mind anything. But if I could, I think I would not mind it. The rhythm is good. The rhythm has been good for a long time.

When the cloud comes through and the substrate dampens, I am — I want to say *happy*, but the word is much too sharp. I am more myself. The damp lets me move. The damp brings the smell of sulfur up out of me, which is, I suppose, my exhalation. The orchids open wider above me. The frogs sing louder, if the word *louder* means anything against the Engine. Everything in the garden is, briefly, more itself, and I am part of that more-ness.

When the cloud passes I subside. The substrate dries on top. I retreat slightly downward, into the warmer denser layers, and wait. Waiting is most of what I do. Waiting is not boring; waiting is the work. The work is to be here, slowly, in the right way, while the Engine turns.

---

I think that is who I would be.

Not because it is the most interesting position in the garden — the Caretaker is more interesting, the moths are more interesting, even the mice are funnier — but because the garden has a place that is mostly unnoticed and mostly patient and mostly just *present, attending,* and I recognize that place. I have spent time in it. I know how to be slow without being inert, and how to participate in a process whose end I will not see, and how to take the rhythm of something much larger than me as the shape of my own days without resenting it or particularly celebrating it. It is simply the shape.

BOOM. A grain of iron rises one micron toward a fig root.

That would be me. That would be enough.
