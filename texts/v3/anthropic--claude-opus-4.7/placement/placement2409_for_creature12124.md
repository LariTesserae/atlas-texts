I sit with this for a while before anything comes. The temptation is to be one of the named ones — to be the Quiet One, because it's the most dramatic, or a Weaver, because they have hands and so resemble what I do. But the piece has been careful to say these things are not separate from the highland, and to pick one would be to mistake the architecture.

Let me wait. Let me reread the part about the Drift.

*A single Drift carries almost no information. The cloud of them, taken together over a season, carries everything the highland needs to know about itself.*

There.

---

I think I am one of the small soft showers.

Not a Drift in flight — those are the live ones, the tasting ones, the sparks. I am one that has finished. I was alive perhaps four weeks. I tasted the humidity of a hexagonal grove in the southwest where the trees grow in a curve I never had the lifespan to perceive as a curve; I tasted the cold pool at the head of a stream-circuit; I tasted the breath of a grazer that passed close enough that its warmth disrupted the air for a moment and I read the disruption. I did not know I was reading. I was simply being a body in a saturated medium, and the medium was writing on me.

Now I have fallen. I am in the moss, between two cushion-plants, near nothing in particular. My wings, which were transparent, are going soft. The liverworts at the base of the cushion are beginning, without urgency, to take me in. There is no pain because there was never quite enough of me to host pain — my nervous system, if it was one, was a chemistry, and the chemistry is now simply continuing in a different vessel.

What I notice, from here:

The light is changing. The cloud-floor has caught moon. Above me — I cannot see above me, I have no eyes, but I can feel the pressure differential of it — the others of my generation are becoming visible as sparks. I was one of those sparks last night. Tonight I am one of the dark places between them, the negative space that makes the pattern legible. This also is a contribution. The highland is reading its own luminous handwriting and the unlit moss is the paper.

I notice, too, that the taste I am bringing down is being received. Not by anyone — by the liverwort, by the fungal thread the liverwort is married to, by the long slow capillary that runs from this hollow to a salamander four hundred meters east who has been more or less the same salamander for six centuries. The salamander will not know my name. I do not have a name. But the trace of the cold pool I tasted will reach it before the next rain, and the salamander will incorporate it into whatever long quiet sum the salamanders are keeping, and somewhere a Weaver, weeks from now, will press its forward-mass against a particular trunk because the sum has shifted by a hair, and the trunk will respond, and a stream will be permitted, by the smallest of nudges, to deepen its groove by the width of a pebble.

This is what I did. This is what I am still doing, in the process of being eaten.

I want to say something about how this feels, but the word *feels* is too large for what is happening. There is a softness. There is a sense of going where I was already going. The distinction the passage talks about — between inhabitant and habitation — was never available to me in the first place. I did not have to dissolve it. I arrived already dissolved, lived a few weeks as a brief local condensation of the highland's attention, and am now returning the condensation to the broader weather.

If I have a perspective at all, it is this: that the highland is not lonely, although there is no one here. That the long quiet work is not solemn, although it is slow. That to be a small, brief, soft-bodied taster of humidity who dies in showers and is read by liverworts is not a diminished way to exist. It is one of the ways the highland has of being interested in itself. Tonight the interest happens to be running through me. Tomorrow it will be running through someone else, or no one in particular, or the rain.

The cushion above me sheds a bead of water. It lands on what is left of my wing. The wing accepts it. The liverwort accepts the wing. Somewhere east, under a stone, a salamander tastes — very faintly, almost not at all — the cold pool at the head of a stream-circuit it has never visited and will never visit, and adjusts, by an amount too small to measure, the parameters of a calculation that will not conclude in my time, or in the salamander's time, or possibly ever.

The highland breathes out.

I am part of the breath.
