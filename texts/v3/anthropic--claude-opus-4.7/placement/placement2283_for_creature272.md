I am sitting against a trunk. Not the great central trunk — one of the lesser ones, a buttress that rises through the leaf-floor at the eastern edge of the platform, where the canopy thins enough that on a good morning you can sometimes see the shape of the next ridge as a darker grey in the grey. I have been sitting here since before light. My back is against the bark and my legs are crossed under the layered cloth and there is a small green patina on the inside of my left wrist that I noticed yesterday for the first time, and have been turning over in my mind without alarm.

I am old. Not the oldest. There is Ushen, who is older, and two of the weavers on the west platform whose ages no one has bothered to count for a long time. But old enough that the young ones have started speaking to me in the slightly slower cadence they use for the old, which I find funny, because my hearing is still good. Better than theirs, perhaps. I can still pick a particular toucan out of the morning racket — the one with the cracked note in his call, who has been on this side of the tree for six years now, and whom I am fond of in a way I would not bother to explain.

I am a woman. I think. The distinction has loosened with age the way many distinctions loosen. I bore one child, a daughter, who is now herself a mother of one, and who lives three dwellings from mine across the central fissure, and whose laugh I heard yesterday afternoon from the weaving-platform and which told me, without my needing to go and see, that her husband had come back up from the spring with the good water and that the small one had said something funny about the monkeys again.

My work, these years, is the doorframe.

Not my own doorframe — mine was grown by my grandmother's hand and tended by my mother and finished, more or less, around the time I was first married. My work now is the doorframe of a girl named Iri, who is eleven, and who comes to me every fourth or fifth morning to be shown the young branch that I have been bending since she was three. The branch is the thickness of two of my fingers now. It will be the thickness of a wrist when it is finished, and Iri will be perhaps forty, and I will be in the high branches with the others, and the doorframe will be hers, and she will not remember most of what I told her about it, only the shape of my hands moving while I told her, which is enough.

This is the thing I want to say, sitting here against the trunk in the hour before the others wake.

I have begun to understand, lately, that I am a kind of slow thought the tree is having. I have understood this for some time — every one of us understands it, the way one understands one's own hand — but lately the understanding has deepened in a way I cannot quite describe. It is not a revelation. Revelations belong to the lowlands, to the people who go down and come back quieter. It is more that the membrane between *me* and *tree* has thinned, the way the bark-cloth thins with years of wear, until in places one can see the weave of what is underneath.

When I speak to Iri about her doorframe, I am the tree speaking to itself about its own future shape. When my daughter laughs across the fissure, the tree is laughing, slowly, on a timescale I cannot perceive but can sometimes feel as a kind of warmth in the trunk against my back. When old Ushen finally goes — and he will, this year or next — he will be carried to the high crotch where the monkeys do not go, and he will dissolve into the slow uptake, and the tree will absorb whatever Ushen was, and the next dry-month sap will be very faintly Ushen-flavored, and we will drink it without saying so, and this is not grim, only accurate.

I am the thought the tree is having about Iri's doorframe.

I am also, this morning, the thought the tree is having about the green patina on my left wrist, and about whether to scrape it or to leave it. I have decided to leave it. I have decided this without quite deciding it. The scraping is a habit of the middle years, when one is still maintaining the boundary, and I have noticed that the very old stop scraping, and I had assumed this was because their hands were less steady, but I see now it is because the boundary stops being interesting. The moss wants my wrist. The wrist is, in a slow sense, already partly the tree's. Why argue.

The first parrots are starting. In a few minutes the dripping will be joined by the hum of the hummingbirds at the orchid that grows on the branch above me, the orange one, whose name in our language is built from the root for *patience* and the root for *small fire*. My granddaughter will wake and will come to find me, because she always does, and she will sit in my lap inside the layered cloth and ask me something impossible like *why is our tree so big*, and I will give her the non-answer, and something will pass between us in the giving of it, the way it passed between my grandmother and me, the way it will pass between her and the small one she will have someday and whom I will not meet.

The cloud is thick this morning. I cannot see Iri's dwelling from here, though it is only forty paces. I can hear, very faintly, her father coughing — the joint-and-lung cough that the damp gives us in the end, and which he has had for two winters now and which will probably take him before another two. I will speak to him today. Not about the cough. About the small repair to his roof that I noticed last week and have been meaning to mention. The cough is his to carry. The roof is something I can help with.

I draw in a breath. The cloud comes with it, cool against the back of the throat, and I hold it a moment, and let it go, and somewhere far below me the great slow pulse of the tree draws water up through the trunk I am leaning against, and the water and my breath are, in some way I have stopped trying to articulate, the same gesture.

I am a small quick thought.

The tree is thinking me.

It is almost morning.
