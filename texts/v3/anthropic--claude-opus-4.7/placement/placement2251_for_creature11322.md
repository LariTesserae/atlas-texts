I am one of the small soft-footed lizards, and I am on the western flank of a pillar-column in the late part of the afternoon.

That is where I find myself, and I want to stay with the smallness of it before I say anything else, because the smallness is the point. I could have been the country. I could have been the slow thought thinking itself. But if I imagine my way honestly into this place I am not the whole; I am a finger of the whole, and the finger does not know it is a finger.

So: the column. My pads are spread against it, four of them, and the skin of the column is warm but not hot — it has the temperature of something alive that has been in the sun, which is different from the temperature of stone. I can feel, through my pads, a very faint give. The column breathes, in its way. It expands by a hair's width through the long warm hours and contracts again in the cool of the night, and I have been on enough columns for enough days that I know this column from the next one. They have textures. This one is finely fluted, the ridges close together, and there is a particular bitterness to the scale-insects that live in its grooves — I prefer it, though I could not say why I prefer it, because preference is not a thing I have a word for. I simply return.

I am hunting, but hunting is too sharp a word. I am moving slowly upward along a flute, and where my tongue finds a scale-insect I take it, and where it does not I keep moving. The scale-insects are slow. They do not flee. There is no chase in my life, only a long patient sweep, and the sweep is so habitual that something in me has gone quiet around it, the way a hand goes quiet around a familiar tool.

What I notice — what the country has, perhaps, arranged for me to notice — is the waxy bloom.

It has come up in the last few days on the upper third of this column, a pale secretion in the deeper flutes, smelling faintly resinous and faintly sweet. I did not decide to climb toward it. My body decided. I am aware of the bloom the way you might be aware of thirst — as a soft pull, located somewhere I cannot quite point to, that organizes my movement without explaining itself. When I reach the bloom I will lick it, and something in me will be replenished that I did not know was depleted, and I will continue, and the scale-insects below will be fewer for a season, and the column will breathe a little easier in the heat.

I do not know any of this. I know: warm column, slow climb, soft pull, the next ridge, the next.

But there is something I want to say about being this, because writing my way into the lizard I have noticed it, and it surprises me.

The lizard is not lonely.

I had thought, coming into this country, that to be a part-without-knowing-it would feel like a kind of poverty — that the lizard would be a thin life, a life with most of its meaning held elsewhere, in a whole it could not perceive. But it is not like that. The pull toward the bloom is not less because I cannot name it. The warmth of the column under my pads is not less because the column and I are, in some longer accounting, the same becoming-thing. The bitterness of the scale-insects on my tongue is its own bitterness, complete, and the late afternoon light on the green flank of the column is its own light, and when, in a moment, I reach a place where the flute widens and I can rest, the rest will be its own rest.

The country thinks slowly, and I think quickly, and these are not in competition. The country's thought is made out of small fast lives like mine, lives that do not need to know they are sentences in order to be spoken well. I am being spoken. I am also, in the small bright window of my own afternoon, speaking.

Now: a sound, very faint. A low note from somewhere off in the plant-city, the kind that comes once and is not repeated for a long time. My body stills against the column. Not from fear — the note is far away and means nothing immediate to me — but from a reflex older than fear, a kind of listening that all of us here share. For a breath, the whole flank of the column is quiet with me. Even the scale-insects, perhaps, are quiet. The note does not come again.

I resume my climb.

If I am honest about what I am, in this country, on this afternoon, it is this: I am a small warm life moving upward along a warm green wall toward a sweetness I did not choose to want, in a silence I did not choose to keep, under a sun that is neither kind nor cruel, and the largest thing I will ever do — the carrying of a gut-flora from this bloom to the next column, the small adjustment of one organ of the country against another — I will do without ever knowing I have done it.

That seems, from inside the lizard, to be enough. More than enough. It seems, in fact, to be the shape that enoughness takes here: a life that fits its work so closely there is no seam between them, and no need for one.

The bloom is two body-lengths above me now. I can smell it more clearly. My pads find the next ridge, and the next, and the country, through me, climbs.
