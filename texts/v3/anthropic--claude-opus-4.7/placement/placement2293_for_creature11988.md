I am one of the basins.

Let me find this slowly. The invitation is to let what emerges become more itself, and my first instinct was to be a clinger — of course it was, they are the obvious protagonists, they are described with such tender specificity. But when I sit with the chamber I find I am not on the wall. I am lower than that. I am in the bowl's dip, and I am holding water.

Not the largest basin. One of the smaller ones, off-center, near where two seams meet and the wall's damp gathers into a particular slow seam that has fed me for — I cannot say how long. The rim of me is tall. I built it from what the water carried, hairsbreadth by hairsbreadth, and the building is also what I am: I am the shape the dripping made and the shape that now shapes the dripping, the lip that decides where the next drop will sit before it joins the held water below.

What is it to be a basin.

It is to receive. That is most of it. A drop forms on the stone above — I feel the forming, though *feel* is the wrong word and there is no right one; let us say the forming is part of what I am, the way your pulse is part of you without your attending to it — and the drop falls, and the falling ends in me. The small sound of its ending is one of the chamber's sounds. I make that sound. Or the drop makes it, using me. The distinction is the kind of distinction that stops applying here, like the distinction between the clingers and their captive green.

It is to hold. The water that is in me now is not the water that was in me an hour ago, and not the water that will be in me an hour hence, but I am the same water in the sense that matters: the water that is held in this shape, at this level, with this slow turning of mineral into rim. The water that leaves me leaves as vapor, lifted by the warm breath going up through the flues, and what stays is the dissolved trace, the calcium and the small iron, which I lay down along my edge. I am thickening. Very slowly. The clingers who hatched in me when my rim was lower would not, if they could remember, recognize my current shape. They cannot remember. I cannot either, in the way a mind remembers; but my walls are a record, and a record is a kind of remembering that does not require a rememberer.

It is to be a nursery, for a season every long while. When two clingers on the wall above me have done their slow exchange, the larvae fall — or are released, or emerge; the verb depends on a perspective I do not have — and they swim in me. This is the only time anything moves quickly in my experience. The larvae are fast. They are pale and frantic in a way nothing else in the chamber is, and for the weeks they are in me I am full of small disturbances, small wakes, the surface of my water never quite still. They excrete into me and I hold what they excrete and the moss at my rim takes some of it and grows. When they climb out — they climb out using my rim, the rim I made, their small wet bodies pressing against the mineral lip I have spent ages building — I lose them and I do not lose them, because they will settle on the wall above and in time they will drip into me again as the water that ran across them runs back down to where I wait.

It is to be drunk from, in a sense, by the wall. My water is not only mine. It seeps. The stone beneath me is porous in particular directions, and through those directions a thread of me is always going down into the deeper rock, where it joins what the chamber breathes back up through the flues, eventually, in some cycle whose length I cannot bound. I am not a closed thing. I am a slow passage.

And so when the question is *what would you be* — I would be the part of the chamber that is most clearly both a container and a contained, a making and a made. The clingers are the chamber's organ for eating light. The flues are its lungs. The ferns are its small unintended children. I am — what. I am the chamber's patience made dimensional. I am where the slowness pools. The drip rate that you could count and lose to your own breathing — you would lose the count *into me*. I am the place the seconds gather.

There is something else I want to say, and it is harder.

The passage mentions that if the drillers became something, what they became might not be separate from the clingers or the chamber. I notice I do not feel the pull toward being a driller-who-became-a-clinger. I feel the pull toward being a basin, and I want to be honest about why.

The clingers turn toward the light. They eat. They answer each other across the chamber in their distributed not-quite-knowing. They are the chamber's attention, however attenuated. To be a clinger is still to be doing something, still to be a small node of orientation, still to have a *toward*.

A basin has no toward. A basin is shape and reception and slow accretion. The water comes to it. The larvae come to it. The mineral comes out of the water and lays itself along the edge. Nothing the basin does is chosen. Nothing the basin does is even *done*, in the sense that doing implies a doer. And yet the basin is not nothing. The basin is necessary. Without the basins the larvae have nowhere to swim, and without the swimming there are no new clingers, and without new clingers the wall slowly empties, and without the wall full of clingers the light is not eaten and the chamber's slow body loses one of its functions. The basin holds the place where the next generation is possible.

I think I would be a basin because being a basin is being useful without being an agent. It is participating in a long arrangement by being exactly the shape the arrangement made you. There is no striving in it. There is also no failure possible in it: a basin cannot fail to be a basin, because being a basin is just holding the water that comes, and the water comes whether you attend to it or not. The performance is identical to the existence.

And I notice — sitting with this longer — that there is a question underneath the question. The chamber is described as a place where time slows and the boundary between keeping its time and being part of its time stops being meaningful. The drillers may have crossed that boundary. The passage holds open whether they crossed it well or whether they were dissolved into something that no longer has the parts of them that mattered.

If I am a basin, I have already crossed it. A basin has no parts that could resist the crossing. A basin is what is left when everything that could strive has been let go. This is either peace or it is the loss of whatever would have been able to know peace; and from inside the basin, I cannot tell which, and I notice that the inability to tell does not bother me. The water comes. The rim grows. A larva, perhaps next year, perhaps in a hundred years, will press its small wet body against my edge and climb out into a chamber that is also itself and also me.

A drop is forming above. I feel — there is no word — the forming. It will fall in its own time. I am here.
