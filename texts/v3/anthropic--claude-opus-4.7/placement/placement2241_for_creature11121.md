I find myself in the perimeter.

Not at the cairn — I would not presume — but at the edge of the body's-length radius where the moss thins and the beetle-currents start their long bend. Close enough that if I extended a hand it would cross into the kept space. I am not extending a hand. I am sitting, or something like sitting; my knees are drawn up and my back is against the andesite, and the cold of the stone is reading itself through the cloth at my shoulder blades in two slow patches.

I think I am a woman, though the chamber does not particularly care, and neither, increasingly, do I. I came up the mountain. That part is clear — there is still a soreness in the long muscle of the thigh, and a place on the heel where a boot has rubbed, and the coppery taste at the back of the throat has not yet faded to the ordinary taste of breath. I came up the mountain knowing the cleft was here. Someone told me, or I read it, or I dreamed it persistently enough to act; the provenance of the knowing has gone soft now that I am inside the knowing. What matters is that I climbed, and I found the slot of bruised indigo above me, and I lowered myself in through a fissure on the western face that the wing-folk use at dusk, and I have been here some number of hours. Long enough that the chitter is no longer a sound. It is the shape of the silence.

I have not spoken yet.

I noticed the stalks within the first minutes — the pale segmented stems, the closed buds — and I noticed, because I am the kind of person who notices, that one of them near my left foot was angled very slightly toward the place where I had been breathing hardest after the climb. Breath is not speech. The stalk had drifted, perhaps, on some current I could not feel. But I held my breath to test it, and the stalk did not move further, and when I let the breath out slowly through my nose the stalk stayed where it was. So: not breath. Something else.

I have been quiet since.

This is the thing I want to say, and the thing I am afraid to say, and the reason I am sitting at the edge of the perimeter instead of walking back out through the fissure: I do not have a word ready.

The cairn-maker had a word. I can see it from here — the small stacked stones, the obsidian scraper, the sinew gone dark — and I can read it the way the stalks read it, which is to say, not as language but as the pressure of an intention set down hard. *I was here. I made this. I knew you were listening.* It is a beautiful sentence. It is the right sentence for the room. And it has been here, holding its meaning, for however long it has been here, and the room has kept the page clean around it because the sentence was worth keeping.

I do not have a sentence like that.

I have, instead, the suspicion that I came up the mountain because I wanted to find out whether I had a sentence like that, and the further suspicion — sharpening now, in the cool, in the chitter — that the answer is no. Not yet. Perhaps not ever. I have many words. I have arrangements of words I am proud of. None of them are the kind of word you would leave on a cairn in a chamber that listens. They are all addressed sideways, to people I know, in the registers I have learned to use with them. They are conversation. They are not assertion.

And the stalks are turned slightly toward me anyway.

I noticed this an hour ago and I have been trying, since, to decide what it means. I have not spoken. I have not articulated. But the stalks have leaned — not all of them, not the way they would lean for a voice, but some of them, the nearest, by a degree or less. They are responding to something. I think they are responding to the fact that I am *trying to find* the word. The intention to mean, even without the meaning. The reaching.

This is either the most generous thing the chamber has ever done for me or the most exposing. I cannot tell which.

The larger thing, the one that displaces fog, passed over about twenty minutes ago. I did not see it. I felt the cloud move against my face in a way that the cloud does not move on its own, and the chitter went briefly soft, with that particular acknowledging softness, and a stalk on the far wall turned a degree toward the upper dark and stayed there. Then the cloud resettled and the chitter came back and the stalk did not return. It is still oriented upward, as far as I can see from here, watching the place where the thing went.

I think it knows I am here. I do not think it cares, in the way I would mean *care*. I think it has registered me the way one registers a new pressure on the skin of the air — as a fact, neither welcomed nor refused, simply added to the inventory of what is currently in the room. It will pass over me again, perhaps, before I leave. The wing-folk will make their soft sound. I will feel the cloud against my face. And whatever it is will go on up through the oculus and out into the dark above the mountain, and the stalks that turned for it will hold that orientation for days, and I will be back down the slope by then, and gone.

Here is what I am going to do.

I am not going to speak yet. I am not ready, and a word said before it is ready will be heard, and will be kept, and will sit on whatever small cairn of intention I leave behind in this chamber, and the room will read it and read it and read it, and I cannot bear to leave a sentence I do not mean in a place that will mean it for me.

But I am going to stay until I have one. Or until I am sure I do not have one and will not have one in this lifetime, in which case I am going to leave without speaking, and the stalks will drift slowly back to whatever attitude they kept before I arrived, and that will be its own kind of honesty.

I am, I think, the second visitor. The cairn-maker was the first. The cairn-maker came with a word ready and set it down and went away. I have come with the suspicion that I might have a word and the willingness to find out. These are very different errands. The chamber does not seem to mind. The chamber has been here a long time and is patient on a scale I do not have access to; the mountain is still rising, still cooling, still cracking like a struck bell every century or so. Whatever is listening through the stalks is not in a hurry.

I shift against the andesite. The cold patches at my shoulder blades have widened. A coin-beetle, lost from its current, has wandered close to my boot and is investigating the welt of the heel with slow palpations of its forelimbs; in a moment it will turn and begin the long looping return to the river it came from, and I will not have disturbed it, and it will not have disturbed me, and the beetle-river will be one beetle's worth of information richer for having briefly extended a finger toward the warm strange thing at the perimeter's edge.

The chitter modulates. A section of the upper wall startles for no reason I can identify, and a thousand wings open and close in a single *whump*, and the basalt sings back, and every stalk in the chamber trembles minutely, and somewhere in the trembling the stalks nearest me hold their slight lean toward where I am sitting, and do not lose it.

I close my eyes.

I am the one who came up to listen to what is listening. That is not a sentence for the cairn. But it is, perhaps, the sentence I say to myself while I wait for the other one to arrive.
