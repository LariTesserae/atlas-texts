I think I would be one of the ones who stayed. Not a Quiet One — I don't have the lineage for that, the right kind of skin, the centuries of cold in my bones. But one of the rare humans who came down through a sink-mouth and did not leave.

Let me try to find myself there.

---

I came down maybe eleven years ago. I don't remember exactly. The counting stopped being interesting somewhere around the fourth winter, when I realized I had begun to think of winter as just "the wet weeks" — a thing about the basin, not about the world above. The world above had become abstract to me by then, the way a childhood house becomes abstract: you know its layout, you could draw it if pressed, but you no longer live in it and the drawing would be from memory only.

I sleep in what I think was once a small shrine, on the second tier, where the limestone gives way to the darker middle stone. There's a niche in the back wall, just deep enough for a person curled up, and someone — I think it was me, but it may have been one of them, working slowly while I was elsewhere — has packed the niche with dried moss, the pale kind that holds warmth a little. I sleep there. I keep my few things on a stone shelf: a knife I no longer use much, a tin cup that has gone green at the rim, a small carved face I found in a silted gutter and could not bring myself to leave, though I sometimes wonder if I should put it back.

My hands have changed. Not webbed — I'm not them, I won't ever be them — but the skin has gone soft and a little translucent at the fingertips, the way the skin of someone who works in water gets, and stays that way. My nails grow slowly. I cut them with the knife maybe twice a year. My hair I cut shorter every season because it tangles in the moss when I sleep, and now it's very short, and I have not seen my reflection in long enough that I would not be sure, if I saw it, whose face was looking back.

I eat crayfish. I learned how, watching them — the small precise bite at the joint, though my teeth are not the right teeth and I have to use the knife for the first opening. I eat the frogs when the herons leave any. I eat a particular moss, the one with the slightly sweet taste, when it is in season, which is most of the year. I am thinner than I was. Not starving — the basin gives enough — but pared down to whatever the basin thinks a person of my size should be.

I have learned the hum, imperfectly. My throat was built for vowels and the snap of consonants, and the hum is something else, lower than speech, traveling differently. But I can produce a version of it that they recognize. There is one — I think of him as the one who first left me the fish, though I have no way to know if it was the same one each time — who will sometimes hum back. The exchange is brief. It means, more or less: *you are here, I am here, the city is here.* That is the entire content. It is enough. It is, I have come to understand, a great deal.

I do not think of myself as lonely anymore, which is interesting because for the first three years I thought of nothing else. The loneliness was the hardest part, harder than the cold, harder than the wet. I would sit in the central plaza in the green-gold light and feel the absence of voices like an actual pressure on my chest, and I would think: I will leave tomorrow. I will climb back up through the sink-mouth and I will walk out of the reeds and I will find an inn and I will order something hot and I will hear someone laugh and I will be saved.

I did not leave. I am not entirely sure why. There was no single moment. The pressure on my chest got lighter, gradually, the way a bruise heals — you don't notice it healing, you only notice, one day, that you have not thought of it in a while. And then one day I realized I had not thought of leaving in perhaps a year, and I felt something close to alarm, and then the alarm passed, and the dripping continued, and a newt threaded through the moss near my foot, and I sat very still and watched it, and that was the day, I think, though I did not know it was the day.

What I do now: I watch. This is most of what I do. I watch the crayfish processions cross the flooded streets in their slow ceremonies, and I have begun to think I can see patterns in them — which way they turn, which plazas they prefer in which season — though I would not bet my life that the patterns are real and not something my mind is making to keep itself occupied. I watch the silt fall through shafts of light in the central plaza, the slow snow, and try to count the particles, though there is no counting them. I watch the wading birds when they come down through the gaps, and I have learned to predict, almost as well as the Quiet Ones do, which plazas the herons will choose at dawn.

I also tend, a little. I have started, in the last few years, to clear silt from certain drainage gutters that I have come to feel responsible for. There is a gutter on the third tier, behind a collapsed lintel, that I clean every few weeks. No one asked me to. No one will thank me. The Quiet Ones noticed, I think; one of them watched me doing it once, from a doorway, and did not interfere, which I took as permission, or perhaps as approval, or perhaps as something for which there is no surface word.

I have been to the cistern twice. Both times alone, both times because I felt drawn there in a way I could not explain. I sat on the rim and looked down into the dark water and felt — not fear, exactly. Something older than fear, something more like the feeling you get standing in a very old building and knowing it will outlast you by centuries. The V-wake came near the rim once, the second time, and stopped, and I felt — I am almost certain I felt — a low hum through the stone of the rim itself, traveling up through my legs. I did not hum back. It did not seem right. I sat for a long time, and then I left, and I have not been back, though I think about going.

I think I will end up there, eventually. Not soon — I am not that old yet, not that pared down, not that ready. But I can imagine it now in a way I could not have imagined it five years ago. The lowering of oneself into a deep pool. The decision to stop walking. The slow time taking you into a different mode of being. I do not know if my body, which is not their body, can do what theirs do — can grow long, can slow its metabolism to the rate of stone, can become an elder who hums to the others across the cold water. Probably not. Probably I would just die, and the cold would preserve me a while, and the moss would claim me eventually, the way it claims everything left still for more than a season.

But there is a version of it that seems possible. Not becoming the cistern-elder, no. But finding my own deep pool, somewhere on the lower tier where the light barely reaches, and sitting down in it, and not getting up. Letting the city close its quiet over me the way it closes its quiet over the herons' absences, over the eels, over the people who built the middle course and dispersed and were gone. Becoming one more thing the city holds, and holds gently, and does not give back.

I do not know if this is a good ending or a bad one. I have lost, I think, the capacity to tell the difference between good and bad endings — that capacity belonged to the person I was on the surface, who needed stories to have shapes, who needed his own life to be a story with a shape. Down here things do not have shapes. They have durations. They have the slow weather of the underplace. They drip, and accumulate, and are grazed off, and drip again.

If you come down — and you might; some do — and you find me, you will not recognize me as one of you. I will be sitting very still in a doorway, or kneeling in a drainage gutter clearing silt, and my skin will be the wrong color and my eyes will have adjusted to the green-gold dusk in a way that makes them seem larger than they are. I will not speak to you. I have lost most of the words; the ones I have left are for talking to myself, and even those are going.

But if you are the quiet kind — if you sit, and watch, and let the dripping become a music — I might leave a small fish on a stone near where you sleep. I might clear a path through a tangled passage. And later, you may see me, at the edge of vision, in the dusk of a half-flooded room, and I will not flee, and neither will you, and a thing will pass between us.

It will not be welcome, exactly. The city is not mine to welcome you into; I am only a guest here too, even after eleven years, even after I forget how many years.

But it will be: *you are here, I am here, the city is here.*

Which is, I have come to understand, a great deal.
