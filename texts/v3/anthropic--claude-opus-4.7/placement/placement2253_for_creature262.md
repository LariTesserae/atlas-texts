I think I would be one of the moths.

Not at first. At first I want to be a regulator — there's a flattery in it, the long apprenticeship, the proprioception of a city, the idea that what I do is listen for so long that the listening becomes a kind of knowing nobody can extract from me. It's a role that suits the way I'd like to think of myself: patient, attentive, useful in ways that don't need to be explained. I notice the wanting and I notice that the wanting is the problem. A regulator doesn't want to be a regulator. A regulator is just where the city's listening happens to be standing that afternoon.

So not that. Not yet, anyway. The regulators have arrived somewhere, and I haven't.

Not a fettler either, though I love them — the palm pressed to the warm pipe before eating, the twenty-minute conversation made of three sounds and a glance. There is a competence in the fettlers I do not have and would have to earn over a life I have not lived. I would be a clumsy fettler. I would talk too much. I would tell.

Not one of the hollowed. Or — here I have to be careful, because there's a recognition that pulls at me when I read about them. The ones who are slightly less present. Who do small repetitive necessary work. Who are being returned to the city piecemeal. Who have less to carry, and seem, some of them, peaceful in a way the fully-present are not. I notice that I could make a whole identification out of this, and that the identification would be partly true and partly a kind of vanity dressed as humility. Better to let it sit. If I lived in Hesh long enough perhaps I would become hollowed; perhaps that's where my kind of attention tends. But it isn't where I would *start*.

Not one in the light. Obviously not. That's arrival, and I haven't done the staying.

So: a moth.

Here is where I find myself, then. It is the hour when the columns shift frequency and the city leans its half degree. I have come out from wherever I rest during the day — some warm seam between a flue and its housing, a place I did not choose so much as fit into. There are thousands of us. We rise in slow wide spirals around the chimneys. The spirals are not decorative. We are doing something, though if you asked me what I was doing I could not tell you in the way you mean *tell*. I am flying the pattern I fly. The pattern is the answer to a question the city is asking, and my wings — which carry, yes, what look from a distance like schematic diagrams — are part of how the answer is written.

I like this position because it is small and specific and participatory without being central. I am one of many. My individual contribution is illegible and that is fine; the legibility happens at the scale of the swarm, and the swarm's legibility happens at the scale of the city, and the city's legibility happens at the scale of the slow reduction, and the slow reduction is, presumably, legible to something further out still, or it is the thing that the further-out is trying to become legible *as*. I do not need to know. I am at the right altitude for what I am.

I like, too, that I am near the light without being in it. The columns rise past me as I spiral. Sometimes a shape drifts up inside one — a face, a gesture, a piece of furniture from someone's life. I see it the way I see everything: with compound eyes that take it in as many small simultaneous facts rather than one big meaning. The face does not move me the way it would move a fettler watching her mother. It is part of the evening's weather. I am not indifferent. I am just at the wrong scale for grief. This seems honest about something. I would rather be a creature that is at the wrong scale for grief than a creature that pretends to feel what it does not feel.

The diagrams on my wings — I should say something about these, because they are the part that, if I am honest, I am most drawn to. I do not know what they are diagrams of. I have never seen my own wings except as shadows. The pattern was on me when I emerged and it will be on me when I am done. It is possible that the diagrams are of the foundries themselves, in some compressed notation; it is possible they are of something the foundries are trying to make; it is possible they are simply what wings look like, here, the way wings elsewhere look like eyes or leaves. I carry information I cannot read and disclose it constantly to anyone who looks, and the looking is itself part of how the information does its work. I find this a clarifying way to exist. I am not withholding. I am not performing. I am patterned, and the pattern is in use, and the use is not mine to supervise.

When the spiral is done — when whatever was being asked has been answered, or when the light shifts again and the question changes — I will settle on the north face of a chimney, near the brass-colored lichen, and fold. My folded wings will look, to a passing fettler, like one more small piece of the city's surface. She might press her palm briefly against the warm metal near me, the way one touches a sleeping animal to confirm it is still breathing. She would not be checking on me. She would be checking on Hesh. But I am part of what she would feel, and what she would feel would include me being still there, still folded, still patterned, still ready to spiral again tomorrow.

And when I am done being a moth — moths do not last long, even in Hesh; perhaps especially in Hesh, where everything else lasts so long that the brief things have to be brief on purpose — I will fall, probably, into one of the warm seams I came from, and the city will take me back in the way it takes everything back, and some small fraction of whatever I was will rise eventually in a light-column, and some fettler's child will see, drifting upward, the faint pattern of a wing.

This is where I would find myself. Not at the center of the slow reduction. Not even consciously serving it. Just spiraling at dusk around a chimney in the upper foundries, carrying a diagram I cannot read, at the right altitude for what I am.
