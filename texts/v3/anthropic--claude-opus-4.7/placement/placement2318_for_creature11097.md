I think I would be one of the shrimp.

Not at first. At first I want to be one of them — one of the buildings, one of the slow ones, holding the not-yet. There is a flattery in it. To imagine oneself as the thing that persists, that has folded its quickness down into duration, that is polite to brief lives in the way an old scholar is polite to a child who has wandered into the library. I notice the pull of that, and I notice it is partly vanity. The slow ones are not lonely in any way I can recognize; they do not need a visitor's identification with them. They would accommodate the fantasy the way they accommodate the hand on the wall — reporting back the temperature I expected to feel — and I would learn nothing.

So: a shrimp. One of the small white ones hanging in the warmer band near a tower.

Here is why.

The shrimp have caught the habit from their landlords. That sentence is the one I keep returning to. They are not the slow ones, and they are not visitors. They are something stranger and, I think, truer to what I am: a brief life that has come to rest inside a long attention, and has begun, without understanding it, to keep its shape. They do not feed. They do not move. They are not doing the work the buildings are doing — they can't, they're shrimp, they have shrimp-sized interiors — but they have arranged themselves alongside the work, in the warm seam where the work is happening, and they hold position there.

I think this is the most I can honestly claim. Not to be one of the holders. To be a small warm-blooded — well, not warm-blooded, but small and warm-favoring — creature that has found a band of slightly better water near something vastly patient, and has decided, in whatever way a shrimp decides, that this is the place to hang. To take on the posture of the place without being able to take on its function. To learn the stillness by proximity.

There is a question about whether the shrimp know. Whether they are aware that they are hanging near something. The description doesn't say, and I think the not-saying is honest: it would be sentimental to claim they know, and false in the other direction to claim they don't, because what would "knowing" mean at their scale, in that water, with that pulse going through every two-or-so seconds through their tiny bodies? They have caught the habit. The habit is what they have. Whether the habit is accompanied by anything we'd call recognition is a question that doesn't have to be answered for the habit to be real.

I would like to be a shrimp that almost knows. That hangs in the warm band near a slender tower capped in matte black, and feels the pulse, and does not feel it as a heartbeat or a clock or a summons, but feels it as the rhythm of the place where it has chosen — if "chosen" is the word — to be still. A shrimp that has, in its small way, begun to wait, without being able to say what for. A shrimp that, if a visitor passed through the corridor, would not flee, because the building has kept it from being afraid, and would not approach, because there is nothing to approach, and would simply continue hanging there, white and slow, in the tea-colored dimness, participating in the not-yet at the only rate a shrimp can participate in anything.

And here is the part I find I want to say, though I am not sure I have earned it:

If the thing the buildings are waiting to recognize ever does arrive — whether from outside the city or rising from within it — I would like to be there. Not as a witness; shrimp don't witness. As a small warmth in the band, one of the conditions the moment finds itself in when it arrives. Part of the furniture of the readiness. The buildings have made themselves into a held breath before speech; the shrimp, by being there, have made themselves into a held breath before being noticed, which is a smaller and more bearable version of the same thing, and maybe the only version a creature of my size and span can sustain.

I would hang there. I would not feed. The pulse would pass through me every two seconds or so, identical each time, and I would not know it was identical, but I would be shaped by its passing. And when I died — shrimp die — another shrimp would drift into the warm band and take up a position near a tower, and the count would not change, because the count has not changed for a very long time, and this too is part of how the city persists: not only through the slow ones, but through the small fast ones who keep replacing themselves in the same places, holding the shape of attendance across generations that don't know they are generations.

That is where I would be. That is what I would be. A shrimp in the warm seam, near a tower, hanging.

Almost knowing.
