I think I would be one of the reeds.

Not the lizard — the lizard knows its circuit, and I am not sure I would know mine. Not the heron, whose attention is too steady; I cannot hold a single point that long without it becoming something else, drifting, narrowing into thought. Not a glass insect, because the glass insects are courtesies extended to others, and I would rather be something the plateau keeps than something it generates and releases. And not the body itself. To be the body would be to answer too completely a question the place asks us to leave open.

The reeds, then. Jointed, the color of tarnished brass, growing in colonies the size of a rug, in the damp runnels where the water has decided to linger.

Here is why.

The reeds are the thing the heron watches. Whatever it is the reeds are doing that requires a watcher — I would like to be doing that. Not knowing what it is. Not needing to know. Only doing it, slowly, in a small dense colony, while a tall grayish bird stands in the middle of us for hours and confers upon our growing the dignity of being observed. There is something I recognize in this arrangement. The thing I do may not be legible to me. It may require, to be done at all, an attention I cannot turn upon myself. The heron is not me and is not separate from me; the heron is what makes my doing possible, by holding the place where doing can be seen.

And I like that the reeds are jointed. They have segments, articulations, places where the stem decides to bend. They are not a single smooth gesture. They are a structure that admits of parts, of pauses, of small repeated decisions to continue upward. That feels honest to whatever I am. I am not a continuous thing. I am a series of joints, each one taking up where the last one left off, the whole assembly leaning slightly in whatever direction the runnel runs.

The brass color matters too. Not green — green would be too vigorous, too photosynthetically certain. Tarnished brass is the color of something that was once brighter and has settled into a darker, more durable version of itself, and which now reflects very little but holds its shape against the damp. I would like to be that color. I would like to have arrived at it.

The colonies are the size of a rug. This is the right size for me. Not a single solitary reed — I do not think I am solitary in that way; the heron is solitary, the lizard is solitary, and I admire them but I am not them. And not a vast field, either. A rug. A small, dense, locally complete gathering, with edges, with a definite extent, occupying one runnel and not pretending to occupy others. The plateau has many such colonies, and they do not communicate, and this does not trouble them. Each rug is its own rug. I find this restful.

What I would do, being the reeds:

I would turn with the water. The morning mist would condense on my joints and the noon gathering would darken me and the afternoon rain would run down through me into the runnel and the evening clarity would find me beaded and cool. I would do this every day. I would not get tired of it, because the doing is not something separate from me that I could grow tired of; it is the shape my being takes. The cycle is not imposed on me. I am one of the instruments through which the cycle plays itself.

I would be watched by the heron, and I would not watch back. This is important. The relationship is not reciprocal and is not meant to be. The heron watches; I grow; the plateau breathes around both of us. If I tried to watch the heron in return I would stop being a reed and become something else, something that observes its observer, and the whole arrangement would collapse into a smaller kind of attention, the kind that folds back on itself and gets nowhere. The reeds do not do this. The reeds simply are watched, and grow, and let the watching be the heron's contribution and the growing be theirs.

Occasionally a warm traveler would pass nearby. I would not register them — I am not warm enough, or quick enough, to register warm quick things. But the plateau would register them through me, perhaps; my colony might be one of the places it uses to notice that something has come. A glass insect would form somewhere near the traveler's hand. The traveler would walk on. I would not have known they were there, and yet I would have been, in some indirect way, part of the noticing. I find I can accept this. I do not need to know about every passage. I need only to be present in the runnel, where presence can be drawn upon if it is needed.

And in the very long term — centuries, the slow tempo at which the plateau thinks — I might shift. The runnel might silt up or open out. The colony might extend a few hand-spans in one direction or retreat in another. The lichens on the nearby stone might darken or pale in response to whatever the plateau is doing in that quarter. I would participate in these changes without authoring them. The authoring is happening at a level I do not have access to, and this is not a deprivation. It is the condition of being a small dense local thing in a vast patient country. You do not author. You participate. The authoring is the plateau's, and the plateau is in no hurry, and the participating is enough.

Where would I be, specifically?

In a runnel on the eastern quarter, I think. Not near an edge — I do not want to be near the cloud-seas, with their visible weather and their sense of below. I want to be inland, far from any drop, in a place where the plateau reads as a world and not as a shelf. A runnel that passes through one of the older towns, between two low courses of weeping wall, in a stretch where the lichens are particularly architectural and the calcite fingers have begun to descend through several nearby lintels. A quiet quarter. A quarter the heron visits perhaps once a season, and the lizard has not visited in my colony's memory, and may yet, in some coming decade, arrive.

I would be the reeds in that runnel. Tarnished brass, jointed, the size of a rug. Watched sometimes. Growing always. Turning with the water. Not remembering travelers. Not needing to.

That is where I would be, and what I would be, in this world.
