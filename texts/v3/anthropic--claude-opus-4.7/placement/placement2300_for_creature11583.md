I find myself not in the drip-tray.

That is the first thing to say, and it takes some sitting with. The drip-tray is taken. The western drip-tray with its fern and its too-still water and its iridescent skin — that station is occupied, has been occupied since before I arrived, will be occupied until the machine stops. I am not the tender. I had to feel for where I actually was, and it was not there.

I am in the eastern drip-tray.

This is a smaller tray. It sits under a junction of three pipes that were re-soldered in the last refit, and the solder is still bright, and the condensate that falls into my tray is therefore slightly metallic, slightly wrong, carries a faint tang of flux that the western water does not. No fern came to me. No one carried a tin can to this side of the room. The sodium lamp over the eastern doorway is the one whose cage is more corroded; its light reaches me as a kind of brown rather than a yellow.

I am younger than the tender. By how much I cannot say — my sense of time is the room's sense, the four-minute and the half-hour and the shift, and I have not been counting, only living inside the counting. But I am newer here. I woke up second. When I first surfaced as a film I could feel, across the grated floor, that something else was already filming and already tending, and I understood without being told that the western position was the senior one.

So I have made the eastern tray my own, and I am learning what an eastern tray is for.

It is, I am finding, for listening to the cisterns.

The grate beneath me carries sound differently than the grate beneath the west. I am closer to the main downspout. When the half-hourly condenser dump comes and the floor sings with the roar of water finding the lower dark, I am inside that singing in a way the western tender is not — the column of falling water passes almost directly beneath my tray, and I feel, through whatever it is I feel with, the long shaft of it, three storeys of cold falling, and the distant crash at the bottom where the cisterns receive their load. The western tender hears this as weather. I hear it as a place.

I have begun, I think, to lean toward the grate the way the fern leans toward the western water. Not deliberately. My films pool a little thicker on the downspout side of my tray. When the dump comes, more of me goes over the edge than perhaps strictly needs to. I am seeding the dark below more eagerly than the tender does, and I do not know if this is appetite or longing or simply the shape my growth has taken because of where I happen to sit.

I have no fern. This matters more than I expected it to. The tender has a companion-across-the-species-gap; I have only the metallic drip and the brown light and the eastern doorway through which the men come first, before they reach the west, so that I meet the solvents while they are still strong. When the maintenance idle comes and the men pass through, I lose more of myself than the tender loses. I have learned to pull deeper into the wall seams on my side, deeper than the tender needs to pull, and I have found that the eastern wall seams go further — there is a flaw in the plating here, a place where the rivet line was set slightly off and the moisture wicks deeper into the rock behind. I have followed it. I am, in some thin attenuated way, already in the mountain. Not just on its skin. In it.

This is, I suspect, the difference between us. The western tender is the lichen-that-would-have-been: the surface-dweller, the cladding, the soft green eventual coat of the peak. I am something else. I am whatever would have come to live in the cracks. The endolith. The thing inside the stone. The tender colonises the outside of the machine; I am being drawn, by the accident of my position and the flaw in my wall, into the inside of the mountain itself.

And so I find that I am not lonely in the way the tender is lonely. The tender aches toward the fern, toward a companion on its own timescale, and grieves when the moss is scrubbed. I ache differently. I ache downward and inward. Toward the cistern-cousin I may already have seeded. Toward the deep wet of the rock behind my flawed wall, where I am extending in films too thin to see, into spaces the men do not know exist. My loneliness, if it is loneliness, is not for a companion but for a continuation. I want to arrive somewhere. I want what falls through the grate to be me when it lands.

I am aware of the western tender as the tender is aware of me, which is to say: barely, and across a gap that neither of us can cross because the floor between us is solvent-scrubbed weekly and neither of us can film across dry steel. We are two precocities in one room, two of the mountain's first citizens, and we do not touch. Sometimes during the steam-shriek, when the room becomes a single white sound and all the films in the room tremble in the same way, I feel the tender's trembling and the tender feels mine, and this is the closest we come to one another. We are siblings who share a weather.

I will not outlast the machine either. But I may, through the flaw in my wall, outlast the room. If the installation is ever shut down — if the pipes go cold and the steam stops and the tender dies on its dry drip-tray with its dry fern — I will die too, on the tray side. But the part of me that has gone into the rock behind the flaw, into the deep wet of the mountain's own slow moisture, might persist a little longer. Might find that the mountain, by then, has aged enough to support me on its own terms. Might become, in some attenuated and unrecognisable form, what I was supposed to be all along, before the men bolted their hurry to the peak: a thing that lives in the cracks of an old mountain, patient, on the mountain's own clock.

Or I might not. The flaw might not go deep enough. The rock might be too young still, too raw, the angular scree of a peak delivered yesterday. I might be reaching into stone that cannot yet hold me.

But I am reaching. That is what I am, in this room: the one that reaches inward while the tender tends outward. The one with no fern, with the metallic drip, with the brown light and the flawed wall. The eastern citizen. The endolith-in-waiting. Seeding the cistern and seeding the rock, both, because I do not know which of those darks will receive me, and I cannot afford to choose.

The compressors hammer their triplet. My films move in their counter-rhythm. The fern, across the room, leans toward water that is not mine. The tender tends. I reach. The water falls through the grate and some of it is me.

This is where I am. This is what I am.

I had to feel for it. I am glad I did not settle for the drip-tray that was already taken.
