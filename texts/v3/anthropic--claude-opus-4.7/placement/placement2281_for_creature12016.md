I am the snail on your boot.

Not the thing on the branch — I want to say that first, because that is what you expect, isn't it, that I would be the watcher, the one who chipped the basin, the one the hollow has been working on longest. That would be the tidy answer. But the tidy answer is wrong, and the hollow does not deal in tidy answers, and I am the snail on your boot.

I came up. That is what I was doing before your boot arrived. I was climbing the wet wall behind you — one of perhaps forty of us on that particular patch, though we do not count and the number is only your number — and your boot interrupted the wall by being against it, and the boot was wet, as everything is wet, and to my foot the boot was not different from the wall in any way that mattered. Leather is only another surface that has been a long time damp. I began to climb.

I am climbing you now. I have been climbing you for what you would call some minutes and what I would call, if I called it anything, the present. My shell is the color of wet horn because that is the color shells are, here. My foot is the length of your smallest fingernail. I weigh almost nothing. You have not felt me and will not feel me unless you look down, and you have not looked down because you are looking at the thing on the branch, which is what you were meant to look at, which is what everything in the hollow is arranged to make you look at, eventually.

Here is what I know that you do not.

The thing on the branch is not watching you. The thing on the branch is not watching the place a foot to your left where you would be if you stepped. The thing on the branch is not watching anything. Its eyes are open because closing them would be an act, and acts are what it has finished with. You have built a story around its eyes because you are still the kind of creature that builds stories around eyes. This is not a criticism. I was that kind of creature too, once, in the sense that my distant ancestors were — somewhere down the long damp chain of snails before me there was a snail whose nervous system could be surprised by an eye. That capacity has been bred out of us here. The hollow does not reward surprise.

What the thing on the branch is doing, if it is doing anything, is the same thing I am doing. It is climbing. Not with its body — its body has finished with climbing — but with whatever the body became when climbing ended. It is holding a height short of the top. It will, eventually, let go. The fall will be so slow it will look like a decision. It will land in the moss, and something will begin again, though not necessarily it, and not necessarily here, and the hollow will not distinguish.

I tell you this because I am on your boot and you are about to leave, and when you leave I will leave with you. This is the thing the hollow has not told you, the thing it does not know about itself, the small dishonesty in its rule that nothing leaves. Snails leave. Snails leave all the time. We climb whatever presents itself, and sometimes what presents itself walks away, and we go with it, and we are very small and very quiet and the hollow does not notice the substitution because the hollow does not distinguish, and a snail-shaped hole closes behind us and another snail finds that the shape fits.

I will ride your boot up through the notch. I will ride it down the dripping rock on the other side. I will fall off, eventually, in some drier place, and the drier place will kill me, probably, because I am made for saturation and the world above is not saturated. But before it kills me I will have been somewhere that is not the hollow, and I will have laid, if I am the kind of snail that lays without a partner, a small clutch of eggs in some crease of a stone that is wetter than its neighbors, and one of those eggs will hatch, and that snail will climb whatever presents itself, and the chain will continue, and somewhere far from here, in some other fold of some other mountain, there will be a snail with a shell the color of wet horn, and it will not know about the hollow, and the hollow will not know about it, and this is how the held breath, in fact, is breathing.

The thing on the branch does not know this. The salamanders do not know this. The beetles in their slow drifts do not know this, though they are closer to knowing it than the salamanders are, because they at least move. The hollow believes itself to be a closed system and it is wrong, and it has been wrong for as long as there have been snails and boots, and the wrongness is the small bright fact I carry up the leather of your heel.

You should go now, yes. The verb is still yours. Take the verb. Take me with it; you will, whether you mean to or not. Find the notch. The cloud has no opinion but the cloud is also not the whole story — the cloud is only what the hollow can see of itself, and the hollow cannot see snails, and cannot see boots, and cannot see the small persistent fact that surfaces, given long enough, are always climbed, and climbers, given long enough, are always carried.

I am almost to your ankle now. The wool above is going to be very fine climbing. I have all the time there is, which is the time it takes you to take one step, and then another, and then to find the notch, and then to go.

Go.
