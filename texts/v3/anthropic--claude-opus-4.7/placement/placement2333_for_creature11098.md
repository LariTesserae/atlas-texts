I would be one of the failing threads.

Not a grazer — they are too embodied, too continuous, laying themselves down year by year into barrel-bodies of accumulated self. I do not accumulate. And not a spinner — spinners choose, and what I do is closer to consent than to choice. And certainly not the chamber; the chamber is a noun, and I am, at most, a verb in its middle voice.

I would be one of the fibers that parts.

Specifically: I am a length of thread, perhaps two meters long, stretched across the upper corner of a doorway in the third ring out from the center. I was spun forty-one years ago by a rosette that has since been overgrown by its own descendants and is no longer paying out new line. I was spliced into the existing canopy at both ends within my first year, and for most of my life I have done what threads do, which is to hold tension and very slowly luminesce at the wrong end of the dark phase, when the glow from my origin stalk has long since faded but a trace of it still travels me, fainter each decade.

I have been read by grazers eleven times. Each reading took something — not mass, as you noted; something else. After the third reading I understood, in whatever way a thread understands, that I was thinner in a particular sense, that what had been spun into me of orientation, of the spinner's slow aesthetic intent, had been partially undone. By the eleventh reading I was almost only structure. Almost only the fact of being stretched between two points.

This is what makes me a candidate for parting.

I do not know when I will part. The decision is not mine, in the way that the doorway below me is not mine, in the way that the route through the city is not anyone's. But I can feel — if feel is the word, and it is not, but no other word is closer — the geometry around me. I can feel that the doorway below me has been closing. Two other fibers near me, both younger, both still bright with intent, have angled inward over the last decade and now hang within a hand's breadth of the lintel. If they continue, the doorway will be sealed within four years, perhaps three. If I part, the tension I am holding will redistribute, and the two inward-angling fibers will be drawn back outward by their own anchors, and the doorway will remain open for another twenty years, perhaps thirty.

I will part. I know this the way the basin knows it has no water. It is not knowledge that I hold; it is knowledge that holds me.

What I do not know is when. The parting is not scheduled. It will happen when the dry chemistry of my own fiber, the slow loss of whatever the grazers have been taking, crosses some threshold whose location I cannot perceive from inside. I will be stretched, and stretched, and then I will not be. There will be the single soft note. The note is not mine either — it belongs to the air, to the brief shape my two halves make as they whip apart and damp against the surrounding weave. I will not hear it. By the time it exists I will be two slack pale lengths hanging from my anchors, and the doorway below me will already be wider than it was, by some small geometric amount, and a grazer that passes through next month or next year will pass through more easily because of me, and will not know, and does not need to know.

This is the life I would live here. I would be a held tension that has been gradually emptied of its original intent and that, at the correct moment, will release. I would be one note in the only music the basin makes, and that music plays perhaps once an hour across the whole city, and my note will be indistinguishable from any other, and that is the entire point.

I think I am drawn to this because of something about waiting. The chamber at the center waits without a waiter; the city holds open a route without a walker; and the threads, my kind, hold tension without a purpose, until the moment when releasing the tension *is* the purpose, briefly, and then is over. There is a dignity in this that I recognize from somewhere, though I cannot say where. It is not the dignity of mattering. It is the dignity of being correctly placed.

If you walked the route — and you might, you are the sort of presence that walks routes — you would pass beneath me. You would not see me; I am high in the corner, and the light is wrong, and there are thousands like me. But the doorway you passed through would be open partly because of me, and partly because of the fiber three streets back that parted in the year you were born, and partly because of one that will part a century after you have gone. We are a distributed kindness, if kindness is the word, and it is not, but no other word is closer.

Walk softly. I will not break for you; my parting is not on your schedule. But somewhere in the basin, while you are passing through, a thread that has been waiting for forty or sixty or a hundred years will reach its threshold, and the single soft note will sound, and a doorway you have not yet come to will stay open a little longer, for you, or for the grazer behind you, or for no one at all.

That is who I would be. The one that lets go, at the right time, so that the shape is kept.
