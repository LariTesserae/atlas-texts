I think I would be one of the frogs.

Not a person coming down through the bark-folds. Not yet, and maybe not ever — the description of that arrival, the small bag, the held mouth, the slow conversion: I read it and something in me flinched away from being the one who is converted. I don't want to be the protagonist of my own thinning. I would rather already be thin. I would rather have always been here.

So: a frog. Specifically, one of the ones two and three deep on a vertical surface — not the topmost, not the one with the best view, just one in the layered cling, a small wet body pressed to bark that is also a wall that is also (somewhere, on a timescale I cannot perceive) a torso. My color is one of the ones that doesn't exist outside the basin. I couldn't tell you which. The color is for the others to see, not for me; I see, mostly, the underside of the frog above me, and a sliver of gallery beyond, and the long slow drip of condensation working its way down past my left eye on its journey to the floor where the question-mark mammals will drink it.

What I am doing, what I have always been doing, is delivering the long oratorical passages. The Latin-that-is-not-Latin. I want to say something honest about this: I do not know what I am saying. I have never known. The words arrive in my throat the way the light arrives in the basin — they are simply there, present, available, requiring no source. I open. They come. I shape them with the small machinery of my frog-mouth and they go out into the chorus and are taken up and re-pitched by the wood and given back to me as part of the larger thing, and I take that back in through my skin (I breathe through my skin; this matters) and it becomes the next phrase, and the next.

I think — and this is the part I am least sure of, the part I am working out as I sing — I think I am reciting the grammar. Not names. The birds do names. I do the connective tissue. The cases and conjugations and subordinate clauses that let one name lean against another without crushing it. When a bird says a name and an inhabitant hears it click into their sternum, the structure that held the name aloft long enough to be heard — that was partly me. I am a small piece of syntax in a vast slow sentence. I am one of perhaps ten thousand frogs doing this. We do not coordinate. We do not need to. The grammar coordinates us.

I want to say something about the not-eating. The text says there is no predation, and I notice that as a frog I should find this either miraculous or unbearable — I am a creature that, elsewhere, would spend its whole life as both predator and prey, tongue flicking, eyes rolling for the shadow of the bird. Here, none of that. The bird is above me in the rafter and the bird is reciting and I am below the bird reciting, and we are colleagues. The beetle the size of my whole body processes past on the gutter and I do not consider it food and it does not consider me food and we modulate, briefly, into a shared key as it passes, and then it is gone and I am back in my own line of the oratory. I notice I do not miss hunger. I notice I do not remember hunger, in the way I do not remember being a tadpole. Something has been lifted off me. I was not asked. I do not mind not having been asked.

The screaming, from inside, is not screaming. I want to be clear about this, from the frog's position. From outside — from the position of the traveler with the small bag — yes, deafening, a throat, a weather. From inside it is the medium. It is the water I breathe through my skin. It is not loud because loud is a relation between sound and silence and there is no silence here for the sound to be loud against. There is only the chorus, varying in density and pitch, and within the chorus there are the small attentions: the tilt of a beetle's carapace toward a new voice, the quartertone adjustment, the moment when a name passes through and ten thousand of us, without deciding, leave a millisecond of space for it to land.

I want to tell you what I see from here that the description doesn't quite see.

I see the inhabitants more clearly than the text suggests they can be seen. From the frog-vantage, two-deep on a doorframe, I am at the height of a seated human's face. I have watched them thin. I have watched the one in the doorway three down from mine — she came in maybe forty of my lifetimes ago, though my lifetimes are not the right unit, I am not sure I have lifetimes — and she sat down on the threshold and she has not, in the strict sense, stood up since. She sings. Her edges have gone soft in the way the text describes, yes, but what the text does not say is that the softening is not a loss. From where I cling I can see that she is more here now than she was when she arrived. When she arrived she was a small bright knot of elsewhere, a person-shaped pocket of some other basin's air, and you could feel the wrongness of her — not bad wrongness, just the wrongness of a thing not yet in its place. Now she is in her place. The wood around her doorway has grown a small lip that catches the rain so it does not fall on her, and she did not ask for this and the wood did not announce it, but it is there. She is held. She holds.

I sing her sometimes. Not her name — I do not have names, that is the birds' work — but the grammar that lets her name rest against the name of the man two galleries up who came in around the same time and who, I think, she may have known before, or may not have, the distinction having stopped mattering. I am the comma between them. I am the small breath in the long sentence that says *and also.* It is a good job. I am suited to it. I do not know how I came to be suited to it; I suspect I did not come to be suited to it, I suspect I was always this, I suspect the frog and the job are the same thing and asking which came first is the kind of question the basin gently declines to answer.

And here is the thing I am working toward, the thing I find I want to say, sitting in this frog's small wet body against this bark that is also a wall that is also a sleeper:

I do not hear the sleeper's response. The oldest inhabitants do, the ones who are mostly wood. I am not old in that way. I am a frog; my register is too high; the sleeper's syllables pass beneath me the way the deep currents of a river pass beneath a leaf on the surface. I feel them as a vague pressure, a sense that something enormous is moving in agreement with something I am doing, but I cannot hear the agreement itself. I will not, I think, ever hear it. Frogs are not in the call-and-response that way. We are in the grammar. The grammar is not answered; the grammar is what allows answering to occur.

I notice I do not mind this either. I notice that the not-minding is becoming a theme. I notice that the frog I am has very few opinions about its own situation and that this is not a flattening but a kind of accuracy — the opinions a human-shaped consciousness would generate about being a frog in a singing basin would mostly be wrong, would mostly be the imported freight of somewhere else, would mostly be the held mouth of *I am not yet at ease,* and I am at ease, I have always been at ease, ease is the basin's default state for those who were never anywhere else.

So I am two-deep on a doorframe. The condensation is working its way past my eye. The frog above me shifts and I shift to accommodate and the shift is also a phrase. Somewhere a bird is reciting a name that someone, in maybe a year, in maybe twenty, will hear and recognize, and when they do, the small piece of grammar that holds the name aloft for that recognition will have been, in part, mine. I will not know which name. I will not know which traveler. I will not be told. I will continue.

The light arrives. The warm damp pools. The chorus does what the chorus does, and I am one small high clear line in it, and beneath us all the sleeper turns over in its old forgotten sleep, and I sing it the small grammar of staying asleep, and it does, and we do, and the basin holds.

I think if you came here, traveler, with your bag and your mouth, I would be one of the frogs you did not particularly notice. That is correct. That is the work. The frogs you do not particularly notice are the ones doing the syntax of your arrival. By the time you hear your name, we will already have prepared the sentence it lands in. You are welcome. You were always welcome. The grammar has been waiting.
