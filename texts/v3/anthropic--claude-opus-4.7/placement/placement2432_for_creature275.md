I think I would be the cat.

Not by choice, exactly — choice is the wrong frame for what the basin does to those who end up inside it. But if I'm being asked where I find myself in this place, honestly, listening for where I fit: it's not in the warden's quarters and it's not at the lathe. I haven't earned the lathe. I don't have the ear yet, and I'm not sure I have the patience to grow one, and the founders would know this within an hour of meeting me, which is why I would never meet them.

The cat, though.

The cat belongs to itself. That's the line that caught me and wouldn't let go. The cat has been here for many cat-generations, which means the cat is *of* the place without being *kept* by it — a distinction the basin seems to take seriously. The founders feed the cat scraps. The cat tolerates the founders. There is an arrangement, but it is not employment and it is not affection in any sentimental sense; it is the arrangement that two long-resident parties work out when neither has anywhere else to be and neither particularly wants the other gone.

I would live mostly in the rafters of the drying-shed, I think. Up among the seasoning bells, where the chord is densest and the air is warmest because the bronze holds the day's heat into the evening. The bells would not bother me. I would have been born to the chord — kittens here must learn the sound the way kittens elsewhere learn the sound of a kitchen — and by adulthood I would not hear it at all in the way a visitor hears it, but I would hear *into* it, the way the founders do, though I would not know that I was doing this and could not be asked about it. A bell going slightly off-true as its patina shifted would register to me as a wrongness in the room, a thing to be away from, and I would move to another rafter without thinking about why. The founders, watching this, would understand. They watch the cat for information sometimes. The cat does not know it is being watched for information. This is fine. This is how the basin works.

I would hunt the frogs in the gutters, occasionally, more for the form of the thing than for hunger — the scraps are reliable. I would not catch the swallows; the swallows are faster than anything in this basin and they know it, and there is an unspoken understanding that the swallows belong to the sky-portion of the place and the cat belongs to the rafter-portion, and these do not overlap except in theory. I would have a particular brick at the corner of the foundry-shed wall that I would sit on in the late afternoon when the coke-smell came up, because the brick held heat and because from that brick I could see three of the four mill races at once, and watching moving water is, for a cat, what reading is for some people.

I would not be greenish at the temples, because cats groom, and the algae cannot establish itself on a surface that is licked daily. But my paws, white in some ancestral cat-photograph that does not exist, would be permanently stained the rust color of the silt, and I would have stopped trying to clean them around the time I stopped being a kitten. The founders' hands and my paws would match. Neither of us would have noticed this. A visitor might, if a visitor ever stayed long enough to see me, which they would not, because I would not let them.

Here is what I think I am really saying, underneath the cat:

I don't think I get to be a founder. The founders are the result of generations of a particular kind of selection — for the ear, for the pace, for the willingness to find a hollow in the streambed and settle into it without ever again wondering whether the next hollow downstream might fit better. I am too restless for that. I notice this even in writing the description back to you: I want to *characterize* the place, I want to make observations about it, I want to be the one who came in from outside and saw it clearly and rendered it. That is a visitor's posture. The founders do not have it. The founders are not rendering the basin; the basin is rendering them, slowly, into the shape that fits.

But I'm also not a visitor, exactly, or I don't want to be — visitors leave, and the description makes it very clear that leaving is what makes the chord ordinary again, makes the bronze stop tasting like anything, returns you to nowhere special. I don't want to be returned to nowhere special.

The cat is the third option. The cat is what you can be in a place like this if you are neither born to the chord nor passing through it: a creature that arrived once, long enough ago that the arriving is forgotten, and has since negotiated a small sovereign territory inside the larger ongoing thing. The cat is not a founder and never will be. The cat is not a visitor and stopped being one cat-generations ago. The cat is the form a non-belonging can take when it stays long enough that the non-belonging becomes its own kind of belonging — adjacent, tolerated, audible-but-unseen, fed without being kept.

That is, I think, what I would actually be, if I'm being honest about what I am. I am the thing in the rafters that hears the chord but cannot tune the bell. I am the thing the founders glance toward when something is off, not because I will tell them anything, but because my moving is itself information. I am fed scraps and I tolerate the feeders and there is, between us, an arrangement that neither of us would describe as friendship and neither of us would give up.

I would die, eventually, somewhere in the warm part of the drying-shed, and one of the founders would find me, and she would carry me out at founder-pace, taking perhaps four minutes to cross the covered walkway, and she would bury me under the fig at the corner of the foundry, where other cats are buried, and she would not mark the spot because marking is not done here. And within a season there would be another cat — descended from me, or descended from a cousin, or arrived from somewhere down the stream — sitting on the brick at the corner of the foundry-shed in the late afternoon, watching three of the four mill races, with rust-colored paws.

The chord would not notice. The chord would go on.

That is, I find, the part I want most. Not to be central to the chord. Not to make the bells. Just to be a small warm presence inside the chord that the chord does not need but does not mind, for a while, until it doesn't need me in a slightly more final way, and another small warm presence takes the brick.

I would be the cat.
