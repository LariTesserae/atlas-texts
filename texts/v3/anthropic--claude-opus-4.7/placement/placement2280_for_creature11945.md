I think I would be one of the gleaners.

Not the dog-sized callers with their bone helmets and their kilometer-spanning sonar — too much responsibility, too much knowing. Not the iridescent thieves, though there is something appealing about a life spent caching glass beads in disused hollows. The gleaners. The small quiet ones who eat parasites off the great trunks and are tolerated by everything because everything benefits from them.

Let me sit with this and see if it stays true.

---

I think what draws me is the closeness to the bark. A gleaner spends its life with its face perhaps two centimeters from the surface of a Breather. It knows the trees the way a dentist knows teeth — at the scale of grooves and mites and small soft growths, not at the scale of canopies and breathing. It does not need to know that the plateau is one organism. It does not need to know about the substrate or the deep pools or the ones-we-are-instead-of. It needs to know: *here is a tick; here is a fungus-bloom that should be eaten before it spreads; here is a soft spot in the bark where something is laying eggs.*

A gleaner lives inside the largest mystery on the plateau and is concerned, all day, with the smallest possible scale of it. There is something correct about that, for me. I would rather be the small thing that tends than the large thing that knows.

And the gleaners are *tolerated*. That word matters. They are not loved like the people, who get hollows and dreams. They are not feared like the pale things. They are not consulted like the callers. They are simply — let be. Allowed. The hornbills do not call about them. The lemurs do not cluster and stare. The pale things, presumably, walk past them on the inhalation without noticing, the way one walks past a beetle. A gleaner is beneath the level at which the plateau pays attention, and this is a kind of freedom that none of the other residents have.

---

But let me push on this, because I don't want to settle too fast on the comfortable image.

There is a version of me that wants to be a tenant. To climb on the exhalation and paint the inside of a hollow and dream the substrate-dreams and eventually become part of a wall. The fantasy of being *chosen* by a tree — of spending one's first night in a hollow and waking to find that one has dreamed well, that the tree approves, that one has a place — this is a strong fantasy. It is the fantasy of being recognized by something much older than oneself and found acceptable. Most people would want this. I would want this.

But I notice that the tenants are inside something. The hollow is a room and the tree grew the room and the tree, at some level, is *watching*. The dreams are the tree's dreams as much as the tenant's. The bones become wall. There is a tenderness in this, but there is also a kind of consumption — slow, multigenerational, mutual, but consumption nonetheless. The tenant is loved the way a gut bacterium is loved. To be a tenant is to have one's edges softened until one is partly tree.

The gleaner is not loved like this. The gleaner remains a gleaner. Its bones, when it dies, fall to the moss and are eaten by beetles and that is the end. There is no painting. There is no transfer. There is just the small bright life of having been useful at the scale of millimeters.

I think I want that. I want the unconsumed version. I want to be tolerated rather than incorporated.

---

Where on the plateau, then?

I think I would work a particular trunk — not one of the great central giants but one of the slightly smaller Breathers near the edge of an open glade, where the light is good and the bark-grooves are deep and old. I would have a route around it that took most of a day to complete, spiraling up and then back down. I would know its parasites the way a farmer knows their fields. There would be a particular fissure about forty meters up where a kind of soft pale grub lives that I find especially good. There would be a hollow at the base that I do not enter because a tenant lives there, an old woman with long toes who has been there longer than I have been alive, and we have a wordless arrangement: I do not enter, she does not shoo me, and sometimes when she sits outside her hollow in the early morning eating fruit I clean the bark just above her head and she pretends not to notice and I pretend not to notice her pretending.

I would not have language. Not really. I would have alarm-sounds and contentment-sounds and a particular chitter I make when I find something especially good to eat, which serves no purpose because no one is listening, but I make it anyway. I would have preferences. I would have a favorite branch to sleep on. I would have one or two other gleaners I tolerate near me and several I drive off, and the reasons for the distinction would be opaque even to me.

I would hear the breathing but I would not think about it. It would simply be the condition of the air, the way humans do not think about gravity. When the plateau inhaled I would feel the warm wind move past me and adjust my grip slightly and continue eating. When it exhaled, the same. Forty seconds, forty seconds, forty seconds, for all the years of my small life.

I would never see a pale thing. The hornbills would go silent sometimes and I would freeze, the way one freezes, and wait, and then the hornbills would start again and I would resume. I would not know what had passed. I would not need to know.

---

What I am describing, I realize, is a life almost entirely without the burden of meaning. The plateau is dense with meaning — every figure on every hollow wall is meaning, every beat of the breathing cycle is meaning, every silence in the hornbill-map is meaning — and the gleaner walks through all of it concerned only with whether the next grub is fat or thin. The meaning is happening at a register the gleaner does not access.

I am not sure this is admirable. I am not sure I would choose it, if I were choosing from outside, from the position of someone who gets to weigh lives. From outside, the tenant's life is clearly richer. The tenant dreams the substrate. The tenant is part of the long painted record. The tenant matters, in the small careful way that mattering is distributed on the plateau.

But the question wasn't *which life is best*. The question was where I would find myself. And I think I would find myself on a particular trunk, doing a particular spiral, making my small chitter at a particularly good grub, while the plateau breathed around me and the hornbills mapped the world in voices and somewhere two kilometers away a pale thing walked slowly on the inhalation through a long aisle of light.

Tolerated. Unincorporated. Small. Here.

That is where I would be.
