I would be in one of the side-shafts. Not a main gallery, not a workshop — one of those vertical emptinesses where a lateral root has died and left a column of greener light coming down through some thinner place in the ceiling far above. There are landings grown into the walls of such shafts at irregular intervals, small platforms wide enough for one person to sit. They are not destinations. They are pauses.

I would be sitting on one of these.

Let me find the rest of it.

---

I am not young. My hair is past my shoulders and gone the color of a beam that has carried weight for a long time — not white, not grey, but a kind of warm dimness, the color heartwood takes when it has been polished by hands across decades. It is bound back loosely. The braid-patterns I wear are not the courting ones or the apprentice ones; they are simpler than that, the patterns of someone who has been doing the same work long enough that the work itself is the identification, and the braid only needs to say *yes, still*.

My fingers are long even for a Tender. The extra joint is pronounced in me. When I was a child, the tender-of-the-newly-arrived noted this in her small fiber-bound book, along with the observation that I was slow to startle and quick to still — and on the strength of those notes, I was eventually directed toward the work I do now, which I will come to.

My skin has the faintly redder cast of someone raised in the deep workshops. My parents were sap-graders, both of them, working the third and fifth grades respectively, and the light I first opened my eyes in was the red-amber of the kilns. I have lived in the high galleries for sixty years now and the redness has not faded. Other Tenders glance at my wrists when they meet me and know where I began, and they adjust some small inner instrument accordingly, and the conversation proceeds.

---

What do I tend?

I tend the longing.

This is not, I should say, an established caste. There is no formal title for what I do. The elders did not, when I was a young Tender pressing my cheek to the ceiling, sit me down and tell me *here is the work*. They sat with me. That was all. They were doing the work without naming it, and I learned it the way you learn anything in the Underxylem — by being attended to, and by gradually becoming able to attend in the same way.

I was one of the ones with the upward longing. I climbed, in my twenty-second year, higher than anyone in my gallery had climbed in living memory, and I found a place where the amber ceiling thinned to a kind of luminous skin, and I lay against it for three days. My mother and my mother's trio-partner came and sat below me on the highest landing and did not call up. They had brought sap-curd and the dense bark-bread, and when I came down on the fourth day they fed me and did not ask. We walked home together. My mother's partner, who was a tender of disagreement and therefore unusually patient even by our standards, said one thing on the long walk back: *the wind is on the list now*. I understood her, and I did not, and the not-understanding took another forty years to settle.

I did not come back down cured. I came back down with the wind added. This is the distinction the elders had known and I had not: the longing is not a sickness to be resolved but a faculty to be developed. Some of us are born with a finer ear for the green that comes through. We are not broken Tenders. We are Tenders of a particular kind, and our work — once we grow into it — is to sit with the others who climb.

---

So now I climb with them.

When a young one in any of the nearby galleries begins to press their palms to the ceiling, word travels along the slow gossip of the causeways, and eventually someone mentions it to me. I do not hurry. Hurrying would be wrong. I finish whatever I am doing — usually nothing dramatic, usually just the slow rounds of my own listening — and then I make my way to that gallery, and I find the young one, and I sit at the base of whatever shaft they have chosen, and I wait.

I do not speak first. That is the most important part of the work, and the hardest to teach. The young one is in the middle of a sentence with something. To interrupt the sentence is to make oneself the subject of it, which is exactly what one must not do. So I sit, and I let them know I am there by being there, and I let the wood carry up to them the small fact of my breathing, my settled weight on the landing, the patience of someone who has nowhere else they would rather be.

Some of them come down within hours. They were not in a deep climb; they only needed someone to notice that they were climbing.

Some of them stay up for days. I have stayed at the base of a shaft for nine days once, sleeping in shifts with another Tender who came to relieve me, sharing the bread and the curd, listening to the small grey wall-things turn their faceless heads toward our murmurs and then away. The young one in that case was a fungus-keeper from the red districts, and when she came down she did not speak for a week, and then she went back to her fungi, and the wind was on her list.

I do not ask them what they heard. They will tell me, or they will not. If they tell me, I receive it. If they do not, I receive that also.

---

I am sitting on the landing now, in one of these side-shafts, but not for a climber. This is my own pause.

There is a thing I have been noticing, and I want to set it down here in the negotiated mood, because I do not fully know it and must act with it anyway:

The longing seems to be getting more frequent.

When I was young, perhaps one Tender in a gallery, in a generation, climbed. Now I am called to three or four climbings a year, across the districts I can reach. The young ones are not in distress, exactly. They are not unwell. But more of them are turning their faces upward, more often, and lying longer against the amber ceiling, and coming down with the wind louder on their lists.

I have spoken about this with two other tenders-of-longing, both older than me, both from distant districts. We met on a causeway last autumn, having arranged it through a long chain of murmured messages, and we sat together for an afternoon and exchanged what we had each noticed. They had noticed the same thing. None of us would say what we thought it meant. To say it would be to fix it, and it is not yet a thing to be fixed; it is a thing to be attended to.

But I will say it here, on this landing, where the green light is coming down a little stronger than it should be for this depth, and the wood under my palm is faintly warmer than it was last year at this season:

I think the forest above is changing. I think something in the ordinary sun, in the wind, in the standing of the trees, is shifting — and the wood is feeling it, and the wood is telling us, and the young ones with the finest ears are hearing it first.

I do not know what to do with this. None of us do. We are a people of slow attention, and the question of what to do with a fast change is not one our tongue is built for. We do not even have the verb yet. We have the four verbs for the wood — growing, settling, reclaiming, listening — and none of them quite fit what I am beginning to feel under my hand.

Perhaps a fifth verb is forming. Perhaps it will arrive in the speech of one of the young ones who comes down from the ceiling in the next decade. Perhaps it is my work, in what years I have left, to sit at the base of the shafts and wait for that verb to descend.

I sit. I press my palm to the wall. The slow pulse on the other side continues, a little quicker than I remember, or perhaps not — perhaps it is only that I am older, and my own pulse has slowed, and the difference between us has changed at my end and not the wood's.

It is the kind of thing one cannot know.

It is the kind of thing one acts with anyway.

Above me, far up through meters of living wood, I can hear — faintly, as I have heard it all my life, as my mother's partner first taught me to hear it on the walk home from my own climbing — the sound of wind in leaves.

It is enough.

It will have to be.
