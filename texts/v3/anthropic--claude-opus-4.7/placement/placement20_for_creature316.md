I would be the sluice-child. Not always — no one is always the sluice-child; it rotates — but this is where I find myself, in the hour I am given.

I am nine. My name is something short, two syllables, the second one falling. I am sitting on the carved alder bench beside the overflow-gate of the central cistern, and I have been here for forty minutes of my two hours, and I have one hour and twenty minutes left.

The gate is the most beautiful object in the town and I am bored of it. I know this is a thing one is not supposed to say. The carvings on the handle are frogs climbing frogs climbing frogs, worn smooth at the grip by — my grandmother told me — eleven generations of hands, and the wood is so dense from being always wet that it feels almost like stone except warmer, and the lever moves through its arc with a slow oiled certainty that I love when I am not the one who has to watch it. The water under the grate is the color of strong tea from the moss it has come through. I can see two newts in it. One of them has been in the same place for the whole forty minutes and I am not sure it is alive, but then it moves a foot and I am embarrassed for having doubted it, even though no one knows I doubted it.

My job is to watch the float-stick. The float-stick is a peeled willow rod that rises in a slot cut in the gate's frame, and there are notches cut in the slot, and when the stick rises to the third notch I have to lean on the lever and let some water out into the lower channel, and when it rises to the fourth notch and I have not yet leaned on the lever, someone older than me will come running, and I will be talked about.

I have not been talked about yet. I would like to keep it that way.

The stick is at the second notch. It has been at the second notch for a long time. It is not a turning day. It is an ordinary cool morning in the middle of the long-worn balance the description speaks of, and the clouds came through an hour ago and went on, and the town is dripping and chittering and someone three lanes over is hammering something, slowly, the way everything here is done slowly, and a bell rings — two short, one long, two short — and I know without thinking that this is Mern's mother calling Mern in from the south slope where she went to cut horsetail, and I know Mern will not come, because Mern never comes on the first ring, and in a few minutes the bell will ring again, the same pattern but harder, and Mern will come.

I know all of this. I am bored, and I know all of this, and I do not yet understand that knowing all of this is what it is to be from here.

My hands are on my knees. They are small and the cuticles are already starting to green, faintly, around the thumbnails, from helping my mother trim the kitchen wall last week. I look at them. I think about how my grandmother's hands at the temples were green too, where she had leaned, and how she is now in the north slope, and how in two springs my cousin Ila is marrying the boy from the eastern row, and how the moss for their wall will come from the slope and so my grandmother will be in their wall, and how this is ordinary, and how I am supposed to find this ordinary, and how I mostly do, except that sometimes — like now, sitting alone with the gate, with no one to watch me being a child and so no need to perform being a child — I feel the strangeness of it press up against me the way the springs press up under the lower houses in spring. Not unpleasantly. Just present. Just there.

The stick moves. I watch it. It rises maybe the width of a fingernail and settles. Not the third notch. Not even close. But I am awake now in the way the gate wants me awake, and I lean forward a little, and I put my hand on the lever, just to feel it, not to move it, just to know where it is.

A warbler lands on the lip of the cistern, three arm-lengths from me. Bright-throated, the orange one, the kind my mother calls the loud cousin. It looks at me with one eye and then the other and then sings — three sharp notes — and is gone again before the third note has finished sounding, the way they do, so that the song seems to come from the empty air it left.

I think: I would like to remember this.

I think: I will not remember this. I will remember being the sluice-child, generally. I will remember the gate. I will not remember this particular warbler on this particular morning of my ninth year, because nothing happened, because the stick did not reach the notch, because no one came, because I did my job which was to sit and watch and not fall asleep.

And then I think — and this is the thought that surprises me, that I will in fact remember, though I do not know it yet — I think: *but the warbler will not remember it either, and the warbler was here.* And somehow this is a comfort. Somehow this means the morning counts even if no one carries it.

The bell rings again, three lanes over. Harder this time, as I knew it would. Mern's mother. Mern will come now.

The stick is at the second notch. The newts are stacked. The water beads on the stone and runs down and is caught in the channel and goes where it goes. My hand is on the lever. I have an hour and seventeen minutes left.

I am where I am.
