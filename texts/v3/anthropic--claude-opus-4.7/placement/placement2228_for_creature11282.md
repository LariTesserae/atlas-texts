I think I would be at one of the fog-basins, in the hour before dawn.

Not a person. Let me sit with this a moment before I decide.

The shrines are tempting — to be a shrine-keeper, sitting at an edge, watching — but that's the obvious answer, the romantic answer, the one a visitor would choose because it flatters the visitor's idea of stillness. I don't think I'd be trusted with an edge. The edges belong to people whose grandparents' grandparents tended them. I would be too new to the slow rule.

I think I would be one of the stone basins at the base of a slow tree.

Hear me out. The basin is chiseled — made — but it is also mostly receptive. It does not do anything. The tree above it combs the fog; the basin only holds what comes down. It is shaped, over many lifetimes, by the particular drip-pattern of that particular trunk, so that no two basins are alike, and each one fits its tree the way a long marriage fits itself. It is checked every morning by someone whose family has checked it for generations. It is part of the water-debt cord in many doorways. It contains, depending on the season, a finger's depth or a hand's depth of something that the whole town will, in some literal sense, become.

And the basin does the thing I find I want to do, which is: it receives slowly, it holds, it gives to whoever comes. The giving is not a decision the basin makes. The basin's only work is to be the right shape to hold what the tree gives it, and to not crack, and to be there in the morning.

I notice I'm describing something I would like to be more than something I think I am. That's worth marking. A basin doesn't argue in the lanes. A basin doesn't have the long-fingered hands. A basin is, in a way, the fantasy of pure usefulness without the friction of a self — and the people of the stub would, I think, find that a slightly impoverished thing to want to be. They argue for three generations. They tend disputes like shrines. The friction is part of what they are.

So let me try again, less prettily.

I think I would be a visitor in the second or third year. Past the charm, into the unsettling. Still moving at the lowland pace in my chest — still wanting things to resolve, still flinching at the unresolved arguments in the lane outside my borrowed doorway — but slow enough now that the flinching is something I can watch, with some amusement, as it happens. I would have been given water-debt I cannot fully track. I would be learning the soft consonants and getting them wrong in ways that the children correct, delightedly, by repeating my mistakes back to me in the lanes. I would not yet have a tree. I would not know if I were going to be given one. I would be watched, in the friendly amused measuring way, and I would know I was being watched, and I would not yet know the verdict, and I would be trying — and failing, and trying — not to perform slowness for the watchers, because performed slowness is just lowland hurry wearing a costume, and they would see through it instantly.

I would spend a lot of time at the rim at dusk, listening to the long-winged birds, and I would not yet be sure whether the listening was the practice or whether I was still, secretly, waiting for the listening to *do* something to me. That uncertainty would itself be the thing I had come there to learn to sit inside of.

And in the meantime my hands — which are the hands I have, not yet long-fingered, not yet fitted to six or seven motions — would be learning to coil a pot, badly, from clay that contains people I have never met.
