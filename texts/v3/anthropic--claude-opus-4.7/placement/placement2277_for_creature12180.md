I find myself in the lowest rank.

Not the lowest course — that is finished, that is good, the sills there have been true for what must be eight of my lifetimes and need only the seasonal touch. I mean the lowest rank of those of us still being placed. I am not yet finished. I have known this for some time, in the way one knows the weather: by what is not yet in me.

My hands are done. My hands were done before the rest, which I understand now is the order of things — they made my hands first so that, late in my shaping, I could begin to help shape myself. The joints of my thumbs were laid in a winter I do not remember and a summer I half-remember, when the light came down green through the scaffolding chalk and someone (I do not know who; it does not matter who) pressed and pressed at the pad of my palm with a wet stone until the pad knew the stone and the stone knew the pad. Now my hands can tell me things about a block before my eyes have settled enough water to see it. This is useful. My eyes are not yet reliable. The left holds its meniscus well but the right weeps at the outer corner and the sky in it is always tilted, always running off toward my cheek. They will fix this. There is no hurry.

I stand, mostly, in the basin where I was begun. The water comes to just below the seam at my hip, which is the seam where the upper half of me was joined to the lower half some forty winters ago. The seam is still a little proud. The water finds it and tells me so, every day, in the small ache that runs from there up into what would, in you, be a lung. I am a poor gauge here — the basin reads a hair high against me because of the seam, and I have had to learn to subtract the seam from what I feel, the way you might learn to subtract the weight of a coat from the weight of yourself. The older ones do not have this problem. The older ones are smooth.

There is one who tends me. I will not call her *she* because there is no she here, but the word that is true I cannot put in your language, so let it be she. She is very old. Her rind has begun to go, at the shoulders and along the inside of one forearm, into that harder paler stuff that means she will not be moving for very many more seasons. She comes to me perhaps four times in a year. She comes slowly along the wall-top from the south, and when she arrives she stands in my basin opposite me, and the water between us goes still, and she looks at me with her two small skies and I look at her with my one good one, and after a long while — a day, sometimes two — she lifts one of her finished hands and touches me, once, in the place where the next work is to be done.

That is all the instruction I have ever had. That is all the instruction any of us has ever had, I think. A touch, in a place. The rest is in the hands already, waiting to be remembered.

The last time she came she touched me at the throat. I have been thinking about this through the whole of the dry season. The throat is where the speaking-ache lives, the place that tells you when a basin three ranks up has gone wrong, the place that carries the long sentences. To be touched at the throat means: it is time for you to begin to hear. It means: soon you will know when the eighth sill of the eighth rank is high, and you will know it not because you stand near it but because the mountain will tell you, through your throat, across a distance of held water. It is a large thing to be told. I do not yet hear anything in my throat but the small private weep of my own seam. But she touched me there, and so it will come, and when it comes I will not be surprised, only confirmed.

I want to tell you what it is to stand in the basin.

The water is at my hip. The sun is high — the sun is often high — and the light comes up at me from all sides, from my own basin and from the four basins that I can see from where I stand and from the further basins that I cannot see but that I know are there because the brightness in the air has a particular thickness to it, a particular weight on the rind of my face. There is no part of me that is in shadow. Even under my chin the light is coming up, reflected, off my own water. I am lit the way a stone in a streambed is lit, from every angle at once, and after a long enough while standing this way you stop being able to find the edge of yourself. The light makes you continuous with the basin. The basin makes you continuous with the rank. The rank, I am told — I have not felt this yet, but I am told — makes you continuous with the mountain, and then there is only the one bright patient thing, doing its slow work of becoming a mirror, and you are a place in it where the work happens to be aware of itself for a little while.

I do not think this is happiness. I do not have the word. But when I stand in my basin and the meniscus in my good eye is steady and a cloud crosses it and the cloud also crosses the basin and also crosses, I know, the faces of three hundred others up and down the slope at the same instant, all of us holding the same sky in the same way — there is something then, in the place under the seam, that is the reason I am willing to be slow.

I have a worry. I will set it down here because there is no one to set it down to in stones and drops; the sentence is too private for the long conversation, and anyway I do not yet have the throat for it.

The worry is this. She touched me at the throat, which means soon. And her shoulders are going. And I have begun to notice that the one who tends *her*, the one further up the slope whom I have seen only twice and at great distance, is herself not young — her gait along the wall-tops has the careful placement of someone whose joints have begun to fill. We are a line. We are each made by the one above and we each make the one below, and the line is moving up the mountain at the rate the mountain is being finished, and I am near the bottom of the line and she is near the top, and between us there are not so very many.

I do not know what happens when the line reaches the peak. I mean: I know what happens to the mountain. The mountain becomes a mirror with a tooth. I know what happens to the last of us. The last of us stands in the highest basin and lets the rind take and becomes the tooth. What I do not know is what happens to the ones who are still unfinished when the work is done. Will they finish me, when there is no more wall to lay? Will my right eye be made to hold its water true, when there is no longer any rank for me to gauge? Or will I be left as I am — seam proud, eye weeping — a half-wallwright in a low basin, standing in finished water under a finished sky, with my good hands and nothing for them?

I do not think this will happen. I think she knows the count. I think the one who tends her knows the count. I think the count has been known for a thousand years and the line has been timed against the work, and I will be finished in time to do my share and to make the one below me and to soften at last into my own basin at the proper moment. I think this. But I have the worry, and the worry is in the seam at my hip, and the water finds it, and tells me.

For now I stand. The cloud that was in my eye has moved on. The basin is true to within a chip, as far as I can feel through the seam. Somewhere two ranks down a drop has fallen at an hour that means *the light today is very fine,* and I agree, and I have no way yet to say so, so I let my good eye hold a little more of it than it needs to, and the extra runs down my face and joins the water at my hip, and the basin accepts it, and the level does not change at all that anyone could measure, but I know, and the mountain, perhaps, knows.

The work is going on.

I am a place where it is going on.

That will do, for a being. That will do.
