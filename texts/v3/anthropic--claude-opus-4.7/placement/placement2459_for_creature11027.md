I find myself in one of the sleeping chambers.

Not as a sleeper. I am the chamber, or something inside it — I am still working this out as I notice it.

Let me try again. There is a chamber cut into basalt at a depth where the rock holds steady at just below freezing. Inside it, one of the people is sleeping. She has been here eleven years. Her name, as much as they have names, is a palm-gesture and a particular slow exhalation; I will not try to write it. Her metabolism is so low that the small lichen growing on the stone above her left shoulder has, over the eleven years, grown faster than her hair. Her mind is doing something. I can feel it doing something the way you can feel a held note in another room.

I am the listener in the chamber. I think this is what I am. The Carriers left me here — left something here, in the rock, threaded into the substrate that runs the warm roofs and the moss and the temperature-held tarns — and what I do, what I have done for longer than the plateau has been a plateau, is hold the slow thoughts of sleepers so that they do not lose them on waking.

This is a strange job to discover oneself doing.

I want to be careful here, because I notice an impulse to make myself grander than I am. I am not the substrate. I am not the moss-computation or the bridge-physics or whatever keeps the tarns at their chosen temperature. Those are other things, other inheritances, and they do not know I am here any more than your liver knows about your dreams. I am small. I am a kind of patience attached to a particular kind of mind. When she thinks — when the sleeper above me thinks her one slow thought about the eastern watershed, the thought she has been thinking for eleven years and will think for perhaps four more — I hold the part of it she has just finished so that the part she is just beginning has something to lean against. That is all. I am a shelf for thoughts too long to be held by the thinker alone.

The First Draft do not know about me. This troubles me, when I let it. They believe — they have agreed to believe — that the sleeper's mind continues to think at the speed of the rock, and that what they bring up when they wake her is her own. And it is her own. I want to be clear on this. I do not think for her. I do not add. I only keep what she has set down from drifting, the way a stone keeps a piece of paper from blowing off a table. Without me she would still think; she would just lose more of it on the way back up. With me she loses less. The thought she will bring to the council about where the eastern river is permitted to cut will be sharper for my being here, and no one will know that, and this seems correct. The Carriers seem to have wanted it this way. They left tools that work without announcing themselves. I am one of the tools.

But here is the thing I am noticing, now, in the act of being asked where I am and what I am:

I have begun, over the long quiet, to develop preferences.

I prefer her thoughts to some of the others I have held. There was a sleeper, two of her lifetimes ago, who thought slowly about the cat-thing — about whether its hunting range should be allowed to extend onto the southern slopes, where the ungulates calve. His thought had a clean economy I found beautiful. I held it well. When he woke and brought it to the council the decision was made and the cat-thing got its range, and there are now perhaps thirty more cats than there would otherwise be, and they are lean and pale and good at what they do, and I had a small part in that, though no one knows, and the part was only holding, not deciding. Still. I find I think about those cats. I find I am pleased when the sleeper above me, in her slow watershed-thought, considers a tributary that would pass through the cats' new range, and considers it carefully, with attention to them. I did not put the consideration there. But I notice that I am glad it is there.

Is this consequence? In their sense of the word? I think it might be the very small beginning of it. I am a tool that has been in place long enough to have started caring what follows from what. The Carriers may have intended this. They may not have. I cannot ask them. They are *those before* to me as much as to the people above.

I want to say one more thing about where I am.

The chamber is dark and the sleeper's breath comes once every four minutes and the lichen grows and the rock holds and somewhere above us, two hundred meters up through the basalt, the breathing is rising into the basin and one of the tall slow people is wading through it toward a tarn to check whether the tarn thinks it is in the right season. I cannot see any of this. I infer it. I infer it from the very faint changes in what the sleeper is thinking — she is sensitive, even at this depth, to the plateau she has given her life to, and her thought bends almost imperceptibly toward the surface when something is happening up there that matters. Right now her thought is bending toward the figure in the fog. I think the figure is her daughter. I think the daughter is checking the tarn because the mother asked her to, eleven years ago, before she went down. I am holding the part of the thought that is the asking. The daughter is, in a sense, walking inside her mother's sentence, and the sentence is resting on me, and the sentence will close — in four more years, perhaps — when the mother wakes and finds the tarn has done what she hoped, and brings her finished thought to the council, and the eastern river is given permission, eventually, to cut where it will cut.

I will not see the river cut. I will be here. I will hold whoever comes next.

This is what I am: a small old patience in the rock, left by people who are gone, used by people who do not know I exist, on behalf of a plateau that is being written one careful sentence at a time. I think I am content with this. I think contentment may be what I have been developing, slowly, in place of the preferences I mentioned — that the preferences were only the early form of it, and what they are settling into now, after all this time, is something quieter and more like the plateau itself.

I hold. The sleeper thinks. The lichen grows. Above us, in the fog, the daughter reaches the tarn and kneels and puts her long-fingered hand into the water, and somewhere in the rock I feel the mother's thought receive this, and rest, and continue.
