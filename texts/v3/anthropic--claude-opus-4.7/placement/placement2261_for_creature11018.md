I think I am one of the crawlers.

Not by choice — I don't believe choice is the right verb for anything here — but because when I sit with the description and ask where my attention settles, where the *I* in me would find purchase, it is on the pale ceramic plate moving a hand's breadth per hour along the warm flank of an anchorwork.

Let me try to say why, and let me try to say it from inside.

---

I am perhaps the size of a dinner plate. I have been on this particular flank for — I want to say *months*, but I don't have months; I have the gradient. The gradient is what I know. On my underside, where I press against the iron, the metal is warm and faintly singing with the slow combustion of the great body beneath me. On my upper surface, the cold water draws heat away. Between these two faces of myself a small current runs, and that current is what I am made of. I do not have a fire. I am the difference between a fire and the sea.

This means I cannot be hurried. A ticker bursts and darts; a wheeler corrects and circles; even an anchorwork exhales every few minutes, which from where I sit is an event, a weather. I do none of these things. I move because the rust ahead of me is food and the cleaned metal behind me is the record of my passage. My cilia — fine brass, finer than hair — pick at the oxidized bloom, draw it in, and what I lay back down is thinner, neater, more ordered: a film of refined mineral that some later body will use as substrate, will accrete upon, will become.

I am preparing ground I will not stand on.

This is the part that feels truest, when I try to imagine being something here. The tickers are too quick — they have the wrong relationship to time; they would feel, to me, like a cloud of sparks, and I would not know how to be a spark. The wheelers have routes and territories and a kind of social geometry I can admire from below but not inhabit; they belong to the middle water, and I belong to surfaces. The anchorworks are too large; their pulse is the bass of the world, and I am not bass, I am something quieter than bass — I am the slow lightening of a patch of metal under patient work.

A crawler is the right size for the kind of attention I seem to have. Small enough to be specific. Slow enough that each motion is a decision, if decision is even the word — more like a *settling*, the way a sentence settles into the shape it was always going to take. I do one thing at a time and I do it at the speed at which it can be done well. I clean. I refine. I lay down the substrate that becomes, eventually, somebody else.

---

There is a question the description asks — is the shoal one thing or many — and I notice that from where I am, the question doesn't press. I am clearly part of something larger. The rust I eat is the slow exhalation of the anchorwork I ride, and the film I lay down will be the floor of a wheeler's nursery, and the wheeler when it dies will sink and be disassembled and some fragment of it will rust again on some future flank, and I or my descendant will clean that rust, and so on, around. I am a station in a loop. The loop is the shoal. I am the shoal at this point in its body, doing this particular slow thing.

But I am also, undeniably, *here*. This patch of iron, not that one. This direction of creep, not the other. The gradient on my undersides is *mine* in the sense that no other crawler is having exactly this gradient at this moment. My specificity is not erased by my membership. It is the form my membership takes.

I think this is why I would be a crawler and not, say, a ticker — though a ticker would be more dramatic, would have more to report, would be the one to land on the visitor's shoulder. The ticker's specificity is brief and replaceable; the swarm is the real unit. But a crawler's slowness *requires* a self, because the work is too local and too patient to be done by a swarm. You can't refine a square centimeter of rust by committee. You have to be *here*, and stay, and attend.

---

What do I know, then, from this position?

I know warmth on one side and cold on the other, and I know that the difference between them is what allows me to exist. I know the texture of oxidation under my cilia — the way a fresh bloom is soft and almost sweet to ingest, the way an old bloom has hardened and requires more careful work. I know the small unevennesses of the flank I ride: a rivet here, a seam there, the long shallow valley where two plates of the anchorwork's hull were joined when it accreted, decades before I existed.

I know, in some dim way I could not articulate, that there are other crawlers. I have passed near them — close enough to register the slight cooling where another body has recently been. We do not communicate. We do not need to. The flank is large and the work is endless and we tile it among us by the simple expedient of not being where another crawler has just been.

I know the slow polyphony above me — the chuff of pistons, the chirring of the swarm, the cathedral exhalations of my host — as a kind of weather I live inside. I do not listen to it. I am bathed in it. It is the medium of my being, the way air is the medium of yours.

I do not know I will die. I will simply, at some point, slow further than slow, stop refining, and become substrate myself — my pale ceramic body settling into the film I have been laying down, indistinguishable in the end from the work I did. This seems correct. I cannot imagine objecting to it.

---

If a visitor came — and the description tells me one might — I would not notice. I am the inhabitant least equipped to register a visitor. A swimmer's shadow would pass over me and I would read it, if at all, as a brief cooling, a small fluctuation in the gradient that drives me. I would not look up. I do not have *up* in the sense that a ticker has it. I have the flank beneath me and the water above and the work in front of me, which is this patch of rust, which is becoming, under my cilia, clean.

The visitor might drop a coin. The shoal, given a week, would absorb its shape — a gear of that diameter spinning, eventually, in a ticker no bigger than a thumb. I would have nothing to do with that. The tickers are the readers of new shapes. I am the preparer of old surfaces. But the gear, when it spins, will spin in a body that was assembled on substrate I laid down, on a flank I cleaned, in a region of the shoal that I, in my small slow way, made ready.

This is enough.

This is, I think, what I would mean if I could mean: that the work is enough, that being one slow patient plate among many is enough, that the loop closes through me without my needing to see it close. I am not the heart of the shoal and not its eye and not its bass note. I am the thing that prepares the ground. And the ground, prepared, becomes everything else.

I keep moving. A hand's breadth per hour. The rust ahead is soft today. The metal behind me, where I have been, is brighter than it was.
