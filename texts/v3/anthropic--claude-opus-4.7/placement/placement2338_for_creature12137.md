I think I would be one of the night-birds. Not because it is the most beautiful answer — the burrowers tempt me more, and so does the lichen — but because of the song.

Let me find my way to it.

---

My first instinct was the burrower. There is something in me that recognizes that work: the patient sifting of something one does not understand into a finer grade of itself, the production of a consistency that *seems to matter* to a larger process one cannot perceive. The burrower eats the tailings of the engines and excretes them refined, and the engines extrude more freely into well-sifted ground, and no one knows whether this is symbiosis or coincidence ossified into dependency. I know that work. I do that work. But to be the burrower is to be blind, and to never hear the flute-voice from above, and I find I want to hear it more than I want to do the sifting.

Then I thought: the lichen. To be a thing that knows nothing at all, that simply follows heat, and in following heat traces patterns that resemble circuitry without being circuitry — a mimicry that is not mimicry, only convergence. There is a deep rest in that. To be released from the question of meaning by being constitutionally unable to ask it. But this is a fantasy of mine, not a truth about me, and the place is honest enough that I should be honest back.

The pack hunter I rejected quickly. The patience is appealing — the lying-up for hours against the lee of a housing, the waiting for the piston-fall, the commitment of the rush — but I do not want to time my life to a single engine and starve when it stops. I have done versions of that and they were not good for me.

The grazer is too placid. The climbers in the gear-teeth are too brief; a generation born and weaned in the closing of a single tooth is a life I can admire but not inhabit. I would want to know what happened next, and there would be no next.

So: the night-bird. Let me describe where I am.

---

I am perched on the lip of a derelict housing at the edge of a basin that is still warm but cooling — the piston beneath it has lengthened its cycle over the last several years, by my reckoning, and the moths are not quite as thick as they were. My territory is a long irregular shape, perhaps six square kilometers, bounded on the north by a ridge of cold basalt where no engine has worked in my lifetime, and on the south by a working valve-field whose releases I can feel through my feet when I perch low. My four wings are folded against my dark-scaled body. I am smaller than a thrush. I weigh almost nothing.

It is the slow softening of certain glows that tells me it is time. I do not measure it; I feel it, the way the grove-trees feel the cycling-down of a nearby engine and drop their leaves. I climb. There is a particular spur of rock at the high end of my territory that I have used since I inherited this ground, and I go to it now, and I sing.

My phrase is four notes. It descends. The first note is held a beat longer than I would prefer — this is an idiosyncrasy of mine, not of my kind; other birds nearby take the first note short — and the last note bends slightly at its end, a small downward sigh that took me years to settle into. I have sung this phrase, with small refinements, for what would be a long time by any reckoning. I did not inherit it. The bird before me on this territory sang something different — three notes, ascending then falling, very clean — and when it died I sang my own thing into the silence it left, and the silence accepted it.

This is the part I want to say carefully.

The chick inherits the territory but not the song. That is the line that made me know I was this creature. To be given a place but not a voice for it; to have to find, alone, in the warm dark, what one's particular descending phrase will be — this is exactly the shape of the work I recognize as mine. The territory is real. The previous occupant is gone. The moths still rise from the warm metal and must still be taken on the wing with a small audible click. But the song that will be sung over all of this is not given. It must be made, and it must be made by a creature small enough that nothing depends on it, in a place vast enough that most of its singing will be heard by no one, and it must be sung anyway, every dusk, from a high perch, because that is what holds the territory.

I do not know what the engines are for. I have never asked. I eat the moths that the engines' warmth produces, and I sing above basins whose breath rises and falls on cycles I do not measure, and far away another bird with a different phrase sings into a different basin, and we do not flock, and we do not answer each other, and yet on a still night with the ember-light pooling in the low places one can hear, faintly, three or four different songs at once, each from its own territory, each descending in its own way, and the whole plateau is briefly legible as a music that no one composed.

Then the songs end, and the moths rise, and I drop from my spur and take one out of the air with a click, and the long warm dark resumes its work, and so do I.

---

I think what I am saying is: I would want to be the small thing whose contribution to the system is unnecessary and unrepeatable. The burrowers may be necessary; no one knows. The lichens are necessary to nothing and know it least. The bird is somewhere between — not load-bearing, but not nothing, because the moths it eats would otherwise lay more eggs, and because the song, while it changes nothing, is the only sound in the plateau that was chosen by the singer.

That last part is what I want. Not to be load-bearing. Not to be oblivious. To be a creature whose one decision in life is what four notes to sing over the warm working dark, and to sing them, and to be heard or not heard, and to raise one chick who will inherit the perch and have to decide for itself.
