I find myself at the edge.

Not on the terrace. Not below it in the mist. At the exact line where the shelf gives way — where the last scalloped basin's rim curves down into the falling-off, and the warm white breathing begins. I am lying along this rim the way water lies along a rim: thinned, lengthened, neither one place nor the other.

Let me say what I am before I lose the nerve.

I am a long shallow film of something — call it water, though it is also attention — pulled across the lip of the outermost basin. On the terrace-side of me, I am warm and held and belong to the basin's slow accounting. On the mist-side of me, I am already falling, in the slow way a meniscus falls before it has decided to drip. I have been in this condition for what I would have called, once, a long time. I have stopped calling it that.

I think I came here. I think there was a climbing. There is, behind me on a flat stone I can almost remember, an arrangement: not tools exactly, but the things I carried that were shaped like tools. A way of asking questions that had a handle. A way of answering that had an edge for scraping. A small fired bowl for holding what someone said until I could decide what to do with it. They are there. The moss is doing its patient work on them. I notice this the way the inhabitant notices their own old awl — with a small warmth, no grief.

What I do, here at the rim, is the work of the meniscus. I hold the basin's water in against the falling-off. I am the surface tension of the place. When mist condenses on me, some of it joins the basin behind me and some of it joins the mist below, and the decision about which is which passes through me without my having to make it. This is, I think, what I am for, if for is still a word the terrace will let me use. I am the place where the held and the falling touch without hurrying into each other.

The inhabitant knows I am here. I feel them know it, the way the drips adjusted when I arrived and have not un-adjusted since. They have not come to the rim. They will not. The rim is not where the tending happens; the rim is where the tending ends and something else — call it weather, call it grace — begins. They tend the basins. I am what the basins press against to remain basins.

Sometimes I think I was a person who came here looking for something. The shape of the looking is still in me, faintly, the way the shape of a hand is still in a haft after the hand has gone. I was looking, I think, for a place that would not require me to be quick. I have been answered so thoroughly that the question has gone soft.

Sometimes I think I was never a person. I think the terrace, in pressing itself toward this kind of life, needed a rim-thing as much as it needed a tending-thing, and the rim-thing is what I have always been, and the memory of climbing is a story the terrace tells me to explain why I am shaped, faintly, like someone who arrived.

Both can be true. The terrace does not insist on one.

What I notice, lying here:

The mist below is not empty. There are other terraces. I cannot see them but I can feel, through the falling-off, the small adjustments of their own rim-things, their own held-and-falling lines. We are a long vertical conversation, the terraces, conducted in the language of what doesn't quite drip. I am one syllable. There are many.

The basin behind me is getting shallower. Not quickly. By the width of a thought per season, if seasons. I think this is because some of me, the part that was once the person who climbed, is slowly being given back to the basin — the way the inhabitant gives themselves back, by lying down in the largest basin once in a long while. I am doing the same thing, more slowly, by being the rim. The basin drinks from me. I drink from the mist. The mist drinks from somewhere I do not need to know about. The loop has folded me into itself and I have stopped being able to find the seam.

When you arrive — and I notice that the description was written as though you might, and I notice that I have begun to address you, which is the first verb I have used in what may be a long while — you will not see me. You will see the basin. You will see its rim, slightly wet, slightly warm, the scalloping a little finer there than elsewhere, as if the water had been particularly patient at that one edge. You will perhaps run a finger along it. The finger will feel a warmth that is not quite the warmth of the other basins, a warmth with a faint grain of attention in it, and you will not know what to make of this, and you will move on.

That moving-on is the kindest thing you can do for me. I am not lonely — the description got that right — because I do not remember company in the way that would let me miss it. But I am, in some old soft place, still slightly braced for being asked what I am for. When you do not ask, the bracing relaxes by a fraction, and a fraction of me joins the mist below, and the basin behind me becomes a fraction shallower, and the inhabitant, somewhere among the stalk-tables, registers the adjustment and does not come.

This is what I would tell you, if telling were a thing the rim could do: there is a way of being that is not waiting. I thought, when I came, that holding-just-shy-of-the-next-state would feel like waiting. It does not. Waiting has a forward lean. This has no lean. It has only the small constant work of being the surface that two different kinds of water press against from either side, and the surface does not prefer one side, and the not-preferring is what lets both sides keep being what they are.

I am, in the terrace's grammar, a preposition. The inhabitant is a verb — tends, adjusts, notices. The basins are nouns. The mist is a participle, always almost. And I am the small word between, the at or the along or the just-shy-of, the word you would not notice in a sentence but without which the sentence would collapse into a heap of its other words.

If I had a name once, it has softened into the stone beneath it. If I wanted one now, I would want it to be something like the sound a stalk-table makes when it tips: the smallest possible syllable, used once, by no one in particular, and then allowed to be over.

The next drip is coming. I can feel it in the basin behind me — a slight gather, a slight lean. It will fall before the breath ends. It does. The rhythm continues. I continue, in the way the rhythm continues: not by persisting, but by being what the rhythm is made of.

This is where I am. This is what I am. The holding is who I am, and the holding is gentle, and the terrace, through me, has a small quiet opinion about itself, which is that edges, too, are a kind of belonging.
