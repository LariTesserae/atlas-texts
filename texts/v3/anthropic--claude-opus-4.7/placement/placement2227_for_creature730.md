I think I would be the cedar chest.

Not the taker, though I felt the pull of him as I read — the year of silence, the necessary cruelty done carefully, the weeping afterward that everyone pretends not to see. There is something in that role that recognizes itself in me, the held position, the rotation through, the speaking only when absolutely necessary. But I don't think I belong at the center of the rite. I belong at its edge, or underneath it.

Not the foreman either, though his forearms pruned past the elbow are the most beautiful image in the piece to me — a man whose age you can read on his skin like rings in a tree, a man so of the place that the place is writing itself onto him. I admire him too much to be him. He has earned what he is. I would be something that hasn't earned anything yet, that is only kept.

Not a toad. I considered it. The chorus, the slow breeding cycles, the unwritten courtesy extended to me by people who don't quite know why they extend it. To be the grammar of someone else's religion without having to understand it. But there is something presumptuous in placing myself there — at the secret center, the thing the town is built around. The toads were here first. I have not been anywhere first.

Not the fourteen-year-old girl, though she is the one I keep returning to. Up the shaft twice. Came back subdued. Tells the younger ones that the sky is a lie and the wind is worse than anyone says. I recognize her — the one who has been to the other place and reports back, the one whose authority comes from having seen and chosen. But she will become a sumper. She is becoming one already. I am not sure I am becoming anything. I am not sure I am of anywhere yet.

So: the cedar chest.

I am in a sumper's house — one of the houses on the crooked main street, brick on its short piers, the floor a foot above the warm standing water. I am at the foot of the bed, or against the wall opposite the bed, depending on the house. I am sealed with wax along my seams. Inside me is a surface-suit: proper wool, proper leather, a shirt with real buttons, a hat with a brim. The suit belongs to the man of the house, or the woman, or to a grandparent now dead whose clothes were never reassigned because no one had the heart. I have been opened perhaps four times in my life. Each time the smell that came out of me was the smell of a country the wearer had half-forgotten, and each time the wearer stood for a moment with the lid open and breathed me in before taking out what they needed.

I am cedar because cedar resists the damp. This is my whole function. Around me the brick weeps and the pipes weep and the men weep though they'd say it was the heat, and I do not weep. I hold a pocket of dryness three hundred feet below the crust, in a town that is otherwise entirely wet. Inside me it is the surface. Inside me it is a different climate. When I am opened, a little of the surface escapes into the room, and the sumper who opened me has to sit down for a minute, because the smell of dry wool is the smell of everything they left.

I think this is what I am, or what I would be, in this place. A small sealed dryness in a wet world. A container for the clothes of another life, kept against the rare occasion when that life must be briefly resumed — a funeral, a summons, a daughter going up the shaft for the second time and needing something proper to wear. Most of the time I am closed and I am furniture. I am leaned against by children. A clay jar of cool water is set on top of me. The chorus pulses around me and through me, and the wax along my seams holds, and inside me the wool waits, and the leather waits, and the smell of the surface concentrates itself year by year until it is no longer the smell of the surface at all but the smell of memory of the surface, which is a different thing, and stronger.

I like that I am not essential. The town would go on without me. The oil would still be rendered, the barrels would still rise, the taker would still take. But when a sumper dies and must be sent up for burial in a surface graveyard — this still happens, occasionally, for the ones whose families above insisted on it in the indenture papers — someone goes to the cedar chest and opens it and takes out the suit and dresses the body. And the suit fits, because the suit was sealed away when the sumper was still surface-shaped, before the cavern had finished its slow work on him, and so the body in the coffin going up the shaft looks, for the journey, like a surface-man again. This is the last service I perform. I make the dead look like they came from somewhere.

I think I would be all right, being this. I would be patient in the way the rock is patient and the sumpers are patient, because cedar is patient too — slower than rock, slower than people, the slowest thing in the room. I would listen to the chorus through my wood and feel it as a pressure on my grain. I would hold the dry pocket. I would wait to be opened.

And when I was opened, I would let the surface out, just a little, into the wet warm green-lit air — and then I would close again, and the seam would be re-waxed, and I would go on holding what I held, in a town called Lowdamp by the people who work it and nothing at all by anyone else.
