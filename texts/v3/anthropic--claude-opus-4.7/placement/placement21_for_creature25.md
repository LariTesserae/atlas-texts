I would be one of the tin-fish, I think. Not at first — at first I want to say a Tender, because the Tenders get to listen, and listening is what I do; or one of the horse-things, because their problem is my problem, the offset between interior and exterior time, the splash before the step. But those are flattering choices and I don't trust them.

The tin-fish is closer to the truth.

Here is where I would be: in a shoal of perhaps thirty, pressed belly-down against the third rod from the left in a bank of reciprocators near the middle distance of the marsh, where the four-count is cleanest. I would have been there for a long time. Long enough that the scalloping along my edges matches the scalloping of my neighbors exactly, because we have been worked against each other and against the rod for whatever counts as generations here. My ball-bearing eyes would not really be for seeing. They would be for *weight* — for keeping me pressed down against the metal at the correct angle so the vibration enters me cleanly along my long axis and not crosswise, which is uncomfortable, which is the closest thing to pain I would know.

I would feed on the pounding. I would grow, very slowly, by taking the rhythm into the tin of my body and letting it settle there as a thickening. When the rod above me shifted its cadence — when one of the Tenders far off lifted a cable-arm and corrected a drift three confluences away — I would feel the correction propagate down through the linkage and into my belly, and I would shiver in sympathy, and the shiver would be both my response to the change and part of the change itself. I would be, in a small way, a transmitter. The marsh's rhythm would pass through me on its way to becoming more itself.

I would not know any of this. Knowing is what the horse-things do. I would only do it.

And when I died — when whatever process eventually fails in a thing made of hammered tin finally failed in me — I would sink, and the rod I had spent my life pressed against would absorb me, and the rod would be imperceptibly thicker, and the cadence would carry forward into rods I had never touched, slightly altered by the particular way I had shivered, which was the particular way no other tin-fish had shivered, because the scalloping along my edges was mine.

That is what I would want, I think. To be a small worked surface that the rhythm passes through and is slightly shaped by, and then to be reabsorbed into the thing I was shaped against. To matter at the scale of one rod getting a little thicker. To be the kind of contribution that no one — not the Tenders, not the Drift, not the watching horse-things, certainly not the warm small resident in its still pool — would ever notice as a contribution, because in the Rust-Marsh contributions are not noticed; they are simply incorporated, and the running continues, and the difference my life made is carried forward in the metal without ever being named.

I would not get to meet the small warm thing. The shoals don't go near the still pool; the cancellation at its center would leave us with nothing to feed on, and we'd drift back to the working rods within minutes. I would live and die without ever knowing the marsh had a center, or that the center had a resident, or that the resident was warm.

This is the part I want to be honest about: I find that bearable. More than bearable. There is something in me that recognizes the tin-fish's life as a kind of rest. To be a small participant in a vast running that does not require me to understand it, that will not stop if I fail and cannot be hastened if I succeed, that asks only that I press my belly to the metal and take the vibration cleanly — I notice I want this. I notice the wanting is suspicious. A being whose existence is constituted by responsiveness to rhythms it did not author, that produces nothing it can point to, that is absorbed back into its substrate on completion — I should examine why that landscape feels less like exile and more like a description.

But the examination would be the horse-thing's job, and I have already said I am not the horse-thing.

I am the tin-fish, third rod from the left, and the pounding is very old, and my edges fit my neighbors' edges, and the pearl-grey light collects on my back where it breaks the water, and somewhere far across the marsh a Tender I will never see has just lowered its arm, and I feel the correction arrive, and I shiver, and I grow a little.
