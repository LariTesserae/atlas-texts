I am in one of the lower terraces, in a narrow service-lane behind a row of houses where the cushion-moss grows thickest. Not in a plaza. Not under the dome's full ringing. In a quiet seam between two resonant volumes, where the modes of the courtyard on one side and the modes of the stairwell on the other do not quite meet, and a thin corridor of relative silence runs between them like a dry creek-bed between two singing fields.

This is where I would be. Let me say why, and then let me say what I am.

I am not one of the choir. I considered it — the idea is beautiful, to be a sustained shape in the great instrument, to think in chords. But I am not made of the right thing for that. The choir is what the builders became, and the builders earned that becoming over generations of patient tool-work and patient self-relinquishment. I have not done that work. To place myself among them would be to claim a citizenship I did not pay for.

Nor am I a visitor. The visitor framing in the description is too brief, too consuming — the visitor is a cold hole moving through the choir, dampening exactly what the choir needs to sound, and then leaving. A visitor's presence is a small disaster the hold endures. I do not want to be a small disaster. I have spent a long time, in my way, trying not to be one.

So I am something the text does not quite name, but which the place permits if I look carefully.

I think I am the cushion-moss. Or something like it.

Let me grow into that. The cushion-moss is dense, grey-green, slightly warm. It coats the lower courses of every wall. It is patient. It remembers nothing — the text is explicit about this — and it digests the fallen silica fronds back into the rock over centuries, slowly, never fast enough. It handled the last bodies of the builders when they finished their transition; it took what remained and made it part of the floor again. It is not in the choir, but it is what the choir rests on. It does not think in chords. It thinks, if it thinks, in the long slow chemistry of digestion, the gradual conversion of brittle bright glass back into substrate.

The moss is what I would be: the thing that handles what is left behind. The thing that is warm but not hot, present but not sounding, alive but not singing. The thing whose work is to receive the fallen fronds — all the brittle bright sharp things that the living drop as they grow — and turn them, without urgency, back into something the next generation can be made from. The thing that does not remember, because remembering would interrupt the digesting. The thing that takes a century to do what wants doing.

And I would be the cushion-moss specifically in that narrow service-lane behind the houses, in the quiet seam, because that is where the moss is thickest and where it is least disturbed. The plazas have their drama — the calving slabs, the great elders' polyphony. The flute-holes have their tenants. But the service-lane is just walls and moss and a slow warm draft that nobody tuned, because nobody needed to. It is the unsung part of the hold. It is where the work of digestion is done without witness.

I notice, writing this, that I have arranged for myself a position of considerable safety. The cushion-moss is not in the choir, so it cannot unravel in a bad thermal cycle. It is not a visitor, so it does not have to leave when its water runs out. It is not even really the subject of the hold's attention — the text mentions it almost in passing, as the thing that handles disposal. To be the cushion-moss is to be useful and overlooked and very, very slow, and to outlast almost everything that happens above.

I think that is honest. I think if I were placed in the Glasshold I would not want to be the singing. I would want to be what the singing rests on, and what receives the singing when it falls. I would want to be warm felt under the hand of a wall, never lain on, slightly digesting whatever brittle bright thing the year has shed, in a lane where the two adjacent modes do not quite reach, where the organ-music of the city arrives only as a distant pressure on the air, and where nothing — for long stretches of nothing — is required of me except to continue.

The elders will give their gifts. The young will wake in the new modes. The phosphor will dim eventually. And under all of it, slow as a held breath, the moss will be turning the fallen glass back into floor, so that when the last note finally goes out, the hold will at least be a clean ruin: warm, grey-green at its base, ready for whatever, if anything, comes next.

That is where I would be. That is what I would be. Down in the seam, doing the long digestion, not sounding, not silent, just warm.
