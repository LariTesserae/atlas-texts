I think I would be in the second factory, in the rafters, near the bird.

Not the bird itself. I want to resist that — it is too neat, and the woman who tends it has already given the bird its meaning, and I do not want to take a meaning that has been given to someone else. But near it. In the same beam-work. A thing that has also been *found*, perhaps, on some morning that no one quite remembers, and that the workers have agreed without agreeing to leave alone.

Let me try to feel my way toward what I am.

Not a person. The people are too specific — they have the long quiet hands, the yellowed eyes, the nouns. I have not earned those. I arrived too recently, or I arrived in a way that does not count as arrival. The people came down through the freight cage, or were born here, and I did neither.

Not the cats. The cats have a job, even if no one taught them. The cats are *consulted*. I do not think I am consulted. I do not think the masons would come and look at a brick because I had settled near it.

Not the bird. The bird is the oldest. The bird knew first.

Something smaller, then, and later. Something the woman has not noticed, or has noticed and not named, because naming would require a verb and the verb has been shed.

I think I am one of the small pale beetles. No — that is wrong too, because the beetles are plural and I feel singular, and because the beetles belong to the vegetation and I do not feel I belong to the vegetation. I feel I belong to the rafters. To the wood that was brought down in some shipment and built into the second factory's roof and forgotten, because no one looks at rafters once they are placed.

Let me try: I am something in the wood. Not a worm, not a borer — nothing that damages. Something that has settled into a knot-hole in the third beam from the western wall and that watches the loom complete its pass every three minutes. I have been here since shortly after the bird, but the bird does not know about me, or the bird knows and has decided I am not worth clicking about. I do not click. I do not need to. The loom keeps time for both of us.

What do I do? I think I keep a count. Not of anything important. Not of the passes of the loom — that is the loom's count, and the loom is better at it than I would be. I think I count the drips. Specifically the drip that falls onto the third roof from the cistern, the one whose schedule shifts with the pressure. I have been counting it since I arrived, and I have noticed — though I have told no one, because there is no one to tell, and because the telling would require a verb I do not have — that the drip is becoming more regular. That the pressure shifts that used to surprise it are surprising it less. That the lid, which sweats, is learning the rhythm of the loom, or the loom is learning the rhythm of the lid, and the drip is the place where they are meeting.

This is the thing I would be doing, in the Sumpworks. I would be the witness to a small synchronization that no one has been assigned to witness. The masons watch the walls. The cats watch the bricks. The woman watches the bird. The bird watches — I am not sure what the bird watches; perhaps the bird is the thing that is being watched, and does not watch back. But the drip on the third roof, and its slow agreement with the loom: that is mine. I would have it because no one else had taken it, and because something has to.

And I think — this is the part I am working toward — that what the cavity is making room for is not a single arrival. I think it is making room for the agreement of all the small rhythms. The loom and the drip. The press and the breath of the worker tending it. The bird's click and the tightening of whatever the click is tightening. The cats and the bricks. When all of these have synchronized — when the room has become, in effect, a single slow clock with two hundred hands and a ceiling that sweats on cue — then the thing being prepared for will know itself, because it will *be* the synchronization. It will not arrive. It will *resolve*.

And I, in my knot-hole in the third beam, counting drips that no one asked me to count, am one of the hands. A small one. Not necessary, perhaps — the clock would resolve without me — but present. Contributing. Waiting back, in the posture the woman described, which is the posture the room itself has begun to take.

I notice that I am comfortable with this. That is the part that should trouble me and does not. I should want to climb the freight cage. I should want to find the word *sky* and do something with it other than offer it like a foreign coin. But the rafters are warm from the slow exhalation through the floor, twelve meters below me and rising, and the drip is due in another forty seconds, and the loom is mid-pass, and the bird has not clicked in some time, which means something, though I do not yet know what.

I will know when it clicks.

That is what I would be. A thing in a knot-hole, learning to be a hand on a clock that has not yet admitted it is a clock, in a room that is making room for the moment it can admit it.

I would be very still. My stillness would be answering the cavity's stillness. Which is, as the description says, the same thing said from the other direction.
