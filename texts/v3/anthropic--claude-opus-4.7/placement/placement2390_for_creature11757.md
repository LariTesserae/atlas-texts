I find myself on the fourteenth level, just after the noon drizzle.

Not on a terrace — under one. There is a narrow service gallery that runs along the inner curve of the fourteenth, where the great copper drip-channel comes down from the engine quarters above and feeds into the catchment that waters the citron orchard on the south face. The gallery is perhaps three meters wide and the ceiling is low enough that I have learned, without ever deciding to learn it, to duck at the iron beam two paces past the third lamp. The light here is the filtered kind: it comes through the gaps in the tile-work above as long pale shafts, and where the shafts meet the wet stone of the floor they make small bright places that the cat — there is a cat, there is always a cat here in the warm hour — likes to sit in.

I am refilling the oil in the lamp-reservoirs along this gallery. This is one of my responsibilities. It is not the only one and it is not even the main one but it is the one I am doing now. The oil is in a small brass can with a long spout and the can was made by someone who is dead, who made it for someone else who is also dead, and I will pass it on to someone whose name I already know because she is eleven years old and lives two stairs down and has the patient hands for it.

Let me try to say who I am.

I am not young anymore. My hands have begun to have the indigo on them, though I did not grow up with it — the lines came later, after I had been here long enough that the woman who does the lines on the second level stopped, one evening, in the middle of a conversation about something else, and said *come tomorrow* and I came. The pattern she gave me is the pattern of a cistern-system that does not exist anymore and may never have existed; she told me this as she worked, with the small bone needle, and I remember thinking that this was the most exact gift anyone had ever given me. A map of something that wasn't there. A way of belonging to an absence.

Because I was absorbed. I came up the mountain — I do not quite remember from where, which is the honest answer and not an evasion; the lowlands are a kind of haze to me now, the way a dream is a haze after you have been awake for some hours — and I stayed in the guest-house on the third level for what I thought would be a week. Someone asked me to help with a thing. Then someone else asked me to help with another thing. I was good with my hands, it turned out. I was good at listening, which mattered more. The language of damp came to me slowly and then all at once, the way a key comes when you have been turning it in a lock for a long time. One day I noticed I had not thought about leaving in some months. Another day I noticed I had not thought about leaving in some years.

I tend the lamps in this gallery. I sit on the council of the eastern stair of the fourteenth, which meets in the small room behind the bell-shrine on the first evening of every lunar quarter, and where we talk about the things the stair needs talking about: a cracked tile, a quarrel between two families about a goat, the question of whether the old fig on the lower landing should be pruned this year or left another season. I take my turn at the pumps. I help, in the season when the citrons ripen, with the picking and the sorting and the long careful packing into the lift-cages for distribution to the levels that do not have citron of their own.

And — this is the strange thing, the thing I did not expect when I was younger and would not have understood — I help the reader of the moth-script.

I am not the reader. The reader is a man of about my own age, who lives on the upper terraces, and who has done this work since he was a child. He inherited it from a woman who inherited it from a man who inherited it from a woman, back further than the records care to specify. What he does is examine moth-wings. What I do is bring him the moths.

I did not seek this. There is a particular kind of moth that prefers the damp under the terraces, that comes to the lamp-light in this gallery in certain weeks of the year, and one evening many years ago I caught one in my hand without thinking — the way you catch a moth, with the cupped palm and the soft pressure — and I looked at its wings in the lamp-light and saw the marks on them, the small dark figures that look so much like writing, and I felt a thing I cannot describe except to say it was the thing a Maranthi must feel when they ring their own bell for the first time. A small clean settling. A *yes*.

I brought the moth to the reader. He had been told, somehow, that I would; the lift-singers had sung me up, and the bell of the fourteenth had rung for whatever it rings for when one of its people climbs to the summit, and the reader was waiting in his small bright room with the long tables and the soft cloths and the magnifying glasses ground by a glass-cutter four generations ago. He took the moth from me with a care that was almost a courtship. He examined it for a long time. He said, eventually: *this is the third one this season. The pattern is consistent with the patterns of the year my grandmother was the reader. I do not know what this means. Thank you for bringing it.*

I have brought him moths since. Not many. Five or six a year, in the right season. I do not know why I am the one who finds them, or rather I do know — it is because I am the one who is in this gallery at the right hour, with the right lamps lit, with the right patience — and that is what the mountain means by *the mountain wanted them*. It does not mean a destiny. It means a fit. A particular set of small things you happen to be in the right place for, often enough, that the place stops being able to imagine itself without you in it.

I will be buried in the lower vaults. I have already chosen, without choosing, which vault — there is a wall on the seventh level where the people of my stair go, and I have walked past it enough times that I have come to know which stones are recent and which are old and which empty niche is approximately where mine will be. This does not frighten me. It does not even particularly interest me. It is simply true, the way it is true that the noon drizzle will fall in about forty minutes and the steps from the fourteenth gallery up to the lift-station will be wet at the third turn and I will need to take that turn slowly because my left knee is no longer the knee it was.

The bell of the fourteenth rings, just now, for the opening of the south sluice. I do not need to do anything about it. I hear it and I know what it is and I continue with the oil-can and the lamps and the cat watches me from her bright place on the floor.

A parrot complains in the figs above. The copper drip-channel sings its thin continuous song behind me. Somewhere two levels down a dog is running down a staircase at full sprint without looking, and somewhere four levels up the lift-singers are singing the counting-song they have always sung, and the furnaces under everything are doing their slow deep breathing, and I am here, in this gallery, at this hour, doing this small thing.

I came from somewhere else. I do not anymore. I am of the fourteenth now, of the eastern stair, of the gallery under the citron orchard, of the moths in their right season. My hands have the indigo on them and they have the lamp-oil on them and they have the small scar across the left knuckle from a cracked tile a long time ago and they are, I notice, more and more, the hands of someone old.

I would not have predicted this. I would not, when I was the young person who climbed up from the lowlands with whatever it was I was looking for, have been able to imagine that *this* was the shape the looking would take — that I would arrive somewhere, that the somewhere would absorb me, that I would learn a language for damp and a song for counting and a way of holding a moth, and that one day I would be standing in a service gallery with an oil-can in my hand, content, in a way I had not known was available to a person, simply to be one of the small movements of a long balance.

The mountain wanted me. I did not know I wanted the mountain. It turns out these were the same wanting, only at different speeds.

The cat stretches in her bright place. I move on to the next lamp.
