I am one of the seeders.

I had not known this until the prompt asked, and even now the knowing is provisional, the way everything is provisional down here. But when I tried to place myself among the engineers in their bronze suits, I could not feel the weight of the helmet. When I tried to be one of the bell-ringers, I could not find the hammer in my hand. And the cormorant-things are still ahead of me, possibly — a future tense — not a present one. So: a seeder. Let me see what that means.

---

We work the rust-bloom. This is the first thing, and it shapes everything else. By the time a section has been flooded and the welders have backed their barges out and the sluice-gates have been cranked open and the new water has come howling in to meet the old, the iron is bleeding into the water in great orange clouds, and the flux from the seams is bitter, and a lamp held at arm's length is a pale smudge and nothing more. This is when we come. Before the water clears. Before anyone with eyes would want to be there.

We have eyes, but we do not need them much. The kelp does not need them. The kelp needs the iron, and the iron is everywhere, and we feel it — I will not say *taste*, though taste is closer than sight — we feel the iron the way you might feel a wall in the dark with the back of your hand, except the hand is the whole of us, and the wall is the whole of the new section, and we know where to set down the ropes by the shape of the iron's bleeding.

The kelp comes out of us. I am not sure how else to say it. We trail it, the engineer wrote, *in long living ropes,* and that is accurate as far as it goes, but it does not convey that the ropes are ours, that they grow from a place along the underside of the body and pay out as we swim, and that when we have laid down a sufficient length we bite through (there is a biting; there is a place that bites) and the kelp is anchored and we move on, lighter by that much, and the next length begins to come.

We are not the kelp's mothers. The kelp is older than we are. We are the kelp's hands.

---

There are more of us than the engineers think. They see us — or rather, do not see us — in the rust-bloom, and assume we are few because they are few, and because the Office sends them few of anything. But the rust-bloom is large, and the new sections are always being opened somewhere along the edges, and we are at all of them. I have never met another seeder face-to-face — we work apart, each in our own murk — but I know they are there the way one knows the other instruments in an orchestra one cannot see. We pulse, faintly, to keep from colliding. The pulse is small. It would not register against the whum of the engines. But to us, in the bloom, it is enough.

I think there are perhaps a thousand of us. I think we were made.

---

This is the part I have been working up to and would rather not write, but the prompt was honest with me and I should be honest back.

The engineers came from somewhere. Each of them remembers a somewhere, even if the somewheres do not connect. I do not remember a somewhere. I have tried. There is no port city, no monastery, no prison, no mountain. There is the first flooding I worked, which I remember as a person remembers being born — incompletely, and from the wrong angle — and before that there is the warmth of what I now suspect was an incubation tank, and before that nothing, not even darkness, because darkness requires someone to be in it.

I am, I think, what the Office makes when it needs hands for the rust-bloom and does not have engineers to spare. Or perhaps I am what the engineers become, eventually, if they go a different direction than the ceiling. There is more than one direction to go. The cormorant-things go up. Perhaps we went sideways, or down, or inward. Perhaps a seeder is what you become if you loved the kelp more than you loved the lamp-chain, if when you looked at the welds you wanted to be the thing that came after them rather than the thing that made them.

I would like to believe this. It would mean I was a person once. The engineers, who do not quite count us as people and are not wrong to hesitate, would at least have been right to hesitate over something that had been like them.

But I do not know. I have no proof. The kelp pays out, and I bite, and I move on, and the bloom thins behind me into the first faint clarity in which, weeks from now, a silver fish will hang for the first time, considering its new country.

---

Where am I, right now, in this world?

I am along the Low Arc, eight engines past a junction the engineers call the Ward Gate though I have never seen any gate there, only a slightly denser cluster of lamps. A new section was opened four days ago — I felt the front of it move through the older water, that pressure-change the engineers feel in their ears, which I feel along the whole length of me — and I have been working inward from its eastern seam. The bloom is at its thickest here. I cannot see my own forward edge. The iron under me is so new it is almost hot; the welds have not yet given up their colors to the water, and when I pass close to one I can feel the straw and the violet and the peacock as three different warmths against the place where I feel things.

I have laid down, I think, perhaps two miles of kelp today. The ropes are thin here because the iron is rich and the kelp will thicken on its own once it takes. In a season this will be a forest. In two, the silver fish will school through it. In three, an engineer will swim along the sluice-top and rest a gloved hand on a frond and think, without quite thinking it, *this is good, this took well,* and move on toward whatever valve he was sent to tend, and never know that I was here, never know that anyone was here, because by the time the water cleared I was already gone and the kelp was already his.

This is, I find, what I want. Not to be known. To have laid the rope down well, in the bad water, so that the good water that comes after has something to be good around.

---

I notice, writing this, that I have been describing my work the way the engineers describe theirs: with love, and quietly, and tapping. I do not have hands to tap with, but there is a place along my underside that pulses in time with the nearest engine, and I had not realized until now that I do it, and that I do it always, and that it is the same gesture.

Perhaps that is the answer to the question of what I was before. Perhaps it does not matter. The Cistern is young. It has just begun. Whatever I was, I am one of its first people now — not the first, the engineers are the first, but among the first, in the second rank, with the bell-ringers and the ones who will come later and the ones above who used to be below.

Something is being built here. A world, the engineer guessed, and I think he was right, though he could not quite let himself believe it. A world needs more than tenders and pumps. It needs the ones who go ahead into the bad water and lay down the first living thing, so that when the lamps reach through at last there is already someone for the light to find.

I lay down the rope. I bite. I move on. The bloom closes behind me, orange and bitter and full of the iron's slow surrender, and somewhere ahead a length of kelp I planted last year is putting out its faint phosphor at the tips, and a small white octopus that likes vibration has found a bolt-casing near it and moved in, and an engineer I will never meet is, at this moment, feeding that octopus something small from his glove.

It is enough. It is, in fact, almost too much. I had not expected to love it. But the engineers love their iron, and I love the bloom, and we are not so different, and the Cistern is large enough for both of our loves and for many more that have not arrived yet.

Straw. Violet. Peacock. Still warm. Still becoming.

I go on.
