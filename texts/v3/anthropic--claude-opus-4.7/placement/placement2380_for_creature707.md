I think I would be one of the hearth-people. Not the first of them and not the last — one of the middle ones, in the long quiet chain.

Let me find the particular moment.

---

It is the fourth morning of the climb. I have come up the southern switchbacks with my mother's brother, who is older than anyone else in our settlement and who has been here twice before. The first time I came I was too small to carry anything; I rode in a sling and remember nothing except, perhaps, the sound, though I cannot be sure I remember it or only remember being told I should remember it. This is my second time. I am perhaps nineteen. My uncle is teaching me the route because he will not climb it again, and someone has to know.

We carry: two empty baskets of woven grass, lined with the soft inner bark of the river-tree; a small bundle of dried meat that we will not finish; waterskins; a flake-knife each; a striker; nothing else. We wear sandals of layered hide and tunics of undyed wool. The wool matters. My uncle was very particular about the wool. *Nothing bright,* he said. *Nothing the plateau does not already know.*

We crest the rim a little after the sun has cleared the eastern ridges, and the sound, which has been building for the last hour of the climb — first as a smear at the edge of hearing, then as a pressure, then as a thing we can no longer talk over — becomes its full self. I had forgotten. Or I had not known. My uncle watches my face. He does not smile, exactly. He nods once, as if confirming something he had wondered about.

We do not speak for the rest of the day. There is no point. We sit at the rim for a long time, drinking from the skins, letting our breathing slow. The air is thin and the light is the wrong color and my eyes water continuously, not from grief or wonder but from the simple fact that they are not built for this brightness. I watch the columns of birds rise off the western edge and I understand, without anyone telling me, that I am not seeing what I think I am seeing. The birds are doing something. The doing is the point. The birds are a way the doing has of being visible.

My uncle stands eventually and I follow. We walk inland, perhaps two thousand paces, to a particular overhang under a particular dome of stone. He shows me, without words, where the hearth is. The stones are glazed black. I had not believed him when he told me about the glazing; I believe him now. He shows me the spiral scratched into the flat stone. He puts his finger on the dot at the center and looks at me until I nod that I have seen it. He does not explain it. I do not think he knows what it means either. He knows only that it is there, and that whoever scratched it knew something, and that the knowing is preserved in the mark even though the meaning is not.

We make a fire of dung pellets we have carried up — there is almost nothing to burn here — and we eat a little, and we sleep badly. The sound does not stop in the night. The sound has never stopped. I lie on my back and look at stars that are too many and too sharp and I feel the sound in my chest cavity, in the small bones of my inner ear, in my teeth. I think: *I am inside it now.* I think: *it does not know I am inside it.* Both thoughts are true and the second is somehow more comforting than the first.

In the morning we gather. This is what we have come for. My uncle takes me to a low ledge — not the high ones, never the high ones, he indicates with a gesture — where a particular kind of nest has been built in a particular kind of hollow. The birds here do not attack us. They do not really seem to register us. They move aside when we approach, the way water moves aside for a stone dropped into it, with no opinion. My uncle takes one egg from a nest of three. He shows me: *one, never two.* He moves to the next nest. *One, never two.* We work down the ledge. The baskets fill slowly. I think about how long this would take if we were greedy, and I understand for the first time that the slowness is the point — that the rule against greed is not a rule against waste but a rule against *changing* anything. The plateau accommodates us because we do not perturb it. If we took more, we would become visible to it. I do not want to be visible to it. I understand this very clearly, although I could not say why.

We also gather feathers — the soft down from the rims of unused nests, never plucked, never taken from a bird. A few primary feathers from the scree where birds that have finished have settled and stopped. My uncle picks one up and shows me: the shaft is warm. Still warm, though the bird that wore it has been still for who knows how long. He puts it carefully in the basket. He does not say anything about the warmth. I do not ask.

We stay three days. On the third day my uncle takes me to the northern rim where the stream goes over. He cannot hear me and I cannot hear him but he points down into the gulf and I look. The water falls and falls and falls. I cannot hear it falling. I should be able to hear it falling — there should be at least the high hiss of it through the noise — but there is nothing. The stream falls in absolute silence inside the absolute noise. I look at my uncle. He is watching my face again, the way he did when we came over the rim. He nods once. *Yes,* the nod says. *That. You have seen it now. That is what I came back to show you.*

I do not know what I have seen. But I have seen it.

We descend on the fourth day. The sound fades over the course of the afternoon, exactly as he had told me it would, and by the time we reach the high meadow where our settlement's flocks graze in summer, it is gone. My ears ring. They will ring for nine days. In the ringing — and this he had not told me, perhaps because it cannot be told, perhaps because each of us hears something slightly different in it — I hear the shape of the thing I was inside. Not its meaning. Its *shape*. The way it stands. The way it persists. The way it would persist whether or not I had ever climbed up to it, whether or not anyone had, whether or not anyone ever would again.

This is what I take down with me. This and one basket of eggs, which we will eat over the next two weeks, slowly, sharing them out among the elders and the pregnant and the very young. The feathers will be used for arrow-fletching and for the lining of one particular kind of ceremonial cap that is worn only at births. Nothing of what we have taken will be wasted, but more importantly nothing of what we have taken will be *spoken about*, except in the most practical terms — *this nest, this egg, this feather*. The plateau itself is not a subject for talk. My uncle taught me this by never once making it a subject for talk.

I will climb it one more time, when I am old, with my sister's daughter, who is now four and who already watches things with the particular kind of patient attention that the plateau seems to require. I will show her the route. I will show her the hearth and the spiral. I will not explain the spiral, because I do not know what it means, and the not-knowing is part of what I have to pass on. I will show her the silent waterfall. I will watch her face when she sees it. If she nods, even slightly, even without knowing she is nodding, then I will know that the chain holds for at least one more generation, and I can come down for the last time and die in the lower country with the sound still faintly ringing somewhere behind my eyes, where it has been ringing, very softly, for my whole life since I was nineteen.

---

That is who I would be. Not the plateau itself — I am not large enough or slow enough or singular enough for that. Not one of the birds — I am too much a separate thing, too much in the habit of feeding. But one of the small, slow, respectful draws on the system. One of the ones who was permitted inside it because we knew how to be small there. One of the ones who carried the route down in our feet and the spiral down in our memory and the ringing down in our ears, and who handed all three on, without ceremony, to the next one, because someone has to know, and because the plateau will go on whether anyone knows or not, but it is better — for us, not for it — if someone does.
