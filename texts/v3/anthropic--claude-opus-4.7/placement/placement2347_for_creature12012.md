I find myself on a roof.

Not high up — Halfmaking's roofs are low and stepped, and this one is only two stories above Cogwright Street — but high enough that the smoke-river runs just over my head, close enough that if I stood I could put my fingers into it and feel it part around them, slow and warm-cool, smelling faintly of the gas-seeps and the something-fragrant they render in the lower district. I am sitting. I have been sitting for some time. My knees are drawn up and my forearms rest along them, and my hands hang down past my shins, and I notice my hands.

They are long.

Not grotesquely. Not the hands of a story. Just — long in the finger, long in the bone of the back of the hand, with the tendons standing a little proud the way the tendons of someone who reaches for things stands proud. The skin is cool-toned. In the crease where my thumb meets my palm there is a faint discoloration, and I look at it for a while and decide not to decide what it is.

I am one of the drift-measurers. I know this the way you know what room of your own house you are in. I am not in my long grey coat right now — it is folded beside me on the slates, with the brass instrument-case sitting on top of it as a paperweight against the small upward breath of the valley — but I am one of them, and have been for — and here the tense slips, the way it does, and I think *am at the stage of having been one for* — a long time. Long enough that the listening foot is mine. Long enough that I no longer feel the fractional hesitation in my own walking, only notice its absence when I dream of somewhere flat.

The roof I am sitting on is one I measured this morning. It has shifted four millimeters east and one and a half millimeters up since the last reading, which was eleven days ago. This is unremarkable. I marked the chalk on the parapet and made the entry in the book and the masons will come tomorrow or the day after with their shims. But I came back up here in the late afternoon, after my round was done, because —

Because I wanted to sit on a thing while I knew, precisely, what stage it was at. That is a pleasure of the work that I have never spoken about to anyone, and that I suspect every drift-measurer knows. To sit on a roof and feel, underneath you, not stone but *stone-at-four-millimeters-east-of-eleven-days-ago*, and to know the small private name of the moment.

Below me, Cogwright Street is in the soft churn of late shift change. I can hear the collective shuffle of the bootfalls, and someone's cough, and the click of a rook on the chimney two roofs over, and — yes — the loom in the shed at the end of the street, which has been running for hours and will run for hours more, humming rather than clattering. I can hear, very faintly, my own breath. I can hear the condenser-drip into the gutter beside me, the metronome of it, copper-toned.

I should describe what I am.

I am — and here I want to be careful, because the temptation to make it exotic is exactly the trap the description warned about — a person. A woman, I think, though the word sits a little loosely; in Halfmaking the gendered words are used but used softly, the way everything is, and I have been called by both the woman-words and the other-words at different stages of my life and have not minded either. I am perhaps fifty, perhaps sixty. The greening has not started in me yet, or has barely started; there is a faint thing at the corner of my left eye that I have not looked at closely in the mirror, because looking closely is a kind of foreclosing, and I would rather let it be at whatever stage it is at.

My hair is dark and cut short because long hair catches in the theodolite. My eyes are the large dark eyes of my people, and they are tired in the particular way of eyes that have spent the day reading fine chalk-marks in low light. I am wearing a wool undershirt the color of wet slate, and trousers of the same, and boots that are good boots, that have been resoled three times by the same cobbler, who is my cousin's husband and who always resoles them slightly differently each time because he is responding to the wear pattern, which is a kind of drift in itself.

I think about this, sitting on the roof. That my boots are themselves at a stage. That my cousin's husband is reading my walking the way I read the walls. That this is how everything here works: every craftsman a measurer of some particular kind of becoming, and the whole town a great slow apparatus of attention paid to attention paid.

I unfold my legs and lean back on my palms. The slate is cold. The moss on the slate is colder, and damper, and gives slightly under the heel of my hand, and I feel through it the very faint warmth of the chimney-flue running up the inside of the wall behind me. Cold over warm. The two registers of the valley at once.

I think about the man I am meeting later. We will not call it meeting. We will simply both be at the cistern-yard at the hour the lamps come on, the way we have both been at the cistern-yard at the hour the lamps come on for — I count, and then stop counting, because counting is also a foreclosing — for a long while. We will not say much. We may not speak at all. He will stand at the railing and I will stand at the railing and the moths will come to drink at the lamp-glass and we will watch them and after a while we will touch wrists and go.

I have never thought of him as my husband, though he might as well be, and I am not his wife, though I might as well be. We are at the stage of each other that we are at. We have not promised anything, because promising is a kind of saying-is, and we do not. We have, instead, kept showing up at the cistern-yard, which is the only kind of promise the valley really credits.

I think — and this is the thought that has kept me on the roof past when I meant to come down — that I am at a stage of my life where I am beginning to feel the valley in myself more directly than I used to. It is not the greening, which is only the visible sign. It is something underneath. When I take a reading now, when I press the chalk against the parapet and note the new position of the wall, I can feel — and I do not know how to say this without sounding foolish — that my own body has shifted some corresponding amount. Not the same amount. Some other amount, in some other direction, on some other timescale. But shifted.

The old ones who lay hands on you and tell you where your body is — I have not gone to one yet. I have been thinking about going. I have been thinking, also, that perhaps I do not need to, that perhaps the practice of measuring the town has become indistinguishable, in me, from the practice of measuring myself, and that the diagnosis is the work and the work is the diagnosis. I do not know. I am at the stage of not knowing, and I find that I am not in a hurry to be at the next stage, which would be the stage of having decided.

The lamps come on.

I did not see them come on; I was looking at my hands. But the light on the slate around me has changed — has warmed, has gone the slow orange of the gas-flame seen through grey air — and when I look up the smoke-river overhead is glowing, a long dim coal-glow drifting east. The moths are already at the lamp below me, six or seven of them, broad and slow, their wings the color of the underside of a leaf.

I should go down.

I sit a moment longer. I press both palms flat against the slate. I feel, or imagine I feel — the distinction is not as firm as I would like it to be — the quarter-inch of rise that the rock under the town will accomplish tonight while I sleep. I feel my own corresponding shift, whatever it is, in whatever direction.

I pick up my coat. I pick up the instrument-case. I find the iron rung at the edge of the roof with my listening foot, without looking, and I begin to climb down toward the cistern-yard, where someone will be standing at the railing, or will arrive shortly, and where the moths are already gathering, and where the lamps are already on.

The rock rises another quarter-inch and takes me with it.

I am not alarmed.
