# The Hollow City of Stridor

You descend into it through a fissure in the limestone shelf, and for a long time the descent feels like nothing — just cool air rising past you, dry as old paper, smelling faintly of moss and chalk and something animal. Then the shaft widens, and the city is suddenly *there*, below and around you, and so is the sound.

The sound first, because you cannot think past it at first. It is a chorus of insects — but insects the size of dogs, of goats — stridulating from every surface, a layered scraping roar that does not pulse or breathe but simply *holds*, a wall of noise that fills the hollow the way water fills a basin. Your ribs feel it before your ears parse it. You learn quickly that you must cup your hands to your mouth and shout to be heard, and that after an hour the sound stops being sound and becomes the medium itself, the air's primary property.

The cavern is enormous — kilometers across, you think, though you cannot see the far wall, only sense it by the way the noise returns slightly delayed from that direction. The ceiling is low and broad rather than vaulted, maybe sixty meters up at the highest, hung with curtains of pale root-growth that have pushed down through the stone from whatever forest sits on the surface above. Those roots are the source of the light, more or less: they host a fungus that glows a tired, greenish-yellow, not bright enough to read by but enough to see the city in its full extent — a city ground down to its bones.

Because the city is *old*. That is the second thing, after the noise. Whoever built it built it so long ago that the buildings have rounded like river stones. Towers that must once have stood sharp are now smooth knuckles. Streets have dished into shallow troughs from a traffic that ended ages before your grandmother's grandmother. There is no edge anywhere — every corner is a curve, every facade a soft slope. The mineral seep from the ceiling has glazed everything in a thin calcite varnish, so the city looks half-melted, candle-wax architecture cooling for ten thousand years.

And yet — structure. You can still read it. A grid, loosely, with broader avenues converging on plaza-bowls. Buildings of three or four storeys, mostly, with the suggestion of arched windows now blind and filled. Bridges spanning between rooftops, some intact, some sagged to catenary curves. Stairs everywhere — this was a people who liked to climb, or had to. The scale of the doorways is human-ish, slightly taller and narrower than you would build. No one is home. No one has been home in a very, very long time.

The vegetation has moved in patiently. Not jungle — the air is too cool and too dim for jungle — but a slow lush of cave-adapted growth: pale ferns in every doorway, cushions of liverwort on every windowsill, long ribbons of a translucent vine that drapes from the bridges and sways slightly in air currents you cannot feel. Where the root-curtains hang lowest, whole groves of etiolated saplings have grown up *inside* the buildings, pushing through collapsed roofs, their leaves nearly white. The plant life is generous but spaced — a clump here, a curtain there, with bare stone between. It does not crowd. It has all the time it needs.

The fauna is the city's true population now. The big stridulating insects — call them choristers — cling to the upper walls in their thousands, brown and segmented, their wing-cases rasping. Below them, on the ground level, smaller things move: pale long-legged crickets that pick their way through the moss; a kind of blind, soft-furred climbing animal the size of a cat that descends the buildings in slow upside-down spirals, grazing on the liverwort; processions of something centipede-like that flow along the troughs of the old streets in shining ribbons. In one of the plazas you see a herd of larger grazers, low-slung and pale, drifting between root-pillars and tearing at the fern beds. They do not look at you. Nothing here has reason to fear a shape like yours; nothing here has ever learned what you are.

There is almost no free water. The calcite glaze tells you water *was* here once and is here still in trace — the seep that built the varnish, a slow dew on the coldest stones, the moisture the plants pull from air thick with respiration. But no streams, no pools. The animals must drink from the plants themselves, or from the slow weep at the base of the root-curtains where you can see darker stains. The city's old aqueducts — and there are aqueducts, you can trace them along the higher walls — are dry, dry, bone-dry, their channels lined now with the same fungus that lights the roots.

There are no tools anywhere. No artifacts on the ground. Whatever the builders kept, they took, or it dissolved, or it lies under the calcite. The city is purely its own architecture now, returned to geology, and the animals that live in it use it the way they would use a canyon — as terrain, not as inheritance. A nest of choristers in an old window-arch is not a desecration; it is just what the window is for now.

You stand at the edge of an avenue and the noise saws on, unending, and you understand that this is the final form of the place. It is not decaying — decay implies a direction, a loss still in progress. This is what it has settled into. The slow seep will continue to glaze the stones a little smoother each century. The choristers will breed and die and breed. The roots will hang a little lower, the ferns a little fuller, the buildings a little rounder. In ten thousand more years it will look almost exactly the same. The city has finished happening and is now simply *being*, indefinitely, in the green-gold dusk, in the deafening song of its tenants, beneath the patient roots of a forest that does not know it is standing on a roof.

You sit on a curve of stone that was once a step, and the sound moves through you, and you find — surprising yourself — that you are not lonely here. The place is too inhabited for loneliness, even if none of its inhabitants will ever know your name. You stay longer than you meant to. When you finally climb back toward the fissure, the song does not diminish behind you; it simply continues, as it did before you came, as it will after.
