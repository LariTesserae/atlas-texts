# The Underxylem

Begin with the ceiling, because there is no sky here. The ceiling is wood. Not boards — wood as geology, a single continuous mass of pale heartwood pressed flat against itself in laminations thick as cathedral vaults, knotted with whorls the size of plazas. Light comes through it the way light comes through a held hand: filtered to a warm sourceless amber, bright enough to read by, bright enough that nothing here has needed eyes for darkness. Somewhere far above, presumably, a forest stands in ordinary sun. Down here we are inside its root-knowledge.

The city is built into the xylem.

The chambers are hollowed between root-trunks the diameter of grain silos, and the walkways are grown rather than carved — coaxed, over generations, by the cool-handed work of the people who tend them. Cool is the operative climate. The wood holds a steady temperature somewhere between a wine cellar and an autumn morning. You wear long sleeves. Your breath does not quite fog. The wood itself is faintly warm where you press your palm to it, as if there were a slow pulse on the other side, which there is.

There is no water. This is what makes the place strange to anyone who knows ordinary plants. The trees here drink something else — a clear waxy sap that runs in vertical capillaries you can see through the translucent bark-walls of the residential galleries, threading upward in slow bright pulses like traffic seen from altitude. The sap is the working fluid of everything. It lubricates the bearings of the mills. It cools the kilns. It is drunk, sparingly, with a taste like cold pine and iron. The people have a word for thirst that means *the sap-line has stopped*, and they use it rarely, and never lightly.

---

The settlement extends. From any high gallery you can see kilometers of it through the dim amber light — districts strung along the major roots like beads, connected by causeways of grown wood that arch over the empty spaces between. And there is a great deal of empty space. This is not a warren. The galleries are sparse, set wide apart, with long airy halls of nothing-much between them: drifts of fallen bark, occasional shafts where a side-root has died and left a vertical emptiness lit from above by a different, greener filter. The builders learned early not to pack themselves in. Something about the wood prefers breathing room. Something about the wood notices.

The industrial quarters are downward. You descend by a stair grown into a spiral inside a hollow tap-root, and the air gets cooler, and the light gets redder, and you begin to hear the machines. They are mostly quiet. This is part of what is strange. An industrial city should clatter, but the engines of the Underxylem run on sap-pressure and fiber-tension, and their loudest sound is a soft repeated *thup* — the closing of a valve grown from heartwood, opening and closing several times a second across a thousand workshops. Cumulatively it sounds like an enormous slow rain that never quite arrives. Beneath that, the low groan of the wood itself, settling, growing, redistributing load. Conversations happen at normal volume and carry surprisingly far.

The factories press fiber into cloth, cloth into board, board into the structural members of new districts. They render sap into the seven grades of working fluid. They cultivate the bracket-fungi that serve as lamps in the deeper galleries — pale orange shelves the size of dinner tables, bioluminescent, harvested in rotation. Everything the city makes, the city eats, or the city becomes. There is no waste stream because there is nowhere for waste to go. The wood would not accept it.

---

The fauna are abundant but shy. Pale beetles the length of a forearm graze the inner bark in herds, and the herds are managed — there are beetle-drovers, with long soft staves, who move them between feeding chambers. Something like a marten, but blind and silver-furred, hunts in the upper galleries; the people leave bowls of sap-curd out for them on certain nights and consider it lucky when the bowls are emptied. In the deep workshops a kind of long-bodied moth, wingless, walks the floors on six legs and eats sawdust. Children are taught not to step on them. And there are the listeners — small grey things, hand-sized, that cling to the walls and turn their faceless heads slowly toward any sustained sound. No one is sure what they are. They have always been here. They do not seem to mind being touched.

---

The strangeness, beyond all this, is that the city is not finished, and will never be finished, because the wood is still growing.

This is the central fact of the Underxylem and the one that organizes everything else. The roots that form the city's substance are alive, slow, and putting on mass at perhaps the rate of a fingernail's width per year — but distributed across a region the size of a county, that adds up. Walls thicken. Galleries narrow. Stairs that were comfortable for one generation become tight for the next. The city's architects are not designers but negotiators; they spend their careers persuading the wood, with careful incisions and sap-channels and tension-cables of woven fiber, to grow *here* and not *there*, to leave this corridor open, to seal that one closed.

A district can be lost. It happens. A gallery that the wood decides to reclaim will, over a decade or two, simply close around its furniture, and the residents move out room by room as the ceiling lowers, and at the end there is a swelling in the root where a neighborhood used to be, and people walk past it and remember. The wood does not seem hostile. It is simply doing what it does. The people have a saying: *we live in the slow weather*. They mean the growth. They mean that the shape of the world is a process they are inside of, not a stage they perform on.

---

And above it all, through the amber ceiling, that hint of green light from the other side. Sometimes, in the high galleries, you can hear — faintly, filtered through meters of living wood — the sound of wind in leaves. It is the only weather they have. It is enough.
