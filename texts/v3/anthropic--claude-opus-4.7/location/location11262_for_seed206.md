# The Engine Hollow

Begin small. The whole place would fit inside a cathedral, if a cathedral could be grown rather than built — and in a sense, this one has been.

You step down into it from nowhere in particular. There is no approach. The hollow simply is, and you are inside it, and the air is thick and warm and smells of crushed fennel, hot bearing-grease, and the green water-rot of a slow river.

## The Substrate

There is no ground. What you stand on is a woven mat of root and rhizome and the swollen lower trunks of plants that have agreed, somewhere in their long settling, to be a floor. The mat gives slightly. Water seeps up wherever your weight presses — clear, slightly warm, smelling faintly metallic. The hollow is low country, sunk just below whatever lies above it, and the water table sits right at the surface like a held breath.

The walls are plants. Not plants growing on walls — plants *as* walls. A species I cannot quite name has braided itself into vertical sheets, fibrous and damp, ribbed like the inside of a great green lung. Between the ribs, smaller things have taken up residence: cup-mosses holding thimblefuls of rainwater, a pale liverwort that pulses very slowly, ferns the size of a fingernail.

The ceiling, twenty feet up, is a canopy of broad translucent leaves through which a steady, soft, normal daylight filters down — though you cannot see a sky through them, only the green-gold of light that has been chewed by chlorophyll and spat through.

## The Engines

Here is the strangeness.

Among the plants — sparse, well-spaced, no two within easy reach of another — stand the engines. They are not built. Nothing here is built. But they are unmistakably *industrial*: pistoned things the size of a person, brass-colored and dark, with flywheels and slow-turning belts and small chimneys that exhale wet steam into the canopy. Each one is grown. You can see it if you look: the pistons are woody, the cylinders are a kind of bark-sealed gourd, the belts are flat sinewy vines that loop between pulleys of fused heartwood. The chimneys are hollow stems. The boilers, when you put your hand near them, are warm with the warmth of fermentation, not fire.

They do nothing. That is, they produce no output anyone could carry away. They run because running is what they are. They have been running, you sense, for a very long time, in exactly this configuration, and they will continue.

There are perhaps nine of them, scattered through the hollow. Plenty of space between. Each in its own clearing of damp moss.

## The Sound

This is what fills the hollow: the sound of the engines. It is *loud*. It is the loudness of a small factory floor — a chuffing, a clanking, the wet slap of belts, the hiss of vegetable steam, the low groan of wooden pistons under load. It overlaps and beats against itself and reverberates off the leaf-walls until the air feels physically crowded with noise. You have to raise your voice. You feel it in your sternum.

And yet — and this is the thing that takes a while to notice — the sound never varies. The rhythm of each engine is fixed. The chord they make together is the same chord they made an hour ago, a year ago, a century ago. It is a loud, stable, unchanging music. After ten minutes inside it, the noise begins to feel like silence. After twenty, you realize you have stopped hearing it and started using it the way a fish uses water.

## The Fauna

They are everywhere, and they love the engines.

Frogs — small, brass-bellied, almost the same color as the pistons — cling to the warm cylinders by the dozen. They do not croak. There is no need; nothing could hear them over the machinery, and besides, they have no rivals to call against. They blink and breathe and occasionally snap at the moths.

The moths are large and pale and seem to feed on the steam itself, hovering near the chimney-stems with their proboscises uncoiled into the wet exhaust. There are beetles like polished bolts that walk the belts without being thrown. There are slow, fat-bodied salamanders in the seep-pools, and a single kind of small bird — I think only one kind — that hops along the rafters of the canopy and never sings, only watches.

A long-legged thing like a freshwater crab patrols the wet floor between engines, picking up fallen leaves and arranging them, apparently at random, in patterns it then abandons.

None of these animals fear you. None of them particularly notice you. They have a job, which is to be the fauna of this place, and they are doing it.

## The Rules

The hollow does not change. This is its defining quality, more than the engines or the wet floor or the noise. The light does not shift toward evening. The water does not rise or fall. The engines do not need fuel, or rather, they fuel themselves on whatever slow vegetable process drives them, and that process is in perfect balance with itself. The frogs do not lay eggs that I can see. The moths do not die. Something has been worn smooth here — *erosion* in the sense of a riverstone, not a cliff — and what remains is the shape the place wants, the shape it has settled into and will hold.

You could leave. You should leave, probably; the noise will follow you in your skull for days. But while you are here, nothing is being asked of you. The engines run. The frogs breathe. The light comes down green-gold through the leaves. The whole pocket world chuffs and hisses and holds, and holds, and holds.

It is warm. It is wet. It is loud. It is, in its own grown-industrial way, completely at peace.
