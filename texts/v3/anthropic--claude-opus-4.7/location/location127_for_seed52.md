# The City of Lenslike Rain

It sits in a shallow basin, the kind of place that must have been a lakebed in some previous geology, though the ridges ringing it are still sharp-shouldered and pale, the rock not yet rounded by weather. From any rise, you can see across the whole of it — maybe fifteen kilometers, rim to rim — and what you see is mostly canopy. Broad-leaved trees the color of old brass, interrupted by the dark verticals of the city's towers, which rise through the foliage like reeds through the surface of a pond.

The towers are the first thing that refuses to make sense. They are tall, yes — some of them pass a kilometer — but they do not seem to bear load in the way towers should. They taper the wrong way, thicker at the top in places, and they cast shadows that lag behind the sun by a visible fraction. You watch a shadow slide across the leaves and realize it is arriving several seconds after the light it should correspond to. No one in the city finds this strange. The shadows have always lagged. It is part of how the city keeps time.

The air is warm and damp without being heavy. Moisture does not fall here in the ordinary sense. Instead, at intervals that feel scheduled but aren't, the air between the towers begins to *focus* — humidity collecting into drifting lenses a few meters across, perfectly transparent, so that walking beneath one you see the canopy magnified overhead for an instant before the lens breaks and releases its rain in a brief vertical column, very localized, very precise. You can stand two steps from someone being rained on and remain entirely dry. The streets are patterned with dark circles where the lenses habitually discharge, and moss grows in those circles in concentric rings.

Between the towers: long stretches of nothing much. The city is sparse. A plaza the size of a neighborhood might hold a single low building and a grove of the brass-leaved trees, and then nothing for half a kilometer but paving-stones grown through with soft grass and the occasional standing column whose purpose is not clear — they hum faintly if you touch them, and the hum continues for about a minute after you take your hand away. Children, when there are children, use them as meeting points. *Meet me at the fourth hum past the seed-market.*

The light is low. Not dusk exactly — the sun is up there somewhere, diffuse behind a high pale haze that never quite clears — but the quality is that of an overcast late afternoon held indefinitely. Colors are muted and specific. The leaves are brass; the towers are the grey-green of oxidized bronze; the paving is warm ochre; human clothing tends toward deep indigos and umbers because brighter colors look garish in this light and nobody wants that.

Animals are uncommon. Once in a while something moves in the canopy — a long-limbed creature, pale, that travels by reaching rather than leaping, and that you mostly only see in the peripheral vision. There are insects, but quiet ones: moths the size of a hand that navigate by the lagging shadows, and a kind of ground-beetle that tends the moss rings. No birds. The silence where birds should be is the city's most noticeable absence, and residents who have traveled elsewhere describe foreign places as *loud with birds* the way we might describe them as loud with traffic.

The sounds that remain are subtle. The rain-columns make a soft discrete *tock* when they break. The humming columns. Wind through the brass leaves, which is a dry papery sound, nothing like the rustle of ordinary foliage. And under everything, if you stop and attend, a very low tone, almost subsonic, that seems to come from the towers themselves — the engineering of them, whatever it is, working. It is not unpleasant. People who leave the city report sleeping badly for weeks because the tone is gone.

The towers' engineering is the parameter that pulls everything else into shape. They are doing something to local conditions — collecting humidity into lenses, slowing shadows, suppressing the weather that ought to come off those sharp young ridges, keeping the basin in its perpetual mild afternoon. Nobody living knows how they work. They were not built in the conventional sense; the prevailing account is that they were *grown* or *precipitated* from an earlier state of the basin, the way a crystal precipitates from solution. The ridges are young because the basin is young; the basin is young because the towers made it, pulling the landscape into a configuration that suited them, and the erosion has not yet had time to soften what they shaped. In a thousand years the ridges will round and the basin will sag and perhaps the towers will move on, or dissolve, or simply stop, and then the birds will come back and the rain will fall ordinarily and the place will be just another warm wet valley with ruins in it.

For now, though, it is active in its slow way. The lenses drift and break. The shadows lag. The moss ring around a discharge point widens by a finger's width each year, and the beetles tend it. Someone crosses a long empty plaza, unhurried, under a sky that isn't quite a sky, and a column hums for a while after they pass, and then goes quiet, and the afternoon continues being afternoon.
