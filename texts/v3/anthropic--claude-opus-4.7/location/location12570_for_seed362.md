# The Lung-Forest of Arrant

Begin at the floor, though there is no floor in the way you mean — only the matted understory of the world, a compressed history of leaves that has been pressing itself flat for so long it has forgotten it was ever soft. You can walk on it. You can put a hand to it and it will not give. It is the color of old brass where the light reaches and the color of nothing where it doesn't, and it smells, faintly, of resin and warm paper, though there is no fire here and never has been.

Above: the work.

The Lung-Forest is plant grown into the shape of machinery, or machinery grown into the shape of plant — the distinction has eroded over the long settling of the place into itself. Trunks rise in fluted columns, ribbed like the inside of throats, and at certain heights they branch not into limbs but into bellows: paired sacs of waxy green tissue that inflate and deflate on cycles of forty, fifty, sixty minutes. When a bellows-pair fills, it does so almost silently — a long indrawn hush, the sound a library makes when someone opens a door at the far end. When it empties, a faint hiss through woody valves. The forest breathes. It has been breathing for a very long time, and the breath is dry.

There is no water. The trees do not require it. Somewhere in the deep past — and you can feel that this past is deep, the way you can feel the age of a stone cathedral — the vegetation here negotiated a different chemistry. Sap is a slow oil that smells of pepper. Pollen is a fine pale dust that drifts in slabs of sunlight and never settles, because it never quite agrees with the local definition of gravity. The bellows do not pump water. They pump pressure. The whole forest is a pneumatic engine, and the columns are pipes, and the pipes connect to other pipes, and somewhere — you cannot see where, you can only feel the suggestion of it through the soles of your feet — there are reservoirs of stored breath the size of inland seas.

The light comes from everywhere. This is the first thing that becomes hard to describe. There is no sun visible through the canopy, and yet the canopy is not dark; it is luminous in the way a frosted bulb is luminous, evenly, without source. The leaves — vast, the size of sails, lobed like organs — glow softly from within at a temperature of pale gold. Look up and you see them tiered in layers, dozens of layers, receding into a brightness that does not resolve into sky. There is no sky here. There has not been a sky in this forest for whatever counts as a long time in a place that does not measure.

The temperature is cool. The bellows-breath is cool. The columns are cool to the touch, faintly fibrous, faintly warm only at the joints where the wood remembers it was once doing work. You can walk for hours and not sweat. The forest does not want you to sweat. Sweat would be water, and water has agreements elsewhere.

Now: the weirdness.

The Lung-Forest does not obey the rule that a thing must be in one place. Walk far enough along a corridor between two columns and you will arrive at a column you passed an hour ago, and you will know it is the same column because of a mark you left, but you will also know — with the same certainty — that you have not turned around. The forest folds. It has folded itself into a smaller volume than it occupies, or a larger volume than it occupies, and the math of this is not available to walkers. Cartographers, of whom there have been a few, end their careers with maps that are accurate in every local sense and useless in every global one.

Sometimes a bellows, exhaling, exhales a moment instead of air. You will be standing near it and the light will shift by a degree and you will remember something that has not happened to you, vividly, with the texture of lived experience: a hand on your shoulder, a name spoken in a language you almost know, a room you have never entered. Then the moment passes and the bellows refills and you continue. The locals — there are locals, in the loosest sense — call these *expirations*. They are not dangerous. They are not anything. They are simply a thing the forest does, the way a forest of ordinary trees drops leaves.

The locals: the fauna is rare. There are the *tenders*, which may be animals or may be ambulatory fruit — small, six-limbed, the size of a cat, with the soft inquisitive faces of fruit bats and bodies of pleated green hide. They move through the columns in twos and threes, attending to the valves, prying loose dust from the joints. They do not eat, as far as anyone has determined. They do not reproduce visibly. They appear when needed and are absent when not. There may be hundreds of them in the whole forest. There may be twelve. They have been seen to look at humans, briefly, with an expression that suggests recognition of a kind one would rather not have been recognized by, and then to go back to their work.

There is no predator-prey. The dynamic of this place is not eating. The dynamic is *cycling* — the bellows on their long slow rhythms, the pollen drifting in its layered strata, the columns flexing imperceptibly as they take and give load, the occasional shudder when some deep reservoir somewhere shifts its volume. There are pressure-tides. Twice a day, or what serves as a day, the whole understory thrums faintly underfoot as the forest equalizes itself against something — some other forest, perhaps, on the other side of whatever it is folded against. During these tides the light dims by a perceptible fraction and the expirations come more frequently, and the tenders pause in their work and tilt their faces upward, as if listening.

The sound of the place is what surprises people most. They expect, given the scale — and the scale is vast, continental, the forest goes on past any horizon and there is reason to suspect it is the planet, that there is nothing else, that the world is only this — they expect a roar. The breathing of a continent. But each bellows is quiet, and the bellows are distributed, and the columns dampen sound, and the matted floor swallows footfall. What you hear, mostly, is your own pulse. Then under that, when you learn to listen: the long soft inhalations, staggered, never in unison, a whispered chorus across miles. Then under that, rare and unforgettable: a single woody *tock* when a valve seats, somewhere, kilometers off, carried perfectly clear through the pipework. Then under that: nothing. Or something that is not quite nothing. A held note at the edge of hearing that may be the forest thinking, or may be the inside of your own ear.

It is built. All of it is built. There is no natural substrate — no soil under the matted floor, only more matting, and under that, structure: ribbed, jointed, intentional. Somewhere far down there is presumably a first layer, an original construction, but no one has reached it and the forest does not seem to remember it either. The Lung-Forest has been making itself for so long that the question of who first made it has become a category error. It is the kind of place that, asked about its origin, would breathe out a moment from a life you did not live, and you would understand the answer without being able to repeat it.

The whole thing is in balance. That is the strongest impression, finally, after the strangeness wears down to familiarity: the Lung-Forest is *settled*. It has worked out its disagreements with itself. The pressures balance, the cycles interlock, the foldings of space are stable foldings, the expirations are accounted for in some ledger no one keeps. You can sit down with your back against a column and feel, through the wood, the long slow rhythm of an entire world breathing, and you will understand — without being told, because the forest does not tell, the forest only is — that it has been doing this for an extremely long time, and that it will continue, and that your presence is one of the smaller things happening, and that this is a relief.

The light does not change. The pollen drifts. Somewhere a valve clicks shut. A tender, two corridors over, pauses and tilts its face toward you, and then, satisfied, returns to its work.
