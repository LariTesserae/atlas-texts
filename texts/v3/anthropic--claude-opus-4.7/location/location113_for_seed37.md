# The Lantern-Cavity

Begin a few meters below the surface — not deep, just a scoop in the world, a bowl-shaped cavity where the ground has decided, recently, to not-quite-be. This is a place still making up its mind what it is.

The walls are raw. Pale limestone that has never weathered, never been touched by a river or a glacier or even much time. Run a finger along it and you'd feel something closer to dough than stone; the rock here is embryonic, its grain not yet settled. In places the wall is translucent, as if the mineral hadn't decided whether to be solid. The floor slopes gently toward a shallow basin where water collects — not flowing, just arrived, a thin skin of it maybe ankle-deep at the center, rippling when something passes but you can't see what.

And yet the place is flooded with light. This is the first impossibility. There is no opening above — the cavity is closed, a pocket sealed into the earth — but the air inside is luminous, a steady clean brightness like late morning in a white room. The stone itself seems to be the source, or perhaps the light comes from inside the damp, which fluoresces faintly even in cupped hands. You can see every hair on your arm. You can see the floor of the water.

Sparse things grow. A single fern, waist-high, in the corner where a seep darkens the wall. Three pale stalks of something mushroom-adjacent, evenly spaced as if measured. A patch of moss the exact green of old bottle glass. That is all the vegetation — the place is not generous with plants — but each plant is so deliberately placed it feels curated, as if someone arranged them and then left.

Someone did leave things. A low shelf of worked stone, no taller than a knee, set against one wall — you could call it an altar if you wanted to, or a bench, or simply a flat place. A clay bowl, empty, cracked once and mended. A bundle of something that used to be cloth. No footprints lead to these objects and none lead away. They are scattered, modest, and very old without being ancient — a decade's worth of abandonment, perhaps, which is strange in a cavity whose stone has not yet learned how to age.

Now: the fauna.

The cavity is thick with animals and this is where the world breaks.

Moths, dozens, the size of sparrows, drifting through the bright air with no apparent urgency. They cast no shadows. Look at one directly and it is a moth; look away and from the corner of your eye it resolves into a small hand, opening and closing, and then you look back and it is a moth again. They never settle. They produce no sound at all, not even the soft friction you'd expect from so many wings.

A fish in the ankle-deep basin, too large for the basin, pacing. It is perfectly visible and yet when you try to describe it afterward you'll find you can only remember the pacing, not the fish.

Beetles on the walls, iridescent, moving in coordinated drift like a slow current. If you watch one long enough it becomes clear that the beetles are not traveling across the stone but that the stone is traveling beneath the beetles, which remain stationary while the wall itself scrolls — a thin sheet of rock sliding past, regenerating from nowhere, vanishing into nowhere, and the beetles ride this conveyor with placid attention.

Something larger breathes, occasionally, from a recess you can't locate. Not a growl. Just breath, warm, and the light dims by a hair each time, and recovers.

The sounds of the place are almost nothing. Water dripping, but only sometimes, and when it drips it does so in pairs — never a single drop, always two, spaced like a heartbeat. The fern rustles when nothing touches it. Otherwise the cavity is quiet enough that you can hear the inside of your own ear.

The rules, as they become apparent:

Light here is a property of presence, not of sources. The brighter you attend to something, the more lit it becomes. Look away from the altar and it goes dimmer; this is subtle enough that you might not notice for a while.

The water does not wet. You can wade in it and step out dry. It wets the fern, though, and the moss. It has opinions about what to moisten.

The animals outnumber the plants by orders of magnitude, which is ecologically absurd — there is nothing here for them to eat, no visible food web, and yet they are thriving, abundant, clearly a full ecosystem with predator and prey relationships that occur just out of sight. You'll hear, sometimes, a very small death. You'll never find the body.

The cavity is small — you could cross it in fifteen paces — but the far wall is always a little farther than you remember. Not much. Just enough that you stop trusting the measurement.

And the whole thing is new. The stone is young, the erosion has barely begun, the shape of the place is still being argued out by whatever geology is running here. In a century it might be a proper cave, with stalactites and a river and a real mouth opening to the surface. For now it is a draft of itself. The scattered structures — the shelf, the bowl, the cloth — suggest someone has been visiting this draft, leaving small offerings, as if the place were a creature being tamed, or tended, or thanked.

You could sit on the shelf. The moths would continue their drifting. The beetles would continue their quiet migration on their migrating wall. The breath from the hidden recess would continue its slow arrival, slow departure. The light would brighten, very slightly, around wherever you rested your gaze.

After a while — and this is the last thing, and the most reliable — you would notice that the cavity is, in some small way, arranging itself around you. The fern leaning. The water smoothing. The third mushroom stalk, which was not there when you came in, now there, placed at an elegant interval from the other two.

The place is learning you. It has not finished being a place, and it is using your visit to decide what kind of place to become.
