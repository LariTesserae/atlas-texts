# The Brasswing Escarpment

The plateau sits at nine kilometers. It should not be breathable here, and strictly speaking, it isn't — but the air has been persuaded otherwise. A person standing on the ridge feels the pressure of a seacoast, tastes salt and iron on the tongue, and watches their breath not fog because the breath is warmer than the body that made it.

Heat rises from below in sheets. The stone is dark basalt veined with something that looks like copper and behaves like copper but hums at a frequency just under hearing, a frequency you feel in your molars. This is the substrate. It was laid down, or grown, or decided, long enough ago that the weather has had its say: every surface is rounded, every edge has been talked out of being sharp. Rain and steam have carved shallow runnels that all lead, eventually, to the same places — the catchment pools — because the stone, at some scale finer than the eye, wants them to.

Water is everywhere but rarely in straightforward form. It weeps from the undersides of overhangs in slow beads that hang, hesitate, and fall sideways before they reach the ground. It sheets down the south faces after the afternoon condensations. It pools in basins lipped with a pale mineral crust that tastes, if you are fool enough to taste it, faintly of ozone and lemon. The marshes — yes, marshes, at this altitude — occupy the terraces: flat shelves stepped down the mountain's flank where the humming stone collects heat and holds standing water at body temperature year-round.

The vegetation is the reason everyone comes, and the reason no one stays.

Fern-analogues the size of cathedral doors unfurl from the marsh edges, their fronds a blue so deep it reads as black until sunlight hits them edge-on and they flare turquoise. Between the ferns grow the brasswings: trees, more or less, but their trunks are hollow resonators and their leaves are thin metallic sheets that ring faintly in the wind, a constant layered chiming that is the keynote of the whole landscape. Vines with fat translucent leaves climb everything; inside each leaf, a droplet of water catches the light and refracts it, so that a brasswing grove in afternoon sun appears to be hung with ten thousand small lenses, each throwing a shifting coin of brightness onto the moss below.

The moss is not moss. It is a carpet of something that photosynthesizes and also, by some accounting, remembers. Step on it and your footprint remains for about a week, a darker green in the shape of your boot, before it forgets you.

Fauna thickens as you descend into the terraces. Long-legged wading things with four eyes and iridescent throat-sacs pace the shallow pools, spearing whatever it is they spear. Flocks of small flyers — not birds, not bats; something with membrane wings and a body like a lacquered seed — sweep through the brasswing canopy and make the leaves chime harder. In the deeper water, slow shapes move. No one has gotten a clean look. The wading things seem unafraid.

The sound: loud is the right word, but loud in a layered, tolerable way. The brasswings ring. The condensation drips off every overhang in a pattern that is almost but not quite rhythmic. The flyers chirr. Somewhere always a distant rockfall or a throat-sac call or the hiss of a steam vent opening. A person learns to talk through it, or under it, after a day.

And then, the built.

Scattered. That is the honest word. There is a tower on the far ridge that is not local stone — it is some kind of grown ceramic, cream-colored, seventy meters tall, tapering, with no visible openings. There is a path of flat hexagonal tiles that runs for perhaps two kilometers along the eastern terrace and then simply stops. There are three small domes near the largest catchment pool, each the size of a tool shed, each warm to the touch and humming at the same frequency as the basalt. And there is, on the plateau summit, a ring of twelve standing objects — not stones; they are too regular, too deliberately not-stones, and they cast shadows at noon that do not match the sun's position by about four degrees.

No one built any of this recently. No one built any of this in a way current engineering can describe. The ceramic tower has been chemically sampled and the report, famously, concluded that the tower was "older than its own material," which satisfied no one but has not been rescinded.

The working theory — and it is only that — is that the entire escarpment is a single slow machine. The stone that should not hum, the air that should not be breathable, the water that pools exactly where the basins want it, the moss that remembers footsteps, the leaves that ring in a key that does not quite belong to any human scale — these are the surface manifestations of something running underneath, very slowly, on a cycle measured in centuries. The brasswings bloom once every forty-one years. The deepest pool drains and refills on a schedule that matches no astronomical period anyone has identified. The standing ring's shadow-offset has shifted by half a degree in the last two hundred years of measurement.

Whatever the machine is doing, it is in no hurry.

Living things have moved in to take advantage: the wading birds, the flyers, the shapes in the water, the ferns and brasswings and memory-moss. They have been here long enough to have become part of the place's balance. They, too, are unhurried. The heat is constant. The water is constant. The chiming is constant. A naturalist who visited the escarpment for a season wrote in her notes that she began, after about three weeks, to feel that her own heartbeat was out of tempo with the landscape, and that the landscape was correct and she was wrong.

She stayed another month adjusting.

At dusk, the whole escarpment exhales. The day's accumulated heat rises off the terraces as a visible shimmer, the brasswings ring louder for about twenty minutes, and the pools give off a faint phosphorescence along their rims. The sun, when it sets, sets behind the ceramic tower, and for perhaps ninety seconds the tower is perfectly backlit and perfectly opaque, a shape cut out of the sky.

Then the stars come through the humid air thick and close, and the standing ring's shadows, for one hour after full dark, are very slightly visible on the ground — lit, apparently, by nothing.
