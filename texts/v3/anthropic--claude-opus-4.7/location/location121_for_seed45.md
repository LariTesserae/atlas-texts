# The Unfinished Summit

Begin high. Keep going. Higher than that.

The place begins where mountains would end, if mountains here had agreed to end. They haven't. The range is still arguing with itself about what shape to take, and the argument is loud in the bones even when the air is quiet.

---

There is no water. Not ice, not vapor, not the memory of water in the rocks. The stone is dry the way a struck match is dry — something has been burned out of it, or was never allowed in. When you pick up a fragment (and there are fragments everywhere, wrist-sized, skull-sized, house-sized) it is warm. Not sun-warm. Warm from beneath, from the ongoing argument, warm the way a loaf is warm an hour out of the oven. The warmth is even. It does not come from a direction.

The darkness is not night. Night has a sky. This has a ceiling of something — ash-haze, or the shadow of peaks that continue past where peaks should stop, or simply the altitude's refusal of light. A dim ochre glow leaks up from crevices and from the undersides of certain cliffs, as if the mountain is lit from its own interior and only the stray beams escape. You can see your hand. You can see the next ridge. You cannot see the horizon, because there isn't one; the land keeps tilting upward into the haze and then is gone.

---

The turbulence is the main fact.

Not wind — the air barely moves. The turbulence is geological. The mountains are *assembling.* A shelf of basalt the size of a cathedral heaves up twenty meters over the course of an afternoon, groaning, settling, then cracks clean down the middle as a second shelf shoulders it aside from below. Scree skitters upward against slopes because the slopes are rotating. A spire that was a spire at dawn is a saddle by what passes for dusk, and by the next cycle it is a spire again, differently oriented, with a different grain.

Every few minutes something large moves. Every few seconds something small does. A pebble hops. A plate the size of a palm flips over with a dry tick. A hairline seam opens across a boulder and the two halves ease apart like a book being opened by someone patient. Nothing is weathered. Every edge is fresh. Every face was, recently, inside.

And yet: it is mostly quiet. That is the strange thing. The scale of the violence suggests a constant roar, but most of the motion happens too slowly for sound, or through a medium too stiff for sound to carry well. What you hear instead are isolated events — a single *crack* that echoes for a long time against surfaces that keep changing shape, so the echo wanders and gets lost. A low sustained hum from somewhere beneath your feet that could be the mountain thinking. The tick of a cooling fragment. Your own breathing, loud.

---

There is something that could be called vegetation, if you were generous.

In the warm crevices, in the seams where the ochre light leaks out, something grows that is not quite fungus and not quite mineral. Pale filaments, the color of old ivory, branching in geometries that suggest they are solving a problem — how to reach the warmth, how to anchor in stone that will not stay still. They are brittle. They are also fast. You can watch a filament extend a millimeter in the time it takes to decide whether you are really watching it. When a rock shifts and crushes a colony, the remaining filaments elsewhere in the same seam seem to *know*, and accelerate. Whether they are alive in any sense you would recognize is not a question the place will answer.

They are the only soft thing. Otherwise: stone, stone, stone.

No animals. None. Not a track, not a shell, not a husk. If anything moves besides the mountain itself, it is not moving here.

---

The built things are the hardest to understand.

On a bench of level rock — level for now — stands a low wall. Dry-stacked, waist-high, perhaps ten meters long. The stones are chosen; they fit. Someone fitted them. The wall does not enclose anything. It does not block anything. It simply stands on the bench, and the bench itself is in the process of tilting by perhaps a degree per day, so the wall is beginning to lean.

Further on, a cairn. Further still, a single doorway — two uprights and a lintel — framing a view of nothing in particular, or of whatever the mountain decides to put behind it next. Half a kilometer up the slope, a shallow carved basin in a boulder, dry, of course, dry as everything is dry, but carved with care, with a lip and a spout that points downhill toward a place where water would go if water came.

Whoever made these things did not make many of them, and did not make them recently, and is not here now. The marks are not degraded — nothing degrades here, erosion hasn't started — so there is no way to tell if the builders left a year ago or ten thousand. The wall is as fresh as the rock that just cracked beside it. Time in this place does not leave the usual receipts.

You begin to suspect the builders were not marking territory or making shelter. They were making *references.* A wall to say: here, a wall could be. A doorway to say: here, a threshold. A basin to say: if ever there is water, let it know where to rest. Instructions for a world that has not finished arriving.

---

The rules, as best you can infer them:

Everything is warm and dry. Everything is high. Everything is new — not young, exactly, but *new*, in the sense that a thing fresh from a mold is new, with flash still on its edges. The mountain is not eroding because the mountain is not finished being made. Erosion is what happens to finished things. This place has not yet earned its weathering.

The dynamism is not destruction. Watch long enough and you see it: the heaving plates, the rotating slopes, the spires that become saddles — they are *settling into* something. The argument has a direction, even if the direction is too slow and too large for you to read. Somewhere, eventually, there will be a shape here. A mountain, a range, a country. For now there is only the coming-into-being, warm and patient and loud in spasms and quiet in between, lit from inside its own seams, threaded with filaments that are trying to keep up.

And the walls, the doorways, the careful empty basin — those are from someone who came through earlier, while it was still being made, and left small marks of confidence. *This will be a place. When it is, let these stones already be here.*

You could add one. A stone on a cairn. The mountain would accept it without comment and carry it upward into whatever it is becoming.
