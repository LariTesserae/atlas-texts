# The Sumpwerks

You come upon it the way you come upon a held breath — first the smell, which is hot iron and wet moss in equal measure, then the sound, which arrives before the seeing: a low industrial respiration, the rhythmic *hush-clank, hush-clank* of pistons working somewhere in the green dimness, overlaid by the constant percussion of water on metal, water on stone, water on water.

The place sits in a shallow basin, no more than a quarter-mile across — you could walk its rim in twenty minutes if the footing allowed, which it does not. Call it a hamlet's worth of ground, but stacked. Stacked is the word. Whoever built here built upward into the canopy of itself, three and four stories of riveted housings and standpipes and catwalks threaded between and through the trunks of trees that were already old when the first bolt was driven. The trees did not move for the architecture; the architecture made its peace with the trees. Pipes elbow around boles. A boiler-house leans on a buttress root the size of a wagon. Brass flanges are swallowed halfway into bark that has grown over them like slow lips.

Everything weeps. This is the first rule. The air itself carries water — not rain, not mist exactly, but a saturation that beads on every cold surface and runs. The iron weeps rust-colored tears down its flanks. The copper weeps green. The stone foundations, where you can see them through the moss, weep clear water that pools in the cupped palms of erosion and overspills into the next cup down, and the next, until the whole basin is a slow vertical conversation of trickles. There is no animal here to drink any of it. No frog in the standing water. No midge in the warm air. Just the patient hydraulics of the place talking to itself.

The vegetation is thick but specific: liverworts in mats like wet velvet, ferns whose fronds have grown to fit the spaces between pipes, a kind of dark ivy that has learned to climb the warm flues and lives there preferentially, glossy and prospering. Mosses upholster every horizontal surface that isn't actively hot. Where a steam-pipe runs, a halo of paler green marks the radius of its warmth, and beyond that halo the moss resumes its darker fur. No flowers that you can see. Nothing that needs a pollinator. The plants here reproduce by spore and runner and patience.

The light is the light of a long late afternoon under a high canopy — dim, but not gloomy; greenish, filtered, and supplemented in places by the orange flicker of firebox doors and the steady amber of a few electric bulbs in wire cages, their glass furred with condensation. You can read by it if you stand close. You cannot read by it from across the clearing.

The machines. This is what takes the longest to understand. They are working — that is unmistakable, the pistons stroke, the flywheels turn, governors spin lazily in their brass cages, and somewhere a low generator hum underlies everything — but they are working at *something the place itself is doing*. There is no foreman. There is no shift. The fireboxes are stoked, but you cannot find by whom; when you look closely at one, the coals are damp peat, and they smolder rather than burn, and a brass arm on a slow cam reaches down every forty seconds or so and turns them. The arm has been turning them for a long time. Its pivot is worn to a polished hollow.

What the engines pump, as best you can tell, is the water itself — lifting it from the low pools at the basin's floor, running it through condensers and separators in the upper galleries, and returning it, slightly altered, to the upper trickles to begin its descent again. The whole basin is a closed circuit of water made to climb so that it can fall, and the falling is the point, and the climbing is the cost. Nothing is produced. Nothing leaves. The dynamic is slow: a given drop of water, you suspect, might take a week to make the full circuit, dawdling in the warm cisterns, sweating through the copper coils, condensing on the underside of a catwalk before letting go.

This is the strangeness of the place, and it took you a while to name it: the industry here is not extractive. It does not serve anything outside itself. It is a town-sized devotional engine, built once with great care and then left to weather, and it has weathered into a kind of equilibrium where every leak is anticipated, every drip falls into a basin shaped to receive it, every rust-stain follows a groove that the rust-stain made over a century of running. The erosion is not damage. The erosion is the *finish*. The place has settled into being exactly itself, and the machines and the mosses and the standing water have agreed on terms.

The sound, once you stop hearing it as noise, resolves into parts: the hush-clank of the main pump, slow as a sleeping heart, perhaps twelve strokes a minute; the higher tick of the cam-arms in the fireboxes; the sustained sibilance of steam bleeding from a relief valve somewhere overhead; and beneath all of these, the water — millions of small percussions, no two alike, a rain that has been falling indoors for a very long time. It is loud. You have to raise your voice. But it is not chaotic. It has a meter.

You will not find any bones here. No nests in the eaves of the boiler-house, no spiders in the corners of the catwalks. Whatever the place is, it is inhospitable to animal life in a way that goes beyond the warm wet air and the lack of forage — as if the *attention* of the place is so wholly turned inward, so wholly committed to its slow self-circulation, that nothing with a quicker pulse can find purchase in it. Even you, standing on the iron grating at the entry, feel your own heart rate drift downward to meet the pump.

The rules of the world here, as best you can articulate them:

Water is never lost, only delayed. Heat is never wasted, only spread thin. What was built has become what grew, and what grew has become what holds the building up. There is no clock but the cam-arm, and the cam-arm is in no hurry. If you stay, you will be made welcome in the way the moss is made welcome — by not being asked to leave, and by being slowly, patiently, incorporated into the circuit.

You can leave. The path you came in by is still visible, just. But you will notice, as you turn to go, that your coat is already heavier with the wet of the place, and that a small green bloom has started on the brass button at your cuff, and that this does not particularly alarm you.
