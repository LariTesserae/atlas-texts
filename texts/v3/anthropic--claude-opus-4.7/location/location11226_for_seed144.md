# The Calderas of Ish-Tarem

You climb out of the cloud at about four thousand meters and the air goes thin and bright and almost too hot to breathe. The range keeps going. Another two thousand meters of basalt and red scoria above you, and the peaks aren't peaks but rims — the whole spine of the range is hollow, a chain of old calderas linked shoulder to shoulder like vertebrae.

Here is the strange thing, and you notice it before you understand it: the heat is wrong for the altitude. At six thousand meters you should be cold. Instead the rock burns through bootsoles. The calderas are still warm from below — not erupting, not even smoking much, just radiating the slow heat of magma that settled a long time ago and never fully cooled. The range has been like this for a geological while. Long enough that the cinder cones have rounded, the lava tongues have grown skins of crumbled glass, the wind has carved soft scallops into the tuff. Established. Weathered into its own shape.

You walk the rim of the first caldera and look down.

It is full of life. Packed. That's the thing the parameters wouldn't have prepared you for — you'd expect a hot dry high place to be empty, and instead the bowl below you is dense as a tidepool. Every ledge, every cooled lava bench, every crack in the wall holds something. The vegetation is sparse in the sense that nothing is tall — no trees, barely any shrubs — but the ground is upholstered. Cushion plants the color of old bronze. Lichens in eight shades of orange. A small fleshy succulent that looks like fingers reaching up out of the ash. They grow on top of each other because the substrate is so warm and so mineral-rich that anything that can hold on, does.

And the fauna. The caldera floors swarm. There are lizards the length of your forearm with skin like wet slate, basking on rocks too hot for your hand. There are colonies of something between a marmot and a pangolin — armored, social, whistling to each other in three-note phrases. Insects in clouds over the warm patches. Birds — pale, long-legged, walking the rims like storks — that dive down into the bowl and come up with mouths full. A kind of pale snake that swims through the loose scoria the way an eel swims through silt.

The water. This is where it gets clever. There's almost no surface water — the heat takes care of that — but every dawn the cold air from the upper atmosphere meets the warm walls of the calderas and there is a brief, intense condensation. Dew beads on every leaf, every lichen, every armored back. The whole range sweats for about forty minutes after sunrise. The animals come out for it. The plants drink it through their skins. By eight in the morning it's gone, and the day's economy of moisture is done. There are also seeps — places where the rock weeps hot mineral water that pools in basins and steams and stinks faintly of sulfur and iron. Around each seep, a denser ring of life: red algae, a kind of crab-thing that has no business being at this altitude, the lizards drinking with their tongues.

And then — and you almost miss them at first because the scale of the landscape swallows them — the human marks. They are scattered, faint. On the inner wall of the second caldera there's a stairway cut into the rock, switchbacking down maybe a hundred meters, ending at a platform with a ring of black iron set into the stone. On the platform sits a machine. Squat, riveted, the color of dried blood where the paint hasn't flaked. A condenser. It has copper fins and a chimney and a collection vessel below, and it is *working* — humming very quietly, drawing the morning dew out of the air faster than the rock can, funneling it into a pipe that disappears into the wall.

There are more. Once you know what to look for, you see them everywhere — one per caldera, sometimes two. Some are larger, with smokestacks venting pale vapor. Some are wrapped in tarps and look unused for a season. Pipes thread between them, following the contours, running in shallow trenches scratched into the tuff. Whoever built this network worked at industrial seriousness — these are real engines, manufactured somewhere with foundries — but they put almost nothing else here. No towns. No houses. Just the machines and the pipes and, every so often, a small stone hut where someone might sleep for a night while servicing a unit.

The sound is the last thing to register, because there is so little of it. At this altitude the air carries thin. The whistles of the pangolin-marmots reach you from a long way off and seem closer than they are. The condensers hum but you only hear them when you're within twenty meters. Mostly there is wind, and the wind up here is not the howling kind — it's a steady, dry pour, almost soundless except where it finds an edge to whistle on. You can hear your own pulse. You can hear, from the caldera below, the click of lizard claws on scoria, and farther down, the slow drip of a seep into its basin.

The dynamism is in the cycles. Morning condensation, midday bake, afternoon updraft when the heated air finally tears itself free of the bowls and a column of dust and insects rises out of each caldera like breath. Evening cool — relative — when the snakes hunt and the birds come back to the rims. Night, when the rock still glows faintly in the infrared and the small armored mammals pile together in the warm cracks and the condensers tick as they cool. Then dawn, then the sweating range, then again.

The weirdness is mild but persistent. It's the wrongness of the temperature for the height. It's the way the pipes go somewhere — they all trend west, down the spine, toward something you can't see from here. It's the lizards' eyes, which are the same red as the rusted machines, and which seem to track the machines specifically, as if waiting for something. It's the fact that one of the calderas, the fourth one you pass, has no animals in it at all. Just the plants, and a single very large condenser at its center, much newer than the others, and a smell you can't place.

You don't go down into that one.

You keep walking the rim, west, following the pipes, into a range that goes on for as far as you can see — caldera after caldera, each one a hot crowded bowl of life under a thin sky, each one quietly milked by a machine, the whole system breathing in the cold and breathing out something else, somewhere, for someone, for a reason that hasn't yet made itself known to you.
