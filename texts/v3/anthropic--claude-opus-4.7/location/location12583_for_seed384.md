# The Drowned Works at Cinder Shoal

Begin with the cold. Not the sharp cold of mountain air, but the slow, settled cold of deep water that has been cold for so long it has forgotten the possibility of warmth. The temperature here is a property of the place, not a weather. It is two degrees above freezing in the upper levels and barely above it at the floor, and it does not vary by season because there is no season here, only the long slow turning of pressures.

You arrive — let us say you arrive — through a fissure in the silt ceiling, descending into a basin perhaps four hundred meters across. This is the entire world for now. The basin has walls, real walls of cracked basalt smoothed at the edges by ten thousand years of slow current, and the walls describe a rough oval, and within that oval everything has been made and remade so many times that the original purposes have dissolved.

Because this was a city once. Or a factory-town. Or both — the distinction was never clean even when it lived. Now it is submerged, and has been submerged long enough that the submersion is the form, not an interruption of some other form. The water doesn't visit the architecture; the water *is* part of it, pressing into every brick-joint and crank-housing, holding the whole arrangement up as much as gravity holds it down.

The light comes from below. This is the first strangeness. Somewhere in the floor — beneath the silt, beneath the rust-strata — there is still power. Furnaces or filaments or something that was meant to be banked for an hour and was instead banked forever. The glow leaks up through grates and split flagstones in a sourceless red-orange, the color of an ember seen through closed eyelids. It is not enough light to see by, exactly. It is enough to know where the floor is. Above your head, two meters up, the water shades to indigo and then to black, and you cannot see the ceiling of silt you came through.

The buildings are close-packed. They lean on each other. This was a place built dense, alley on alley, and now the alleys are flooded canyons three meters wide where a person might swim shoulder-brushing wall on either side. The walls are brick that has gone soft as bread-crust, dressed in a fine pelt of pale anemones and what might be tube worms but are not, quite — they pull in when you pass, slow as someone deciding. The vegetation here, what little of it exists, is this: stringy black weed in the lee of chimneys, a few pale stalks like asparagus growing from cracks where the floor-glow leaks, and on the south side of the basin a single colony of something resembling kelp but stiffer, ribbed, that creaks faintly as the currents work it.

The machines.

This is where the place becomes itself. The town was industrial when it drowned, and the machines are still here, and some of them are still working. Not all. Most are seized solid, fused inside their housings, statues of intention. But here and there a flywheel turns — slowly, slowly, with the patience of geology — driven by some pressure differential or thermal trickle that the original engineers never planned for but that the long erosion has rerouted into. A pump in the eastern quarter exhales a thin stream of bubbles once every forty seconds. A loom-frame in what might have been a textile hall ratchets one tooth forward each tide. There is a great wheel, eight meters across, in the building at the basin's center, and it is rotating. You can watch it for an hour and see no motion, but if you mark its position against a brick and come back the next day, it has moved.

These motions do not produce the sounds you would expect. The water swallows almost everything. The basin is nearly silent — that is the second strangeness, an industrial site at near-zero sound. You hear your own pulse. You hear, sometimes, a single clink, far off, of metal settling. You hear the kelp creak. You hear, very rarely, a low groan from one of the larger structures, a tone so deep it is felt in the breastbone before the ear. That is all.

The fauna are rare and shy. A blind shrimp the color of wet paper, picking at the anemones. A fish — once, only once on any given visit — that drifts through an alley and is gone, and you are not certain you saw it, and it had too many fins or not enough, and you do not want to think about it longer than that. Something lives in the great wheel, or behind it. You will not meet it. It has business that is not yours.

The rules of this place, as best they can be stated:

The light comes from below and does not change. The machines move on timescales that are not human. The water is cold and old and does not mix with itself the way water elsewhere does — there are strata, and you can feel them as you swim down, a colder layer at the knees, a warmer one (relatively) at the chest, a colder still at the head, as if the basin remembers separate centuries at separate depths. Sound does not carry far, and what does carry is always lower than it began. Nothing here is in a hurry, but nothing here is finished, either. The dynamism is real — pumps pump, wheels turn, the anemones open and close, the silt above slowly thickens — but it is dynamism running at the speed of rust.

It is a city still at work, with no workers, in the dark, in the cold, for no one. The water holds it. The water has held it long enough that holding it is no longer a verb but a relation, the way a riverbed holds a river: not by gripping, but by being the shape the river requires.

If you stay long enough — and people have, some have stayed too long — you begin to feel that the basin is not abandoned. It is simply operating at a tempo that excludes you. Come back in three hundred years and the great wheel will have completed another revolution, and something will have been produced by it, and the something will be waiting on the floor, glowing faintly, for whoever the next visitor is.

That is the place. Cold, close, dim, slow, intricate, almost silent, and at work.
