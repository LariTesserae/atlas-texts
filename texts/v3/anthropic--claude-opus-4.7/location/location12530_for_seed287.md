# The Sweatworks

Down a sloped tunnel of red-orange brick, perhaps thirty meters below whatever surface there once was, the air thickens until it has weight. You taste it before you see anything — iron, wet leaf, hot oil, the faint sugar-rot of overripe fruit. The temperature climbs as you descend. By the time the passage opens, you are inside a chamber roughly the size of a parish church, and the heat is a physical pressure against the chest, somewhere near 44 or 45 degrees.

The chamber is what the place has decided to be after a very long time of being it.

The walls were once machinery. You can still read the intent in them — the curve of a flywheel housing, the grid-pattern of a furnace grate, a row of cast-iron valve wheels welded into a column. But the brick has slumped around the metal the way wax slumps around a wick, and the metal has bled rust down into the brick, and the rust has fed something, and now there are vines. Thick ropes of dark-leaved vine, the leaves waxy and almost black-green in the dim, growing from the gaps in the masonry, threaded *through* the machinery as if the machinery had grown up around them and not the other way around. In several places it is genuinely unclear which came first.

The light comes from three sources. First, a row of filament bulbs strung along a cable that loops between iron hooks set into the ceiling — most of them out, four or five still burning a low orange, the kind of orange that suggests the current is wrong, has been wrong for decades, and nobody is coming to fix it. Second, the boilers. Three of them, squat and riveted, set into the far wall, and through the small mica windows in their fronts you can see fire — not roaring fire, a deep slow fire, banked, the kind that has been burning so long it has forgotten how to go out. Third, certain of the leaves. A particular vine, broader-leaved than the others, gives off a weak greenish phosphorescence from the undersides of its foliage, strongest where it grows nearest the boilers, as if it were photosynthesizing the heat directly.

The sound is the thing that takes the longest to parse, because at first it is simply *loud* and you cannot find its sources. Then you begin to separate the layers. There is a low thrum, almost subsonic, that comes from the boilers — not a hum of operation but the sound of expansion, brick and iron settling against each other in microscopic adjustments. There is dripping, constant, from at least a dozen points, water condensing on the cool upper surfaces of pipes and falling onto hot lower surfaces, where it hisses and is gone. There is the creak and tick of metal that is always slightly too hot. And underneath all of it, a rhythmic *chuff* — long, slow, perhaps once every nine seconds — that seems to come from deeper still, from some chamber beyond this one, as if the lungs of the place were still working.

There is no animal life. There should be — the warmth, the damp, the vegetation all suggest it — but nothing flies, nothing scuttles, nothing watches you from a corner. Whatever the conditions are, they are wrong for animals in some way that is not immediately obvious. Perhaps it is the air, which has a faint metallic tang that catches in the back of the throat. Perhaps it is the slow pulse of the chuffing, which a small body would find unbearable. The absence has its own quality: the place feels *attended to*, but not inhabited.

The water collects in a basin at the chamber's center, formed where the floor has worn down around a drain that no longer drains. The basin is perhaps two meters across, irregular, and the water in it is dark and still and exactly body-temperature. Steam rises from it in thin curls that catch the bulb-light. Around the basin's lip, a ring of pale fungus grows, the only pale thing in the room. Vines lean toward the basin from every direction without quite touching its surface.

The built things, beyond the boilers and the bulbs, are small and personal and old. A wooden stool, the wood blackened with damp, set near one of the boiler windows as if someone used to sit and watch the fire. A row of three enamel mugs on a shelf, each with a different chip pattern. A page of paper pinned to the wall with a nail, the paper now so saturated with humidity and so darkened with whatever the air carries that no writing can be read, only the rectangle of the paper itself, a held shape. A length of canvas hose, coiled, with brass fittings green from verdigris.

The rules of the place, as best they can be read:

Nothing here is in a hurry. The vines grow, but slowly; the brick slumps, but over centuries; the fire burns, but at the lowest sustainable rate. The chamber has reached an equilibrium that involves heat and moisture and slow plant-growth and slow metal-decay, and it has been at this equilibrium long enough that the equilibrium is the chamber's actual nature. If you stayed an hour, nothing visible would change. If you stayed a year, you might notice that one of the vines had reached the basin.

The strangeness, which you feel before you can name, is this: the boilers feed the vines, and the vines, in some manner, feed the boilers. There is no woodpile, no coal bunker, no fuel source. The fire has been burning since before anyone alive remembers, and the only living things in the room are the vines, and the vines lean toward the boilers, and where they touch the hot iron their leaves blacken and curl and *are gone*, with no ash, and the fire does not diminish. It is a closed loop that should not close, and it has been closing, quietly, for a very long time.

The chuffing, from the deeper chamber, continues.

You can leave the way you came. The place will not notice. It has its work.
