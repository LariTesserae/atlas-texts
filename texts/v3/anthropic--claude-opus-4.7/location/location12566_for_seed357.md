# The Risen Loom

You come up over the last ridge and the valley opens — wide, hot, breathing.

What you are looking at is not a forest, though forest is the closest word. It is built. Every surface in the basin below has been *grown into shape* by something, and that something is the vegetation itself. There are no animals here. There never have been. The plants did all of it.

The valley floor sits high — you can feel it in your lungs, that thin scrape — and the air is wet enough that distances blur green-on-green. Steam lifts in slow columns from vents in the construction. The basin is young. You can see this in the way the towers haven't yet learned to lean; they stand straight up out of the marsh-floor with the sharp, unweathered edges of something that finished forming the week before last.

The towers are what you notice first. They are made of plant. Not *covered* in plant — made of it. Trunks the diameter of houses have braided themselves into fluted columns thirty, forty meters tall, and at their tops they flare into broad living capitals that drip a clear sap which is also, somehow, the rain. The sap falls. It hits the floor of the valley. It pools. From the pools, new shoots rise — fast, visible to the eye — and within an hour they have thickened into wrist-width, within a day they are structural, within a week they are wall.

The valley is rebuilding itself constantly. This is the first rule.

The second rule is the spacing. For something so total, the place is strangely open. The great green towers stand far apart — a hundred meters, two hundred — with long flats of low marsh-moss between them, lakes of standing warm water reflecting the bright overcast sky. The plants have decided, collectively, on a kind of cathedral logic: separation, breath, room for the heat to move. You can walk between the columns. You can see for kilometers down the nave of them, towers receding into the haze, each one a different chord of green: olive, jade, the near-black of wet kelp, a pale chartreuse where new growth is happening fastest.

The third rule is sound. The valley is loud. Not with animals — there are none, no birds, no insects, no skittering — but with the plants themselves. The towers groan as they grow. Sap-falls drum on the lower canopies. There is a wet ripping noise, irregular, somewhere always behind you, where a buttress is splitting because the tower above has decided to widen. The pools hiss with the heat coming up from below. And there is a sound you don't have a name for, a low woody chorus that comes from the joints where one tower's aerial roots have begun braiding into a neighbor's flank: a slow keening that rises and falls like a held breath. The valley sings to itself in the way a rope creaks under load.

There is no quiet hour. The growth does not stop at night, if there is a night here — the light only dims to a kind of green dusk, the towers continuing to luminesce faintly from the metabolic heat of their own construction.

Walk down into it. The marsh between towers is warm, knee-deep, the floor a felted mat of something between moss and lung tissue. It gives. Where you step, the surface registers your weight and *withdraws* — not in alarm, the plants have no nerves for alarm, but mechanically, the way a sponge accommodates a hand. Behind you the footprints fill in within minutes.

The towers near you are doing things. One has begun, in the last hour, to split its capital into two capitals, the crown rotating slowly apart like a flower opening in time-lapse, and from the wound a clear thick fluid sheets down its trunk and ribs the air with steam. Another, further off, has fallen. You didn't see it fall but the crash is still echoing — a column has come down across the marsh, and already its length is being colonized by the shoots of its neighbors, who reach across the gap with pale exploratory tendrils, eating the fallen one and re-using it. By tomorrow there will be a new tower where the old one fell across, fed from the corpse.

Nothing here is stable. Everything here is built. The two facts do not contradict each other; they are the same fact, observed from different distances. The valley is a city in permanent construction, where the masons are also the bricks, and the bricks are also the weather, and the weather is also the architecture's own sweat.

There are no tools. There have never been tools. The plants do not need tools because they *are* the work and the worker. You will not find a path, because paths are a memory of feet, and there are no feet here but yours, and yours will be erased by morning.

Stand still long enough and you will feel the floor under you flex with the slow pulse of the whole basin — the tide of the sap-system, the valley inhaling once every several minutes, the towers swaying microscopically in unison.

It is hot. It is wet. It is loud. It is sparse and total at once: a built world with great open rooms between its pillars, every surface alive and remaking itself, no creature in it but the architecture, which is also the population, which is also the storm.

You came up over the ridge an hour ago. The ridge behind you, when you turn to look, has a new tower on it that wasn't there when you arrived.
