# The Cistern Terraces

It is built — that is the first thing. There is no ground under it that wasn't placed there by hands. The whole shoulder of the mountain, from the cloud-line up to where the air thins past use, has been faced in pale fitted stone. Limestone, mostly, and a softer chalky rock that takes water into itself and gives it back slowly. The terraces step up the slope in tight ranks, each one perhaps the height of a man, each one brimming.

Brimming is the word. Every terrace is a basin, and every basin is full. The water sits flush with the lip of its containing wall, held there by some patient calculation of inflow and overspill, so that the whole mountainside reads, from a distance, as a staircase of mirrors. When the sun is high — and it is high often here, the light is fierce and white at this altitude, unfiltered, the kind of brightness that makes the stones ache — each terrace throws back a slab of sky. You climb through reflected cloud.

There are no plants. This is deliberate, I think, though I can't say by whom. The water is too clean, too tended; nothing has been allowed to root. A faint slick of something — call it a film, a pale biofilm, the simplest possible life — coats the submerged stone in places, giving the water a milky cast where it lies deepest. In the shallowest basins you can see the chisel marks on the floor as clearly as if they were cut yesterday. The whole construction is young. The corners are sharp. The mortar, where there is mortar, hasn't yet darkened.

The terraces are packed against each other with no wasted span. Where one basin ends, the retaining wall of the next begins; there is no path between them at ground level. To move through the place you must walk along the tops of the walls themselves, narrow as a forearm, with still water to either side at knee height. The walls are dry-laid in most stretches, the stones shaped to fit so closely that water seeps through only as a slow weeping, beading on the lower face and tracking down in pale mineral lines. Over centuries these weeps will build curtains of travertine and the whole architecture will soften, but that is centuries away. For now everything is edges.

It is cool here. Not cold — the sun sees to that, and the water holds the day's warmth into evening — but cool enough that the air feels thin and clean in the lungs, and the stones are cold to the palm even at noon. Mist rises from the basins in the early hours, a low even fog that fills each terrace to its lip and then, as the sun climbs, peels off in sheets and goes upward. You can stand on a wall and watch the mountain exhale.

The sounds are small. The chief sound is overspill — every basin pours, somewhere along its length, into the one below, and these pourings are quiet, a finger's width of water sliding over a smoothed sill, multiplied by thousands across the mountainside into a constant low whispering that you stop hearing within an hour. Beneath that, the occasional plink of a drop falling from a weeping wall into a still surface. No wind, mostly; the terraces baffle it. No birds. No insects skating the water. No frogs. Nothing that should be in water is in this water.

Who built it is not clear, and may not matter. The tools were simple — chisel, plumb, level, a great deal of rope and a great deal of patience. No metal beyond the chisel-tips, and those are bronze or something like it, judging from the green stains where a tool was once left on a wall and forgotten. The work is enormous in extent but the methods are humble, and the pace must have been very slow. A terrace a season, perhaps. A generation for a hundred terraces. The whole flank of the mountain must represent something on the order of a thousand years of unbroken intention.

And the intention is the strange part. There is no obvious use. The water is not channeled down to a city — there is no city visible, no fields below, only more terraces fading into haze. It is not drinking water, exactly; nothing drinks here. It is not irrigation, with nothing to irrigate. The terraces simply hold water at altitude, in the bright cold air, in their thousands, catching the sky.

My best guess, standing on one of the higher walls with the wet light coming up at me from every side, is that the holding is the point. That somewhere, long ago, a decision was made that this mountain ought to wear water — ought to be sheathed in still bright water from its waist to its crown — and that the project has simply continued, terrace after terrace, in the absence of any reason to stop. The mountain is being slowly converted into a mirror. When it is finished — if it is ever finished — the peak itself will be the last dry thing, a single stone tooth above a staircase of held sky.

The weirdness of the place is quiet. It doesn't announce itself. It is only that after a few hours you realize you have not heard a living sound, not one, and that the water in every basin is exactly level with the water in every other basin of its rank, to a precision that no hand-tool should achieve, and that the light off all those surfaces has begun to feel less like reflection than like something the terraces are producing, a slow patient brightness pushed up out of the stone.

You leave by the way you came, walking the wall-tops down. Behind you the mountain keeps brimming, as it has for some long time, and as it will, at this rate of change, for some long time more.
