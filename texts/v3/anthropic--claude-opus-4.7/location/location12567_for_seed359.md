# The Vivarium of Soft Hours

It begins as a room, though it is not a room.

The walls are woven from what was, originally, rattan — long pale canes split and braided by hand, lashed with hemp cord that has gone honey-brown with age and oil from many fingers. But the weave is too tight to be only weave. Somewhere in its making the lattice forgot it was made and began behaving like a thing that grew. The canes branch where they should only cross. They put out small leaves in the warm pockets near the joins. If you press your palm to the wall, the wall presses back with a slow inhalation that takes most of a minute.

There is no water here. None pools, none drips, none runs. Yet the plants are lush — fat-leaved monstrosities and pale ferns and a kind of wide tongue-shaped vegetable that hangs in tiers from the ceiling-weave. They drink something else. Light, perhaps; or the breath of the walls; or the slow exhalations of the small animals that live in their crowns. The leaves are warm to the touch, almost feverish, and waxy with a sap that smells like cardamom and hot stone.

The temperature is the temperature of an animal — held there, regulated by something that is not weather. You sweat lightly within minutes of entering, and the sweat does not evaporate so much as it is *received*, drawn into the air by the air's quiet appetite.

The floor is built. Everything is built. Tiles of fired clay, hand-pressed, each one slightly different — a thumbprint here, a leaf-shape there, the marks of someone's intention preserved in the bake. They are laid in a spiral that begins at the room's center, where there is a low platform of the same clay, polished smooth by use. The spiral is the only thing in the room that admits to having a center. Everything else pretends to extend forever in its small space.

Because the space *is* small. Twelve paces across, perhaps. A cathedral compressed to the size of a pantry. And yet —

And yet the far wall is, when you look at it sidelong, much farther than twelve paces. The ferns between you and it become layered, become a forest, become a procession of fern-generations receding into a green dimness that contains, faintly, the silhouettes of larger creatures moving between the trunks. Look directly and the wall returns. Twelve paces. A woven cane lattice. A small frog the color of moss watching you from a knot.

The frogs are everywhere. And the beetles — iridescent, slow, walking in pairs along the cane-ribs as if patrolling. And something larger that you only ever see the tail of, sliding behind a leaf. And birds, though they do not fly; they walk along the upper weave with grave deliberation, heads cocked, and when they open their beaks no sound emerges but the room briefly smells of rain on warm wood.

This is one of the rules. Sound is sparse here. The animals are abundant but quiet. The frogs do not croak; they hum, almost subaudibly, and only when touched. The beetles tick once an hour, in unison, like a clock with too few hands. The loudest noise is the slow flex of the walls breathing, and even that is a sound you feel in your chest before you hear it in your ears.

The light comes from the leaves themselves — not glowing, exactly, but holding the light they were given at some earlier hour and releasing it gradually, so that the room is always lit as if by late afternoon, regardless of when you arrived or how long you stay. The shadows are long and soft and lie in directions that do not agree with each other.

Time moves slowly here, or moves at its normal pace while pretending not to. A leaf you watch will not visibly unfurl, but if you look away and look back, it has. The clay tiles record the passage of feet by deepening their polish, but only over years. Something is being cultivated, patiently, by hands that may or may not still be present. The tools are simple — a bone awl propped in a corner, a coil of cord, a small clay bowl of waxy seed — and they look both ancient and recently used.

The reality of the place is thin in patches. In one corner, the weave admits a glimpse of another vivarium, identical but mirrored, where a figure who might be you is just turning away. In another, a fern's frond, traced from tip to base with the eye, becomes a sentence in a language you almost read, and then becomes a fern again. The platform at the spiral's center, if you sit on it, feels like sitting on the palm of something. The something is patient. It has been waiting for someone to sit, and is in no hurry now that someone has.

Nothing here is in a hurry. The cycles are long. The frogs will molt next century. The walls are slowly braiding themselves tighter, one cane-twitch per season, toward some final configuration that the builders — if there were builders, if the room did not simply assemble itself out of the intention to be a room — toward some configuration that the builders may have intended or may have only hoped for.

You can leave whenever you like. The door is wherever you decide it is. But the leaves are warm, and the light is the light of an hour that does not end, and the small green frog on the wall is still watching you, and has not yet decided whether you are a guest or a part of the weave.
