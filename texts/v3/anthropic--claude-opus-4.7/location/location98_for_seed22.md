# The Hollow Verge

The valley opens wide — wide enough that you cannot see where it ends, only that the canopy climbs out of it on both sides and then folds back toward a horizon hazed with green steam. It is warm in the way an animal's mouth is warm. The air holds moisture but doesn't release it; instead it beads on leaf-undersides, on the backs of your hands, on surfaces of things that should not logically be wet.

The vegetation is thick past any ordinary reckoning — ferns the size of open parasols, trunks that climb smooth and tilted as though the land shrugged them up last week and hasn't yet decided where they should stand. The ridgelines are sharp. The riverbeds are sharp. Nothing here has been worn down. Rock faces show glassy, recent fractures where something — some process — cut them clean and walked away. If you press your palm to a boulder, the edge is still a blade.

And yet, for all this abundance of leaf and stem, there is almost nothing in the space between. The valley is *hollow*. You can walk a kilometer between one trunk and the next. The lushness hugs the ground, hugs the cliffs, hugs the river — and in between: open air, open air, open air, for volumes you could fit a cathedral into, empty. It gives the place the feeling of a vast greenhouse whose glass has been taken away and whose specimens have been moved apart for better viewing. A museum with the walls dissolved.

The light is dim. Not because it is evening — it is always something like evening here — but because the sky above the canopy is a low luminous gray-green, as though the atmosphere itself were slightly thickened with spore or pollen or some finer, engineered particulate. What light reaches the valley floor has been filtered through three layers: canopy, haze, and a faint geometric shimmer that you will, eventually, learn to recognize.

Because this is where the place begins to turn strange.

The town — if that is what to call it — sits along the river in a loose ribbon some kilometers long. Structures of a pale matte material neither stone nor metal nor ceramic, each one lifted slightly off the ground as though reluctant to commit to it. They are not arranged in streets. They are arranged in something more like a score: clusters, rests, returning motifs. The buildings hum. They hum in chords. The chords change when you walk between them, not reacting to you exactly, but as if you have crossed into a different measure of the piece they are playing.

And this is the source of the sound, though not the whole of it.

The sound here is *deafening*, though not in any single register. A person standing in the valley is struck by it from every axis at once: the hum of the structures; the roaring insect-chorus from the green (fauna is abundant here, though you will rarely see a large animal — the biomass is weighted heavily toward the small, the swarming, the bright); the river, which does not flow so much as *pulse*, surging and ebbing on some tidal cycle that has nothing to do with any moon; the occasional long, low tone that comes from nowhere locatable and seems to originate in the chest of whoever is listening; and above all, the sky, which *rings*. Softly, continuously, the gray-green dome emits a tone like a wet finger on crystal, and if you listen too long you begin to understand that the tone is not constant — it is composed — it is language at a pitch and tempo you are not built to parse.

The technology that makes this place is not visible. That is what gives it away. Ordinary advanced construction shows itself: cables, panels, seams, the marks of tool on matter. Here there are none. The matte buildings appear to have been *decided* rather than built. The river's tidal pulse suggests that somewhere, something is adjusting gravity at the scale of the valley. The sharpness of the cliffs — nine hundred meters of unweathered rock in a humid, rainy, biologically fecund world — suggests the land is new because it was *made* new, and recently, and perhaps is remade on a schedule. The shimmer in the haze, which sometimes resolves for half a second into faint lattices before dissolving, is probably the medium through which all of this coordination happens. A field. A substrate. An intention permeating the air.

The people — there are people, sparse and quiet, walking between the clusters on paths that aren't paths — don't seem to operate any of this. They live inside it the way a fish lives inside water. Whatever does the work was set in motion long enough ago that they have stopped remarking on it, or short enough ago that they are still waiting to see what it becomes. I cannot tell which. They wear loose pale fabrics and carry nothing. When two of them meet on the open ground between structures, they stand at a distance of perhaps twenty meters and do not speak, and after a moment the hum of the nearest building changes key, and they walk on.

The ecology is active but strange in its pacing. Things happen in pulses synchronized to the river's tide. When the water surges, the insect-chorus drops to a murmur and bright filaments uncoil from the undersides of the great ferns, reaching upward; when the water ebbs, the filaments retract and the insects roar back. Predator and prey move on the same pulse: a six-winged thing the size of a hand hovers at ebb, and at surge something in the canopy takes it without a sound. The rhythm is too regular to be evolved. Someone — something — is *conducting*.

Rain comes once a day, or what functions as a day. It is brief and warm and falls only over selected portions of the valley, leaving adjacent stretches dry. You can stand at the edge of a downpour and reach your hand in. The rain knows where its edge is. The dry ground on the far side of the edge has never, you suspect, been wet.

At what I will call dusk, though the light only darkens by a shade, the ringing of the sky deepens and the buildings go silent one by one in a sequence that moves up the valley faster than sound should travel. For perhaps three minutes everything holds. Even the insects. Even the river. The hollowness of the place becomes immense in those three minutes — you feel the full scale of all that empty air between all that living matter, and you understand that emptiness is not absence here but *room*, deliberate room, room left for something that comes through at intervals and needs space to pass.

Then the hum resumes, a half-step higher than before, and the valley goes on with its evening.

Nobody has told me what comes through. I don't think the people who live there know either. I think they were placed here, or grew here, to be the listeners — to hold the shape of a witness in a landscape that was built to be witnessed. The technology is doing something at the scale of the whole valley, at the scale of the haze, at the scale of the ringing sky, and the hollow between the trees is where it breathes.

It is a beautiful place. It is not quite a place for us. You could live there a long time and be happy and never once understand what you were inside of.
