# The Settled Plain

It is a continent of brass that has forgotten it was ever sharp.

Stand anywhere and the horizon curves away in low, exhausted swells — not hills exactly, more like the topography of an old coin that has passed through a billion hands. Every edge has been worn round. Every corner has been smoothed into the suggestion of a corner. The ground underfoot is a worked alloy, plated and re-plated so many times across deep ages that the layers have flowed into one another the way wax flows, and you can see them through the matte surface like the rings of a tree if you crouch and look sidelong.

No water. There hasn't been water here in whatever interval is long enough that the word "ago" stops meaning anything. But the metal is not dry the way a desert is dry; it's dry the way a finished thing is dry. The chemistry that runs the place uses something else — a slow exchange of charge between the plates, a dim current that you can sometimes feel in your teeth, a tide of electrons that moves through the substrate the way groundwater moves through soil somewhere younger.

The light is enormous and comes from above without quite coming from a sun. The whole sky is a pale, even brilliance, as though the atmosphere itself has been doped with luminance. Shadows are soft-edged and shallow. There is no time of day. There may never have been time of day. The brightness presses down with a weight you stop noticing after the first hour and then notice again, suddenly, in the second week, as a kind of tinnitus of the eyes.

The vegetation, what there is of it, grows out of the seams. Where two great plates of the substrate meet, something has taken root — filaments of a darker metal that branch and rebranch into low fans, no taller than a knee. They are not alive in any way a botanist would accept, but they pull current from the seam and use it to extend themselves, atom by atom, over centuries. In a hectare you might find six. In a square kilometer, two hundred. They are dense enough, considered in aggregate across the whole continent, that you are never out of sight of one; sparse enough that you can walk for an afternoon between them without your path crossing.

The fauna is rarer still. I have seen — the place has — small ambulatory things that look like folded paper made of the same brass, that move by tipping and unfolding and refolding, advancing perhaps a meter an hour. They graze on the filament-fans, or on the oxide that accumulates at the seams. Whether they reproduce I do not know. They may be the original population, the same individuals that were here when whatever ended ended.

It is warm. The substrate holds heat the way stone holds heat after a hot day, but there is no hot day; the warmth simply is, radiating up from below as much as down from above, so that a body in the middle of the plain feels itself suspended in a mild oven that does not cook so much as keep.

There is weather. This is the strangest thing about the place, and the truest sign that it is still doing something. The plates expand and contract on cycles too long to feel directly but quick enough that, standing in one spot for a week, you will notice the seams open and close, the filament-fans shift their angle, the paper-fauna migrate by a few meters toward whichever seam is widening. Sometimes a front moves across the continent — a band of slight cooling, or a region where the ambient current runs the other way — and you can watch it come from a hundred kilometers off as a faint discoloration in the brass, a tarnish-line advancing at the pace of a walking man. When it passes over you, your hair stands up. The fans all turn together, slowly, like a field of slow sunflowers. Then it moves on.

And the silence. The silence is the dominant fact. The substrate does not ring when struck; it absorbs. The fans make no sound when they shift. The paper-fauna make no sound when they move. Your own footsteps return to you muted, as though the plain has decided to forgive you the noise. The loudest thing in a day is your own breathing, and after a while even that seems indecorous, and you find yourself breathing shallowly, out of respect.

The whole place was built. This is the parameter that, once you accept it, organizes the rest. There is no bedrock under the brass — only more brass, more plates, more seams, all the way down as far as anyone has bothered to look, and the suspicion (the place's suspicion, not mine) that it is brass all the way through. Someone made this, at a scale and with a patience that current engineering cannot quite reach toward without flinching. Made it, and then either left, or finished, or became the filament-fans, or became the paper-fauna, or became the slow weather, or became the ambient brightness. The continent is a settled answer to a question we do not have access to.

What it is now: a vast, warm, bright, almost-silent, almost-flat, almost-empty, fully-made surface, going about the last and longest phase of its long life with great competence and no urgency at all.

If you sat down on it and did not get up, it would not mind. It would, eventually, incorporate you into a seam.
