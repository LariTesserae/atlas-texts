# The Cistern Hall

You find it by ducking under a low brick arch in the side of what looks, from above, like an ordinary green hummock in the floodplain. The hummock is the roof. The roof has been a roof for a very long time.

Inside is a single room, though "room" undersells it — it is a vaulted hall perhaps forty paces across, the ceiling held up by a forest of cast-iron columns, four ranks of them, fluted, painted once in a deep red lead that has long since gone to a soft oxide bloom. Between the columns: nothing. No furniture, no machinery on the floor, no debris. The space is almost entirely hollow. You could lie down anywhere and have the place to yourself by a wide margin.

The floor is flooded. Not deeply — ankle-deep at most, in some places only a film, a wet shine over the great pale flagstones. The water is warm, blood-warm, and absolutely clear, and it never drains and never rises. Somewhere under the stones there must be a balance: an inflow equal to an outflow, set in the long ago and undisturbed since. The columns rise from this shallow lake on plinths that are green with a velvet of moss and liverwort, and higher up they wear a fur of ferns — hart's tongue, maidenhair, a small soft polypody — that has rooted in every seam where the iron has rusted into a soil. The ferns drip. The whole ceiling drips. Condensation gathers on the bricks and falls in slow individual drops, each one ringing the standing water as it lands. The air is so saturated you can feel it bead on your eyelashes.

The vault itself is brickwork, Roman-bonded, half-lost under a hanging garden of bryophytes — long beards of moss, pale liverworts in rosettes, and in the deeper corners a phosphorescent-looking green algae mat that the lamps make jewel-bright. The hall is lit, you realize, by gas. Six iron sconces along the long walls hiss steadily, mantles glowing a clean daylight white behind clouded glass. The flames have been burning for as long as anyone remembers. The gas comes from somewhere — a pipe in the floor, a buried main running back to a works that may itself be derelict by now — but the supply is steady, the pressure is steady, the light is steady. Nothing in here changes.

And then there is the sound.

The sound is enormous. It fills the hall to the brim and overflows it. It is the sound of the engine.

The engine is in the next chamber, behind the far wall, behind a riveted iron door that nobody opens. You cannot see it, but you can feel it through the soles of your feet and through the water, which trembles in concentric rings around every column continuously, never going still. A great beam engine, by the sound of it — a deep regular thump-and-sigh, thump-and-sigh, the slow heartbeat of a Cornish pumping engine working at maybe twelve strokes a minute, each stroke followed by the long exhale of steam and the chitter of valve gear. Underneath that, the constant white roar of a boiler at pressure. Above it, the shriek-and-clack of governors, the bark of a relief valve cycling, the hammer of a connecting rod somewhere catching its bearing. It is the loudness of standing inside a working factory floor, except there is no factory floor here — only this empty wet hall, and the engine on the other side of the wall, pumping something, somewhere, to somewhere else, as it has done every minute of every hour for longer than the bricks have been mossed.

You cannot speak in here. You can shout and not hear yourself.

The engine is what makes the place possible. The engine, presumably, is what keeps the floor at ankle-depth and not at neck-depth. The engine is why the gas pressure holds. The engine is why the warm water is warm. Everything in the hall is downstream of the engine, and the engine never stops, and so the hall never changes. A frog could be born here, live a frog's whole life, and die, and the water level would not have moved a hair, the ferns would not have grown a finger's length, the moss would not have advanced a thumb's breadth across the iron. There is an equilibrium here that has gone past stability into something like geological repose. The room has reached its final shape.

Life, what little of it there is, has settled into the equilibrium too. A single pale newt rests on a plinth, gills feathered out, possibly the only vertebrate in the hall. A few cave-spiders ride the upper bricks on long legs. A moth — there is somehow always one moth — circles one of the lamps, never reaching it, never tiring. That is all. The hall is too loud for birds, too enclosed for bats, too steady for anything that needs weather or seasons. The fauna is what can tolerate a single unending afternoon.

The water around your boots is warm. The drops fall from the ferns and the bricks at a rate that, if you counted them, would be the same rate as last year and the same rate as a century ago. The engine breathes through the wall. The mantles hiss. Nothing is happening, and nothing has happened, and nothing is going to happen, and the place is wholly itself, and has been wholly itself for so long that you understand, standing in it, that you are the only thing in the room that is in any way new.

You back out under the low arch. The hummock closes behind you. From outside, in the reeds, you can still feel the engine through the ground — thump, sigh, thump, sigh — but only just, and only if you know to listen for it.
