# The Cistern Room

It sits beneath a hill somewhere — you can feel the weight of earth above, in the slight dampness of the air near the stone lip of the stair, in the way sound behaves when you descend. But the room itself is not dark. It is almost painfully bright.

The cistern was cut, not found. Every surface bears the marks of chisels — the walls are lime-washed brick laid in a herringbone that someone took pride in, and the vault above is a shallow dome of glazed terracotta tiles, fired to a color between bone and honey. Set into the dome are nine oculi, circles of thick pale glass about a handsbreadth across, and through each of these the sun pours in a hard white shaft. The shafts strike the water and shatter. From the water they climb back up the walls as a moving mesh of light — wavering nets of gold that crawl across the brick, re-forming and dissolving. It is the brightest place you have ever stood underground. There are no shadows except your own.

The room is mostly water. It fills the chamber to within a cubit of the vault's springing, so that you stand on a narrow ledge of brick, ankle-deep in overflow, and the rest is a warm still pool. Warm — almost bath-warm, the heat of sun-soaked cistern water that has been gathering light all morning. When you lower a hand into it the warmth goes down past your wrist and keeps going; the pool is deep. Twenty feet, perhaps. The tiles of the floor are visible only as a suggestion of pattern far below, greenish through the stacked depth.

It is loud here. This is the surprising thing. Water enters the cistern from four bronze spouts set high in the walls, shaped like the mouths of fish though no one today would call them any particular fish — just open pouring mouths. Each spout runs continuously, fed from some rain-catchment on the hill above, and each falls a full body-height before it meets the pool. The four falls together make a roar that fills the dome and comes back doubled, so that you have to raise your voice to be heard by someone on the opposite ledge. Under the roar is a second sound, subtler: the drip and trickle of overflow running out through a channel cut in the floor at the doorway, and the small slap of wavelets against brick. The room is never quiet. It has not been quiet since it was sealed and filled, which by the sharpness of the toolmarks and the unweathered crispness of the glaze was not long ago. A generation, maybe. The mortar lines are still bright.

Things grow here, but not much. A cistern wants to be clean. Along the waterline where light and damp meet, a narrow band of bright green algae has established itself — a thin fur, no thicker than felt, interrupted here and there. In two of the upper corners, where a seep has started despite the builders' care, small ferns have taken root in the mortar and trail down: four fronds in one corner, seven in the other, their undersides silvered with the reflected light from below. A single water-plant grows from a crack in the submerged floor and reaches almost to the surface — a pale ribbon of stem with narrow leaves, lifting and settling in the slow circulation the spouts create. That is all. No fish. No insects. No skater-bugs, no mosquitoes — the builders put something in the water, perhaps, or the constant turnover does not let them settle. It is a made place and it enforces its madeness.

The rules of the room are simple and strict. Water comes in at four points and leaves at one. Light comes in at nine points and does not leave. Heat accumulates through the day and bleeds out slowly through the night, so the water is coldest at dawn and warmest in late afternoon, and the room breathes this cycle as reliably as lungs. When clouds pass over the hill the nine shafts dim together and the woven light on the walls goes soft; when the clouds pass the light snaps back. During rain the spouts run harder and the roar deepens and the water rises by a finger's width before the overflow catches up. During drought the spouts thin to threads and the room grows quieter than it has ever been, and in that quiet you can hear the ferns drip.

It is a working room. Someone built it to hold water for a town, or a household, or a temple, and someone still tends it — there is a wooden ladder leaning against one wall, and a long-handled copper dipper hung from a peg, and a scrub brush of stiff plant fiber drying on the ledge. The tools are plain. No mechanism, no pump, no valve more complex than a wooden plug in the overflow channel. The cistern works by gravity and patience. It has been working, loudly and brightly, every hour since it was finished, and it will keep working until the hill above it changes or the spouts wear through.

Stand on the ledge a while. The light moves on the walls. The water is warm around your ankles. The four falls roar. Nothing is going to happen here that has not already been happening, and that is the point of the room.
