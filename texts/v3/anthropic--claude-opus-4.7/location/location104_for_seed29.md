# The Crucible Pocket

Imagine a cavity inside a mountain that nobody knows about — a sealed bubble maybe four meters across at its widest, pinched at the ends, the whole of it lodged somewhere near the heart of a young volcanic range still pushing itself up out of the crust. No entrance. No one has ever been here. No one will.

The rock is new. Not new like a fresh lava flow cooling in the sun — new like it has barely finished being rock at all. The walls are basalt and something more complicated, studded with crystals that are still growing, facets catching on facets, druses of something pale green and something iron-black in the same nodule. Run a finger along a wall (you cannot) and you would feel the grain of minerals still deciding what they want to be. In one corner, a vein of native sulfur weeps slow yellow tears that harden halfway down. The floor is not really a floor; it is the bottom of a pocket, uneven, bristling with points.

It is hot. Not lava-hot — the kind of hot where leather would not survive an hour, where the air itself feels thick and brassy in the lungs, where metal left on the stone would begin to sag by morning. The heat comes through the walls from below, everywhere at once, a patient bake. There is no water. There was never water here. The chemistry is dry: sulfides, oxides, a faint reek of things that in a wetter world would be acids but here are only vapors, volatile and sharp.

And yet it is packed. This is the thing about the pocket — it is full.

Beetles. Or call them beetles for lack of a better word: hard-shelled, finger-joint-sized, their carapaces the blue-black of gun-barrel steel, and they are everywhere, crawling over each other in layers three and four deep, covering every crystal, every ledge, the ceiling, each other. There must be tens of thousands of them in this small space. They feed on the sulfur, or on the heat itself, or on some chemotrophic film that grows (if grows is the word) on the hotter surfaces — a bacterial varnish, barely alive, the only suggestion of anything like biology besides the beetles themselves.

The sound they make is enormous. In a space this small, with this many small bodies, the scraping of chitin on stone and chitin on chitin is not a rustle — it is a roar. A continuous industrial grinding, like a mill in the next room, like a thousand tiny lathes. Their mandibles click. Their wing-cases rasp. When one shifts, fifty shift around it, and the noise rolls through the pocket and comes back off the far wall and meets itself. If you could stand inside you could not hear yourself think. You could not hear yourself scream. The pocket is a bell, and the beetles are its clapper, struck endlessly.

It is dark. Almost. The only light is what the beetles themselves make — not all of them, but enough: a dim bioluminescence in the seam between thorax and abdomen, greenish, cold, the color of old phosphor. In aggregate it is enough to see by, barely, the way a single candle lights a closet. Shapes resolve. Edges glint. The crystals catch the light and throw it back in tiny constellations. The overall impression is of a furnace seen through smoked glass, except the furnace is alive and the glass is the dark.

And here is the part that makes this place what it is: the beetles are not random. They have a pattern. They are running something.

Watch long enough — and time here is long, the pocket has been like this for maybe ten thousand years, maybe a hundred thousand, the mountain doesn't hurry — and you begin to see the flows. Columns of beetles move along certain veins in the wall, always the same direction. Other columns move back along parallel routes. Where the sulfur weeps, workers gather and carry away droplets in their mandibles, bringing them to specific nodes where other beetles process them, passing the material on, shell to shell, a bucket brigade. There are heat-gradient tenders who cluster against the hottest wall until their carapaces glow and then move outward to radiate. There are what look like nothing but are probably the sorting caste, standing in specific positions, touching each passing beetle with an antenna, directing traffic.

It is a factory. A tiny, dark, deafening, chemical factory, inherited and unplanned, running a metabolism that belongs to the colony rather than to any individual in it. The output of the factory is more beetles, and a slow accumulation of refined metals in certain corners — little heaps of reduced copper, a growing bead of something that might be silver — that no one will ever collect. The input is heat and sulfur and the patience of stone.

Nothing changes quickly. A beetle lives perhaps a year; the colony's rhythms run in decades. A particular column of traffic has been going the same direction since before the first human city. The mountain rises a millimeter a century and the pocket, sealed tight, rises with it. Somewhere far above, wind and rain are working down toward this place, but they are a hundred thousand years away, and the pocket does not know about them.

For now it simply continues: hot, dark, loud, full, grinding out its small industry in the rock, a sealed ember of life in a place life was not invited.
