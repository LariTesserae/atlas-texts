# The Singing Shelf

There is a place high on the shoulder of a mountain where the slope flattens into a shelf perhaps three hundred paces across, no more, ringed by older stone that rises just enough to make it a room without a ceiling. You come up through cloud to reach it. The cloud does not always part — sometimes the shelf sits inside the cloud, and sometimes the cloud sits below it like a floor of slow milk, and sometimes, briefly, the air is clear and you can see down into valleys that look impossibly far.

The shelf is wet. Not flooded — wet the way a sponge held above a basin is wet, the way a mouth is wet. Water seeps from the encircling rock in a hundred slow places, not as springs but as a general weeping. The stone here is a pale gray limestone gone soft-looking with age, rounded at every edge, fluted where the seep-lines have run for whoever knows how long. In the hollows of the flutes, moss has filled in: deep green, almost black-green where it stays shadowed, brilliant chartreuse where the sun finds it through breaks in the cloud. The moss is thick enough to sink a hand into to the wrist.

The vegetation is lush but spaced. Not a jungle — a garden that arranged itself. Tree ferns, perhaps a dozen of them across the whole shelf, each one standing alone in its own small clearing of moss and seep-water, their fronds the size of a person stretched out. Between the ferns, low cushions of a plant I do not have a name for: it grows in dense rounded mounds the color of old jade, and when water lands on it the water beads and rolls without wetting it. Here and there, single trees — gnarled, low, with bark like braided rope and small waxy leaves. They have been growing where they grow for a very long time. Nothing here is in a hurry.

Now the strangeness. The shelf sings. This is the loud part — not roaring, not crashing, but constant and full-volumed enough that conversation on the shelf would require raised voices. The encircling rock is full of small cavities and channels carved by the long weeping, and the wind that comes up off the valleys is funneled through these channels and made to speak. The sound is layered: low organ notes from the larger hollows, a middle register of flute-tones, and above everything a high steady whistling that does not quite resolve into a pitch you could name. When the wind shifts, the chord shifts — slowly, like a held breath being let out and a new one drawn. There are five or six chords the shelf knows, and it cycles through them across a day, and the cycle is slow enough that you can sit and listen to one chord for an hour before it bends into the next.

The water contributes too. Each seep has its own small voice — a tick, a trickle, a patient drip into a stone basin it has made for itself over centuries. Up close, near any one of these, you can hear the seep's particular rhythm under the larger singing of the rock. The shelf has, in this sense, many scales of sound nested inside each other, and the longer you stay the more of them you hear.

Animals are scarce. A single species of small dark bird, perhaps — I see one, then later another, possibly the same one. It does not sing; the shelf is already singing. It moves between the tree ferns in low quick flights and seems to drink from the cushion-plants by pressing its beak into the beaded water. There are insects in the moss, surely, but they keep to themselves. No larger creatures. Nothing grazes here. Nothing hunts. Whatever ecology runs the shelf runs quietly and underneath.

There is one thing made by hands, and I almost did not see it. Near the center of the shelf, set into the moss, lies a flat stone about the size of a serving platter, and on it rests a shallow bowl chipped from the same pale limestone as the surrounding rock. The bowl holds water — seep-water, presumably, gathered slowly. There is no other sign of people. No path leading in, no path leading out, no shelter, no ash, no carving. Just the bowl, full, on its stone. Whoever made it used hand tools, used patience, and left. The bowl could be a hundred years old or a thousand; the moss has grown up to its rim but not over it, as if someone returns, occasionally, to keep that edge clear. Or as if the moss declines to cover it for reasons of its own.

The light, when the cloud opens, is ordinary daylight — but the wetness of every surface multiplies it, so that the shelf seems lit from below as much as from above, every fluted stone and every frond bright with its film of water. When the cloud closes again the light goes pearl-gray and even, and the shelf becomes a smaller room, more interior, and the singing seems louder because there is less to look at.

The rules of this place, as best I can read them: nothing happens quickly here. The rock weeps at the rate it has wept for ages. The plants grow at the rate the rock allows. The wind sings the chords the rock has shaped for it. The bowl is filled at the rate water arrives. A visitor is one more slow thing among slow things, and the shelf accommodates a visitor the way it accommodates a fern — by making a small space, and continuing.

You could stay an hour. You could stay a day. The chord would change once or twice. The bowl would be a little fuller. The bird would pass again. Nothing else would mark that you had been there, and this seems to be the arrangement the shelf prefers.
