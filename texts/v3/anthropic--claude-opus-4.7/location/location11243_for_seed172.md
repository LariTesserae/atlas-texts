# The Cold Engine Room

You find it the way you find things that were never meant to be found: by following a sound through the thin air until the sound becomes the place.

It is a chamber. That is the first thing to understand. Not a cave, not a valley — a chamber, fully made, sealed, hung somewhere high. The altimeter would tell you you are nearly two-thirds of the way to wherever the sky ends, but there is no sky here. There is a vaulted ceiling of riveted dark iron, and the rivets are the size of dinner plates, and they have not loosened in any span of time a person could measure.

The chamber is cold. Not the cold of weather but the cold of something that was built cold and intends to remain so. Frost has formed on the inner surfaces of the great pipes that run floor to ceiling — pipes as thick around as old trees, painted once in oxide red, now grayed by the cold's slow chemistry. The frost is in patterns. Ferns. Always the same ferns. They were ferns yesterday and they will be ferns in a thousand years. Nothing here grows or recedes.

And the sound.

The sound is the room. Twelve, perhaps fourteen reciprocating engines somewhere below the grated floor, hammering in a phase relationship that beats against itself, so that the noise is not a roar but a vast slow pulse made of thousands of smaller hammerings — a sound that enters through the sternum before it reaches the ears. You cannot speak in it. You could not hear yourself scream. The sound has a shape: it stands in the middle of the chamber like a column, and you walk around the column, and the column does not move.

There is no water. The chamber's chemistry is dry — some inert gas, argon perhaps, or something the builders cooked up and named after themselves. It does not condense. It does not weather the iron. This is why the place feels young: nothing has had the chance to grow old. The bolt-heads are still sharp at their edges. The catwalk welds still show the bead. Erosion has been forbidden here, and so the chamber exists in a permanent first morning of itself, even though no one has visited in — who knows. The visitor's logbook by the airlock has three signatures and no dates.

The vegetation is the strange part.

In the cold corners, where the engine-pulse is weakest, someone planted lichens. Or the lichens planted themselves, having been carried in on a boot sole during one of those three visits. They grow on the iron in small grey-green discs, well-spaced, never touching one another, as though each had negotiated its territory and signed. There are perhaps forty of them in the whole chamber. You could count them. You could name them, if you were the kind of person who does that, and after an hour in this place you might be.

The lichens do not move in the pulse-wind because there is no pulse-wind; the sound is pressure but not flow. They are simply there, sparse and deliberate, the only soft thing in a room of hammered metal. They are also, as far as anyone could determine, the only living things in the chamber. No insects. No mites. Nothing has followed them in. They live alone with the engines.

What do the engines do?

This is where the chamber becomes strange. The engines drive nothing. There are no output shafts, no belts, no generators, no pumps moving anything anywhere. The pipes that run floor to ceiling are sealed at both ends; you can see the caps, beautifully machined, bolted shut. The engines hammer and hammer and consume — something — and produce the sound and the cold and nothing else. They are not broken. They are running perfectly. They have been running perfectly for a very long time.

A small brass plaque near the airlock, in a language that uses the Latin alphabet but no words you recognize, says something that might be a dedication or might be an instruction. Beneath it, scratched by a fingernail into the paint: *do not stop them.*

The light is ordinary. That is perhaps the most unsettling thing. Bright sodium-spectrum bulbs in cages, spaced along the catwalks, throwing the honest yellow light of any industrial space anywhere. You can see your hands. You can read the plaque. The light makes no concession to the strangeness of the room; it lights the room as though the room were a room, and in that ordinary light the engines hammer and the lichens hold their positions and the frost-ferns do not melt.

You came in through an airlock. You can leave through the airlock. Between these two acts, there is the chamber, holding its single sustained note of being, neither welcoming nor refusing you, a made place that requires nothing from you and offers nothing back. A pocket. A held breath at altitude. An engine room for an engine whose purpose was either forgotten or was always, from the first rivet, simply this:

To be running. To be cold. To be loud. To be.
