# The Cold Sept Beneath

There is a city under the ice-shelf, and no one made it.

To find it you would have to be small — smaller than a finger, perhaps smaller than the joint of one — and to be already inside, because there is no entrance from the world above. The shelf is a hundred meters of grey-blue compression overhead, and the city sits in a pocket of brine that the ice has refused to freeze. A lens of liquid, oval, perhaps the size of a modest cathedral if you measured cathedral-by-cathedral, but the inhabitants do not measure that way. To them it is the whole sea.

The water is cold enough that a warm-blooded thing would die in it within minutes, and salty enough that it slides against itself in slow, denser-than-fresh curls. Light comes down through the ice as a diffuse blue, filtered and re-filtered, so that everything below has the quality of a dusk that never advances. In the deepest part of the pocket — its floor, where the brine pools darkest — the light gives up entirely and a soft phosphor takes over: pinpricks of green, the slow blink of something living that decided long ago to be its own lantern.

The city is built of tubes.

I don't know what else to call them. Calcite, mostly, with a banding of darker mineral where iron has worked its way in. They are the abandoned and the inhabited dwellings of a tube-worm that builds upward into the brine and outward against its neighbors, and over generations — over what passes for generations down here, which may be very long or very short — the tubes have layered, branched, fused, collapsed, been built upon. There are districts. There are towers four or five worm-lengths high, leaning together at the top like reeds tied at a peak. There are alleys between them where the water moves slower, eddied, and where smaller things have learned to live in the slack.

Nothing here is finished. That is the strangest thing, perhaps. The mineral is still precipitating out of the brine. Walls are growing as you watch — not visibly, but if you stayed for a week you would see the new pale rim around a tube's mouth, the way a fingernail lengthens. The geology is embryonic. The buildings are alive in the sense that they are accreting; they have not yet decided their final shape, and may never. A district that looks like a district today was a reef-shoal a thousand tides ago and will be a sediment-plain a thousand tides hence. The city is a moment in a slow weather.

The vegetation, what there is of it, is bacterial mat and a few stringy cold-tolerant algae that have found their way down through micro-channels in the ice. The mats are tea-colored, rust-colored, sometimes a startling violet near the warmest seeps. They drape the older tubes like long hair. Things eat them. Things eat the things that eat them.

The fauna is the density. This is what I want to convey: the volume of brine here is not mostly water, it is mostly creature. Copepods in clouds, so thick the water seems to grain. Amphipods like translucent commas, hinged and twitching, picking through the mats. A flatworm the color of bruise, gliding the tube-walls. Something I can only call a sea-spider, eight legs and almost no body, stalking on tiptoe across the rooftops of the worm-towers. Larval fish, transparent, with eyes that are the only opaque part of them, hanging motionless in the mid-water and then gone, struck by something faster. The worms themselves, in the tubes, are crowns — feathered fans of red and white that open from the tube-mouths to filter, to taste, to breathe, and that snap closed in an instant of collective recognition when a predator's shadow passes. When the fans are out, the city is in bloom. When they retract, the city looks abandoned, every door slammed, and the predator drifts down empty avenues with nothing to show for itself.

This happens constantly. Bloom, snap-closed, bloom. It is the city's pulse, and it has no sound. That is the other strange thing. For all this teeming, for all this hunting and feeding and growing, it is nearly silent. The brine is dense and the pocket is small and the sounds that exist are below the threshold of what we would call sound — the tick of a copepod's exoskeleton against a wall, the faint rasp of an amphipod's mandible on bacterial mat, the slow exhale of a seep somewhere venting a thread of warmer water up through the floor. If you held still and listened with the right kind of attention you would hear the city the way you hear a snowfall: as a presence rather than a noise.

The rules of this world, as best I can articulate them:

Up is ice and down is dark and outward in every direction is also ice; the pocket is closed. Nothing leaves. Nothing arrives, except occasionally a meltwater trickle bringing fresh strangers — a spore, a bacterium, once perhaps an egg — that either die or are absorbed into the order of things. Inheritance, therefore, is everything. Every creature here is descended from whatever was trapped when the shelf closed over, however long ago that was, and the descendants have specialized into each other's company with the intricacy of long marriage.

There is no technology because there are no hands, and no fire because there is no air, and no language because there is no need; the snap-closing of the worm-fans is already a kind of conversation, and the chemical trails the amphipods leave on the tube-walls are a kind of map, and the phosphor-blinks at the bottom may be a kind of love.

The dynamic is high. Predators stalk. Prey scatters. The seeps pulse warmer and cooler on a rhythm that may be tidal, may be something else, and the whole pocket has weathers — a current that rises along the warm wall and sinks along the cold one, slowly, slowly, turning the city over once every long while, so that what was on the floor comes to the ceiling and what was on the ceiling settles. Generations of worm-tubes have been built to face whichever way is currently up; older sections, now sideways or inverted, persist as ruins, their fans long dead, their mouths slack and home to squatter-creatures.

If you were small enough to be there, and cold-tolerant enough, and patient — you could spend a life in one alley between two leaning towers and not learn it. The light shifts. The fans open and close. A flatworm passes. Something in the mat moves that you did not know was alive. The wall beside you, imperceptibly, thickens by the breadth of a single crystal of calcite, and the city, which is not finished and will not be, becomes by that much more itself.

Above, the ice does not know any of this is happening. Above, there is wind, and a sun low on a horizon, and no one for a thousand kilometers. The pocket holds. The pulse continues. The silence, which is not empty, goes on.
