# The Sumpworks

Below the surface — not deep, just under — there is a city that the world forgot to bury properly. You come down to it through a collapsed stair worn into a smooth chute, and the first thing you notice is the light. It is too bright for somewhere underground. The ceiling, which is also the underside of the world, is encrusted in pale mineral scale that catches and throws every gleam from below, so that the whole cavern-roof glows like an overcast noon. There is no sun here. The light comes up from the floor.

The floor is the reason.

It is mostly dry — flagged in some places with hand-cut limestone tiles set so long ago they have rounded at the edges like old soap — but threaded through with shallow runnels of water that glow. Not brightly, not magically; the water carries something fine and suspended, a slow-settling silt of luminous grit, and where it pools it pools brilliant, and where it trickles it makes a vein of cold daylight running between the stones. The runnels braid. They split around the foundations of buildings, rejoin, drop into cisterns, climb back up through stepped weirs that someone, once, cut by hand.

The buildings are everywhere and nobody is in them.

This is the strangest part of the Sumpworks: that it is unmistakably a city — densely built, layered, organized — and almost entirely silent. Houses stacked on houses, three and four storeys, their lower courses limestone, their upper courses a kind of fired clay brick the color of old teeth. Streets narrow as corridors. Square plazas with dry fountains at their centers, the fountains carved to shapes no longer legible. Workshops with stone benches and the rust-ghosts of tools long since taken away or fallen to powder. A bathhouse with its tiles still mostly in place, the pool floor a slow ripple of glowing water that has been seeping in for centuries through a crack.

And space. So much space between things. The city is open in the way an old man's mouth is open — you can see all the gaps. Whole blocks have settled into themselves, the upper storeys collapsed onto the lower, the lower compressed to knee-height heaps that the runnels now flow through. Then a half-mile of intact street. Then another collapse. Then a tower, somehow, still standing, leaning maybe two degrees off true, its windows full of pale light.

It is cool. Not cold — there is no breath-fog — but cool the way a wine cellar is cool, the temperature of stone that has not seen a season in a very long time. Damp clings to everything. Condensation beads on the lintels. Walk a wall with your fingertips and they come away wet.

There is almost no plant life. A few mineral crusts that might once have been lichens and are now something simpler, more like a stain that grew. In the wet places, mats of something gelatinous and faintly phosphorescent — call it a slime, call it a colony, it doesn't reproduce so much as smear. That's the whole flora. The city was built before plants were the point and has aged past needing them.

But there are animals. This is what makes the silence strange — the Sumpworks is full of fauna, and you almost never hear them.

They are pale. They are mostly small. They move on the surfaces of the water without breaking it, six-legged, the size of a thumbnail, drinking light. Larger things — eyeless, soft, the rough shape and size of a cat but built more like a sea-cucumber — pour themselves over thresholds and down stairs in absolute quiet, their undersides making the faintest wet kiss against stone. Something with a long neck and translucent skin wades a deeper runnel and dips its face under and comes up with a mouthful of the glowing silt, and you can see the silt traveling down its throat like a swallow of stars. None of them call. None of them sing. The loudest sound any of them make is the click of a horn-plate when they fold up to rest, and they fold up to rest often.

The water itself is the loudest thing in the city, and the water is very quiet.

Now: the turbulence.

You would not see it standing still. You'd say *this place is dead, settled, finished*. But watch for an hour.

The runnels shift. Not erode — *shift*. A channel that fed the eastern plaza dries in the space of twenty minutes, the silt going dim, and somewhere two streets over a new vein opens between paving stones and floods bright. The cisterns fill and drain on no rhythm you can find. A collapse you walked past — fine, stable, an old heap — gives a long sigh and resettles three inches lower, and the animals nearby do not even flinch; they expected it. The light from the floor brightens and dims in slow waves across whole districts, as if the city were breathing through its capillaries. Sometimes a whole quarter goes nearly dark for an afternoon and the eyeless cat-things come out in numbers and do whatever they do in the dimness, and then the light returns and they pour back into the cracks.

Nothing here is building. Everything is unmaking and remaking the same finished thing, endlessly, at the level of the water and the silt and the slow give of stone. The city reached its final form a long time ago and has been fidgeting in that form ever since. Turbulence without progress. A storm held inside a museum.

The rules, as far as one can tell:

The water carries the light, and the light is alive in some sense that doesn't require it to be an organism. The animals eat it. The slime hosts it. The stone, having drunk centuries of it, has begun to glow faintly on its own, which is why the ceiling-scale shines.

Nothing here was made by anything that lives here now. The tool-marks on the stones are the marks of hands — five fingers, a thumb, hammer-and-chisel work — but no hand has held a chisel in this city for an age you could not count without giving up partway. The builders left, or stopped, or became something that no longer builds. Perhaps they are the slime. Perhaps they are nothing. The city does not seem to miss them.

You can walk for days. The Sumpworks goes on — not infinitely, it has an edge somewhere, but past the horizon of any one visit. There are districts you'll find that contradict each other: a quarter of round houses, a quarter of square ones, a quarter where the streets spiral, a quarter that seems to have been built by people half the size. The city was a long project. Then it was a finished project. Then it was a long, quiet, twitching afterwards, which is what it is now, and which it will go on being, glowing softly from underfoot, while the small pale animals drink the light and the runnels braid and rebraid through stones worn to the smoothness of held things.

If you sit on a doorstep and don't move, after a while one of the cucumber-cats will pour itself across your foot, find you neither food nor threat, and continue. That is the closest thing to a greeting the Sumpworks offers. It is enough. It is, in its way, the whole place saying: *yes, you may be here, we are not using it.*
