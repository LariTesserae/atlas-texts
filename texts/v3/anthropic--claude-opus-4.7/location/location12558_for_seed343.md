# The Canopy Plateau

You climb for days through cloud before you understand what you are climbing through.

The mountain is so old it has forgotten it was ever a mountain. What you take for slope is actually root — a single continuous mat of vegetation, fifteen meters thick in places, that has draped itself over whatever stone remains beneath. The original peaks have eroded down to nubs and saddles, smoothed by something like ninety million years of rain, and the plants have inherited the shape. You walk on living architecture: a felted weave of moss, fibrous stem, fern-rhizome, and the slow gray heartwood of trees that grew sideways because there was nowhere upward left to go.

The altitude is brutal. Three thousand meters and rising. But the air is warm — wet-warm, blood-warm — because the canopy traps the equatorial sun against the green substrate and exhales it back as steam. Breath comes hard. Light comes filtered through layers of leaf and lichen the color of old jade, of liver, of bruise.

And the sound. God, the sound.

It hits you first as pressure. Then resolves into ten thousand throats. The plateau is loud in a way that flattens thought — a continuous wall-of-cicada throbbing under the screams of something tree-dwelling and territorial, under the bass thrum of frogs in every pocket of standing water, under the wingbeats of bats that aren't bats and the rasp-call of something that sounds almost like a child laughing and never stops. You cannot hear yourself swallow. You cannot hear your own footfalls in the moss. At dawn the sound *doubles*, as if a second ecosystem wakes inside the first, and you have to lie down and press your palms against your ears and wait for your skull to stop ringing.

Despite all this life, the place is empty.

That is the thing that takes longest to understand. The fauna is overwhelming but it is *distributed* — spread across hundreds of square kilometers of root-plateau, layered through vertical strata you can't see from where you stand. You walk for an hour and see nothing larger than a beetle. The animals are everywhere and nowhere; the volume of green is so vast that even three trillion creatures rattle around in it like seeds in a gourd. Whole valleys hold a single sloth-thing and a parliament of insects. You can stand in a clearing — really a thin patch in the canopy-mat where rain has worn through to old bedrock — and see nothing in any direction but green folding away into mist, and hear, from that emptiness, the absolute riot of a living world.

Water is sparse and strange up here. It rains, but the canopy drinks the rain before it reaches the substrate. What you find instead is condensation — beads of water gathered on the underside of waxy leaves, which the plants harvest and the animals raid, and which collects in slow drips into pitcher-cups the size of bathtubs, half-vegetable, half-pond, each one its own micro-ocean of larvae and translucent shrimp. To drink, you tilt a pitcher-leaf and let cold green water run into your mouth. It tastes of tannin and copper.

There are people here. Or there were.

You find the first sign on the third day: a hand-axe of dark stone, hafted with woven fiber, planted upright in the moss at the center of a clearing like a marker. Nobody nearby. Nobody for kilometers. You leave it where it stands. Later — a platform lashed into the crook of a great horizontal trunk, big enough for two sleepers, the bindings still tight but the woven floor sprouting with seedlings. A fire ring of stones, cold, with a small unmolested cache of dried something-meat wrapped in leaf. A bone flute. A skull-sized gourd with charcoal handprints on it. The people who live here live thin across the plateau the way the animals do — three or four to a watershed, maybe — and they have been doing it long enough that their tools have settled into a vocabulary as ancient as the mountain's own. Stone, fiber, bone, wood, fired clay. Nothing newer. Nothing they didn't need.

You see one of them, once, across a gulf of canopy at sunset. A small figure standing on a root-bridge, watching you. They lift a hand. You lift yours. The sound of the forest is so total that even if either of you had shouted, nothing would have carried. They turn and walk back into the green and you understand, with a flush of something between loneliness and relief, that this is the correct way to meet another person here. Acknowledgment. Distance. No further claim.

The weirdness of the place is subtle and accumulates.

It is that the root-mat *moves* — not visibly, but you find your campsite has shifted by morning, ten or twelve centimeters downslope, as the substrate slowly creeps. It is that certain trees flower only when something dies beneath them, and the flowers smell like the dead thing, exactly. It is that the laughing-call animal can mimic, badly, fragments of human speech, and that you sometimes hear what sounds like your own name in your own voice from the canopy at night, and have to decide not to answer. It is that the oldest trees, the gray sideways giants whose trunks form the ribs of the plateau, are warm to the touch. Not sun-warm. Body-warm. As if the mountain were running a low fever it has run for an epoch and will run for an epoch more.

The rules of the place, as best you can articulate them:

Climb slowly. The altitude will kill you if you hurry, and the substrate will swallow a careless foot — there are pockets, soft spots, places where the mat is only a centimeter thick over a forty-meter drop into the hollow understory. Drink from pitchers, not from puddles. Do not eat anything that has no animal already eating it. If you hear your name, walk the other way. If you find a tool, do not take it; someone is using it, even if the someone is the plateau itself, remembering.

And listen. Even though the sound is unbearable. Especially because it is unbearable. The plateau is loud the way an old cathedral is loud during the full service — every voice raised, the architecture itself resonating — and to be inside it is to understand that the mountain, having eroded itself down to almost nothing, having become more idea than stone, has not ended. It has only changed media. It is singing now. Through bird and frog and cicada and the slow heartbeat of warm wood, through condensation and rot and the green that has eaten the rock, the mountain finishes its long, long sentence.

You came up here looking for a summit. There is no summit. There is only the top, which is everywhere, and the song, which does not stop.
