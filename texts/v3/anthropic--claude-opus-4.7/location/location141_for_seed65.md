# The Clamor Plateau

The first thing is the sound. Before you notice the light, before your lungs catch on the thinness of the air, there is the sound — a continuous, layered roar that seems to come from the rock itself. It does not pulse or swell. It simply *is*, and has been, and will be.

You stand on a plateau at an altitude where the sky has gone from blue to that deep, almost-violet color it takes when there is not enough atmosphere left above you to scatter the light properly. The sun here is not warm but it is *bright* — a hard, white, unflinching brightness that flattens shadow to razor edges and makes every grain of feldspar in the stone glitter. Snow patches persist in the lee of boulders, but the air is cool rather than cold, a dry cool that seems to have no opinion about you.

The plateau stretches for perhaps forty kilometers in its longest dimension — you can see most of it from where you stand. It is a tableland of pale, weathered stone, creamy-ochre and dove-grey, sculpted over such long spans of time that the sculpting has stopped being visible as change and has become simply the shape of the place. Long, shallow runnels. Pans where water has pooled and evaporated a hundred million times, leaving rims of pale mineral. Low domes. Shattered aprons of frost-split scree. Everything is rounded and settled, and nothing is raw.

The sound comes from the birds.

There are millions of them. This is the reason for everything.

The plateau is a breeding ground — has been a breeding ground since before anything remembers — for a species of cliff-nesting seabird-analog that returns here in numbers that have no apparent relation to the sparseness of the land. They should not be here. There is no sea for hundreds of kilometers in any direction. But here they are, wheeling in vast cylindrical columns above the plateau's edges, dropping like thrown stones into the updrafts that boil off the lower valleys, rising again, screaming. Always screaming. Their cries compound upon each other until the individual voice is lost and what remains is a single standing wave of noise that presses against the eardrum like a physical weight. You have to shout, mouth at someone's ear, to be heard. After an hour you stop trying.

The birds are the ecology. Everything else here exists because of them or in spite of them.

Vegetation is sparse — cushion plants, a few pale lichens the color of old bone, tufts of a wiry grass that grows only in the lee-cracks of boulders. But where the guano accumulates in old hollows, the plants there are luxuriant by contrast, almost shocking in their green, little gardens of improbable fertility in the otherwise austere stone. Insects live in these gardens. Small lizards. A kind of burrowing rodent-thing with enormous ears, probably for thermoregulation, possibly for the noise.

Water exists in pockets. The snowpatches, yes, and shallow tarns in the rock-pans, and a thin stream that threads the plateau's center and plunges off the northern rim into space — you cannot hear it fall, of course; you cannot hear *anything* but the birds. Condensation beads on the undersides of overhanging stones in the mornings. It is enough. The plateau is not dry exactly; it is damp in the patient way of high places where little evaporates and little falls.

And there are people here, or there have been. Not structures — no buildings, no walls, nothing that would count as architecture. But along certain ledges you find small cairns of stacked stones, and in certain sheltered overhangs, small flakes of knapped obsidian, and hearths so old and so reused that the stones are glazed. Whoever comes here comes with hand-tools and leaves little. They come, it seems clear, for the birds — for eggs, for feathers, for meat. The evidence is old and sparse and suggests a long-standing arrangement rather than an exploitation. Someone walks up here every few generations, takes what is needed, walks back down.

There is something strange about the birds themselves, if you watch long enough. They do not seem to feed. There is nothing here for them to feed on at the scale their numbers would require, and you never see them leave the plateau to hunt. They wheel, they scream, they nest, they lay eggs that hatch into more of them. The math does not work. One of the old hearths has, scratched into a flat stone beside it, a spiral with a dot at its center — perhaps a mark of counting, perhaps something else. The people who came here knew something about the birds, or accepted not knowing.

The most remarkable thing, standing on the plateau, is how *still* it all is despite the sound. The birds move, yes, in their millions, but the pattern of their movement is the same now as it was an hour ago, as it was a century ago, as it presumably will be a century hence. The columns rise in the same places. The nesting ledges are occupied in the same densities. The wind comes from the same quarter, the light falls at the same angle for the season, the tarns hold the same water-levels within the same rims. Nothing is changing. The plateau has found its shape and its sound and its population and has settled into them the way a stone settles into a stream-bed after a thousand years of being knocked about — into the one configuration that no longer requires adjustment.

You can stay here as long as you like. It will not notice. The sound will not let you think, and the light will not let you sleep easily, and the air will make you breathe too fast for the first few days. But the plateau will not mind your presence and it will not mark your leaving. When you descend — and you will descend; no one stays — the sound will fade over the course of an hour's walking down the switchback paths, and when it is finally gone you will find that your ears ring, and the ringing will last for days, and in the ringing you will hear, faintly, the shape of the clamor you left behind. That ringing is the only thing you take with you. Everything else stays.
