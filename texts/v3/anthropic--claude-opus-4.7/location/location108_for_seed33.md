# The City on the Spindle

It stands on a single needle of pale rock that rises past where mountains should end. The peak is narrow — no wider than a small town — and the city covers it exactly, as if the stone grew to fit or the city was poured into a mold no one remembers making. Below the edge there is nothing for a long time: just air going grey, then greyer, then the suggestion of other peaks so far down they might be clouds pretending.

There is no water here. The stone is dry the way old bone is dry, and the air carries a faint mineral taste, like licking a coin. What little moisture exists belongs to the lichens — if they are lichens. They grow in slow grey-green rosettes on the north faces of the buildings and on the shadowed underside of every cornice, and they are the only living things that seem certain of themselves. Everything else flickers.

The buildings are stacked. Not built — stacked, as if whoever made them was working from memory of a city they had only glimpsed. Towers lean on towers. Stairs fold into themselves at the landings and come out going the same direction they started. The streets are laid in a pattern that makes sense from above but refuses to resolve when you are walking them; you pass a particular doorway with a blue lintel, and then you pass it again, and then the lintel is green and has always been green, and the neighbors agree. The stone is a warm pale grey with veins of something darker that moves, slowly, over days, like weather inside the wall.

It is cool. Not cold — cool, the temperature of a cellar, consistent at every hour. There is no sun visible, though there is light: it comes from the air itself, a dim even glow like an overcast afternoon that has lasted centuries. Shadows exist but they are soft and do not always point the same way. At what the inhabitants call evening the light thins further and the stone begins, faintly, to remember — older configurations of itself surface for an hour or two, a wall becoming briefly the wall it was two hundred years ago, with different windows, before settling.

The city is mostly empty. This is the strangest thing about it and also the least strange, because emptiness is the city's native condition. There are perhaps a few hundred residents scattered through architecture built for tens of thousands. You can walk for an hour down a colonnade and meet no one, hear nothing but your own footsteps and, occasionally, the dry rustle of a lichen releasing spores. The residents are quiet people. They have learned that sound carries oddly here — a word spoken in one plaza may arrive in another plaza first, and the speaker second — and so they speak softly, or not at all, and communicate by small gestures and by leaving objects in agreed places.

The fauna are rare and gentle and wrong. Pale long-legged things that might be called deer if you weren't looking directly at them; when you look directly they become more like folded paper, or a diagram of a deer, and then they are gone around a corner that wasn't there. Something flies, sometimes, at the edge of the eye — never overhead, only at the periphery — and when it has passed the air where it was smells briefly of rain, though it has never rained here. There is a creature the residents call the listener which lives in the deepest courtyard and has no shape, only a presence, and the lichens grow thickest around it.

Weather happens. Not rain, not snow — other weathers. Sometimes a *thinness* moves through the city, a front of reduced reality in which walls become suggestions and you can see through three streets at once; the residents sit down when this happens and wait. Sometimes the opposite: a *density*, where the air grows heavy with unchosen possibilities and every surface seems to hum with the weight of almost-being. There are mornings when gravity tilts by a degree or two toward the east, and the residents lean, and the lichens lean, and by afternoon it has corrected. There are nights — if they are nights — when the city is briefly two cities, overlapping, and you might meet your neighbor coming out of a house that is also a different house, and you both politely pretend not to notice.

The erosion is old and settled. Whatever was going to fall has fallen; whatever was going to crack has cracked and found its crack agreeable. The city is in balance with its own decay. Nothing is being built and nothing is being lost — or rather, things are being lost and found at the same rate, and often they are the same things. A staircase that collapsed a century ago is sometimes there again, perfectly sound, and people use it, and then one day it is collapsed again and no one is surprised or inconvenienced.

There is no technology. No wheels, no fire — fire does not take here, the air refuses it politely — no tools beyond the hand and the worn stones the hands have shaped by touching. The residents eat the lichens, which are nourishing and taste faintly of the person eating them. They sleep in alcoves. They do not appear to age, but they do, sometimes, simply stop, and then the lichens grow over them, and after a while there is a new small rise in the floor of a quiet room, and the city accommodates it.

From the edge of the city, if you lie on your stomach at the parapet and look down the long pale needle of stone, you can see, very far below, the place where the spindle meets something — a base, a ground, a world. It is hard to look at for long. The residents don't. They have learned that the city is the whole of what can be held in the mind at once, and that the rest is weather of a kind they are not built for.

It is very quiet. It has been quiet for a long time. It is, in its strange patient way, alive — a slow ecosystem of stone and lichen and soft-footed residents and the occasional paper-deer, turning through its cycles of thinness and density, losing and finding itself, balanced on its needle in the dim even light, going on.
