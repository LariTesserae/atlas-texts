# The Hollowing

It begins, as far as I can tell, with the realization that you are inside something alive.

Not a cave. The walls breathe. They are pale gold where the light strikes them and a deeper amber in the recesses, and they are made of wood — but wood is the wrong word, because wood is dead and what surrounds you here is the interior of a vast, slow, vegetable organism. The chamber is perhaps two hundred meters across at its widest, sloping downward and outward like the inside of a gourd that has grown into the shape of a valley. Above, the roof is webbed with thick fibrous beams — buttressing roots, or veins, or both — and where they cross, the gaps between them are sealed with a tough translucent membrane that admits a honeyed, filtered daylight. So you can see. You can see everything.

There is no water. The chamber is dry the way the inside of a seedpod is dry — the moisture is locked into the substance of the walls themselves, slow-cycled through xylem too deep to tap. The air is warm, blood-warm, and it smells of pollen and resin and the inside of someone's mouth. Your skin feels the warmth coming from the walls themselves. The whole organism is metabolizing, and you are inside its heat.

And the sound. This is the first thing that overwhelms and the last thing you adjust to. The sound is unbearable.

It is the fauna.

The chamber is *crowded*. Not in the way a forest is crowded — in the way a hive is crowded, a coral reef is crowded, a marketplace at festival is crowded. The walls and beams and hanging tubers are *occupied*. Six-legged creatures the size of dogs cling to the vertical surfaces and shriek to each other in chiming, bell-like cries. Long ribbon-things slide through the upper canopy of root-beams making a constant papery rasp. Something with a flat translucent body the size of a dinner plate is pulsing along a wall, leaving a track of glistening sap behind it that smaller things rush to lick clean. Clouds — actual clouds — of bright orange wing-cased insects rise and fall in the warm air currents, and when one cloud meets another they crash together in a sound like dry rice poured onto a drum, scatter, and reform. A creature like a small pale ape, all elbows and no eyes, is hanging upside down from a root-beam directly above you, chewing a piece of the wall with slow contemplative jaws. It is not afraid of you. Nothing here is afraid of you. There is no room in the local economy of attention for fear of a single new thing.

The noise totals into something that cannot be parsed: a roaring chorus, ten thousand voices, mechanical and musical and wet and dry all at once. You cannot hear yourself think. After a while you stop trying to think and just receive it.

The floor underfoot is not soil. It is a thick fibrous mat — fallen scales of the walls, husks of insect-things, droppings, mycelial fluff — perhaps a meter deep, and it is also alive: small pale roots are pushing up through it, and the husks are being slowly digested back into the substance of the chamber. Erosion in any geological sense is absent here. There is no rock. There is only the ongoing construction of the place by itself, the slow architectural decision-making of the great plant that you are inside, and the rapid scribble of the fauna writing and rewriting the surfaces. Everything is *coming into being*. Nothing has settled.

And outside this chamber — through the four or five rough openings in the walls, openings that look gnawed rather than carved — you can see that there are *more chambers*. The Hollowing extends. It is regional. You glimpse, through one archway, a slope going down into a second chamber where the light is greener and the air shimmers with something flying in numbers too large to count. Through another archway, a chamber where the walls are vivid red and the noise is even louder, a deep thrumming bass underneath the shrieks. The whole valley, you realize, is the interior of a single embryonic plant-organism the size of a town, still growing, still sealing itself shut overhead, still extruding new chambers as its roots dig farther under whatever surface lies above.

You begin to notice the structures.

They are small and few and easy to miss against the riot. Near the center of the chamber, somebody has stacked three flat stones — *stones*, real stones, not part of the plant, brought here from elsewhere — into a small cairn. On one of the walls, at about chest height, somebody has scratched a series of vertical marks into the bark, a tally or a calendar. A length of plaited fiber hangs from a low beam with a loop at the end of it, swinging slightly in the warm draft, clearly the work of a hand. In one corner, half-buried in the floor-mat, the rim of a clay bowl. Nothing more recent than these. The cairn-makers are not here now. Whether they left an hour ago or a thousand years ago, the Hollowing has not preserved the difference; the floor swallows time at its own pace.

Whoever they were, they had hands and patience and a few simple tools, and they made these marks the way a person makes marks in any overwhelming place — not to control it, but to confirm that they had stood inside it and remained themselves.

The rules of this world, as best I can read them:

The plant grows. It grows constantly, slowly, in directions decided by something we would not call thought but which is not nothing. The fauna live inside the plant and feed on the plant and on each other, and the plant tolerates them or uses them or both — pollen is carried by the wing-cases, seeds are spread in the droppings, the walls are pruned by the chewing of the eyeless apes into the shapes the plant prefers. Nothing here arrived from outside; everything here was produced by the relationship between the great organism and the creatures it hosts. It is a closed garden, dry and warm, lit by its own translucent ceiling, beneath some other surface we cannot see.

And humans — or human-shaped things — pass through. Not often. Not many. They leave a cairn, a tally, a bowl. They do not stay, because no one could sleep here, because the noise does not stop, because to remain inside the Hollowing for long would be to become part of its turbulence and forget you had ever been anything else.

You stand a while longer in the warm amber light. The eyeless ape above you finishes its piece of wall and drops it, and the piece falls onto the floor-mat near your foot, and three small scuttling things are on it before it has stopped moving, fighting over it with the bright clean violence of creatures who have never known scarcity, only competition, only the endless surplus of a world that is growing faster than it can be eaten.

You step toward one of the archways. The next chamber is greener. The noise from it is already reaching you, layered under this chamber's noise, a different chord.

You go in.
