# The Cathedral Lung

Below the surface — but how far below, no one quite knows — the world opens.

It opens *upward*. That is the first wrongness, the first thing the body refuses. You descend through a tunnel of root-fiber, hand-cut by someone long ago who left chisel marks in the wood-flesh, and you expect the dark to thicken. Instead the dark thins. Light pours up at you from a ceiling that is also a floor that is also a horizon. You step out onto a balcony of cartilage-bark and the chamber is so large you cannot find its end.

This is the inside of a plant. Not metaphorically. The whole subterranean cavity — and it goes on for what would be countries, were there countries here — is filled, packed, *substantiated* by a single living tissue. Pale green, faintly luminous, the color of the inside of a new leaf held up to sun. The light comes from the tissue itself. Every cell a small lamp. Together they make a day brighter than any day above.

The architecture of the place is the plant. Vast fluted columns, hollow, ribbed inside like the throat of a horn. Sheets of membrane stretched between them, translucent, big as weather-fronts. Bladders the size of cathedrals hanging from nothing, slowly inflating and deflating on a rhythm of perhaps an hour each breath. And vast — vast — empty volumes. The density here is wrong for a living thing. Most of the cathedral lung is air. The plant has chosen *space* as one of its organs.

It is cold. The light is bright but gives no heat — chlorophyll-cold, mineral-cold, the cold of something photosynthesizing a sun that isn't there. Breath plumes. Frost lies in white feathers along the lower edges of the membranes, where condensation gathers and freezes and falls in slow papery flakes that aren't quite snow. This is the only water: the breath of the plant, condensing on its own walls, dripping intermittently from the high bladders in long unhurried strings. A drop falls past your ear and you hear it land somewhere a full minute later.

Otherwise, quiet. The plant does not creak. It flexes. The columns lean by inches and the membranes belly outward and a wind moves through the chamber that is the plant *inhaling* — and this is where the turbulence lives. Because the lung is young. The whole formation is young, geologically speaking; the chisel marks at the tunnel mouth are older than most of the architecture you're looking at. New columns are extruding from the floor right now, glassy and soft, hardening as you watch. Old membranes split with a sound like wet silk tearing and fall in long slow drifts. A bladder ruptures somewhere far off and the pressure-change shoves you against the railing of cartilage-bark; the wind reverses; frost-feathers lift from the floor in a great pale exhalation; somewhere, a new bladder begins to inflate to take the dead one's place.

This is its weather. Not storms — *growth-storms*. The plant rearranges itself on the scale of hours. Walk away from a landmark and turn back and the landmark is gone, has been re-absorbed, has been replaced by a colonnade. There is no map that lasts. The tool-makers who came before knew this. Their marks are everywhere and nowhere — a chiseled notch in a column that the column has half-swallowed; a bone ladder lashed to a rib with sinew, the rib having grown around the lashings until the ladder is part of the plant now, fossilized in living tissue. A single small structure stands intact on a high shelf: four walls of woven fiber, a roof of pressed leaf, no door, nobody inside. It has been there long enough that the plant has begun to grow *up around* it, gently, the way a slow tide laps a stone.

Fauna is rare and strange. Things drift. A pale moth-shape the size of a hand, with no eyes, navigating by the pressure-changes of the breathing chambers. A long ribbon-animal grazing on the inside of a membrane, leaving a clean translucent track behind it where the light shines through more strongly. Once, perhaps, a larger thing — you find a print in the cartilage-bark, a print with too many toes, already healing closed as the bark grows over it.

The rule of the world, if there is one: nothing here is finished. The lung is becoming. Every surface is provisional. The bright cold light, the slow papery falling, the great soft thunder of the bladders changing places — all of it is one organism still working out what it wants to be, in a chamber large enough to hold its own indecision.

You can stay. Many things have. The plant does not mind. It will grow around you eventually, gently, in its own time, and your chisel marks will become part of the wall, and someone else, much later, descending the tunnel of root-fiber, will find them and wonder.
