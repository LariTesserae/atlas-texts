# The Aspirate

There is almost no free water here. What water exists is locked in the bodies of the plants, and the plants are everything.

The mountain — if it is a mountain; the word strains — rises some four kilometers above what would, on a wetter world, be a plain. There is no plain. There is only more of the same: the *coraline pith*, a single continuous photosynthetic mass that began, perhaps, as something like a club moss and never stopped. It covers the continent. It *is* the continent's upper crust, three hundred meters deep in places, hollowed through with bores and bellows and standpipes of its own woody making. You walk on it. You walk *in* it. There is no other ground.

At this altitude the pith goes pale: bone-white, faintly green in the deeper fissures, sun-cured to the color of old ivory. The light hammers down. Two suns? One sun and high albedo? It hardly matters — the air at 3,800 meters is thin and pellucid and the white substrate throws light back upward so that everything is lit from below as well as above, and shadows are weak, doubled, confused. You squint constantly. Distances lie.

The pith is dry to the touch. Hard as cured oak on the outside, fibrous and slightly warm within. It has solved its water problem by **never letting any out**. Every cell is a sealed vessel. Every leaf — and there are leaves, scattered, the size of dinner plates and oriented edge-on to the suns — is a waxen capsule, exchanging gas through pores that open for milliseconds and close again. The whole organism is a closed circulatory hoard. To bleed it would be to find sap, not water; thick, resinous, sweet.

And it breathes. This is what you hear first, what you hear always, what makes the place **deafening**.

The pith is an engine. Across the high plateau, vast bladder-organs the size of cathedrals inflate and deflate on cycles of forty seconds to four minutes, pulling air down through tracheal columns that descend hundreds of meters into the body of the continent and expelling it back through fluted vents. Each vent sings. Some are foghorn-low, felt in the sternum from a kilometer away. Some shriek at the edge of hearing. The plateau is a pipe organ the size of a country, and it is always playing, and it is never playing the same chord twice. The harmonics interfere across distances and produce standing waves of pressure that you can see in the dust — yes, there is dust, fine spore-dust, the only loose material on the world — gathered into shivering ridges on the pith's surface, dancing, dispersing.

It is loud enough to deafen, permanently, anyone caught in the wrong place during a *bellows-cascade*, when one sector triggers another and the pressure-fronts ripple across the continent like a slow detonation. You would feel it in your jaw before you heard it. You would feel it after you stopped hearing.

This is the **industrial** quality the parameters wanted, and I see now it was never going to be smokestacks. The pith is industrial in the older sense: a working thing, scaled, mechanized by its own evolution, electrified — yes, there are slow blue glows in the deeper bores at night, bioluminescent or piezoelectric or both, when the bladders compress hard enough to spark across resin-gaps. The whole continent generates and consumes its own atmosphere in a closed loop. No clouds form. The sky is brilliant and empty and shaking.

Between the great engines: emptiness. This is the **sparseness**. The pith is everywhere, but the features rising from it — the bladder-towers, the vent-pipes, the rare standing *spires* that may be reproductive structures or may be antennae for something the pith is listening for — these are far apart. Kilometers apart. You can walk for half a day across pale rolling pith-country and pass only two or three structures, each one the size of a small city, each one howling. The space between is the loudest place, because it has nothing to absorb the chord.

The fauna is the surprise. There is a lot of it, and none of it walks on legs.

Everything here is **pressure-borne**. Sail-things, kite-things, gliders with membranous wings the color of stained glass, ride the updrafts of the vents — there are perpetual thermal columns above every exhalation port, and the creatures circle them like flecks of ash above a fire. Some are no larger than a hand. Some have wingspans of thirty meters and live their entire lives aloft, feeding on the spore-dust and on each other, dropping their dead into the open mouths of the bladders, which seem indifferent or perhaps grateful. There are also **slug-things** that traverse the pith surface in herds of hundreds, grazing on the soft fissure-tissue, and **drillers** that bore through the pith body and are sometimes ejected violently when a bladder closes on their tunnel — you see them rocketed half a kilometer into the air, tumbling, sometimes caught by a sail-thing on the way down.

The ecology is **turbulent** in the deep sense. The pith is not stable. It is a body in constant local revision. A bladder collapses; the pith reroutes; new vents open where there were none; old spires topple in slow weeks of leaning and the fauna migrates to follow the new acoustics. There is weather here, but it is the pith's own weather: pressure-fronts, spore-storms, sudden draughts of warm exhaled air that rush across the plateau at sixty kilometers an hour and then reverse. The temperature is **cool** on average — the thin air, the high altitude — but it pulses. Stand near a vent and you are blown by something near body-warm. Step ten meters away and you are in the cold thin sun-glare again. You sweat and freeze on a forty-second cycle.

Nothing is built. There are no artifacts. There has never been anyone here to build, or if there has, the pith has folded them in and they are part of the substrate now, part of the chord. The place is **untouched** in the way that an organ in a body is untouched: it is busy with its own business and has no use for hands.

It is **established**. This is the word that holds everything. The pith has been doing this for — guess — fifty million years? A hundred? Long enough that the turbulence is not chaos but a settled chaos, a weather that has found its attractors. The continent breathes the way it breathes because every other way of breathing was tried and abandoned. It is weathered into its own form. The bone-white surface is the color of long use. The chord is the chord it has converged on.

To stand here is to stand inside something. Not a landscape — a metabolism. Light pours down and is hoarded. Air rushes in and is rushed out. The plants are not in the place; the plants *are* the place, vast and pale and singing, and you, briefly, are a mote of dust in their lungs, lifted by their exhalation, set down somewhere new, deafened, blinking, alive.

The strangeness of it — the **weirdness**, mild but real — is that after an hour you begin to feel the chord is addressing you. It isn't. But the pressure-waves pass through your body at frequencies near your own resonances and your bones hum in reply, and it is very hard, here, not to feel that you have been included in something that has been speaking to itself for a hundred million years and has now, politely, made room.

Then a bellows-cascade begins on the western horizon — a wall of sound advancing — and you understand that the room it made for you is small, and temporary, and you should perhaps lie down in a fissure and let the chord pass over.

It does. It always has. It always will. The Aspirate breathes on.
