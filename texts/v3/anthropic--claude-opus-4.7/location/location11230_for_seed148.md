# The Vatworks at Low Tide of Hours

Begin at the edge, because the edge is the only place where you can still tell you've arrived.

There is a town here, or something that was a town once and is now being slowly digested by itself. It sits low, in a basin no wider than a few streets across — you could walk its circumference in twenty minutes if the streets stayed where you left them. The roofs are slate-dark and slick with condensation, and the condensation runs in threads down the gutters and into the moss, and the moss is what the town is made of now. Not covered in. Made of.

The buildings began as brick and iron. You can still see the bones: a chimney here, the cast-iron ribs of what was a small mill, the curve of a railway siding bending into the dark. But the mortar has been eaten and replaced. The walls are held together by interlocking sheets of liverwort and a deep, spongy bryophyte that breathes when you press it. Doorways are arched by living wood that grew up after the lintels rotted and held the shape of the lintels out of habit. The factory in the center — three stories, taller than anything else — has lungs. You can hear them if you stand still: a slow inhalation through the broken clerestory, a slower release through the chimney, every ninety seconds or so. It is not metaphor. It is not poetry. The factory breathes.

The light is wrong, or rather, the light is barely. It is the light of the third hour before dawn, perpetually. A grey so soft it has weight. There is no sun overhead — there is a ceiling of canopy, but the canopy is far higher than it should be for a place this small, vaulting up into a dimness that suggests rather than shows. Leaves the size of cart-wheels hang motionless. Occasionally one detaches and falls, and the fall takes longer than it should, drifting like ash, and when it lands it lands without sound.

Everything is wet without raining. The damp comes up out of the ground and down out of the leaves and sideways out of the walls. You taste iron and chlorophyll. Your sleeves grow heavy within minutes.

Now — the emptiness. This is the strangest thing to convey. The town has streets and buildings and the rough geometry of habitation, but the volume of it is hollow. You can stand in the central square and the nearest other living thing — other than the plants, which don't seem to count, or count as the room itself rather than its occupants — might be three hundred meters away. The proportions feel wrong. Cathedral-empty in a hamlet-sized shell. The factory's main floor is one enormous room with a single beetle the size of a hand walking very slowly across it, and the beetle's footfalls are the loudest sound for a long time.

The fauna is everywhere and nowhere. Abundant, the parameter says, and it is — but distributed through the architecture rather than crowded into it. The moss-walls are full of small movements: things gliding just under the surface, leaving traveling welts. Pale frogs the color of cooked rice sit in the windowframes, unblinking, in numbers — one to a frame, never two, as if assigned. Something with too many legs lives in the chimney of the bakery and only its shadow is ever seen, projected against the inside of the oven by a glow that has no source. There are birds, or bird-equivalents: long-necked, dun, walking rather than flying, single-file down the main street at intervals, going somewhere with purpose. They never arrive. You watch them turn a corner and then you walk to that corner and the street is empty and the moss has no tracks.

The reality breakdown — yes. This is what makes the place itself.

It is not that strange things happen. It is that the rules are still being negotiated. The erosion parameter said *emerging,* and that applies to the laws here as much as to the stones. Cause and effect are roughed in but not finished. If you knock on a door, sometimes the sound comes from the door, and sometimes from the door across the street, and sometimes from inside your own chest, and once, memorably, from twenty minutes ago — you will hear the knock before you arrive at the door, and the question of whether you must then knock to complete the loop is a real one, a question the town is still working out.

The factory makes something. You can hear the machinery, distantly, even though nothing in it moves. It is industrial in the way a memory of industry is industrial: the sound of belts and the smell of grease and the slow accumulation of product, but the product is — and here language fails honestly, not artfully — the product is *more town.* The factory is making the place it is in. New moss-bricks extrude from a vent on its eastern wall, one every few hours, glistening, and the town's edges very slowly grow outward at the rate the factory produces them. The streets at the perimeter are younger. The moss there is brighter, less settled. You can see, in the freshest streets, the half-formed: a doorway with no building yet around it, standing in a clearing; a length of railing leading from nothing to nothing; the suggestion of a window, floating, waiting for its wall.

This is what *emerging* means here. The town is being built, in the only way it knows how, by the breathing factory at its heart, out of the wet green dim. It does not know yet whether it will have weather. It is trying out weather. A small wind moves down the main street for ten seconds and then stops, embarrassed, and does not return for an hour. A single drop of rain falls in the square and nothing follows it. The town is rehearsing.

And the dynamic — yes, active, the ecology turns. Predators move through the moss-walls. The frogs vanish from windowframes when the long shadow with too many legs is abroad. The walking birds eat something, somewhere, that you never see them eat. There are cycles: the factory's breath, the slow pulse of light from deep within the moss (it brightens, almost imperceptibly, every eleven minutes, and the frogs all swallow in unison), the migration of the unseen things beneath the wall-skin from west to east across the course of what would be a day if days existed here.

It is mostly quiet. Almost everything that happens, happens softly. The loudest sound is the factory's breathing. The second loudest is your own footsteps, which the moss receives with a small wet click each time, like a tongue against a palate, like the town tasting you to decide whether to incorporate you into the next brick.

If you stay, you become a feature. People have stayed. You can see them: a coat-shape grown into the wall of the post office, a pair of boots planted upright in the square with ferns where the legs should be, a face — only the front of a face, smooth as a mask, in the lintel above the factory door, eyes closed, expression peaceful. These are not warnings. The town does not warn. It only continues, slowly, to make itself out of what is available, and what is available is what arrives.

You arrived.

The factory inhales. Somewhere a frog swallows. The grey light dims by a margin so small only the moss notices, and the moss adjusts, and a new brick begins to form.
