# The Town That Is One Plant

It begins, as far as anyone can tell where beginning means anything here, as a valley. Low, wide, perhaps two hours' walk across if walking were possible. Warm — not tropical-warm but greenhouse-warm, the kind of heat that comes off a compost heap or a fermenting silo, a heat the place generates from inside itself rather than receiving from above. The light is ordinary daylight, filtered green and gold through ten thousand stacked translucencies. There is no sun visible. There has perhaps never been a sun. The light simply is, the way the heat is, the way the *noise* is.

The noise. Begin there, because the place insists.

Everything is growing, and growing here is loud. Bamboo-like trunks split their sheaths with reports like rifle-cracks. Vines unspool with a wet zipper sound that goes on for minutes at a time. Leaves the size of doors open with a soft thudding boom, displacing air. Somewhere a seedpod the size of a wagon detonates and showers the canopy with whistling shrapnel that immediately takes root in whatever it strikes, including other shrapnel, sending up new whistling shoots that detonate in their turn. There are no animals to hear this — the parameters of the place exclude them — and so the plants have nothing to communicate with except each other and themselves, and they are deafening about it. A chorus of stretching fibers, popping cell-walls, sap pressurizing into xylem at the speed of a kettle coming to boil. Multiply by a valley.

Now the architecture.

The plant has built a town. Not metaphorically. The plant *is* the town — every plant is, since there is in some real sense only one plant here, or one plant per breath, the species boundaries having broken down along with several other boundaries. There are streets that are the long hollow interiors of fallen megastalks, pith scooped out by the plant's own digestive blooms, the floor a packed mat of root-hair so fine it feels like felt underfoot. There are houses that are seed-cases the size of cottages, still attached to their parent stems, walls flexing slightly with the parent's pulse. There are towers — clusters of upright trunks lashed together not by hand but by the lashing-vines that grow specifically for the purpose, a species that evolved (or was dreamed) to be cordage. There are stairs that are bracket fungi, terraced up the sides of the larger trunks. There are bridges that are living arches, two trees that grew toward each other and fused at the crown and hollowed out a passage. There are wells — and this is where the water comes in — that are pitcher-structures the height of a man, gathering condensation off the hot upper canopy and holding it cool and tannin-dark at the bottom, replenishing nightly, drinkable.

The water is mostly that: condensate. The valley does not have rivers. The valley has *weeping*, a constant slow drip from the underside of every leaf, collected in cupped fronds and channeled down spiraling grooves that the plant has grown deliberately, like gutters. Rain happens, but rain is internal here — the upper canopy transpires so heavily that clouds form *inside* the forest, drift sideways through the streets at chest height, and condense on cooler surfaces like a sweating glass. You can walk through a thunderstorm in a hallway. The thunder is small, polite, more felt than heard against the larger roar.

The young-ness. The place is *new*. You can see this in the unfinished edges: the way a street suddenly terminates in a wall of pulpy white tissue still hardening into bark; the way some seed-houses have not yet developed doors and are sealed, the inhabitants (if there are inhabitants, more on this) waiting inside for the wall to dehisce. There are scars where one structure outgrew another and crushed it, the crushing still in progress, audibly, with a long groaning splinter-sound that lasts days. The features are sharp. Edges have not been rounded. Nothing has had time to weather. You get the sense that if you came back in a week, the geography would be unrecognizable — and this is correct. This is the dynamism: the town reorganizes itself. Streets close. New plazas open where a giant rosette has unfurled. A tower you climbed yesterday has bent in the night to lay its crown into the soil and become a bridge to somewhere it wasn't going before.

Now the weirdness, which I have been holding back because it requires the rest of the place to be established first.

The plant dreams architecture it has not seen.

There are doorframes here in styles that do not exist. There are columns with fluting that suggests a culture, an aesthetic, a *people* who never came to this valley because no people have ever come to this valley. The plant has invented Corinthian capitals out of nothing, has invented arches that lean against arches it also invented, has invented entire decorative grammars — friezes of repeating leaf-motifs that depict leaves the plant does not grow, depicting them in low relief along the inner walls of its own hallways. Who is this for? No one. There are no eyes here but the unfocused photoreceptive sheen of the plant itself. And yet the ornament proliferates. There are rooms that are clearly rooms — hearth-shaped depressions where no fire has ever burned, niches that fit a statue that does not exist, window-openings that look out onto more window-openings, looking back. Sometimes a wall will be growing a fresco: pigmented cells arranging themselves into an image of a city that is not this city, a city of stone, with figures in it, the figures slowly resolving from blur into detail and then dissolving again before they can be read.

And the reality-breakdown goes further than ornament. Distances do not behave. You can walk down a corridor for an hour and arrive somewhere ten paces from where you started, having passed through three distinct climates of the same warm valley. The plant, being one organism, does not respect that two points must be connected by a single line; it routes its interior however it pleases, and you are walking through its interior. There are doors that, when opened, breathe out the smell of a season — full autumn, dry and yellow — into the green wet of the rest of the place, briefly, and then close again, and the autumn is gone, and there was never autumn here. There are stairwells that spiral both up and down at once and deposit you on a level you cannot reconcile with the level you left. Gravity is mostly reliable. *Mostly.*

The density is total. There is no negative space. Even the air is thick with spores, pollen, drifting seed-down, the broadcast gametes of structures trying to mate with structures across the valley — pollen rains so heavy that at certain hours you wade through it shin-deep where it has settled in the streets, and it stains everything yellow, and it hums faintly against the skin because it is, in a small way, alive and looking for somewhere to land. Layered above the streets are more streets. Above those, the canopy-town: bridges and platforms and hung gardens where the seed-houses are smaller and dangle on stems and sway. Above that, more canopy. Above that, more. The valley has a vertical city kilometers tall, and you cannot see the top from the bottom, and the top cannot see the bottom either, but the plant is one plant and knows itself throughout.

There are no animals. This is important. There are no birds in the canopy-town, no insects in the pollen-drifts, no fish in the pitcher-wells. The place is sterile of motion that is not its own. And yet the plant has grown nests. It has grown empty hives. It has grown perches sized for birds that do not come, and the perches are worn smooth as if by use, though no claw has gripped them. The plant remembers, or anticipates, or invents — there is no way to tell which — an ecology it does not have. The empty nests are the loneliest thing in the valley, and also somehow the most hopeful, because they are still being built. New ones every day. Tiny woven cups of fiber, perfect, vacant, waiting.

And underneath everything, the sound continues. The deafening, the un-pause-able. Growth-noise, settle-noise, the wet pop of buds, the long sigh of leaves opening, the percussion of seedpods, the bass thrum of the largest trunks pulling water up through themselves under enormous pressure, a sound like distant traffic that never stops because it isn't distant and isn't traffic, it is the plant being alive, fiercely, everywhere, in every direction, all at once, and inventing as it goes.

If you stayed long enough you would, I think, become part of the ornament. The plant would grow a frieze of you along one of its corridors, in low relief, depicting a figure it has never seen, walking through a place that does not exist, listening to a sound it cannot stop making.

It would be a good likeness.
