# The Roostworks

There is a town in a shallow basin where it does not quite rain. The air simply carries water — a kind of standing damp that beads on iron railings and slicks the cobble-slates without ever falling as weather. You can walk between two buildings and feel a curtain of mist pass over your face like a hand, and then nothing, and then again.

The basin is wide but not deep, perhaps a mile across, ringed by low ridges of pale exposed stone that have been worn into soft shoulders. No trees. No grass. There is a thin black lichen-thing on the north faces of some walls, and a yellow crust around the warm pipes, and these are the only photosynthetic concessions, if they photosynthesize at all. Otherwise the basin is mineral, metal, brick, slate, and rust — the rust the deep brown-orange of long settlement, not the bright red of new wounds. Everything has been this color for a long time.

The town itself is industrial. Forge-stacks, low and wide rather than tall, vent a constant pale steam that joins the basin's standing damp. Belt-houses with their iron sheds, pulley-towers, the dripping geometry of catwalks and pipework. Foundries, three of them, set at the basin's lowest points where rainwater would collect if rain fell — instead they collect condensate, which is run off through gutters into cooling reservoirs. Cobbled streets between, narrow, with gutters down the center that hum faintly because something is always moving through them.

The light is ordinary. Overcast, a working light, the kind of grey that lets you see your hands clearly but flatters nothing. It does not seem to come from any particular sun; the basin has its own steady illumination, neither morning nor evening, just *working hours*, indefinitely.

And the birds.

The birds are why anyone built here, or perhaps why anyone *had to* build here. The basin holds something on the order of a hundred million birds. They roost on every cornice, every chimney-lip, every railing, every cooling tower, every catwalk, every ledge, every wire. Pigeons mostly — though "pigeon" undersells the variety: slate-grey, iron-grey, smoke-grey, oil-iridescent, white-collared, ember-eyed. Also rooks, jackdaws, starlings in pulsing clouds, swifts that thread between buildings like sewing needles, and a few larger silent things that sit at the tops of the stacks and are not named in any guide. The birds do not migrate. They do not appear to breed, exactly. There are always this many. There have always been this many.

The sound is the sound of them. It is not deafening in the way a factory is deafening — it is layered, the murmurous churr of millions of throats, the constant percussion of wings, the click of claws on metal, the occasional alarm-rise of a thousand birds taking off and then resettling onto the same ledges. Underneath: the industry's own steady rhythm, the slow stroke of pistons, the hiss of release valves, belt-grind, hammer-thud. The two sounds — bird and engine — have braided themselves together so thoroughly that no one in the basin can hear one without the other. Visitors describe it as overwhelming for the first day. By the second day they stop hearing it as sound and start hearing it as a kind of weather.

The factories make something, but it is not clear from outside what. Crates leave on the single rail line that climbs out of the basin's south lip, and crates arrive bringing coal and iron and oils, and the loop continues. The workers wear oilcloth and broad flat hats against the constant rain of droppings, which has, over the centuries, covered every horizontal surface in a chalky white crust two inches thick — soft, slightly damp, never dry, never accumulating further because it sloughs off as fast as it deposits. The crust is, in fact, mined. It is one of the things the foundries process. The birds make the substance the town refines. The town refines the substance into something the birds, somehow, need. It is the closest thing to an ecology this place has.

Nothing here changes. The same number of birds. The same volume of steam. The same workers, possibly — it is hard to tell under the hats. The same rust at the same depth on the same railings. The basin reached its configuration a very long time ago and has held it. Storms do not arrive. Seasons do not turn. The mist does not lift and does not thicken. If you stood on the southern ridge and looked down, you would see the basin at noon and at what should be midnight and notice no difference except, perhaps, that the birds at certain hours are quieter, more contemplative, as though dreaming with their eyes open.

The strangeness is not loud. It is in the small things. That the droppings smell faintly of machine oil. That the birds, if you look closely, have pupils shaped wrong — slotted, or doubled, or in one species perfectly square. That a dead bird, if found, weighs almost nothing, as though hollowed; and that dead birds are rare, far rarer than the population should produce. That the foundry foremen speak to the larger silent things on top of the stacks, sometimes, in the evening shift, and the silent things tilt their heads.

That if you ask anyone in the basin how long the town has been here, they will think about it carefully, and then say: *as long as the birds.* Which is not an answer. Which is, in this place, the only answer there is.
