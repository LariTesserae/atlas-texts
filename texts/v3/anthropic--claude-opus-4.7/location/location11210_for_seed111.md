# The Roost in the Black Spire

High up — so high that the air thins to a coppery taste at the back of the throat — there is a cleft in a young mountain. The mountain itself is one of those raw, recent peaks that hasn't yet learned to round its shoulders: black basalt and a paler grey andesite, fractured along clean planes, edges sharp enough to part skin. The rock is glassy where it sheared and matte where it cooled slow. Nothing has had time to soften it. No talus skirts have settled. The slopes drop in fresh, angular faces, and meltwater hasn't yet carved channels — only stained the rock in dark vertical tongues where it seeps.

The cleft is not large. You could walk its long axis in two hundred paces. Call it a room in the mountain, or a chapel — open to the sky above through a narrow oculus of stone, walls leaning inward as if the peak were trying to close its hand. From below it would be invisible. From within, the sky is a slot of bruised indigo even at midday, and the light that reaches the floor is the light that has bounced twice off wet rock, gone grey and uncertain.

It is cool here. Not cold — the stone holds a memory of the planet's heat from when it was extruded, not so long ago — but cool enough that breath shows, and the constant condensation on the walls beads, runs, beads again. There is no rain in the usual sense. Instead the cleft breathes: cloud rises through the oculus in slow inhalations, fogs the chamber to milk, then thins. Water arrives sideways, on the skin of the air. Everything is damp. Nothing is wet.

Vegetation has found this place but barely. In the cracks where condensation pools: cushions of a black moss, almost lichen, that smells faintly of iron when crushed. A few twisted things that might be ferns if ferns had given up on light — pale, translucent fronds no taller than a hand, growing in tight colonies near the seeps. One plant I cannot name: a single stalk per crack, segmented like a thin bamboo, ending in a closed bud that never opens, that vibrates very slightly when sound passes through the chamber. There are perhaps forty of them throughout the cleft. They seem to be listening.

And the chamber is full.

This is the thing about the Roost — its density, its overwhelming animal presence. The walls are not walls. The walls are alive. Layer on layer on layer of creatures cling to the rock: something between a bat and a swift, smaller than a fist, with skin like wet leather and no visible eyes. They roost wing-to-wing, ten thousand deep, in tessellated columns from the floor up into the dark above the oculus. Beneath them, in the lower cracks, beetles the size of coins move in slow rivers over the moss. In the seeps, blind salamanders with white gills hold themselves against the current of dripped water. The air itself has occupants: pale moths drifting, midges in faint columns, something larger and unseen that displaces fog as it passes.

The sound is the second thing you notice, after the damp. (The first thing is the smell — ammoniac, mineral, alive.) The roosting creatures are never wholly silent. They make a low, layered chitter, a sound like a forest of small bones being shaken in cloth, and it never stops, only modulates. Every few minutes a section of the wall will startle for no apparent reason and a thousand wings will open and close in a single percussive *whump*, and the chamber rings with it, the basalt singing back, and the listening-stalks tremble visibly. Then the chitter resumes, slightly louder, and slowly settles.

This is the rhythm of the place. Not fast. Slow swells of agitation across hours. The cloud rises and falls on a daily cycle. The creatures shift their roosts seasonally — though seasons here are subtle, registered only in which face of the cleft holds the densest colony. Nothing changes quickly. The mountain itself is the fastest-moving thing in the geological sense; it is still rising, still cooling, still occasionally cracking with a sound like a struck bell that sets the whole chamber into a minute-long roar of wings.

There is one made thing in the cleft, and only one: a low cairn of stacked stones near the largest seep, perhaps knee-high, perhaps older than it looks. The stones are chosen — flatter than the surrounding rubble, fitted without mortar. On top of the cairn rests a single tool: a scraper of worked obsidian, edge still sharp, hafted to a piece of bone with sinew gone stiff and dark. No one is here. No one has been here, by the look of it, in a long while — but the cairn has not fallen, and the scraper has not been carried off by the beetles or the wings, though either could. There is a perimeter around the cairn, perhaps a body's length in radius, where the moss does not grow and the creatures do not roost. Whether they avoid it or whether something keeps them out — that is one of the rules of the place, and it is not explained.

The other rule, the one that takes longest to notice: the listening-stalks bend toward whoever speaks. Not toward sound in general — the constant chitter does not move them — but toward articulated speech, toward language, toward a voice that means something. If you stand in the cleft and say a word aloud, every stalk in the chamber turns its closed bud a degree or two in your direction, slowly, over the course of a minute. They do not open. They do not respond. They only orient. And when you leave, they hold that orientation for a long time — days, perhaps — before drifting back to whatever attitude they kept before you arrived.

The cairn-maker knew this. That is my guess. That is why the cairn is here, and why the scraper has been left as it has: as a word, set down in a place that listens, in a chamber high enough that almost nothing will ever come to hear it answered.
