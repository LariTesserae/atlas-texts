# The Underworks at Caul

You come into it down a staircase that was never meant to be walked — a service ramp of cast iron grating, still slick from the condensation that falls in the vault like a slow indoor rain. The stair descends maybe forty meters below what was once a surface, and the surface itself is gone now. There is no sky. There is a ceiling.

The ceiling is the first thing to understand. It is made. Riveted plate, ribbed with girders the size of rail cars, blackened by a century of soot and weeping where the seams have sprung. It arches across a space that could hold a small town — a valley's worth of floor, but roofed. You cannot see the far wall clearly; the dim sodium-colored lamps set into the vault at wide intervals give out before the distances do. Things fade into a rust-colored murk at about six hundred meters out.

Everything is built. There is no ground here that is not plate, or poured slag-concrete, or the packed clinker of old spoil-heaps that have been walked smooth. Where water has pooled — and it pools everywhere, in the low places between catwalks, in the dished centers of disused turntables, along the gutters that run beside the rail — it pools on metal. The water is cold. You can feel the cold radiating up off it through your boots. Somewhere a thermocouple would read just above freezing; the vault keeps itself in a kind of permanent early morning of the year, a pre-dawn November that has no dawn coming.

The sounds arrive in layers. The nearest is the drip — not one drip but ten thousand, a hissing percussion from every overhead beam and condenser fin. Behind that, the slow tidal breathing of the big ventilators, which exhale every forty seconds or so with a sound like a cathedral clearing its throat. Further off, deeper, are the engines. You can't see them. You wouldn't want to. They run somewhere beyond the third bulkhead, and their work comes through the floor as a low subsonic shove you feel in the sternum, arriving in irregular pulses — a stamping mill, maybe, or the slow reciprocation of a pump so large it takes six seconds to complete a stroke. The pulses are not synchronized with anything. The place has a heartbeat, but more than one heart, and they argue.

The vegetation is the surprise. It shouldn't be here and yet here it is: pale growths between the plates, in the silt-traps, around the weep-holes. Horsetails, mostly, or something very like horsetails — jointed, segmented, the color of old tin. They like the minerals leaching out of the walls. In one broad flat where a cooling pond has overtopped its lip, a stand of them has grown up waist-high, and among them sit cushions of a lichen-thing that glows faintly yellow when disturbed, probably chemiluminescent, probably feeding on some run-off the engineers never accounted for. The plants are sparse. You walk a hundred paces between one clump and the next. They have the fresh, startled look of things that arrived recently and have not yet decided what they are.

This whole landscape is young. That is the strangest thing about it, given how industrial it feels. The rivets are still bright under their soot where you scratch them. The concrete has not yet taken on that soft, rounded look old concrete has; its edges are sharp, its form-marks from the pouring still visible, crisp as the day the shuttering came off. The spoil-heaps have not slumped into their final angles of repose. The water has not yet found all its channels — it's still making them, cutting small bright rivulets across the flats, discovering which direction is downhill in a place where downhill was decided by a draftsman and not by geology. You get the sense the Underworks was commissioned perhaps thirty years ago and the landscape inside it is still settling into being itself. Erosion has begun but erosion has not won.

Fauna: rare, but not absent. Something crosses a gantry two tiers up — quick, low, four-legged, pale. You don't see it clearly. There are supposed to be rats but the things here are not quite rats anymore; the cold and the mineral water and the long dim generations have done work. Later you find tracks in a silt-pan: five-toed, splayed, the prints of something that swims as readily as it walks. Once, very far off, you hear a call — three descending notes, metallic, possibly a bird, possibly a piece of machinery learning to grieve.

The rules of the place, as best you can work them out:

Water moves downward through the vault in a slow cascade system — condensing on the ceiling, falling, pooling, draining through grilles to a lower level, condensing again. It is always raining somewhere in the Underworks, and always not-raining somewhere else, and the weather (such as it is) migrates according to where the engines are running hottest. You can tell a shift has started at the unseen foundry because the drip intensifies over the east flats within the hour.

Light comes from the sodium vault-lamps, from the lichen, and from occasional shafts of arc-welding blue where maintenance is happening in the far galleries — brief, intense, throwing huge jittering shadows across the whole floor before cutting out. Real daylight is not a concept here. People who work here, if there are people, measure time by the shift-horns, which sound four times per cycle and can be heard everywhere.

And the weirdness, which I have been circling: the place feels watched, but not by anything above. It feels watched from below. The floor, where you stand, is not the bottom. There are grates, and through the grates is another level, and from that level comes occasionally a warmer exhalation, humid, smelling of iron and something organic, and once, when you crouched to look, you thought you saw a light move past — not a worker's lamp, too slow for that, too steady, pale yellow, the color of the lichen but the size of a dinner plate, drifting. It did not come back.

The Underworks is new. It is cold. It is vast and sparsely populated by its own strange plants and its own strange traffic. It was built for purposes that are still being carried out, by engines that have never stopped, and the landscape — the real landscape, the ecology of rust and horsetail and drip and pale swimming thing — is only now beginning, in the crawlspaces of what someone made, to figure out what it wants to be.
