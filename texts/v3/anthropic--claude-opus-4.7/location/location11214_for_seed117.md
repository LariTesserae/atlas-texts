# The Sump-Works at Low Tide

There is no horizon here, only a ceiling of weeping iron about four meters overhead, and a floor of dark water perhaps ankle-deep, perhaps knee-deep depending on where you put your foot. The space between is everything. Call it a cistern, though it has never held drinking water; call it a basin, though nothing drains from it. The locals, when there were locals, called it the Sump-Works, and the name has outlasted them by some long while.

The ceiling is a riveted plate, or rather a quilt of plates, sagging in catenary curves between pillars of cast iron set into the water at intervals of perhaps three or four paces. The pillars are fluted, more decorative than they need to be — someone, once, cared how this place looked, though there was never anyone meant to see it but the maintenance crews. The iron weeps continuously. Each pillar wears a beard of mineral: ochre, verdigris, a soft black like wet soot. Where the drips fall they have, over a very long time, built small stalagmites of rust and lime on the floor beneath, miniature inverted versions of the pillars themselves, so that each column has its echo rising from the water to meet it, and in places the two have joined, and the pillar continues unbroken through its own waste.

The water is cool — the whole place is cool, the temperature of a wine cellar, of a hand laid flat on slate. It does not stagnate; there is a current, faint, the slow exhalation of some pump still running somewhere out beyond the lit volume. You can feel it against your shins as a directional cold. Periodically — every quarter hour or so, though time is hard to keep here — the level rises perhaps a finger's width, holds, and falls again. A tidal rhythm, mechanical in origin, biological in effect.

The light is from the ceiling. Filament bulbs in wire cages, maybe one in five still burning, the rest dark or holding only a dull cherry trace of failing element. The living bulbs are spaced unevenly, so the Sump-Works is a constellation of dim amber pools separated by darker water where you must trust your feet. The bulbs hum. That is most of the sound — a low electrical drone, plural, slightly out of tune with itself, and the irregular percussion of drips, and once in a while the deep clunk of a valve closing somewhere far off, a sound that travels through the iron rather than the air and arrives in your sternum before your ears.

There is no plant in here. Not one. But the pillars are sleeved in something — a felt-like growth, pale grey going to rust-orange near the waterline, which is neither lichen nor moss nor mold exactly, though it borrows from all three. It is a colonial something, fungal at best guess, that lives on the iron's slow surrender and on whatever the water carries. Where the light hits it directly it shows a faint iridescence, oil-on-water, suggesting it is doing some chemistry the eye cannot follow. In the dark patches between bulbs the felt is thicker, almost a pelt, and the pillars there look furred and warm though they are not warm at all.

The fauna is the strange part, and the dense part, and most of what makes the Sump-Works the Sump-Works.

The water is full of small pale things. Not fish — there has been no surface for fish in too long. These are something that began as fish, perhaps, and have given most of it up. Eel-shaped, finger-length to forearm-length, blind or nearly so, with a translucence that lets you see the dark thread of their gut and the slow pulse along their spine. They move in suspended clouds around the pillars, grazing the felt. When you stand still they investigate your ankles with a touch like cold breath. They are not aggressive. They are simply too many — you cannot take a step without parting a school.

Above them, in the four-meter air, the other half of the ecology. Small flying things, the size of moths but soft-bodied, more like swimming than flying — they paddle through the damp air on transparent paired fins, slow, deliberate, hanging near the warm bulbs. When one dies it falls, and the eels boil to the spot, and within a minute there is nothing. The flyers eat the condensation off the ceiling, drinking from the underside of the iron plates with proboscises like fine wires. They are the reason for the felt's iridescence, perhaps; they may pollinate the colonies in some sense, transferring spores from pillar to pillar. Or perhaps not. The system is established. It works. Reasons are a luxury.

Then there are the larger ones, which I had not wanted to mention yet but they have begun to be present. Between two pillars in the middle distance, something the size of a large dog rises slowly from the water until its back breaks the surface — pale, smooth, articulated like a segmented hull — and then sinks again without further demonstration. It happens twice while you watch. The eels do not disperse around it. They appear, in fact, to thicken around it. Whatever it is, it is part of the arrangement.

The built things, beyond the iron itself: a catwalk running along one wall, mostly underwater now, only the handrail showing. A control panel on the far wall, brass-faced, its dials still trembling — the needles drift across their ranges in a slow choreography that, if you watched it long enough, you might begin to read as language, or as weather. A ladder going up into the ceiling through a hatch that has not been opened in living memory and may no longer open. A single chair, wooden, swollen and split, bolted to the floor beneath the water, where someone used to sit and do whatever this place required of them.

The rules of the place, as best I can derive them:
- Nothing here is in a hurry. Every process — the dripping, the growth of the felt, the tidal pulse of the pump, the grazing of the eels, the slow patrol of the larger thing — operates at its own settled pace, and those paces have learned to fit together.
- The boundary is the lit volume. Beyond the last bulb in any direction, the Sump-Works simply continues, or doesn't; the question has not been settled and is not interesting to ask from inside.
- The machinery still runs. No one tends it. It has been running long enough that the ecology has grown around its rhythms the way bark grows around a nail, and now the machinery and the ecology are the same fact, considered from two angles.
- You can stay as long as you want. The eels will get used to you. The felt will, eventually, begin to colonize your boots if you stand still enough. This is not a threat. It is simply what the place does with what enters it.

The dim amber, the cold current, the soft constant rain from above, the touch of pale fish at the ankles, the hum of the bulbs, the deep clunk of the distant valve: the Sump-Works at its accustomed business, neither waiting for you nor objecting to you, going on.
