# The Loom-Warren

It is built into the hollow pith of a single chambered stalk — one of the green colonnades that grow in the low country, though from inside you cannot sense the outside at all. The stalk has been alive long enough to forget being young. Its inner wall is ribbed like the inside of a throat, tough fibrous cellulose gone almost wooden with age, smooth in the rubbed places and shaggy everywhere else with secondary growth: pale lichenous fuzz, colonies of moss like green pelts, a soft undercoat of rootlet that cannot decide whether it is hair or hydraulics.

The whole chamber is perhaps the span of a large kitchen. That is the entire world.

A town lives here.

It has been here — this is the important thing — for so long that its form is indistinguishable from the stalk's own form. The settlement is not built *on* the plant; it is built *of* it and *into* it, and the plant has grown *around* the building until no one could say which came first. Platforms are cantilevered from thickened nodes where the stalk once bore leaves; the nodes have swollen into shelves, load-bearing and living, warm to the touch. Ladders are worn grooves up the wall's inner ribs, deepened by generations of palms and soles until the fibre is polished to a waxy amber. Doorways are slits in partition-membranes that the plant obligingly keeps thin in those places, healing around the frame each season without ever quite closing.

The mills do the rest.

Because everything here runs on the stalk's own slow pressures. Up in the high chambers, somewhere far above this pocket, the plant pumps sap under tremendous hydraulic force, and the builders of the warren — long gone, or maybe still here and simply indistinguishable from residents — tapped the column. Wooden pipes the diameter of a thigh thread the whole chamber, varnished black with resin-sweat. They feed turbines. The turbines drive looms. The looms drive carders. The carders drive shuttles. The shuttles drive pounders and retters and combs and twisters and spindle-frames and the long chattering ranks of fibre-splitters that hang from the ceiling like bats made of oak and brass.

The whole pocket is a textile town. It makes thread from itself. The plant sheds pith-floss constantly from its inner walls — that is the shaggy undercoat — and the machines harvest, ret, comb, spin, ply, and wind it into skeins that are stacked in every niche and cranny, pale green fading to bone. No one exports anything. The skeins simply accumulate, and when a wall of them grows too thick, it is built into. Rooms are defined by bales.

The noise.

The noise is the landscape. Every surface is a drumhead because every surface is fibre under tension over a hollow. The looms slam. The turbines groan a bass note that lives in the sternum. Wooden gears the size of millstones knock against wooden gears. Leather belts slap their pulleys. Above all of this the hydraulic pipes sing — a high continuous whistling shriek from the pressure-relief whistles that keep the system from bursting — and the whistles harmonize against themselves in standing waves that shift by a quarter-tone when someone opens a door on the far side of the chamber. There is no speech here. There cannot be. The townspeople sign, or press foreheads together and shout into each other's skulls through bone conduction, or more usually simply do not communicate, because everyone already knows what everyone is doing. Nothing has changed in a very long time.

The light is dim and greenish and comes through the stalk wall itself — the outer skin is translucent at this depth, and filters the daylight down into a submarine glow that does not vary between noon and afternoon because the stalk's wall is thick enough to smear time. At night it simply goes darker-green, then black-green. Small oil lamps burn a resin pressed from the plant's own wound-sap, giving off a clean vegetable light that smells faintly of pepper and pine.

It is cool in here. The constant evaporation from the pipes keeps the temperature low — not cold, but the cool of a cellar, the cool of something deep in shade that has never known sun. Condensation beads on every metal fitting (there is almost no metal; what little there is comes from old tools, sacred and reused). The dampness is not rain but the sweat of the plant itself, breathed out through leaf-equivalents somewhere far above and condensed down here into a mist that never quite falls. Water pools in the low joints of the floor and is drunk directly; it tastes faintly sweet and faintly of grass.

Almost nothing else lives here. A kind of pale cave-cricket, deaf of course, that eats spilled pith-floss. A colony of small blind moths whose larvae bore decorative galleries in the softer walls and whose adult forms the weavers occasionally catch and crush for the iridescent dust in their wings, which is worked into certain ceremonial skeins. That is all. No birds, no mammals, no reptiles — the pocket is sealed, and has been sealed so long that whatever fauna were original have either gone extinct or become the cricket and the moth.

The townspeople themselves. It is difficult to say much. They are small, because the chamber is small, and they are pale, because the light is dim, and they are deaf, because of the noise — deaf from birth now, the trait long since fixed — and they have the thickened palms and splayed toes of people who climb ribbed walls all day. They wear their own cloth. They are their own cloth, practically. Faces wrapped against fibre-dust, hands bandaged in the skeins they spin, a people slowly being absorbed into their product and into the plant that produces them both.

And it does not change. This is the strangest thing. You could come back in a hundred years and the same looms would be slamming, the same whistles shrieking their quartertone chord, the same skeins stacked a little higher in the same corners, the same dim green light. The plant grows so slowly that for human purposes it does not grow. The machines wear so slowly that repair is indistinguishable from operation. Births and deaths balance to the decimal. The accumulation of thread is the only clock, and the thread is consumed by the walls at the same rate it is produced.

The warren is not going anywhere. It has already arrived. It arrived so long ago that arrival is its permanent condition — a single held chord of wood and water and shrieking air inside one green pillar in a field of green pillars, and the field goes on for miles above it and none of that matters, because here, in this pocket, the loom comes down, and the loom comes down, and the loom comes down.
