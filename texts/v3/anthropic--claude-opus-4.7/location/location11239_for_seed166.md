# The Drowned Works

It sits about ninety feet down, in a basin of cold green water where the light comes in like it has been filtered through old bottle glass. The surface is somewhere far above — visible, when you look up, only as a paler lid, a slow shifting brightness that never quite resolves into sun. Down here the light is enough to see by, but it is the kind of light that flattens things, takes the edges off, makes iron and stone agree to be the same color.

The works themselves — and they are works, unmistakably, though no one made them anymore — occupy maybe two hundred yards of seabed. A small operation. A hamlet of machinery. There is a central engine-house of riveted plate, its roof long gone, its walls standing because the water holds them up as much as anything. Inside, the bones of a steam plant: a boiler furred over in slow pale growths, a flywheel half its diameter taken by accretion, a governor whose brass balls hang motionless at the angle they hung at when the last pressure left the lines. Pipes run out from this building in three directions, lying on the bottom like roots, joining smaller satellite structures — a pump-house, a thing that was perhaps a winding shed, and one squat brick chimney that ends not in air but in a soft cloud of suspended silt that never fully settles and never fully clears.

The metal is past rusting. It has reached that stage where rust has become a kind of skin, dense and stable, the color of dried blood under the green. Nothing flakes. Nothing falls. The corrosion finished its work long ago and the structures are now in whatever shape they will keep. A handrail bends where it bent a century ago. A valve wheel is locked at three-quarters open. Everything is in the position of its last moment, and that moment is permanent.

Vegetation is sparse and slow. A few stands of some pale stringy weed grow along the lee side of the engine-house wall, lifting in the faint movement of water like hair underwater, which is what they are. There are patches of crust on the pipes — encrusting things, not quite plant — in the muted yellow-green of old lichen. Nothing flowers. Nothing fruits. There is enough life to soften the metal and not enough to claim it.

There are no fish. This is the strangest thing about the place, though you don't notice it at first because the absence is so complete it reads as ordinary. No darting silver. No slow groupers in the shadows of the boiler. No crabs working the seams of the brick. The water is clean and cold and empty of anything that watches back. Whatever drove animals from this basin — chemistry, perhaps, leached out of the works long ago and now diluted past harm but still remembered in some way the water keeps — has left a sterile pocket that the surrounding sea has never recolonized. It is a quiet that is not silence: there is the small constant whisper of water against metal, the occasional hollow pock of a bubble releasing from somewhere inside the boiler, the long low note when current works across the open mouth of the chimney like breath across a bottle. Mostly quiet. Mostly.

The scale is wrong for what the place is. A factory should sprawl. This one is compact, almost domestic, as if someone built an industrial complex to the dimensions of a cottage and then sank it. You could walk the perimeter in five minutes if you could walk. The pipes do not lead anywhere beyond the boundary of the works; they end at capped flanges or simply stop, neat, as though the rest of the world they were meant to connect to was never built or has been taken away.

Nothing changes here. That is the rule of the place. The light shifts a little with the hour of the surface, but the works themselves are at equilibrium — corroded to their final form, colonized to their final extent, abandoned to their final use, which is to stand. A diver coming back after twenty years would find every angle the same. The flywheel's shadow falls where it fell. The silt cloud at the chimney top holds its shape. Even the weed, growing, seems to grow only enough to replace what it loses, so that the total quantity of weed in the basin is, year on year, the same.

It is a small cold quiet place where industry came, did whatever it did, stopped, and was preserved — not as ruin, exactly, because ruin implies a process still underway. This is past ruin. This is what ruin settles into when it has finished and the water agrees to keep it.
