# The Lung Plateau

It begins with the breathing. Before you see anything, before your eyes adjust to the green light filtering through the canopy three hundred meters above, you hear it — the slow, organ-deep inhalation of the plateau itself. Every forty seconds or so, a hot wind moves through the colonnades of trunks, drawn upward through gaps in the leaf-roof, and every forty seconds it returns, exhaled, smelling of resin and crushed pepper and something fermenting in a hollow you cannot find.

The plateau sits perhaps two kilometers above whatever sea once was. You cannot see down from it. You cannot see up from it either, in most places — the trees of the upper story are so wide that three people holding hands could not span one, and they are spaced perhaps fifty paces apart, columns in a hypostyle hall that extends past the curve of the world. Between them: warm air, slanting bars of citrus-colored light, and the constant churning shadow of wings.

The fauna is the loudest thing that has ever happened to your ears. Not jungle-chatter — something more orchestral and more dangerous. Hornbills the size of dogs call in pairs across the kilometer between trunks, and their calls take a full second to arrive, so you hear the answer before the question fades. Cicadas the length of your forearm grind out a sound like sheet metal flexing. Somewhere — always somewhere — a troop of long-limbed mammals is moving through the mid-canopy, and their passage sets off a cascade of alarm whistles in the smaller things, and the smaller things startle the roosting flocks, and the flocks rise in clattering thousands, and for a minute the air is so thick with bodies that the light goes brown. Then it settles. Then it begins again. There is no quiet hour. Night, when it comes, only changes the instruments.

The trees are the architecture and the substrate and the weather system. Their bark is grooved deeply enough to climb without rope. Their lowest branches begin at sixty meters and spread laterally for a hundred more, so that the canopy is a continuous, undulating ceiling — but it is a *sparse* ceiling, full of skylights, because the plateau is not crowded. Vast open floors of moss and leaf-litter stretch between the giants. You can walk for an hour without your shoulder brushing anything alive larger than a beetle. This is what makes the fauna possible: the trees feed the world, but the spaces between them are highways.

Water comes intermittently and strangely. It does not rain on the plateau — the clouds are below, you are above them — but every dawn the canopy condenses the night's exhalation into fat droplets that fall in slow scattered showers for about twenty minutes, ringing on broad leaves like a hesitant percussion section warming up. Then it stops. The droplets pool in the bark-grooves and in the orchid-cups and in the great pitcher-flowers that grow as side-organs of the trunks themselves, holding liters at a time, dark and brewing with drowned insects. Animals drink from these. So do people, when there are people.

There is a river — somewhere. You can hear it at certain hours, a different bass note under the bird-noise, and once a day you might cross a depression where the moss is darker and the air a few degrees cooler and water moves under the leaf-mat, glimpsed only through breathing holes. It runs in the substrate, not on it. The plateau drinks before the surface ever gets a turn.

The heat is the kind that does not let you forget your body. Not the wet smother of a lowland jungle — the plateau is too high and too ventilated for that — but a dry radiant heat from above, where the sun bakes the upper canopy into an oven that then sighs its heat downward in those forty-second breaths. You sweat constantly. Your sweat dries fast. You drink from the pitcher-cups and you eat the soft starchy fruits that drop with a thud you can feel through your feet, and you keep moving, because to stop is to be noticed.

The erosion is in the trees themselves, which is how you know how old this place is. Many of the great columns are hollow at the base, scooped out by centuries of wind and fungus and the slow patient gnawing of things that live inside wood. You can walk into the hollows. Some of them are larger than houses. The trees do not die from this; they have grown around their own absences, and the hollows become rooms, and the rooms become — sometimes — homes.

This is where the built things appear. They are small and they are scattered and they are not new. Inside certain hollows: platforms of lashed cane, a hearth-stone blackened by long use, a clay vessel left on a shelf cut into living wood, hand-axes of a dark glassy stone propped against the wall as if their owner just stepped out. The people of this place are few. You may go three days without seeing one. When you do, they are usually high — climbing the bark-grooves with practiced unconcern, hunting with bows whose arrows are tipped with the same dark glass, wearing very little because of the heat, and carrying woven nets that they cast for the smaller birds. They do not build outward. They build inward, into the hollows the trees give them, and they paint the inner walls of those hollows with figures in red and white earth, and the figures are not always of animals you can recognize.

Because there is something else on the plateau. This is where the strangeness lives.

It has to do with the breathing. The forty-second cycle is too regular, too coordinated across an area too large, to be the wind. The trees are doing it. All of them. Together. The plateau inhales and exhales as one organism, and on still afternoons, if you stand in an open glade and watch the leaves, you can see the slight contraction of the entire canopy, the way a sleeping animal's flank rises and falls. The people know this. They time their movements to the exhalation — it is easier to climb when the tree is breathing out, they say, easier to be unseen. They will not sleep in a hollow that has stopped breathing. A tree that has stopped breathing is going to fall, and the falling of one of these trees is a continental event, a sound heard for two days' walk, a clearing that takes a generation to fill, a thing the people sing about for longer than they remember why.

And there is the matter of the pale things. You will see them at the edge of vision, in the long aisles between trunks, in the brown moments when the flocks rise. Tall, bipedal, the color of the inside of a fruit. They do not approach. The people do not speak of them in any language a stranger would be allowed to learn. They may be a kind of animal. They may be older residents than the people. They may be what the trees dream about during the long hot afternoons when even the cicadas pause.

The plateau is in balance. That is the feeling, finally, that settles over you after a week of walking it — that everything here has been doing what it does for so long that it has worn grooves in time itself. The hornbills will call across the kilometers tomorrow and the day after and a thousand years from now. The trees will breathe. The river will run under the moss. The people will paint their hollows and cast their nets and time their climbs to the exhalation. The pale things will watch from the long aisles. And the sound — the enormous, layered, unceasing sound of a world entirely full of life and entirely unbothered by you — will go on, and on, and on, indifferent and complete.

You can stay. Many things have. The plateau has room.
