# The Engine Plateau

Start with the height. You are nearly two kilometers up — not on a mountain exactly, but on a great level table of pale ochre stone that the world hasn't finished deciding about. The plateau extends in every direction past where the eye can follow, and at its edges it doesn't fall away into valleys so much as simply *stop*, as though the geology had been laid down yesterday and the work of carving canyons hasn't begun.

There is no water here. None. Not a stream, not a puddle, not even the dark stain where dew might have gathered. And yet the place is lush — that is the first thing that refuses to make sense, and the second is how quietly it refuses.

The vegetation grows from the stone itself. Broad, fleshy leaves the color of old jade, ribbed and waxy, opening from rosettes that root directly into the rock without soil. They are warm to the touch. They breathe through pores you can see if you bend close — tiny puckered mouths that draw something from the air, something that isn't water vapor, because there is no water vapor. The air here is dry as a kiln and smells faintly of warm metal and crushed pepper.

Between the plant-clusters: enormous distances. A rosette here, another a hundred meters off, a third on the horizon. The plateau is *empty* in the way a cathedral is empty — most of what is here is the space between things. You can walk for an hour and pass four living things and not one animal, not a beetle, not a worm in the rootless stone. There is no fauna. There has never been fauna. The plants do not need pollinators; they reproduce by some slower arithmetic.

And then, scattered through this emptiness at intervals of roughly a kilometer, the engines.

They are not built. That is important. No one constructed them and no one tends them. They have grown the way the plants have grown, out of the bedrock, in shapes that suggest pistons and flywheels and great segmented bellows — long cylinders of the same ochre stone fused with veins of darker metal, paired and counterweighted, with rocker arms that rise and fall with a slowness that is almost imperceptible. One full stroke might take an hour. They are warm. They are the source of the warmth on the plateau: a low, even, industrial heat that radiates from their casings and keeps the air at the temperature of a sun-warmed wall.

What do they do? They breathe, perhaps, in concert with the plants. They turn nothing. They drive nothing. Or rather — and this is the suspicion that grows the longer you stand among them — they drive the *stability itself*. They are the reason nothing changes here. The reason the wind doesn't pick up. The reason the stone hasn't begun to weather. The reason the plants neither grow nor die but simply persist at whatever size they reached when they reached it.

The sound they make is almost no sound. A deep, slow *hush* — not a hiss, not a hum, but the noise a very large lung makes from very far away. If you put your ear to the casing of one, you hear nothing closer; the sound stays at the same distance no matter how near you stand. It is the ambient note of the world. Under it: nothing. No insect chorus, no rustle of leaves (the leaves do not rustle; there is no wind), no birdcall, no water. Just the slow vast breath of the machines that are not machines, and the brighter silence around them.

The light is ordinary. That's the strangest part. A clean midday sun in a sky the pale blue of a robin's egg, casting hard shadows from the rocker arms across the ochre stone. You expect, in a place this odd, dim light or colored light or the wrong number of suns. But it is just daylight, the kind you've known your whole life, falling on a landscape that has not yet decided to become a landscape.

Nothing erodes. Nothing accumulates. The shadows of the rocker arms creep across the rock as the day proceeds, but tomorrow they will creep the same way, and the day after, and the year after that. The plateau is in equilibrium with something we cannot see — perhaps with itself, perhaps with the engines, perhaps with whatever the plants are quietly drawing from the dry air.

If you stay long enough — and people who come here always stay longer than they meant to — you begin to suspect that the plateau is not a finished place that has lost its water and its animals and its weather. It is a place *before*. Before erosion. Before fauna. Before the loud business of a world getting underway. The engines are not running it down; they are holding it open. Keeping the page blank. Maintaining the long warm pause before something begins.

And nothing begins.

That is the rule of this world: the pause is the place. The pause is permanent. The engines breathe their slow breath and the jade rosettes hold their leaves to the dry light and the ochre stone waits, patient as a bell that will not be rung, for a beginning that has been indefinitely deferred.

You can leave whenever you want. The plateau will be exactly the same when you return.
