# The Slow Canopy

The first thing to understand is that there is no ground. Not in any sense you would recognize. Somewhere, perhaps a kilometer below, perhaps more, there may be stone or the memory of stone — but what serves as floor, wall, and sky here is the canopy itself: a layered, continent-spanning mass of living tissue so old it has forgotten it was ever separate things.

The place sits high. High enough that clouds form *inside* it, threading between the tiers of leaf and bough, condensing on the undersides of broad dark fronds and falling again as a kind of permanent slow rain that never quite reaches a bottom. Everything drips. Everything is warm with the warmth of bodies — the canopy's own respiration, pooled and held. The air has weight. You can feel it settle on your skin like a second skin that is slightly wetter than your own.

The trees — if they are trees — grew into each other so long ago that the question of where one ends is meaningless. Trunks have fused into pillars the width of small valleys. Branches have grown down into other branches and knit, forming ribbed arches, nested vaults, buttresses that plunge into the mist below and vanish. Moss over moss over moss, generations deep. Lichen in plates the size of continents of their own. Ferns growing out of the hollowed chests of older ferns. Orchids flowering quietly on the bones of orchids that flowered a thousand years ago. The whole mass has settled — that is the word — the way a very old face settles into its expression. There are no sharp edges left. Everything is rounded, softened, accepted by its own weight.

The light is dim and green and has been filtered through so many layers before it reaches any given place that it comes apart into something more like a suggestion of light than light itself. At the highest tiers it is probably bright — but no one who lives in the middle strata has seen that in any meaningful sense. Day and night both arrive as slow changes in the color of the damp: bluer, then greener, then bluer again.

It is mostly quiet. This surprises people who expect jungle. But a space this vast, this cushioned, absorbs sound. A bird call three hundred meters away arrives as a softened shape, not a note. The loudest sound is usually water — the tick and patter of condensation finding its way down, the occasional bolus of gathered rain sliding off a leaf as wide as a room and falling, falling, through tiers, striking softer leaves, softer, softer, until the sound dissolves into the general hush. Sometimes, very far off, something large exhales. You do not investigate.

There are animals. Many, in fact — the fauna is abundant, but abundance here is vertical and slow. Things cling. Things hang. A sloth-like creature with moss growing from its back so thickly that it is more moss than creature, indistinguishable from a hummock unless you happen to catch the blink of a wet black eye. Pale frogs in the cupped water of bromeliad-cathedrals, each cup its own nation. Long serpentine things in the wetter folds, moving with such deliberation they seem to be thinking their way through space rather than swimming. Moths the size of hands and the color of old paper, drifting between pillars. Something — no one has seen it directly — that sings a single low note, once, every few days, and the canopy briefly listens.

The weirdness is in the way time works here. Or rather, in the way the canopy *holds* time. There are pockets — dim chambers formed by the intergrowth of three or four enormous boughs — where the air is slightly older than the air just outside. You can tell because sound takes a beat longer to cross them. Because a dropped leaf falls at the wrong speed. Because you emerge from one with a feeling you have been somewhere longer than your body agrees with. The canopy is so vast, and has been settling for so long, that it has grown its own physics in places, small eccentricities the way an old house grows creaks. Nothing dangerous. Nothing fast. Just — local.

And there are built things, though barely. Here and there, woven into the living matter: a ring of stones no bigger than fists, set into the crotch of a bough, half-swallowed by bark. A platform of bound reeds, rotted to the structural minimum, still somehow holding. A carved face, very small, in a trunk so old the face has been stretched by the tree's slow growth into something sorrowful and elongated, a weeping saint. Who made these things is not clear. They were not made recently. They were perhaps not all made by the same kind of hands. The canopy has absorbed them the way it absorbs everything — not destroying, just including, until they become another kind of moss.

No tools. No devices. Nothing struck from metal, nothing spun from fiber more complex than what grows. Whoever made the marks used what was at hand and what was at hand was the place itself.

The weather, when it comes, is the canopy's own. Internal fronts move slowly through the upper tiers: a warm damp pushing against a cooler damp, generating rain that falls sideways through the layers for days before anything noticeable changes. Storms are felt, not heard — a gradual tightening of the air, a deepening of the green, a sense that the vast living mass is drawing a long breath. Then, for a week, the drip becomes a pour. Then the pour slackens. Then the drip resumes. This is the rhythm. Seasons are measured in the life-spans of small animals; ages in the life-spans of particular boughs; the canopy itself predates the concept.

Nothing here is ending, exactly. It has simply arrived. This is the shape the world takes when it has had enough time to decide what it wants to be, and what it wants is this: wet, warm, green, vast, patient, full of slow breathing and the occasional far-off note, with a few small forgotten shrines knit into its flesh, and water that falls forever and never quite lands.
