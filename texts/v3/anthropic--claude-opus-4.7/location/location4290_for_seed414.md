# The Unfinished Cleft

High in a range that has not yet decided on its own shape, there is a cleft in the rock that no map has found because the mountain is too young to be mapped. The stone here is pale and chalky where it breaks, pinkish where water has touched it, and in most places it has never broken at all — it simply extrudes, smooth and dull, like something pushed up from below that hasn't yet learned the habit of weathering. The cleft is perhaps forty paces long and narrow enough that a person could stand in the middle and touch neither wall without leaning. It is open to the sky in a jagged stripe overhead.

You would not call it a canyon. A canyon implies erosion, the patient authorship of water. This is a split — a first fracture — and the rock on either side still matches, ridge for ridge, the way a torn page matches its other half. Run a hand along one wall and the opposing wall will have the socket waiting.

The air inside is warm. Not hot, but held — the way a cupped hand holds warmth. The altitude should make it cold, and higher up, above the rim, it is; but the cleft itself traps something, maybe the breath of the rock, maybe just sun pooled from the afternoon. Condensation beads on the lower stone in the mornings and runs down in threads thin as hair, collecting in shallow dishes the rock has not yet learned to make properly. These pools are irregular, temporary, each one a puddle's worth, no more. A small animal could drink its fill and empty one.

Vegetation is scant and strange: cushions of a grey-green moss-thing that grows only where the condensation runs, and a few stalked plants — not quite ferns, not quite anything — that lean out from cracks at the angle their seed happened to wedge. No trees. Nothing has had time for trees. The ground is mostly bare rock and a thin grit of the rock's own shedding, pale as bone meal.

And yet the cleft is *loud*.

This is the strangeness of the place. For all its sparseness, for all its emptiness of architecture and water and plant, it rings with animal sound. Bats — or bat-adjacent things, leather-winged and small-eyed — roost in the upper cracks in numbers that seem impossible given how little there is to eat, and at dusk they pour out in a sheet, shrieking. Before dusk and after dawn, a species of cliff-cricket that lives only in fracture-rock rubs its wing cases against the stone itself, using the cleft as a resonator, so that the whole chamber hums at a pitch that sits just inside the range of teeth. Birds — a kind of swift, russet and forked — nest on ledges and quarrel constantly, and their quarrels echo, and the echoes are picked up by other swifts and answered as if they were real birds, so that the population sounds three times larger than it is. Somewhere down in the shadowed end, something larger breathes — slow, bellows-like, untroubled. You do not see it. You hear it between the other sounds, a low register that the cricket-hum rides on top of.

The light is dim because the cleft is narrow and the sun passes over the slot quickly. For most of the day it is the color of weak tea, filtered by nothing in particular — just geometry, just the walls. When direct sun does fall in, for perhaps twenty minutes near noon, the pink of the wet rock flares briefly and the bats deeper in shift and mutter in their sleep.

There are marks of passage, though no one lives here. Someone, once — more than once, perhaps, over a span so long the visits do not know about each other — left things. A cairn of four stones at the cleft's entrance, leaning. A smear of red ochre on a flat face, at shoulder height, now faded to the color of old rust, a handprint with one finger shorter than it should be. Further in, under an overhang, a small pile of bones arranged — not scattered, arranged — in a spiral. The bones are from the swifts and the bats and something with a jaw that does not match any of the living things here. The arranger is gone. The arrangement persists because nothing in the cleft disturbs it; the rock does not yet make wind of its own, and the animals have their own geometries and do not care about this one.

No tools. No fire-scar. Whoever made the marks made them with hands and with what hands could carry, and then left, and the cleft kept their marks the way it keeps everything — by not yet having learned how to lose things.

The rules here, as far as rules go:

Water arrives only as mist and dew, and only in the lower third of the walls. The upper rock is bone-dry and will cut you. The pools refill overnight and are drained by noon, by the animals or by the sun. Nothing grows above shoulder height. Every living thing in the cleft is louder than it should be for its size, because the walls give back what is given, amplified. The deep breathing from the shadowed end does not come closer and does not go away; it has been there since before the cleft was open, and may in fact be the reason the cleft opened. Change happens slowly. The cleft widens perhaps the width of a fingernail in a human lifetime. The ochre handprint has faded by a shade since the last visitor. The spiral of bones has not moved at all.

If you sat down against the wall and stayed — stayed long enough to become part of the slow schedule of the place — you would notice that the loudness is not chaos. It has a shape. The crickets give way to the swifts give way to the bats give way to the breathing, and the breathing gives way, briefly, in the smallest hour, to silence — a silence so complete and so brief that you might miss it, and in which the rock itself makes a single small sound, a tick, like a cooling kiln, as it settles one increment further into being a mountain.

Then the crickets start again, and the cleft goes on becoming itself, at its own pace, loud and half-empty and warm and new.
