# The Pumpworks

Begin with the water, because everything here begins with the water.

It comes up through the floor. The floor is iron — pitted, scaled, riveted in panels the size of barn doors — and the water seeps between the rivets, beads on the rust-blooms, runs in thin sheets toward grilles set every twelve paces. You can hear it under everything: the slow continuous suck and gargle of drainage, a city-sized throat swallowing in its sleep. The whole plain is a floor. The whole floor is a roof. Somewhere beneath, in cisterns nobody has opened in centuries, the water collects and is pumped, and pumped, and pumped.

The pumps are why the air shakes.

They stand at uneven intervals across the flats — squat black engines the height of three men, their flywheels turning at the patient speed of old labor, each one slightly out of phase with the next. From a single pump the sound would be a heavy reciprocal thud, almost musical. But there are hundreds of them visible from any rise, and thousands beyond the visible, and what they make together is not rhythm but a standing wall of noise: the iron sigh of pistons, the wet cough of valves, the long groan of crank-throws turning in oil that hasn't been changed in any lifetime. You feel it in your teeth. You feel it in the soles of your boots through the iron floor. Conversation is impossible. People here have developed a slow flat-handed sign language and a habit of not needing to speak.

The landscape, if you can call it that, is nearly level. Whatever this place was before being plated over — hills, a valley, a river-delta — has been worn and worked and built upon for so long that only the gentlest undulations remain, long shallow swells in the iron that the rainwater traces in slow rivulets. The plating itself is ancient in the way of cathedral floors: each panel cast in some forgotten century, its maker's mark blurred to a smear, its surface dimpled by the passage of innumerable feet and the slow chewing of weather. In places the iron has rusted clean through and been patched, and the patches patched, until a single square yard of floor is a palimpsest of seven repairs in seven alloys, each a different shade of brown-black-green.

It is cool. Not cold — the pumps throw off a faint warmth, and the rising water is the temperature of cellars — but cool enough that breath shows, and the constant condensation beads on every vertical surface. The atmosphere is a held breath of mist. Visibility is perhaps two hundred yards in the better hours; the pumps fade into silhouette and then into mere darker patches of dark, and then into the suggestion of pumps, and then into the indeterminate.

Because there is little light. Whatever sun this world has does not reach here, or reaches only as a high grey diffusion that might be cloud or might be a ceiling — it's genuinely unclear, looking up, whether the dimness above is sky or vault. Some of the larger pumps carry lanterns on their crowns, oil-flames behind soot-fogged glass, and these mark the middle distance as a scatter of dull orange pinpricks, like a town seen from very far at night. Between them: gulfs of unlit iron and mist.

Vegetation is sparse and improbable. In the seams between panels, where rust-silt and rain have made a thin acid soil, things grow that have learned to live on iron and noise. A black moss, mostly. A pale reed with a hollow stem that the wind from the pump-exhausts plays like a row of badly tuned organ pipes — you hear these only in the seconds when the pumps' phasing accidentally aligns and lets a high thin note through. There are no flowers, but in one or two places a kind of fungus has colonized an abandoned pump-housing and made of it a furred grey tower, soft-looking, almost warm.

No animals. None. Not a fly, not a worm in the seam-soil, not a bird overhead. Whether they were driven out or were never here is not a question with a remembered answer. Sometimes a person, walking, becomes convinced they have heard a dog bark somewhere in the mist, and stops, and listens, and the pumps go on, and after a while they walk on too.

The strangeness of the place is not in any single feature but in the proportions. The pumps are too many. They cannot all be pumping the same water; there is not that much water in any reasonable world. And yet they pump, and the floor stays roughly dry-wet, neither flooding nor draining, as though the whole apparatus is in some long equilibrium with a reservoir whose size is the size of the planet. People who have walked the flats for years develop theories. The most common is that the pumps are not removing water but circulating it — that what falls as condensation here was pumped up somewhere else, and what is pumped down here falls as mist somewhere else, and the whole flat is one organ of a weather that has replaced weather. This theory is unprovable and widely believed.

The dynamics are slow but real. Pumps fail; you can tell from a great distance when one has stopped, because a hole opens in the wall of sound, a quiet shaped like a missing tooth. Repair crews go out — small figures with carts, with the long-handled wrenches that are the universal tool here — and within a day or a week the hole closes again. Sometimes a pump cannot be saved and is left to rust, and around its silence a small ecology of mosses and reeds expands, and after a generation it is a green-black hummock in the iron plain, a little island of the world before pumping. There are perhaps a dozen of these within walking distance of any given point. They are where people go to be quiet, which is to say, to hear the pumps from slightly further away.

At the regional scale — and this place is regional, valley-sized, the iron going on past any horizon you can verify — there are gradients. To what some call the north, the pumps grow larger and slower and the floor lower, and the mist thickens until it is essentially rain held in suspension; the water there runs deeper between the panels and the sound deepens too, into a register you feel rather than hear. To the south the pumps are smaller and faster and more numerous, a finer-grained machinery, and the floor rises by imperceptible degrees until one is, without having climbed, somewhat higher than one began.

Nobody knows the edges. People have walked for weeks in one direction and reported only more pumps, more mist, more iron, the lanterns going on forever like a slow constellation. It is possible the pumpworks has no edge. It is possible it is the whole surface of something. It is possible — and this is the thought one has, lying down at night in a shelter built into the lee of a pump-housing, with the iron warm against one's back and the great patient thudding going on and on through the bones of the floor — it is possible that the pumps are not draining a world but making one, and that the mist and the seam-moss and the cool damp air and the long flat plain are all the slow exhalation of a machine that was built so long ago that no one alive remembers what it was for, only that it works, and that the work is the place, and that the place is the work, and that to live here is to be one more small wet thing inside the breathing of it.
