# The Sump Works

Three hundred feet below the crust, in a pocket the maps don't name, there is a town called Lowdamp by the people who work it and nothing at all by anyone else.

You come down through a shaft lined in sweating brick. The air thickens as you descend — by the third landing it is wet enough to drink, and by the fifth it is simply the atmosphere of the place, warm as blood, heavier than what your lungs expect. Condensation beads on the iron rungs of the ladder and runs back down onto your hands. Everything here weeps. The walls weep. The pipes weep. The men weep, though they'd say it was only the heat.

The cavern opens wider than you'd guess — not vast, no grand nave, but room enough for perhaps forty buildings arranged along a crooked main street and two lanes branching off it. A hamlet's worth of structures, pressed close against the rock, with dark gaps of wet stone between them. Brick kilns, a pump-house, foundry sheds with their roofs missing because the ceiling of the cavern is already a roof. Boiler stacks vent upward into fissures that carry the smoke somewhere the miners don't ask about. Gaslight — real gas, piped from god knows where — burns at the street corners in greenish globes, enough to see your neighbor's face but not enough to read by. Most work is done by feel and by long habit.

The stone itself is young. That's the word the old foreman uses and it's the right one. The walls are not shaped the way proper cavern walls should be — no flowstone, no mature curves worn by millennia of patient water. Instead the rock looks half-made: raw fracture planes still sharp at their edges, mineral crusts just beginning to accrete in the corners, little stubs of stalactite that haven't yet learned how to drip straight. The geology of the place is in its infancy. You could watch a feature form, if you were patient — and patient is what everyone here becomes, because the rhythm of the cavern is slow, slow, the slow pulse of something still deciding what it wants to be.

But what you notice, after the heat and the wet and the brick, is the fauna.

They are everywhere. They are the reason the town exists.

Call them toads, though they aren't quite. Fist-sized, palm-sized, some as big as loaves of bread, skins the color of wet clay, and they cover the walls — cluster on the pipe insulation, squat along the gutters, mass in the gaps between buildings in heaving carpets that shift when lantern-light falls on them. Their throats pulse in a staggered chorus, and the chorus is the sound of Lowdamp: a thick low croaking that rises and falls in waves, punctuated by the hiss of steam valves and the iron clang of the foundry and the suck-and-gurgle of the pumps. You cannot hear yourself think in the usual way. You learn to think underneath the sound, the way fish think underneath current.

The toads secrete something. A pale mucus that runs off them in the heat and collects in the channels cut along every street. The channels lead to the works: vats, retorts, cracking towers in miniature, where the secretion is rendered and distilled into an oil that burns clean and long and is, apparently, worth the trouble of maintaining a settlement three hundred feet underground. Nobody in Lowdamp speaks about the buyers. Barrels go up the freight shaft weekly. Payment comes down. This is the economy.

The vegetation is an afterthought. Pale fungal rosettes push up between the cobbles where nobody's boots crush them, and a few determined ferns — true ferns, improbable — grow near the gaslights, living on the piped gas's thin emission of something they can use. A scatter of moss. That is all. The cavern is not a garden. It is a damp hot brick town with frogs.

The rules of this world, as I make them out:

The water table is exactly at the level of the streets. Dig down a foot anywhere and you will find standing water, warm as bath. This is why the buildings stand on short brick piers, why the foundry floors are raised, why the children (there are a few children) wear rubber at the ankle always.

The heat comes from below, not above. Somewhere further down the rock is hotter still, and the cavern sits in the upper thermal shell of that deeper furnace. In summer — and it is always summer here, the concept of seasons having been left at the surface — men work stripped to the waist and the women wear shifts of cheesecloth, and everyone drinks constantly from clay jars kept cool by evaporation in the draft near the vents.

The toads do not leave. They were here before the town and they will be here after. The town was built around them, tucked into the spaces they don't occupy, and there is an unwritten courtesy: you do not harm a toad outside of the rendering season, and during the rendering season you take only the old ones, the ones with the clouded eyes, and you take them quickly. Nobody remembers who made this rule. Everyone keeps it.

The dynamic of the place is patient. Nothing changes quickly. A new building takes a year of argument before the first brick is laid. A child grows up not noticing the cavern changing around her, and only when she is old does she see that the stalactite near the pump-house is now as long as her finger, where it used to be nothing at all. The walls accrete. The toads breed in their slow cycles. The oil flows. The barrels rise. Above, the world does whatever the world does. Down here, the chorus pulses, and the gaslights burn green, and the brick weeps, and the rock — slowly, slowly — learns how to be rock.
