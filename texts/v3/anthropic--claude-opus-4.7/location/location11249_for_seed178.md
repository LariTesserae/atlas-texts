# The Cold Loud Valley

Begin with the sound, because the sound is what you would notice first, before your eyes adjusted to the dimness — before you registered the cold pressing through your clothes, before you saw that the air itself was so thick with mist you could draw your finger through it and leave a track that held for several seconds.

The sound is water. An astonishing amount of water, falling.

The valley is perhaps six kilometers across at its widest, narrowing northward into a slot where black cliffs lean toward each other. The cliffs are new — geologically new, raw — their faces still bearing the conchoidal fractures of whatever event tore them open, no lichen yet, no softening. From every crack and seam in those cliffs, water emerges. Not in single falls but in hundreds. Some are ribbons no thicker than a wrist; some are full rivers leaping outward and disintegrating into vapor before they strike the talus below. The largest, on the eastern wall, is a wall of water itself, perhaps two hundred meters wide, and its impact at the base produces a basso vibration you feel in the marrow of your sternum, beneath the higher registers of all the other falls layered above it. The combined sound is past noise. It is a medium. You speak and the words leave your mouth and are simply gone; there is no echo because there is no silence for an echo to occupy.

The light is the blue-grey of an hour before dawn that never advances. Somewhere above the mist there must be a sun, or something playing the role of one, but here at valley-floor the illumination is diffuse and gentle and just sufficient — you can see twenty meters clearly, fifty meters as suggestion, beyond that only the dim massing of trees and the paler smear of falling water.

The vegetation has answered the abundance of water with abundance of its own. Mosses first — every surface furred in them, half a dozen species visible in the small patch beneath your boot, the dominant one a deep emerald that holds beads of condensation like a chainmail of glass. Then ferns, taller than a person, their fronds arching and dripping. Then the trees: something between cedar and tree-fern, with deeply furrowed bark and frond-like crowns that interlace overhead. They grow close, often touching, often fused at the base where two trunks have decided in their slow way to become one. Between the trunks are vines, horsetails, a low groundcover with leaves like tiny black spoons that fill with rainwater and tip when full.

Cold. The temperature hovers just above freezing. The mist on the moss freezes in places into delicate rime; in other places, where some warmth seeps up from below, the moss is merely sodden. Your breath joins the general fog and is lost in it.

The animals.

You begin to notice them only after you have stopped moving. They are everywhere, but they are mostly silent — they cannot afford to vocalize in a place where vocalization is futile, so they have gone over almost entirely to vision and to vibration. There are amphibian things in the pools at your feet, pale-bellied, with skin so translucent you can see the dark cluster of their hearts. There are six-legged climbers on the trunks, the size of cats, with eyes turned the size of plums to gather what light there is. There are flying things that are not birds and not bats but something like a swimming motion through the heavy air, their wings rippling. They communicate, those flying things, by patterns of bioluminescence along their flanks — soft greens and ambers that pulse and stream as they pass each other in the gloom.

And now the strangeness, which is not really strange so much as it is *unaccounted for*.

Threaded through all of this — the cliffs, the trees, the air itself — is a technology so advanced it has stopped looking like technology. You become aware of it gradually. The mist does not behave correctly: it organizes itself, sometimes, into long laminar streams that flow uphill against the breeze. The falling water from the eastern cliff, when you watch it long enough, performs subtle impossibilities — droplets that pause mid-fall for a fraction of a second, sheets that braid themselves into helices before resuming ordinary descent. The black stone of the cliff faces, examined closely, is not stone at all in places: it is something denser, something that drinks the dim light rather than reflecting it, and in the deepest fractures you can see, if you look carefully, faint geometric regularities — hairline patterns at the edge of visibility that suggest the rock is a skin over something else.

There are structures, too, though scattered. On a low rise in the middle distance, a single dark spire rises perhaps thirty meters, perfectly smooth, perfectly vertical, with no door or window or seam. It is wet with condensation like everything else; moss has begun, tentatively, on its lower meter. Further on, half-buried in the moss-mound of a fallen tree, an arc of the same material protrudes — fragment of something larger, broken, very old or made to look very old. There is a footbridge over one of the streams that is clearly meant for walking but no path leads to either end of it. There is, on the cliff face above the great fall, a small terrace cut at impossible scale into the rock, with what might be the suggestion of a door at its rear.

Whoever made these things — or whatever made them — is not present now, or is present in a way you cannot register. The place feels neither tended nor abandoned; it feels *posted*, the way a wilderness can feel posted, signs you can't read at the edges of a thing you're walking through.

The dynamic quality of the valley is its final and most disorienting feature. Nothing here is still. The cliffs themselves are shedding — not catastrophically, but constantly: small spalls of rock fall into the pools, new fissures open and begin to weep within hours, the eastern wall is visibly different in shape from morning to morning. The watercourses braid and rebraid across the valley floor; the river you crossed yesterday is gone today, replaced by three smaller channels and a long oxbow lake choked with new growth. The trees grow visibly. You can sit by a fern for an hour and watch its frond unfurl. The flying things wheel through the canopy in clouds of changing geometry, and the amphibians migrate in slow currents from pool to pool, and the bioluminescence ripples through the canopy in waves that suggest, almost suggest, a conversation, and the mist breathes in and out of the slot canyon at the valley's head as if the whole landscape were respiring.

The valley is young. It has not had time to settle into the configurations a landscape eventually finds. It is still becoming itself, loudly, in the cold and the dim and the perpetual rain of itself upon itself. The technology in it — whatever it is, whoever left it — is perhaps the reason the valley is so young: perhaps something opened it, recently, out of older rock, and the water and the cold and the life have all rushed in to occupy the new space, and the spires and the terrace and the broken arc are the evidence of the opening, half-swallowed already by the green.

You stand at the edge of it and the sound passes through you and you cannot hear yourself think and that is, you realize after a while, a kind of peace.
