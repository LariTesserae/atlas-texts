# The Cistern-Quarter

Three hundred meters under what used to be a river delta, there is a room the size of a small town that has only just learned it exists.

You come down through a shaft cut so recently the walls still smell of fractured mineral — wet rock breath, the iron-sweet exhalation of stone that has never been open to air. The shaft opens into the Quarter from above, and for the first dozen seconds your eyes register only the dim — a slow grey-green luminance with no obvious source, the color of light filtered through a great depth of standing water, though here it diffuses through air.

The Quarter is laid out as a settlement. That is the first surprise. Streets, low arcades, the geometry of habitation — but no inhabitants, and almost nothing per block. A single column-thing every fifty meters. A long curved wall, and beyond it a plaza, and beyond that another wall, and the plazas are mostly empty space, warm and damp and resonant. The density is wrong for a town and the silence between structures has weight. You can stand in a courtyard meant for a hundred people and be the only mass in it, your breath the only weather.

The construction is the second surprise. The walls are not built so much as **grown precise** — surfaces too smooth for any tooling, edges that meet at angles which seem to refuse the usual compromises of matter. Run a hand along a column and you find it neither warm nor cold but exactly skin-temperature, as if it were correcting itself against you. In places the walls are translucent for a centimeter or two before opacity resumes, and inside that translucent skin you can sometimes see slow motion — lattices reorganizing, fluid moving along channels finer than hair. The Quarter is doing something. It has been doing it for some time. No one knows for whom.

Water is everywhere and the Quarter is friends with it. The walls weep — not from failure but as function. Every vertical surface carries a thin film of condensate that runs down into grooves cut at the base, and the grooves carry it to channels, and the channels carry it under the streets to somewhere lower still. The air is at the edge of saturation; breathe deep and you taste the mineral of the rock above and the faintly electrical taste of whatever the walls are made of. After a few minutes your clothes are heavy. After an hour your hair drips.

Plants have found the Quarter and the Quarter, apparently, allows them. Not the plants of the surface — these are pale, fleshy, semi-translucent things growing from the wet seams between blocks, rooted in the runoff channels, hanging in mats from the eaves of the low arcades. Some are clearly fern-descended; some are something else, with bodies more like sponge than leaf, that pulse very slowly when you watch them long enough. Patches of moss-equivalent coat the lower walls in a soft luminescent green that contributes most of the room's light. Where the wet plants cluster thickest the light is brightest, so the Quarter has a kind of geography of brightness — luminous gardens at the low places, dim plazas at the high, and between them long avenues of half-light. There are no animals. Not a beetle, not a worm, not a swimming thing in the channels. The plants have the place to themselves and seem unbothered by the absence.

The sound is the third surprise and the one that takes longest to settle into. The Quarter is **loud**, in a way that has nothing to do with any source you can find. The walls hum. Not a mechanical hum — a chord, low and complex, that shifts every few minutes into a different chord, and the change is felt in the sternum before it is heard in the ear. Water adds its layer: the constant fine percussion of drip and trickle, amplified and re-amplified by the geometry until the plazas ring with it like struck bowls. Occasionally — perhaps every twenty minutes — a deeper pulse moves through the whole Quarter, a sound like a vast slow bell heard through deep stone, and during these pulses the weeping of the walls intensifies, the plants visibly contract, and the light dims by perhaps a fifth before recovering. Then the chord resumes its slow drift and the Quarter goes on with whatever it is doing.

Things move here even without animals. A film of water reorganizes on a wall and you realize it has spelled something briefly in its rivulets before dispersing. A patch of moss is denser this hour than it was the last. One of the columns in the central plaza is, you are almost certain, half a meter to the left of where it was when you came in, though you cannot catch it moving. The Quarter has a metabolism. It is just slow enough and just fast enough to make you doubt your own steadiness.

The rules of the place, as best as can be inferred by standing in it:

- Water is not waste here; it is the medium of whatever process the Quarter performs. Do not block a channel.
- The walls correct toward you. Lean against one for long enough and it becomes the exact shape of your back. Step away and it returns, slowly, to its prior geometry. Whether this is hospitality or sampling is unclear.
- The pulses are not addressed to you. Do not try to time your breathing to them; people who have tried report a sense of being gently but firmly ignored.
- The Quarter is young. The shaft above is recent; the discovery is recent; the *Quarter itself*, by every measurement that has been attempted, is also recent — younger than the rock it sits inside, younger perhaps than the week. It does not feel ancient. It feels as if it has just finished arriving and is settling into being a place.

Stay long enough and the empty plazas stop feeling empty. The space between presences begins to seem like a presence of its own — the architecture's real inhabitant, the thing the columns and arcades are arranged to hold. The Quarter is not a ruin and not a city and not a machine, though it has the manners of all three. It is a room that the deep earth has only just opened, warm and wet and humming, sparsely furnished with the beginnings of a grammar, lit by its own slow plants, waiting — patiently, but not for you — to find out what it is for.
