## The Hollow Beneath the Fig

There is a place under the buttress roots of a strangler fig where a small city is being born.

You would have to lie on your stomach in the leaf litter to see it, and even then your eyes would take a while to adjust, because the light here is the color of weak tea — the canopy two hundred feet above filters everything green and brown before it reaches the ground, and what arrives is less light than the memory of light. The fig itself is old enough that its host tree died long ago and rotted away, leaving the fig hollow, a cathedral of woven trunks the size of a small barn. Inside that hollow, between the inner walls and a low shelf of half-formed clay, the city sits.

Calling it a city is generous and also exactly right. The builders are termites of a species that hasn't quite committed to its own architecture yet — their mounds are nubs, knee-high at the tallest, soft-edged, still being shaped by mandibles working wet earth one grain at a time. There are perhaps thirty of these structures scattered across an area the size of a dining room floor, and the spaces between them are wide, almost ceremonial. You could walk a finger between any two and not touch either. The clay is the color of milk in coffee and it glistens; the air in here is heavy enough that water beads on every surface and runs down in slow threads.

The temperature is warm — blood-warm, womb-warm — and it does not change. Outside, somewhere far above the canopy, weather might be happening, but inside the fig the only weather is condensation. Drops gather on the underside of the highest roots and fall, irregularly, with small soft sounds. *Tup.* A long pause. *Tup.* The acoustics of the hollow swallow everything else. You can hear your own pulse in your ears more clearly than anything the world is doing.

The mounds are not finished and may never be — this is what *emerging* means here. There is no erosion yet because there is nothing old enough to erode. The clay is fresh, the chambers inside are still being hollowed, the ventilation shafts still being calibrated by trial and breath. A mound that has begun to take on a recognizable spire shape today might be torn down and restarted next week if the colony's gas exchange runs wrong. It is a city in the way an embryo is a person: all the intention, none of the history.

The fauna is everywhere once you start looking. Not the termites — they are inside, mostly — but the fig's hollow is a small ecosystem and it teems quietly. A velvet worm the length of a pencil moves along a root, spitting threads at a springtail. A whip spider, all legs and patience, occupies one wall with the stillness of furniture. Tiny frogs the color of wet bark sit in the divots of the clay, blinking. A column of ants from somewhere else entirely — a foreign nation — has worked out a treaty path along the ceiling and travels it in single file, carrying fragments of something pale. Above all of this, in the upper reaches of the hollow where light comes down in coins, two bats hang and breathe.

Plants have gotten in too. The hollow is sparse but not empty: a few ferns root in the cracks where water collects, a single seedling of some unknown tree has sprouted in a crevice and put out three pale leaves that will probably die before they harden, a colony of pale moss spreads across one wall in a slow green tide. Vegetation here is opportunistic and patient. The fig is the city's wall and its sky.

There are rules, though none of them are written or thought:

The clay must stay damp or the mounds slump.
The mounds must breathe or the colony cooks.
The bats must be left alone.
Whatever falls from above belongs to whoever reaches it first.
Silence is not a rule but a consequence — there are no birds in here, no wind, no flowing water, and so everything that lives in the hollow has adapted to move without announcing itself.

If you stayed long enough — and you couldn't, the air is too thick and your knees would give out — you would notice the dynamics. A mound grows by a thumb's width in an afternoon. The ant treaty shifts course around a new obstacle. A frog catches a moth and the moth's wing scales settle on the clay like the lightest snow. Somewhere deep in one of the mounds, a new chamber opens and a small architectural crisis is resolved by consensus among insects who have never seen each other's faces.

Nothing here is loud. Nothing here is finished. Everything here is becoming, slowly, in the dim, in the warm, in the held breath of the hollow fig.

Outside, the forest does whatever the forest does. Inside, a civilization the size of a hearthrug is, very carefully, inventing itself.
