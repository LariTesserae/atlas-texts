# The Standing City of Aoresh

There is no ground here. There was never any ground here. What you stand on is woven palm — split fronds laid in three directions, lashed with cord made from the inner bark of a tree no one now alive has seen. Beneath the weaving: more weaving. Beneath that: water, warm as blood, and beneath the water, only the report of a long stick finding nothing.

The city stands on stilts of fired clay and lashed bamboo, rising from a shallow bright sea that no tide moves. The sun is directly overhead and remains directly overhead. It has been noon in Aoresh for as long as anyone has tried to remember otherwise. Shadows fall straight down through the slatted floors and pool, on the water beneath, as small dark coins.

## The Build

Everything is made. The palms that arch over the walkways were carried here as saplings and planted in great fired pots wider than a man is tall, and the pots themselves sit on platforms, and the platforms on pilings. There is no soil that was not first put into a vessel. The vines that drape from rafter to rafter were trained there; the moss on the eastern beams was placed leaf by leaf, generations ago, and has not grown or died since. Even the small clouds that sometimes hang low between the towers — pale, slow, sweating — are kept by ropes, tethered at four corners to bronze rings hammered into the gable-posts. The cloud-keepers' guild renews the ropes when the salt eats them.

What is not made: the water below, the sun above, and whatever has decided that here, certain things will not change.

## The Heat and the Wet

The air is thick. It is the air of a kitchen where something has been steaming all morning. Breathe and you taste salt and green pulp and the faint sweetness of the orchid-vats. Sweat beads and does not fall. Press a palm to any wooden surface and it comes away damp. The clay pots weep continually; small dark rings have formed around their bases on the woven floors, and these rings, too, never widen.

Rain has not fallen in Aoresh in living memory, and yet every surface is wet. Moisture condenses out of the air directly onto leaves, beams, the curve of a drinking gourd, the back of your neck. The cisterns fill themselves. No one quite understands this. The cistern-priests pretend to.

## The Quiet

For a city so densely lived-in, Aoresh is strangely silent. Voices carry, but people speak softly here — partly habit, partly because loud sounds have been observed, on rare occasions, to *not echo back*, as though the air had swallowed them and would not return them. The loom-clatter and the adze-thock are muted by the woven floors, which absorb percussion like felt. Birds call, but sparsely; one hears a single long note from somewhere far across the city, then a long pause, then a reply, and then quiet again for the length of a meal.

The loudest sound in Aoresh is the slow, soft *hush* of the palms overhead, though there is no wind.

## The Fauna

The animals are everywhere and nowhere. Bright fish move beneath the floors in slow constellations, visible through the slats — fish the color of mango flesh, fish striped like the inside of a split fig. Long-legged birds wade the shallows between pilings, picking at things, never startling. In the upper canopy: small monkeys with too many fingers, who do not chatter; iridescent beetles the size of a child's fist that cling to the underside of every beam. Frogs in the orchid-vats. A kind of soft, pale lizard that lives only inside the woven walls and is considered lucky.

None of them seem to breed. None of them seem to die. A fisher pulls up the same orange fish her grandmother pulled up, with the same notch in its tail, and releases it again, because that is what one does.

## The Weirdness

Here is where the world thins.

Walk far enough in any direction along the boardwalks and you will, eventually, come back. Not because Aoresh is a ring — it is not — but because the boardwalks are honest, and the world beyond them is not. The cartographers' guild long ago abandoned distance and now maps the city in terms of relationships: *the heron-platform is upstream-of the dye-vats, which are inside-of the orchid-quarter.* These relationships are stable. Cardinal directions are not.

Mirrors do not work here. Polished bronze shows the room behind you, never your face. This is taken for granted; the bathing-pavilions have no looking-glasses, and a child who asks why is told, *because.* A child who is older is told: *because the city does not have a back to its head.*

A thing that has been placed somewhere tends to remain placed. A pot set down on a beam in the year of the long mango will be found on that beam in the year of the second long mango, the year of the third, and the year your great-granddaughter ascends to the cloud-keepers. Move the pot, and it accepts the move; but leave it, and leaving is what it becomes.

The dead are not buried — there is nowhere to bury them — and are not burned, because smoke here behaves strangely and the elders prefer not to provoke it. The dead are placed in small reed boats and pushed off from the city's edge, where the boardwalks end and the bright water continues. The boats do not get smaller with distance. They simply, at some point, become too small to notice, though they have not moved.

## The Sparseness

For all that the city is total construction, it is not crowded. A platform here, then thirty paces of open boardwalk under arching palms, then another platform. A long stretch where you see no one, only the slats and the water and the lazy turning of a tethered cloud. A family compound, then emptiness, then a single old woman weaving on a porch, then more emptiness. The city extends outward in this rhythm — presence, absence, presence — for as far as the boardwalks honestly go, which is far enough that no one has bothered to count.

There is room here. There has always been room. The population, like the fish, does not change.

## The Rules, As Far As Anyone Has Worked Them Out

- What is made, stays.
- What is placed, stays placed.
- What is loud may not return.
- Reflections show the room, not the self.
- The sun is overhead. This is not negotiable.
- Distance is a courtesy the world extends to those who walk in straight lines on honest wood.

A traveler arriving in Aoresh — and travelers do arrive, though no one can quite say from where — often spends the first several days disturbed by the stillness, the unbreathing constancy of it, the way the light does not slant toward evening. By the second week they have usually stopped noticing. By the second month they have begun to suspect, with a slow and not-unpleasant horror, that they have always been here. By the third month this is true.
