# The Roostflats

The land lies low and warm, a wide basin of pale clay and cooling basalt that has not yet decided what shape it wants to be. Water seeps everywhere — not in rivers, not yet, but as sheets a finger deep that slide across the flats and pool in every shallow depression. The rock beneath is fresh, glassy in places, still smelling faintly of sulfur where the older vents have gone quiet. Nothing has had time to wear anything down. Edges are sharp. Stones sit where they fell.

There is no green. No moss, no lichen film on the wet stone, no mat of algae on the standing water. The world here skipped that chapter, or hasn't reached it. The clay is clay. The water is water. The light, dim and tea-colored, comes through a low ceiling of vapor that the warm ground keeps lifting back into itself — a slow, eternal exhalation that never quite condenses to rain and never quite clears.

And into this raw, unfinished basin: the birds.

Hundreds of thousands of them. Tens of millions, perhaps — the count doesn't mean anything once the eye gives up. They are something like flamingos and something like ibises and something like nothing you've named, long-legged waders standing shin-deep in the warm sheet-water, packed so tight their bodies touch, their necks weaving above the crowd like a second, restless layer of the landscape. Beyond them, more. Beyond those, more. The flock extends to where the vapor swallows sight, and continues past that, and you understand without being told that it continues much further still.

The sound is the whole sky. Not a cry — a *substance*. A continuous roaring chatter, layered honks and trills and the wet percussion of millions of bills sifting the shallows, the slap of webbed feet, the low boom of wings shaken out and refolded. It presses on the chest. Speech would be pointless here; even thought arrives muffled, as though the noise had texture and had filled the inside of your skull along with everything else. You feel it in the soles of your feet through the warm mud.

Among the birds, the city.

It is low and primitive and everywhere. Mud-brick platforms a half-meter above the waterline, linked by causeways of the same packed clay, roofed in woven reed-substitute — long pale fibers the inhabitants gather from somewhere, perhaps from the birds themselves, perhaps shed quill-down matted and pressed. The structures step and stagger across the flats in dense clusters, never tall, never separated. From above it would look like a great pale crust spread over the basin, pierced everywhere by the heads and necks of waders. The buildings press right up against the flocks; the flocks press right up against the buildings; in many places the birds simply walk through the lower rooms, and the people do not mind, and the birds do not mind.

The people — and there are people — work in soft leather and fired clay and the occasional dull hammered copper. No wheels that I can see. No writing visible on the walls, though they paint, in long horizontal bands of red ochre and bone-white, patterns that resemble the standing flocks. They move slowly between the platforms on barefoot causeway-paths, carrying baskets, carrying eggs, carrying nothing. They speak with their hands. They have learned, over uncountable generations, that the mouth is not for talking here.

And here is the strange stillness of the place: nothing changes. The vapor ceiling holds at the same height it held yesterday and the year before. The water is the same depth. The birds are the same birds — or rather, the flock is the same flock; individual birds must die and hatch, but the mass never thins, never thickens, never migrates, never falls silent. There is no dry season. No wet season. No dawn that brightens into noon. The light is the dim warm tea-light it has always been. The clay city neither grows nor crumbles; what is built stays built, and little new gets built, because little is needed, and erosion has not yet been invented in this part of the world.

It is a landscape caught at a single sustained note. The geology is young — too young to have weathered — but the life on it acts ancient, locked into a pattern so deep and so loud that change cannot find a foothold. The birds were here when the basalt was still warm. The people came later, or maybe they came with the birds; no one remembers, and no one asks, because asking would require speaking, and speaking is not what mouths are for.

If you stood at the edge of the basin and looked in, you would see: a flat warm world under low gray vapor, sheet-water glinting copper in the dim, a pale crust of mud-roofs stretching to the limit of sight, and above and through it all the trembling restless second-canopy of necks and wings, and you would hear, before you saw any of it, the sound — that vast unbroken roar that is the only weather this place has ever had.

You would not stay long. The noise would drive you out. But the people there, born into it, find silence frightening on the rare occasions they encounter it, and return quickly to the roar, which is the shape of the world, and has always been, and — in this basin, under this ceiling, on this raw young stone — always will be.
