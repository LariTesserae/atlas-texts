# The Roost-Town on the Stub of Aigir

At this altitude the air is thin and dry and warm in a way that surprises — sun-baked rather than humid, the warmth of a stone that has held heat since some earlier age and never quite given it back. There is no water here, or almost none: a few seep-stains on the undercliffs where minerals weep slow yellow lines, a clay jar in every doorway holding what was carried up on someone's back.

The mountain itself is finished. Whatever it was when it was young — fanged, glacier-shouldered, the kind of peak that throws weather — is gone. What remains is a broad flat-topped stub, a mesa lifted impossibly high, its sides scalloped and softened by millions of years of wind. The rock is a pale apricot color where the sun strikes it and a deep tobacco-brown in the lees. Run your hand along any wall and it powders slightly; the whole massif is in the patient business of becoming dust, but so slowly that a human life is not long enough to notice the change in any one ledge.

The top is not large. You could walk from edge to edge in the time it takes a meal to cook. But it is dense — saturated with life and with making.

Vegetation has rooted into every crack and shelf: a knee-high forest of bristle-stemmed shrubs with leaves like waxed coins, and above them, leaning out over the drops, the slow trees — squat, broad, with bark like cracked leather and crowns of dark needles that hum in the wind. They drink fog. On nights when cloud rolls up the cliffs and sits on the summit, the trees comb it, and water beads down their trunks into stone basins that the townspeople have chiseled at their bases. This is most of the water the town has. The rest comes up the long stair on the backs of climbers.

The town is built into and onto the trees and the rock together. Walls of dry-fitted stone, no mortar, the courses laid so tight that a knife-blade won't enter. Roofs of thatched needle-bundle, weighted with stones against the constant updraft. Ladders of bone-pale wood lashed with braided fiber. There is metal here but not much — copper hammered thin for cookpots, a few iron knives traded up from below and treated like inheritances. Most tools are stone, bone, fired clay, hard wood. The people knap, they weave, they grind, they coil pots from the apricot clay of the cliff-seeps. There is no wheel because there is nowhere on the summit far enough to roll one.

The light is the strange thing. Even at midday the town reads as dim, because the trees layer over the lanes and the lanes are narrow and the buildings lean. Sun comes down in shafts, in coins, in the dapple that pools on a swept threshold. People live half in shadow. Lamps are stone bowls of rendered fat with a fiber wick, kept low and frugal. The brightest places in town are the edges — the rim-walks, where you step out from under the canopy and the sky opens, and you can see the curve of the world below you, terracotta and dust-blue, and you understand again how high you are.

The animals make most of the noise. This is a roost. The cliffs below the summit are pocked with thousands of hollows, and in every hollow lives something — pale long-winged birds that scream at dawn and dusk in waves that pass across the mountain like weather; smaller crevice-dwellers that chatter all day; a stocky six-legged climber with a voice like a struck bell that the children imitate. Inside the town itself, beasts share the lanes: a domesticated cousin of the climbers serves as pack animal and companion, and broad placid grazers with shaggy coats keep the shrub-forest cropped. There are insects in numbers — bright pollinators, tree-borers whose tapping is a constant background, and a particular evening moth the size of a hand whose wingbeat you can feel on your face.

The sound is layered and continuous. Bird-cries, the hum of needle-crowns, the bell-call of climbers, the soft pong of someone shaping a pot, children, looms, the wind always wind. Silence here is not a thing that happens; it is a thing you remember from below.

The rules of this place are slow rules. Nothing changes quickly. The trees grow a finger's width in a generation. The shrines at the four cardinal edges are tended by families who have tended them for longer than the families remember. New houses are rare; old houses are repaired forever. A child born here inherits not only a name but a doorway, a tree, a stretch of rim, a particular shelf where a particular bird nests in a particular month. The town has been the same size, more or less, for as long as anyone can name — bounded by the summit, bounded by the water that can be carried up, bounded by the slow trees, which cannot be made to grow faster and which the people will not cut.

To leave is to descend the stair, which takes days. To arrive is to be exhausted and to be given water in a clay cup and to sit down in the dim warm dappled lanes and listen, for a long time, to the birds.
