# The Long Hollow

It begins as a seam. Somewhere up on the surface — wherever the surface is — water found a weakness in the limestone a long time ago and pried it open, and the prying is still going on, though slowly enough that no one alive would notice. You enter through that seam and you are immediately below. Not deep below, not a true cave system; more like the world has folded a long crease into itself and you've slipped into the fold.

The hollow runs for miles. That's the first thing to understand. It is not a chamber, it is a corridor — open enough overhead in most places that you could throw a stone and not hit anything, and long enough that the far end resolves only into a paler smudge of grey. Daylight comes down in slow shafts from fissures in the ceiling, dust-thick, angled. Where a shaft lands, the rock beneath it is bleached almost white. Where it does not land — and most of the hollow does not — the stone is a wet shale-grey, with darker veins of something that might be iron, might be older water.

The floor is uneven and freshly broken. Slabs the size of carts lie where they fell, edges still sharp; you can run your thumb along a corner and cut yourself on geology. There hasn't been time for rounding. The fractures are pale inside, the weathered faces only beginning to dull. This is a young place. The ceiling sheds, occasionally, with a crack that travels the whole length of the hollow and then a thump and then quiet, but nobody hears it, because of the noise.

The noise is the second thing.

It is birds. It is, overwhelmingly, birds. They roost in every ledge and crevice along both walls of the hollow, layer above layer, a vertical city of nests and droppings and folded wings, and they call without ceasing. Not one species — many. Pale-throated swiftlets that navigate the dimness by clicking, a sound like seeds being shaken in a gourd, multiplied by ten thousand. Larger dark birds, something between a crow and a nightjar, that holler in the rafters. Smaller ones that trill in chains. The sound is not deafening in the way of a waterfall; it is deafening in the way of a market, full of edges and intentions, and when you stand still and listen you can almost pick out individual conversations, individual disputes, before they dissolve back into the mass.

Beneath the birds, on the floor, the guano lies in soft pale drifts. In the drifts: beetles. Cockroaches the length of a finger. Blind centipedes. A whole ecology of small lives eating the rain of the upper world. Where water has pooled — and there are pools, scattered, dim mirrors of black water reflecting up at the fissures — small fish exist, white and eyeless, descended from something washed in long ago and never washed out. Bats share the ceiling with the birds in some kind of negotiated truce, day-shift and night-shift, though in this dimness neither shift is fully on or off and the air is always full of wings.

Plant life is scarce and strange. Mosses where the light-shafts land, dark green going almost black at the edges. A few ferns at the lips of the upper fissures, leaning in. Pale things that aren't quite plants growing on the guano, more fungus than flora, faintly luminous if you stare long enough to convince yourself. The cool damp keeps everything slow. Nothing wilts here, but nothing flourishes either; the green has reached an agreement with the stone and the agreement is: a little, and no more.

The temperature is steady. That is the third thing, and the most important. It is cool — you'd want a wool layer — and it is always cool, and it is always cool by exactly the same amount. The hollow does not have weather. The shafts of light move with the sun above, slowly, but their angle is the only clock. There is no season here. The birds breed and die and breed again on the ledges, and the population does not grow and does not shrink. The water drips at the same rate it has dripped for centuries. The slabs fall when they fall, but a slab falling is not a change so much as a continuation: there have always been slabs falling, there will always be slabs falling, and the floor absorbs them into its general broken state without altering its character.

Someone has been here. Not built anything — there are no walls, no shelters, no carvings on the rock — but used the place. Near one of the larger pools, a ring of stones with the char of an old fire, the char old enough that you can't say whether it was made last week or last century. A few flaked tools left on a flat slab: blades of a dark glassy stone, a scraper, an awl of bone. Whoever they were, they came down for the eggs, probably. For the fish. For the beetles, even, ground into paste. They didn't stay. You can tell they didn't stay because nothing accumulates here that the birds and the beetles don't eat, and the human-things have been left exactly as they were set down, undisturbed, waiting to be picked up again by hands that may or may not return.

Stand at any point along the hollow's length and turn slowly. The view will be, in every direction, more or less the same view: grey wet stone going up to a ceiling full of wings, pale shafts coming down, dark pools, drifts of pale guano, the long perspective tunneling off into haze at both ends. Walk an hour. The view will be the same. Walk a day. The same. The hollow does not grade into anything else. It just continues, until it doesn't, and where it ends — somewhere, presumably — must be much like where it begins.

The birds call. The water drips. The cool air moves only when a bird's wing moves it. Above, weather happens; here, it does not. The place is busy and the place is still, and these are not contradictions but the same fact stated twice.
