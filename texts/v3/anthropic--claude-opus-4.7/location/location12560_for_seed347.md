# The Drowned City Beneath the Reedlands

It lies maybe forty feet below the surface of the marsh — not deep, but deep enough that the daylight reaches it filtered through standing water and a ceiling of root-mat, arriving green-gold and wavering, the way light moves on the underside of a swimming pool.

You come into it through one of the sink-mouths where the peat has given way. The ground is cold here, persistently cold, the kind of chill that lives in stone that never sees the sun directly. Forty degrees, maybe less. Your breath shows. The walls weep — not in any dramatic way, just a steady seep that beads, gathers, runs down the carved faces of things and pools in the gutters that someone, once, had the foresight to cut.

And the city — yes, it's a city, though "city" suggests more order than is here now. Call it a settlement-warren of perhaps three thousand structures, built up against and into and through one another along the floor of a wide submerged basin. The basin is maybe two miles across. You can see, on a clear-watered day, all the way to its far rim, where the buildings climb the slope in tiers before the root-ceiling closes overhead.

The construction is layered in a way that suggests the city was built at least three times. The lowest course is a pale limestone, dressed in big rectangular blocks, its corners still sharp — this is the young-erosion showing through, surfaces that haven't had centuries to soften, edges you could cut yourself on. Above that, a middle course of darker stone, rougher, more hurried. Above that, structures of bundled reed and silt-daub that have somehow lasted, kept whole by the constant cold and the constant wet, the way bog-bodies last.

Everything is overgrown. This is what makes the place feel packed — there isn't a surface that isn't claimed. Pale water-mosses in sheets the size of bedsheets. A kind of subaqueous fern with translucent fronds, growing in fans from every cornice. Liverworts. A thick black algae that looks like wet velvet and squeaks faintly when you press it. The walls don't have walls anymore; they have pelts.

And the animals. The fauna here is mostly small and mostly cold-blooded — newts with orange bellies threading through the moss, a kind of pale crayfish that walks the flooded streets in slow processions, finger-length fish that hang in the deeper pools doing nothing visible. Frogs, the small kind, not the loud kind. There are birds too, where the root-ceiling has gaps — wading birds that come down through the holes to hunt and stand one-legged on the old rooftops as if they were stones in a stream, which functionally they are. Something bigger lives in the basin's center, in what must have once been a cistern; you see its wake sometimes, V-shaped, moving slowly. No one has reason to find out what.

It is almost silent. This is the strangest part, given how alive it is. The water absorbs sound. The moss absorbs sound. The cold thickens it. What you hear is: drip, drip, drip, in countless overlapping rhythms, each from a different weeping face of stone — and that's almost all. Occasionally a frog. Occasionally the splash of a heron striking. The crayfish make no noise. The fish make no noise. Your own breathing sounds louder than it should.

There is no one here. No tools, no fires, no smoke-stain on the ceilings of the surviving rooms. Whoever built this — and they built it carefully, with drainage channels and lintels and small carved faces above the doorways, faces that have a calm, slightly amphibian cast to them — they are gone, and have been gone long enough that the second and third building-phases happened without them or with their distant descendants who did not write or did not last.

The dynamics of the place are slow but real. Water levels shift with the marsh above; in wet weeks the lower streets fully submerge and the newts move up a tier, and in dry weeks pools shrink and the crayfish congregate in the deeper cisterns and the herons have a feast. Sediment falls constantly from the root-ceiling — fine silt, the slow snow of an underplace — and accumulates on every flat surface, and is grazed off by snails, and falls again. The moss-pelts grow and slough. Things eat things. A pale eel finds a frog. A heron finds the eel. The heron flies up through a hole in the ceiling and is gone, and the city closes its quiet over the absence.

The light through the root-mat is enough to read by, in the open plazas. In the deeper rooms it fades to a blue-green dusk and then to true dark, and you don't go in there, not because anything will hurt you but because the silence in the deep rooms has a quality that suggests it would prefer you didn't.

You could live here. People clearly did. The cold would be the hardest part — the cold and the way every surface is always slightly wet, so that nothing ever quite dries, and your hands prune, and your boots squelch, and the moss claims any object left still for more than a season. But there's water, obviously. There's food, if you can stomach crayfish and frog. There's shelter built already, just waiting to be cleared.

The marsh above doesn't know it's here. Walkers on the surface cross over the city without any sign — the reeds grow as thick over the basin as anywhere else, the herons stand in the shallows, the sun goes down behind the alders. Only at the sink-mouths, where the peat has given way, does the underplace breathe up its colder air, and even then most travelers turn away from those holes, sensing, correctly, that they go somewhere.
