# The Sump

There is a basin in the low country, no wider than a market square, that the people of the surrounding villages have always called the Sump, though it has a older name that nobody uses anymore.

To reach it you walk down. The land bowls gently — not a crater, nothing so sudden, just a long shallow dip in the warm flat country, and at its bottom the ground gives way to a floor of wet clay and stone, ringed by a low lip of weathered limestone the color of old teeth. The lip is no higher than your hip in most places. You step over it and you are inside.

The first thing is the warmth. The air in the basin is several degrees warmer than the country above, and thick — not foggy, but heavy with held water, so that within a few minutes your skin feels glazed and your hair lies down. There is no plant life in here. The lip seems to mark a boundary; outside, ordinary grasses and scrub; inside, nothing green at all. The clay floor is bare. The stones are bare. Whatever it is in the ground or the water that forbids vegetation has been forbidding it for a very long time, and the place has settled into the shape of that refusal.

What fills the basin, instead, is mineral. The floor is packed with formations — low domes and lobes and stacked plates of pale travertine, the kind of slow stone that grows where warm mineral water seeps up and gives up its dissolved load to the air. Some of the lobes are the size of loaves, some the size of sleeping dogs. They press against one another. There is no bare ground between them; the basin is paved in stone that grew there. Between the lobes, in the small valleys and seams, standing water sits — shallow, warm, faintly milky, hardly moving. Every surface weeps. Run a finger along any of the pale stones and it comes away wet and slightly slick, faintly mineral on the tongue if you are foolish enough to taste it.

Here and there a slow bubble rises in the standing water and breaks without much sound. That is most of what one hears. Bubbles, and the very soft trickle from the higher stones where water beads and falls a hand's width into a pool. No wind reaches the bottom of the bowl. No birds call from inside it, though sometimes one can be heard distantly from the country above, muffled, as if heard through wool.

The light is dim even at noon. Partly this is the steam — a low haze sits over the basin always, never thick enough to be fog, never thin enough to disappear — and partly it is the color of the place, which absorbs more light than it returns. The pale stones look pale only by contrast; in truth they are a muted cream, a soft ochre where iron has stained them, here and there a band of grey. There is no glitter anywhere. Everything is matte with wetness.

The fauna is almost nothing. In the warm pools between the lobes live small soft things — a kind of pale shrimp, blind, no longer than a fingernail; some threads of bacterial mat that wave slowly when the water is disturbed. Once in a great while a frog finds its way in from the wet country beyond, but frogs do not stay; something about the water or the warmth sends them back out within a day or two, or kills them, and their small bones go to the stone. You can find frog bones in the travertine if you look. Also, in places, the bones of mice, and once — old, half-swallowed by a lobe near the eastern lip — what looked like the finger bones of a child's hand, though it could have been something else.

People have come here for a long time. Not many at once, and not often. The signs of them are scattered and small: a low cairn of unmatched stones near the center, dressed with nothing, decorated with nothing, just stacked; a few clay cups left on the rim, the older ones half-grown into the travertine, the newer ones still recognizably cups; a wooden post driven into a seam in the floor, its grain swollen and softened by years of wet warmth, leaning slightly. None of these things were made by skilled hands. They are the work of people who came down with what they could carry and a simple intention, and left a small mark, and went away. There is no shrine, no carving, no inscription. Whatever the Sump is for, the people who use it have not felt the need to explain it to anyone, including each other.

The most striking thing, to a visitor who stays more than an hour, is how little changes. The light shifts as the sun moves, but the haze diffuses it so evenly that the shadows barely travel. The water bubbles at the same rate it bubbled when you arrived. The same shrimp drift in the same pool. No breeze stirs. The travertine grows, of course — it must, since it is travertine — but it grows at the speed of stone, a hair's breadth in a human life, and from any single visit it is identical to itself. People who come here a second time, years later, find it exactly as they remembered, down to the position of a particular pebble, and this is one of the things that brings them back. The country above changes: harvests fail, children are born, a road is cut through, a war passes. The Sump is the same. It is the same in the way that a held breath is the same, except that the breath is not held; it is simply that the basin has arrived at the shape it wanted and stopped.

The villagers do not have a use for it, exactly. They go down when something in their life has begun to move too fast, or has broken in a way that can't be set, and they sit on one of the warm lobes with their feet in a milky pool, and they stay an hour or a half a day, and they come back up. They don't speak of it much. There is nothing in the Sump that answers a question or grants a wish. It only continues, warm and wet and quiet and packed full of its own slow stone, and after a while, if you sit long enough, you continue too.
