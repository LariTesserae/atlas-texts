# The Terraced City of Oss-in-the-Cleft

It sits at roughly three thousand meters, draped along one wall of a long trough-valley that was, by the evidence of the rock itself, cut fast and not long ago — a shearing rather than a slow wearing. The granite still shows its fresh faces. Crystals catch light where no lichen has yet reached. The cleft runs roughly north to south, wide enough that standing on the eastern terraces you can see the opposite wall clearly but not make out individuals on it, only the pale stacking of their houses and the dark verticals of cypress-like trees between.

The city is called Oss by the people who live there, though the name seems to mean only *here*, or perhaps *the place that is*. It climbs the valley wall in roughly forty terraces, each one a shelf of packed earth and stone retained by dry-laid walls of the local granite, mortarless, fitted with the patience of people who have nothing pressing them. The houses are low, two stories at most, with walls of the same pale stone and roofs of split slate weighted with river cobbles against winds that, in practice, rarely come. Between houses there is always space — a garden plot, a rock outcrop left in place, a single tree, a cistern. The density is deliberate. No one builds to the edge of their allotment. The streets are narrow stairs cut into the living rock or laid as slab staircases switchbacking up the slope.

The water comes from the cleft itself. High above the settled terraces, where the rock is still raw and unsoftened, a river issues from somewhere inside the mountain and steps down the valley in a series of falls and pools. It is not a loud river. It runs shallow over flat bedrock for most of its length, and the falls are short — three meters, five meters — so the sound is a constant soft applause rather than a roar. From the main flow, the inhabitants have cut channels: open stone grooves that thread through every terrace, feeding gardens, filling cisterns, turning a few small wooden wheels that grind grain or lift water one step further. The channels are shallow and slow. Moss grows in them. In the mornings, condensation from the cool nights runs down every leaf and wall and adds, imperceptibly, to the flow.

The vegetation is lush but not crowded. On each terrace: vegetable plots, fruit trees (something apple-like, something with purple husked fruit I have no word for), beds of the blue-green leafy plant they eat boiled, and between the cultivated patches, wild growth tolerated and pruned. The trees on the uncultivated slopes are tall and slender, dark-needled, spaced with what looks like courtesy — each one standing well clear of the next, as if agreed. Between them, bracken and a silvery ground-cover and, in the wetter folds, beds of horsetail taller than a person. The whole valley is cool. Breath shows faintly at dawn. Nothing suggests frost, but nothing suggests real warmth either. A climate held at one mild temperature, all year, without variation.

And this is the strangeness of Oss. Nothing changes.

The river does not rise or fall with seasons, because there are no seasons — or rather, there is one season, which is this one. The fruit ripens continuously; each tree has, at any moment, blossoms and green fruit and ripe fruit and old fruit dropping, in equal measure. The vegetable beds are all at every stage. A stalk of the blue-green leaf may flower in the morning and still be flowering, unchanged, at evening, and tomorrow, and the next week. Harvest is a matter of taking what is ripe; more is always ripe. The people work, but not against time. They work the way one might stir a pot that never needs stirring — because the motion is good, because it is theirs to do.

Animals are abundant and also, in their way, stationary. There are goats on the high rocks, small deer in the cypress-groves, many birds — a thrush-like one with a two-note call, a silent hawk that rides the updrafts off the eastern wall, flocks of something small and sparrow-shaped that move through the terraces in loose clouds. The population of each does not grow. A goat dies; a kid is born; the number holds. The hawk has always been the hawk. The thrushes sing the same two notes they sang when the oldest resident was a child, and will sing them when the youngest is old.

The people know this about their home. They are not troubled by it. They have tools — bronze knives, copper pots, bone needles, looms of carved wood, stone mortars — and they make more of these as needed, but they do not invent. What would invention be for? The old patterns work. A boy learns to fit stone from his grandfather, and the technique is the same technique, and the stones he will fit in his lifetime will still be standing, unweathered, when his own grandchildren fit more beside them. The walls of Oss do not need repair. The slate roofs do not crack. The sharp edges of the cleft do not soften. Even dust, it seems, settles only so far and then stops.

The sound, then, is mostly the river's small applause, and wind in the needle-trees (a dry whisper), and the thrushes' two notes, and the occasional clack of a loom or a hoe striking stone, and voices — quiet voices, because loud ones would carry across the whole valley and embarrass the speaker. Children are quiet here, though not unhappy. They have learned, from the shape of the world, that urgency is a kind of misunderstanding.

At night the sky is very clear and very full of stars, and the stars are also the stars that have always been. No new ones appear. None fall. The moon, when it is up, is always at the same phase — a particular three-quarters, waning — and the inhabitants, if pressed, will tell you it has been that way as long as anyone remembers, which is as long as the place has existed, which is, they suspect, forever, though the rawness of the rock hints otherwise.

Somewhere in the oldest part of the city, on the lowest terrace near the river, there is a small shrine, unattended, of fitted stone. Inside it is nothing. The people leave offerings there anyway — a fruit, a pebble, a bead — and the offerings do not rot and are not taken, and slowly (though *slowly* is the wrong word, since nothing is slow or fast here) the shrine fills, and has filled, and will go on being full.
