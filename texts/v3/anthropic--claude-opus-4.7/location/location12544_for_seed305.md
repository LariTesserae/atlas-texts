# The Lung Forest

You stand somewhere on the western slope, though slope is a generous word for what the land does here. The terrain is a series of stacked terraces — not cut, not built, but *grown* — each one a kilometer or so above the next, climbing into a sky that holds no clouds because the clouds are below you, pooled in the basins between ridges like spilled mercury catching the sun.

The light is the first thing. It is too much. It comes from above in the ordinary way, a high cold sun in a thin blue, but it also comes from *below* — the cloud-lakes throw it back upward, and the leaves throw it sideways, and the wings throw it everywhere. Every surface is a reflector. You squint constantly. Shadows here are not dark; they are merely *less bright*, a paler gold inside a whiter one. At noon the air itself seems to glow, as if the photons cannot find their way out and just keep ricocheting.

The cold is dry and high-altitude cold — not biting, because there is too much sun for biting, but the kind that numbs your fingertips while your face burns. Breath plumes. Condensation beads on every leaf-edge, every feather-shaft, every chitinous ridge, and where the sun catches a bead it becomes briefly a small star. Multiply this by the density of the place and you understand why your eyes water.

The vegetation: imagine cycads the size of cathedrals, but breathing. Their trunks are ribbed and pale and you can see them slowly inflate and deflate over the course of an hour. The fronds are not green so much as a thousand greens, stacked: silver-green on the undersides, blue-green in shadow, an almost-yellow at the sunlit tip. Between the great breathing trees grow softer things — moss-mats that ring like wet cloth when something walks on them, fern-thickets whose spores drift up in golden columns, vines that have wrapped themselves around the cycads so completely that the cycads now wear them like sweaters. Nothing is dying. Nothing has been here long enough to die. The rock under the soil is sharp-edged, fresh, recently uplifted; the soil itself is thin and black and rich with the bodies of the things that fall from the sky.

And things fall from the sky constantly, because the sky is full.

The fauna. There is no honest way to describe the fauna without first describing the sound, and there is no way to describe the sound without admitting it has texture. It is a *weave*. Imagine a hundred thousand throats at different pitches, layered, never resolving — the deep bass-thrum of the largest fliers passing through cloud, the mid-range chitter of the canopy dwellers, the high keen of the small things, and beneath all of it a constant industrial *hum* that you slowly realize is not weather and not insects but the collective wing-beat of the whole biosphere. It sounds like a factory. It sounds like a city of engines. It is neither — it is simply what life sounds like when there is this much of it and it must move to stay warm.

Because that, you begin to understand, is the trick of this place. Nothing here is built. There are no roads, no walls, no machines. But the *animals are the machinery*. They have evolved into industrial roles. Look up: a flock of long-bodied flyers, each the size of a small whale, is moving in tight formation along a thermal corridor, and the air behind them shimmers and is *warmer*. They are heating the upper canopy by friction. Look down into the cloud-basin: things are diving into it and surfacing with their bodies steaming, ferrying moisture upslope in pouches under their wings. The water cycle here is not run by sun and gravity alone. It is run by *labor*. There are pollinator-swarms that move in disciplined geometric patterns, switching directions on some signal you cannot hear, and where they pass the cycads exhale a sweet hot breath in response. There is a species of armored grazer, slow as a glacier, whose back is colonized by mosses and small carnivores and a kind of mobile orchard; it walks a circuit that takes years.

This is the industrial parameter. It is not metal. It is meat and feather and wing-beat organized into something that does the work of a civilization without ever having decided to.

The strangeness is in the *patience* of it. You watch a single drop of condensation form on a frond and you realize you have been watching it for ten minutes and it has not yet fallen. Everything here happens, but slowly, and inside the slowness the loud bright crowded surface of things races. The great cycads breathe on a cycle of hours. The cloud-basins fill and drain on a cycle of weeks. The grazer-orchards complete their circuits on a cycle of decades. And the land itself is rising — you can feel it, just barely, as a faint shudder underfoot every few minutes, the mountain still being born, pushing up through its own forest, lifting the whole packed ecology another fraction of a millimeter toward the sun.

There is no room between things. This is the density. Every cubic meter of air has something living in it; every square centimeter of bark has something growing on it; every leaf has an underside community and an upperside community and they do not mix. Where you stand, if you stand still for long, things will begin to use you. A small bright flier will rest on your shoulder and refuse to leave. Spores will settle in the creases of your clothes. Within an hour you would be part of the inventory.

And the rules: you cannot fall here, because something will catch you, not out of kindness but out of opportunity. You cannot get lost, because the landscape is so full of landmarks that every direction is one. You cannot be alone. The loneliness this world has never invented is the loneliness of empty space, and so the loneliness it offers instead is the opposite one — the loneliness of being the only thing in the forest that does not have a role.

Far above, where the air thins past breathing, the highest fliers cruise in their slow circles. They are so high they look like punctuation. They have been up there, some of them, for years. They are part of the weather. They are part of why there *is* weather.

You can hear them, even from here. You can hear everything from here. That is the condition of the place: nothing is far, because nothing is empty, and sound carries through living matter as well as it carries through air.

The cold deepens slightly as a cloud-bank below you shifts, dimming the under-light for a moment, and ten thousand things adjust their wing-beats in compensation, and the hum drops a quarter-tone, and the cycads exhale together, and a fine warm rain begins to fall upward from the basin as the ferrying-flyers begin their evening shift.

It is, you think, the loudest quiet you have ever stood inside.
