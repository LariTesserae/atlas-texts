# The Cold Loom

At this elevation the air thins into something that isn't quite air — it's a suspension, water half-becoming-vapor, vapor half-becoming-water, and the distinction never resolves. You breathe it and your lungs come away wet. You exhale and your breath joins the general condensation without a seam.

The plateau is vast. I think it was mountains once, a whole range of them, but erosion has worked on this place for so long that the peaks are memories held in the slope of things. What remains is a tableland the size of a small country, tilted maybe three degrees to the east, and across its entire back grows the moss.

*Moss* isn't right. It's what you'd call it at first. A continuous pelt of low green-black-silver vegetation, ankle deep in most places, thigh deep where it has pooled in the old valleys. It covers everything. There is no soil beneath it — the moss *is* the soil, layer on layer on layer of its own compressed dead, going down probably a hundred meters before you'd hit the ancient bedrock. This is a planet-skin made of one organism, or one consortium, and it has been making itself for longer than anything here has memory of.

The moss breathes. I mean this literally. Across kilometers you can watch the pelt rise and fall on some slow respiratory cycle, maybe one breath every four or five minutes, and when it exhales the cold mist above the plateau thickens into visible bands that drift east with the tilt.

And there — here is the thing — scattered across the plateau at intervals of maybe a kilometer or two, there are the **engines**.

No one built them. They grew. They are made of the moss, grown into shapes the moss should not be able to hold: great cog-wheels of living fiber twenty meters across, turning slowly on axles of compressed root-matter; pistons of bundled stem rising and falling in vertical shafts the plateau has opened for them; flues of hollow vegetable-ceramic venting pale steam into the dim grey sky. They clank. They hiss. They produce a low industrial thunder that rolls across the plateau and echoes back from nothing, because there is nothing to echo from, and yet it echoes. The sound has weight. You feel it in the sternum.

What do the engines do? They don't do anything, in the sense of producing anything. Or — they produce the plateau. They are the plateau's metabolism made visible. One engine's pistons pump groundwater up through the moss-strata; another's cogs wind slow cables of living fiber around drums, tightening some vast subterranean tension; a third simply burns, slowly, a green flame that does not consume its fuel, and the heat of that flame is what keeps the plateau from freezing solid.

Between the engines, the fauna.

They are abundant and they are wrong. Not malevolent — just wrong in the way the engines are wrong, which is to say they are the moss's other thoughts. Six-legged grazers the color of wet slate crop the pelt and do not leave bare patches because the pelt regrows as they eat. Long pale things like herons with too many joints wade in the moss-pools, spearing smaller things that may be fish or may be the moss pretending to be fish for reasons of its own. Flocks of something small and dark wheel overhead, and when they pass in front of one of the engine-flues their silhouettes resolve, briefly, into written characters in a language you almost know, and then scatter back into birds.

This is the reality-breakdown part, and it is quiet about itself. The plateau does not perform its weirdness. The weirdness is just the operating condition. Shadows fall in directions the dim sun doesn't justify. A stone you pick up (there are a few stones, worn to lozenges, the last hard remnants of the old mountains) will sometimes be warm and sometimes be humming and sometimes be, for a moment, the memory of a stone rather than the stone itself — and then it is just a stone again. Distances do not quite add up. You walk for an hour toward an engine and it is no closer; you look away and look back and you are standing beside it, and its great wet cog is turning past your face, and the fiber of the cog is braided so finely you can see, in the braid, smaller cogs, and smaller, down past where your eye can follow.

The turbulence is not in the weather — the weather is just the slow breath and the slow cold — but in the *configuration*. The plateau is constantly rearranging which engine drives which, which pool feeds which flue, which flock writes which character. Walk the same route twice and the second walk is through a different grammar of the same place. The moss is thinking, and its thinking is the landscape, and the thinking does not stop.

And yet, for all this: sparseness. Between the engines, kilometers of nothing but the pelt and the mist and the slow grazers at their distance. You can stand in a place and see four engines at four horizons and between them only the long tilted plain, and feel how *unpopulated* the thinking is, how much room this mind keeps empty around each of its thoughts. It is not a crowded world. It is a world with a few enormous thoughts, widely spaced, turning.

The light is the light of an overcast afternoon that has lasted a geological age. There is no sun visible but there is a brightness in the west that might be one, filtered through the permanent condensation. Nothing here has ever seen the sun directly and nothing here needs to. The moss makes its own small contribution — a faint bioluminescence in the deeper pools, greenish, that you only notice when you've been still for a long time.

The rules, as I can work them out:

Nothing is built, but much is made.
Nothing dies, but everything is ancient.
Nothing is cruel, but nothing is for you.
The engines will turn whether you are here or not.
If you lie down in the moss it will not harm you, but when you stand up you may find you have been lying down for longer than you thought, and that some part of you — a question you were carrying, a name — has been taken into the general metabolism and put to use, turning one of the far cogs, and you will not get it back, and you will not, after a while, particularly miss it.

This is the end-state of a world. Not dead — the opposite. A world that has finished becoming, and now only circulates. The cold keeps it slow. The water keeps it soft. The moss keeps it whole. The engines keep it turning. And somewhere under a hundred meters of its own green memory, the old stone of the mountains waits, patient, for nothing in particular, having arrived long ago at what it is.
