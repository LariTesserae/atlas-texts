# The Standing Loom

At four thousand meters, the ridge wakes wet. Not with dew, which implies a transaction with night, but with a permanent slow weeping — the rock itself sweats, beads forming on the dark basalt faces and tracking down in slender silver paths that never dry and never quite fall. The mountains here are young in the geological sense and also in another sense: they have the look of something recently extruded, sharp-cornered, unfinished, as if the crust beneath this part of the world is still in the act of pressing them upward and has paused only for the duration of a long breath.

The range runs farther than the eye admits. From any high point you see another high point, and behind it another, and the chain bends past where weather should bend it but doesn't — the clouds here obey the mountains rather than the other way around, hooking in long combed banks along the ridgelines, never crossing, never dispersing. This is one of the first things you notice: the sky has been arranged.

Cold sits in the valleys like a held note. Not bitter cold — the kind that numbs the lips slowly, that makes your breath visible as a thin permanent ribbon. The moisture in the air seems suspended rather than falling; it accumulates on the wool of your sleeve, on the lichen, on the long pale grasses that grow everywhere there is a handful of soil. The vegetation is lush in a low, clinging way: bryophyte mats two hands deep, ferns whose fronds are stiff with chill, dwarf rhododendron-things with leaves that look lacquered. No trees. Or rather — and this matters — no trees that grew here. There are tall vertical shapes among the slopes that read as trunks from a distance, but closer they are pipes.

This is the second thing you notice. The Loom.

It rises through the valleys and across the saddles and over the shoulders of the peaks: an industrial apparatus of continental extent, but threaded so finely through the landscape that for long stretches you walk past it without registering it as a single thing. A pipe a meter thick runs along a streambed, draped in moss, indistinguishable from a fallen log until you tap it and hear the resonance. A row of tall iron stacks crests a ridge two valleys over, each stack venting a column of white that joins the cloud-comb above. Cable trays bridge ravines, slung between basalt pinnacles, festooned with the same dripping bryophytes that cover everything else. There are turbine houses the size of cathedrals tucked into cirques, their windows glowing the soft amber of filament-light, their roofs gardened over by a century or a thousand years of slow lush growth.

And it runs. The whole apparatus runs. This is the third thing, and the loudest: the sound.

The valleys boom. Not the boom of a storm — a sustained, layered, almost musical roar of working machinery. Deep notes from the great pumping stations come up through the rock into the soles of your feet. Higher in the register, a constant industrial sibilance — steam being moved, gases exchanged. Somewhere a flywheel the size of a town turns at the speed of a slow clock, and you can hear it the way you can hear a river: not as an event but as a condition. Closer to hand, the smaller pipes hum at pitches that change when you press your ear to them. The Loom is loud in the way a city is loud, except no city is this large and no city sustains its sound this evenly. There is no rush hour. There is no night shift. The volume at noon is the volume at midnight. The volume in summer (if there is a summer) is the volume in winter.

Because nothing here changes.

That is the strangest property of the Standing Loom and the one that takes longest to accept. The clouds in their combed banks are arranged the same way they were arranged a year ago. The water beading on the basalt is, in some sense, the same water — it tracks down, it pools, it evaporates into the saturated air, it condenses again on the rock above, and the visible film of moisture on any given face has reached an equilibrium so exact that mosses grow on it in patterns that have not shifted in human memory. The vents emit at constant rate. The turbines turn at constant speed. The valleys flood to a certain level and no further; the streams run at a certain volume and no other. Step into a stream and your boot displaces water that flows around and rejoins itself downstream and the stream is, again, the stream.

There are no animals. None. No insects in the lush moss, no birds in the unmoving clouds, nothing scuttling under the ferns. The vegetation grows — must grow, to be this lush — but it grows into a shape it has already attained and replaces itself leaf for leaf. A frond that browns is replaced by a frond identical to it. The Loom and the landscape together form a single self-maintaining figure, and animal life would be a perturbation.

There are people, though. This is the fourth thing, and the one I keep almost forgetting.

Settlements cluster around the larger machine-houses: stone-and-iron towns of a few thousand souls each, terraced into the steep slopes, roofed in slate that the wet keeps black. They are urban in their density — narrow streets, multiple stories, the layered construction of places that have had to make do with little flat ground — but they are also few and far between. You can walk for three days between towns and pass only a single trackside shelter and the eternal pipework. The towns are lit at night by the soft amber of the Loom's own current, and the windows glow in a way that, from a high pass, looks like constellations laid out along the dark veins of the valleys.

The townspeople tend the Loom. This is not quite the same as operating it. The Loom does not need them, exactly — it runs whether they tend it or not, and has run, by their own accounts, for longer than any record. But they walk the lines, they oil what needs oiling, they note what needs noting in ledgers that are mostly identical to last year's ledgers. They are, in the most literal sense, the only thing in this landscape that is allowed to vary. A child is born; an elder dies; a couple moves from one terrace house to another. These small human dynamics happen against a backdrop so perfectly still that they take on the weight of weather, of season, of everything the world itself has chosen not to do.

Ask them what the Loom is for and you will get different answers in different towns. In one valley they will tell you it holds the sky up. In another they will tell you it is a prayer made out of pipes, set running long ago and never since interrupted. In a third they will say, with a certain practical shrug, that it is what makes the water bead on the rocks and the moss grow and the clouds comb along the ridges — that the world the Loom describes is the world, and to ask what it is for is to ask what the world is for, which is not a useful question at this elevation.

What is true is this: stand on any high pass at dusk, when the amber light from the turbine-houses begins to compete with the failing sky, and listen. The roar of the working never stops. The clouds do not move. The water on the basalt traces the same paths it traced an hour ago. And from somewhere very far below, where the great pumping stations breathe in their cathedral-sized rhythm, you can feel the mountain itself keeping time — slow, slow, slow — to a measure that was set before anyone arrived and that will continue, identical, after everyone has gone.

The Loom stands. The world stands with it. And the rain that is not quite rain goes on beading on every surface, holding its shape, refusing to fall.
