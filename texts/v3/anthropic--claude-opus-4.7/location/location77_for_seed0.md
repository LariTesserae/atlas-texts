# The Drum-Floor

Call it a valley, though it isn't one yet. Call it a forest, though the word is too small. The ground here is only a few thousand years old — still soft in the geological sense, still deciding what it wants to be — a low basin of black volcanic silt laid down by some event nobody remembers, now so thoroughly buried under root and leaf and living weight that you could walk across it for a week without touching stone.

It is night. It is almost always night here, or what passes for night: the canopy is so layered — three, four, five vegetal strata stacked atop one another — that even at noon the floor receives only a greenish dusk, a light that seems to come from the leaves themselves rather than through them. Some of them may, in fact, glow faintly. It's hard to tell what glows and what only reflects.

The first thing is the sound.

You cannot speak here. You cannot hear yourself think, not in the ordinary way — thinking has to be done differently, in pulses between the overlapping calls. The air is a standing wave of animal noise: a low frog-drone beneath everything, felt in the sternum, and above that the chittering of insects so dense it becomes a kind of white static, and above that the territorial shrieks of something with a long throat, and above that — higher, intermittent — a clean bell-like call that might be a bird or might be two stones struck together somewhere deep in the trees. There is no silence to compare the sound against. Silence, if it came, would feel like deafness.

The ground is warm. Not hot — warm the way a sleeping animal is warm, about the temperature of blood. Steam rises from it in places where the damp meets the heat of decomposition. The humidity is not quite rain but is not quite not-rain: every surface beads, every leaf drips onto the leaf below it, and the whole forest ticks constantly with this secondary weather of its own making. Once or twice a day the real rain comes, sudden and hammering, and for those ten minutes the sound-ceiling rises another register and the forest floor runs with black water that sinks immediately back into the silt.

The fauna is the thing that will undo a visitor.

There is no ground that is not animal. The silt itself moves — not metaphorically; the top two inches are a seething of worm-things, beetles with wet backs, small pale amphibians that have never seen sky. Every trunk carries a vertical traffic: things going up, things coming down, things clinging sideways and watching. The canopies are loud with wingbeats. At any given moment within arm's reach of where you stand there are perhaps four hundred creatures, and this is without counting the insects, which are weather.

They are not afraid of you. This is the part that takes adjustment. Nothing here has learned to be afraid of an upright thing, because there have been no upright things — or because the upright things that do exist are not feared, are part of the loud ordinary. Moths the size of a hand land on your face and stay there, pulsing. Something with six legs and a curious soft snout investigates your ankle and moves on. A snake the color of wet bark pours itself across your shoulders, tests the air, pours off again.

There are people here. A few. This is the surprise, given the lack of any built thing — no walls, no paths cut, no cleared ground, no pottery, no ash-pits that last more than a day. But there are people, and they have tools: chipped blades of a dark glassy stone (from where? the basin is all silt — they must walk days to find it), cord braided from a particular vine, long thin spears fire-hardened at the tip. They do not build because the forest will not permit it; anything set down is overgrown in a week, anything cut heals in a month. So they carry everything, or they let the forest keep it and find new things when they need them. They sleep in the high forks of certain trees that, for reasons they understand and you don't, are safer than others. They communicate mostly by touch and by a small repertoire of whistles pitched to slip between the frog-drone and the insect-static — a narrow acoustic window, the only one available.

They are not primitive in the sense of unsophisticated. They are primitive in the sense of early, unaccumulated. Their knowledge lives in their bodies and in each other and nowhere else, because nowhere else will hold it.

The weirdness, when you notice it, is structural rather than spectacular.

It is this: the forest is changing faster than forests should. A tree that was here yesterday is not here today — not fallen, gone, replaced by a different tree of a different kind, already mature, as if it had always been there. The people know this and have a word for it, or a gesture. They do not find it remarkable. The basin is young and the life in it has not yet settled into the slow rhythms that older ecosystems enforce; everything is still trying things, still in the first frenzy of colonization, and the forest composes and decomposes itself on a timescale closer to weather than to geology. Walk the same route twice and it is not the same route. The people do not walk routes. They walk *toward* things — toward the sound of a particular water, toward the smell of a particular fruit — and they find them, because those things are still there even when the arrangement around them has shuffled.

From any small rise — and there are small rises, this being a basin with some relief — you can see the canopy extending outward for what must be thirty or forty kilometers before it meets a horizon of low hills. The canopy from above (if you could get above it, which you can't) would look like a single dark green organism, breathing. At night it has faint patches of luminescence that drift slowly across it like weather on a gas giant. No one has seen it from above. No one here even has the concept of above in that sense. Above is just more forest, louder, wetter, full of different animals.

The rules of this place, insofar as there are any:

Do not sleep on the ground. Do not follow a sound you don't recognize; it may be mimicry, and even if it isn't, the forest will have moved by the time you arrive. Do not try to keep anything. The forest eats everything that stops moving, including silence, including memory, including — eventually, patiently — you.

But while you are moving, while you are part of the churn: you are not alone for a single second of a single day. You have never been less alone. The density of life presses on you from every direction at once, warm and wet and loud and indifferent and completely, overwhelmingly present, and there is a particular feeling that comes after the third or fourth day, when the panic of the noise wears off and you begin to hear *into* it — a feeling the people here have a gesture for but no word, a feeling of being held inside a single enormous living thing that is too busy becoming itself to notice you, and that this is, somehow, a kindness.
