There is a place high in the shoulders of a young mountain range where the air itself has learned to hold water the way a sponge does — not quite cloud, not quite rain, not quite fog, but something denser than any of these, a suspended ocean you walk through rather than swim in. The altitude is great enough that your ears feel the thinness of it, and yet the saturation is so complete that a held breath tastes of leaf-sap and wet stone and some faint mineral sweetness that might be the mountain exhaling.

The peaks here are sharp. They have not had time to be softened. The ridgelines cut upward in clean facets, pale grey shot through with veins of a paler mineral that catches the diffuse light and returns it milkily. Where water has run — and water runs everywhere, slowly, without hurry — it has cut only the shallowest grooves, fresh-edged, the lips of them still crisp. In a hundred thousand years these will be gorges. Today they are scratches.

Between the peaks the valley opens wide enough that a small town could rest in it, if anything here built towns. Nothing does. The valley floor is not floor so much as a layered canopy seen from above, because the vegetation has filled the basin to its brim — tier upon tier of broad waxen leaves, cupped ferns the size of wagon wheels, vining growths that have climbed one another into towers of green so dense that the actual ground, wherever it is, has not seen direct sun in a very long time. The saturation in the air means every surface is beaded, every leaf is a small cistern, and the plants drink from the atmosphere rather than the soil. Many of them have no roots worth the name. They simply hold on.

The strangeness is this: the water in the air moves in slow coherent bodies. You can watch a denser mass drift through the valley like a whale-shape of almost-liquid, shouldering aside the thinner mist, rolling over a stand of tree-ferns and leaving them dripping heavier than before. These bodies do not disperse. They have surfaces, of a kind — you can see the boundary where the saturation changes — and they travel the valley on currents you cannot feel on your skin. Sometimes two of them meet and merge with a sound like a long sigh exhaled into cloth. Sometimes one settles into a hollow and sits there for days, and the plants beneath it grow visibly faster, reaching up into its belly.

It is warm. Not hot — warm the way bathwater is warm when you have been in it a while and stopped noticing. The warmth is even, without the sharp gradients of sun and shade, because the light itself is even: filtered through the suspended water until it arrives from every direction at once, a soft ambient daylight that casts almost no shadows. Colors here are saturated in the way colors are saturated underwater, greens going deep and complicated, the pale mineral of the rock glowing gently.

Animals are rare. Something large moves occasionally through the canopy below — you hear a leaf-rustle disproportionate to any wind, and then nothing. Small translucent things drift in the denser water-bodies, neither quite fish nor quite insect, their bodies the same refractive index as the medium they live in so that you see them mostly as distortions, little lensings of the background. They seem to feed on whatever nutrients the slow water carries. They do not come near the thinner air where a walker would be.

The place is quiet. The saturation damps sound — your own footsteps on wet stone come back muffled, as if heard through a wall. Water drips from ten million leaves, but each drip is soft and they do not synchronize, so the overall effect is a hush with texture, a white noise so gentle it reads as silence with grain in it. Occasionally, far off, one of the drifting water-bodies moves through a stand of hollow reeds and draws from them a low slow chord that lasts most of a minute before fading. These chords are the loudest things that happen. They happen perhaps twice a day.

Nothing here is in a hurry. The mountains are rising, but only by the width of a fingernail in a human lifetime. The water-bodies drift at the pace of grazing cattle. The plants grow steadily but without urgency, because their resource — the wet air — is inexhaustible and unchallenged. Even erosion, which elsewhere is the impatient sculptor, here works with the patience of something that knows it has all the time that will ever exist. The grooves deepen. The ridges soften, eventually. The valley fills, slowly, with more of itself.

If you stood still long enough, one of the drifting water-bodies would find you. It would come up the slope at its grazing pace and fold around you without violence, and you would be, for some minutes, inside it — breathing air three-quarters water, feeling the translucent drifters pass through the space your body occupied, seeing the valley through a lens that magnified and softened everything beyond. Then it would move on, leaving you heavier, beaded all over, slightly altered in a way you could not name. This is the closest thing the place has to an event. It is also, perhaps, the closest thing it has to a greeting.
