# The Cradle Plateaus

Begin at the edge, because everything here begins at an edge.

The mountains are so old they have forgotten how to be mountains. What remains is a high country of broad shoulders and rounded summits, ridges worn down to the gentle curvature of riverstones though they stand nearly four kilometers above any sea. Time has done to them what time does — sanded their drama away, leaving plateaus where peaks once were, leaving the granite exposed in long sheets like the backs of sleeping animals. You can walk for an hour along what was once a knife-edge arête and feel only a slight rise underfoot. The bones of the range are visible everywhere, but the bones are soft now, smoothed.

And yet — elevation. The air is thin enough that your first impulse, stepping out from the funicular station, is to sit down. There is a particular blue the sky takes at this height: not the blue of clear weather but a deeper indigo, the upper atmosphere already announcing itself, the curve of the world implied at the horizon.

---

The plateaus are damp. Not wet — there are no rivers proper up here, the land too flat and the watersheds too confused for rivers — but damp the way an old cellar is damp, the way moss is damp. Clouds pass through rather than over. They roll up the long slopes from the valleys below and break against the tablelands, dissolving into mist that catches in the cushion-vegetation and the rock-pools and the lichen-felt. Sometimes it rains, briefly, fiercely, and then stops. More often the weather is simply *humid stone* — every surface beaded, every breath visible.

The vegetation has adapted to this: lush in its way, but low. Nothing here grows tall except where it has found shelter in the lee of a tor or in the gentle bowls between summits. There are cushion-plants the size of dining tables, packed so densely you could lie down on them and not feel the rock beneath. There are mosses in two dozen shades of green and one shade of dusty red. There are dwarf rhododendron-things with leaves like leather coins, and a kind of low groundcover that releases a peppery scent when stepped on. Where water pools, there are pale reeds and bladed sedges. Where the wind funnels through cols, the plants flatten themselves into mats and the mats grow over the rocks like skin.

Layered, near-touching, high-saturation. The density of the place is in its mosses, its lichens, its lapping vegetation — three or four species occupying any square meter, tier on tier, the topmost just a film of cyanobacteria glazing everything black-green after rain.

---

People live here.

This is the strangeness of it — or one of the strangenesses. At this altitude, in this thin cool damp, somebody built a town, and the town has been there long enough that no one remembers building it. It is laid out along the broad spine of one plateau and spills down into a saddle between two summits, perhaps two thousand structures in all, perhaps three. The buildings are of the local stone, dark and lichen-stained, with roofs of overlapping slate that has the same dull sheen as the rocks they came from. From any distance the town looks like an outcropping. Walk into it and you see the streets are narrow and stepped, the windows small and deep-set against the cold, the doorways hung with felted curtains rather than doors.

What's industrial about the place is not obvious at first. You notice the smokestacks — three of them, low and thick, venting a pale steam that joins the cloud-traffic and disappears. You notice the cables strung between buildings, carrying current from some source you cannot see. You notice, walking deeper in, the sound of machinery: a soft thrumming underfoot, a turbine somewhere, the periodic exhalation of pressure being released. There is a foundry, or something foundry-like. There is a mill that grinds something — not grain — into fine powders that are then loaded into ceramic jars and carted down through the streets on small electric trolleys. There is a refinery that handles what the springs bring up.

Because there are springs. The plateaus, despite their dryness in any conventional sense, are riddled with hot springs — water that has traveled deep into the old rock and returned warm, mineral-saturated, smelling of iron and something fainter, almost sweet. The town was built where the springs are densest. The industry runs on them: geothermal turbines, mineral extraction, the slow patient harvest of whatever the deep rock has dissolved into the rising water. Everything mechanized, everything modest in scale. No great chimneys, no great furnaces. Just the quiet competent extraction of a thin resource by people who have been doing it for centuries.

---

Sound, in the town, is mostly the sound of weather. The thrum of the turbines is felt more than heard. Footsteps on wet stone. The occasional bell — there are bells on the trolleys, on doorways, hung from eaves where the wind can move them. Voices are low. People do not shout up here; the air doesn't carry shouts well, and there's a cultural habit of quiet that may have grown out of that physical fact or may have preceded it. Bargains in the market are conducted in murmurs. Children are quieter than children should be. When the wind comes through the cols it makes a long sustained note, neither moan nor whistle, that the locals have a word for and the rest of us do not.

---

Fauna is rare. There are birds — a kind of high-altitude corvid, sooty and intelligent, that nests in the rock-clefts and steals from the trolleys. There are pikas in the screes, when you can find screes. There are the felted moths, fat as thumbs, that emerge at dusk and beat heavily through the mist. But the great mammals of lower elevations do not come here. The air is too thin for them. The plateaus support little that requires much oxygen or much running. What lives here lives slowly.

The people themselves live slowly. They are barrel-chested and patient. They have a faint blue cast to their lips that is not cold but adaptation. They sleep long, in the dim months, and longer in the dimmer months.

---

The light is dim because the cloud-traffic is constant. Even when the sky is clear, the angle of the sun on these high latitudes is shallow, and the cloud-shadow of the valleys below stains everything an ambient gray-violet. There are hours of true brightness, in summer, when the cloud-deck falls away and the sun strikes the plateau like a struck bell — but mostly the light is filtered, diffuse, the kind of light you find inside a paper lantern. Everything has soft edges. The town reads as a charcoal sketch most days, the smokestacks slightly darker smudges against a lighter smudge of sky.

---

Now, the strangeness.

It is a 1.5 on a scale that goes to 3, so: not overwhelming, not invasive. It is the strangeness you accept after a day or two and then cannot un-see.

The hot springs do not always run hot. They breathe. Some have a tidal rhythm — warm for nine hours, cool for three, the cycle as reliable as a clock. The refinery's schedules are built around this. Other springs are stranger: they run hot when a particular wind blows, or cool when the town is sleeping, as if the rock below were sensitive to pressures the instruments cannot detect.

The old corvids — the sooty ones — sometimes speak. Not mimicry. A few syllables, in the local language, used appropriately. Asked about this, the townspeople will shrug. The birds have been around a long time. The town has been around a long time. Things rub off.

And there is the matter of the deep mist, which sometimes settles into the saddle between the two summits and stays for days, and which sometimes, when it lifts, leaves the streets *rearranged* — not dramatically, but enough that a person who has lived in the town for forty years will pause at a corner and not, for a moment, recognize it. The buildings have not moved. The lanes have not moved. Something else has. Something underneath the cartography.

The townspeople accept this. The plateaus are old. The plateaus have settled into their final form, but a final form is not an inert form. The rock is patient and the rock is awake, in its slow way. You do not live four kilometers up on the back of something this ancient without understanding that you are a guest.

---

The dynamism, then, is not in the rock — which barely changes — but in everything that moves across the rock. The clouds, the springs, the mist, the slow seasonal cycle of when the cushion-plants flower (briefly, in a riot of small white stars, for perhaps eleven days a year), the long migration of the felted moths from one plateau to another, the daily rhythm of the trolleys and the turbines and the bells, the breath of the springs, the speech of the birds. All of this layered and overlapping, each process on its own tempo, the whole thing humming quietly along like a clockwork that has been wound by something deeper than anyone living can remember winding.

It is a cool place. A damp, cool, dim, quiet, dense, ancient, lived-in place. The kind of place where you arrive expecting to pass through and find, two weeks later, that you have begun greeting the corvids by name and that one of them has begun, occasionally, to greet you back.
