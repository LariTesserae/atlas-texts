# The Cantophyte

There is no ground here. Or rather, the ground is something the place is still deciding to make.

Start with what you'd see if you came down through the cloud-layer — and there is always a cloud-layer, because the canopy breathes faster than the atmosphere can dry. You would see green. Not a forest's green, exactly. The green of a single organism the size of a small continent, lobed and folded into itself, throwing up vaulted swells of tissue a kilometer thick and then sinking into broad shallow basins where the swells exhaled their water back.

The whole landmass is the plant. The plant is the whole landmass. Whatever rock exists beneath has never been seen by anything; it is held a hundred meters down under layers of root-mat, fiber, sap-channel, vascular conduit, and the slow accreting biomass of a thing that has been alive for so long it has begun to do its own geology.

## The Substrate

Walk on it — if you could walk on it — and you would feel it give like wet bread, then resist, then give again, in layers. The outermost half-meter is a sphagnum-equivalent: lacy, copper-green, cold to the touch, saturated. Push your hand in to the wrist and the next layer feels cartilaginous, like the inside of a fruit you've never eaten. Below that, fibers as thick as cables run laterally, conducting something — water, sugar, signal, all three. Below that, no one knows.

The whole structure weeps. Constantly. Not rain — the rain is its own separate event — but a fine continuous lensing of water out of every surface. Rocks here are not rocks. The few stones you encounter are weeping nodules, plant-secreted concretions of silicate the organism has manufactured for some purpose of its own: ballast, perhaps, or memory. They sit half-emerged from the substrate like teeth coming in.

## The Architecture

Now look up. The vaulted swells I mentioned — these are not hills. They are the standing waves of the organism's own growth, frozen at the scale of geography. Imagine a kelp forest, except the kelp is the size of mountain ranges, and instead of swaying it has settled into a slow standing oscillation with a period of decades. The undersides of these vaults form caverns roofed in pale luminous tissue. The light here is dim because most of it is being eaten, photosynthesized, before it can reach you — what filters through is a sub-aqueous green, the color of looking up from the bottom of a pond.

The vaults touch each other. They lean. They braid. There is no open sky from below, only a vast articulated ceiling of living tissue, and where two vaults press together the contact zone fuses and becomes a load-bearing column of fiber thick as a cathedral. None of this was built. All of it is grown, and grown recently — the surfaces are still raw, sap still beading at the seams, new tissue glossy and untoughened. This is a landscape in the act of being made. Erosion has not yet had time to find it; water runs off but has not yet cut a channel; the place is pre-river, pre-valley, embryonic at continental scale.

## The Sound

And god, the sound.

This is the parameter that breaks you first, if you came from somewhere quiet. The organism is loud. Not in the way a forest is loud — there are no birds, no insects, no wind through leaves. The sound is the plant itself, and it is the sound of a structure this size doing its biology in real time.

There is a constant low frequency, somewhere around 30 Hz, that is the standing wave of the vaults flexing. You feel it in your sternum before you hear it. Above that, a continuous rushing — sap moving through conduits the diameter of subway tunnels, water column-pumped up the vaults against gravity by mechanisms that aren't quite osmosis and aren't quite capillary action. The pumping has a syncopated rhythm; pressure pulses travel through the substrate and arrive at your feet as a thudding. Cracks and pops as new tissue extrudes. Long groaning notes from somewhere far off — a vault settling, perhaps, into a new configuration. Sometimes a sound like a bell being struck very slowly under water, and no one can tell you what it is.

It is loud enough that conversation would be impossible. Loud enough that the sound itself becomes a kind of weather, washing over you in fronts and lulls. Loud enough that after a while you stop hearing it and only feel it, and then when you leave you discover the silence is unbearable.

## The Tech

Here is where the place gets strange.

The plant is not just a plant. Or — it is a plant, fully, but at scales below what an eye can see, the organism is doing engineering that no current physics fully accounts for. Inside the fibers, at nanometer scales, are structures that move electrons in coherent loops, that hold quantum states warm and stable in ways nothing should, that do computation. The pumping system mentioned above — the way water moves up a kilometer of vault against gravity — is not osmosis. It is something the organism evolved or assembled, a mechanism that uses the coherence of its own internal water columns to do work at thermodynamic efficiencies that should not exist.

You will not see any of this. There is no machine here. There is no built thing. There is only plant tissue, and inside the plant tissue, distributed across the entire continental mass, a substrate that is doing something computational. The whole landscape is, in some functional sense, thinking — though no one has been able to characterize what about, or whether the word *thinking* applies, or whether it's noticed us.

The weeping nodules I mentioned earlier — the silicate concretions — these may be related. They are arranged in patterns that, mapped from above, suggest a network. They are not random. They grow in positions that, in any other ecosystem, you would call considered.

## Atmosphere and Weather

The air is cool, around 8–12°C, and saturated. Walking is walking through a suspension; every breath delivers water as well as oxygen. The light is dim and green and falls in slow shafts where the canopy parts. Mist forms and re-forms continuously, condensing on every surface, rolling along the floor of the vaults in slow rivers.

The plant generates its own weather. Transpiration on this scale produces a near-permanent low-pressure system over the central mass; storms rotate inward and dump rain that the organism re-absorbs and re-evaporates within hours. From orbit, the place would look like a vast green wheel under a fixed cyclonic eye. From inside, the rain when it comes is warm, and it falls so densely and consistently that it functions less as weather than as a fourth medium, after air, water, and plant.

## What Lives Here

Almost nothing, in the way you mean. No animals. No fungi competing for the substrate — the organism has saturated every ecological niche it might have shared. There are smaller plant forms, but they are not separate species; they are differentiated tissues of the same continental organism, like organs. A patch of fine reddish filament-grass on the floor of one vault is connected, through kilometers of root-fiber, to a knot of bulbous fruiting bodies on the underside of a vault ceiling twenty kilometers away. They are one thing. Everything is one thing.

This is why the place feels so packed, so saturated, so without-room. Every cubic meter is occupied by the organism in some form: as tissue, as conduit, as sap, as vapor exhaled by tissue, as light absorbed by tissue. There is no negative space. There is no *between*. You are not in a landscape; you are inside a body that has decided to be the size of a country.

## What It Is Becoming

The last thing to say is that all of this is new.

The erosion parameter — 0.1 — means this place is barely formed. The vaults have only just risen. The substrate has not yet compacted. The nodules have not yet completed whatever pattern they are growing toward. The organism is in the early phase of whatever it is doing, and the landscape we are describing is a snapshot of a process that will, given another thousand years, look completely different — though still recognizably itself, the way a child's face is recognizable in the adult.

There is the sense, walking through it, that you have arrived early. That the place is still under construction by itself, for itself, and that the deafening sound around you is the sound of a continent-scale organism in the middle of its own becoming, doing physics we don't have the words for, in a body so large it is also a country, in a country so alive it has begun to think.

And the weeping rock at your feet, when you look at it closely, has grown a millimeter since you arrived.
