# The Cleft at Aoun-Sur

High up, where the air thins enough to make your fingertips tingle but not enough to hurt, there is a place the masons call Aoun-Sur — the Half-Made. It sits in a cleft between two mountains that have not yet decided to be mountains. Their flanks are still glossy, still wet from the inside, the rock so young it shines like cut liver where the light strikes it. Nothing has weathered here yet. Edges are sharp the way a child's teeth are sharp — not because they were ground that way, but because they came in already finished and have not been used.

The cleft itself is perhaps four hundred paces across and a thousand long. A grove and a town fit inside it, with room to spare. You could walk out of it in an hour if you knew the paths.

## The Stone

The rock is dark green, almost black where wet, and it is wet almost everywhere. Water doesn't run here so much as *seep* — it emerges from the stone itself, beading on the surface like sweat on a forehead, gathering in the hollows of every shelf and stair. There are no streams cut into the floor. There are no streambeds. The water has not been here long enough to carve anything. It collects in standing pools that brim and overflow sideways in slow sheets, then re-condense further down, never quite becoming a river. When you press your palm against the cliff face, your hand comes away slick and faintly warm, as if the mountain has a fever it is trying to break.

There are no scree slopes. There is no talus. Nothing has fallen yet. The geology here is pre-erosional in a way that feels almost embarrassing, like walking in on something not yet dressed.

## The Grove

Vegetation has found the cleft and thrown itself into it with the abandon of a thing that knows it has won. Tall feather-pines, blue-needled, grow from the wet ledges in tiers. Their roots have not had time to crack the stone — instead they spread flat across the surface like spilled candle wax, gripping by adhesion and weight alone. Between them, broad-leaved understory plants with leaves the size of cloaks hold standing water in their cupped centers. Moss of three colors — a black moss, a moss the color of new copper, and a pale celadon moss that glows faintly at dusk — covers everything that hasn't been deliberately scraped clean.

Despite the lushness, the air feels empty. The trees are large and the leaves are many, but they are spaced. You can see a long way between trunks. Birdsong is occasional, not constant. A single deer-like creature with too-long legs might cross the open floor once a day; you might glimpse a striped marten in the rocks once a week. The biomass is overwhelmingly vegetable and microbial. The animal kingdom has barely arrived.

## The Town

And yet — *and yet* — there is a town here. A real town, layered and complicated, with three or four hundred souls and the infrastructure to keep them. This is the strange thing about Aoun-Sur: it is densely built and sparsely inhabited, in a landscape that is geologically newborn.

The builders are masons of an old order, and they have terraced the cleft on both sides with platforms of fitted stone, joined by stairs and covered walkways. The buildings are stacked — three, four, five stories, climbing the wet walls — connected by wooden bridges that creak constantly in the updraft. The roofs are steep and shingled with split slate, channeling the perpetual condensation into clay pipes that feed cisterns and bathhouses and the long stone troughs where the wash-women work.

There is no metal here beyond what is hammered cold: copper pins, lead seals, a few bronze tools brought up from below. The masons cut with stone chisels and bone wedges and patience. They build slowly. A house takes a generation. A bridge takes two.

## The Sound

Aoun-Sur is loud. Not with voices — the population is too small for that — but with water and stone-work and wind. Water drips from ten thousand surfaces in a constant percussive shimmer, each drop falling onto another surface that drips in turn, so that the cleft hums with a layered, nested rainfall that never stops. The wind, channeled by the geometry of the cleft, moans through the open galleries and the tuned stone vents the masons have cut into the cliffs for exactly this purpose — they say a town must sing, or the mountains will not accept it. Hammers ring. Chisels tap. The cisterns gurgle. The bathhouses hiss steam into the cold air.

It is loud the way a forge is loud, or a waterfall, or a cathedral during a service — loud with purpose, not with chaos. You can sleep in it. People raised here cannot sleep anywhere else.

## The Rules

A few things are true here that are not true elsewhere.

The stone grows. Slowly — a finger's width in a lifetime — but measurably. The masons mark their courses with copper pins and check them against the pins their grandfathers set. A wall built flush against the cliff will, in two hundred years, be embedded in it. Old buildings are slowly swallowed. This is understood and planned for; the deepest cellars of Aoun-Sur are the ground floors of houses built nine generations ago.

Water that condenses on the ceiling of a room takes on the character of whatever was said beneath it. The wash-women collect ceiling-water from the meeting halls and sell it as a kind of tincture: argument-water for cleaning wounds, oath-water for setting dyes, lullaby-water for infants who will not sleep. Whether this works is not the point. The point is that it is done, and that the meeting halls are built with low, sloped ceilings, and that the drip-cups are emptied on schedule.

Nothing here is old. This is the deepest strangeness. The mountains are young, the water is new, the soil is barely soil. The town is the oldest thing in the cleft, and even it is recent by the measure of any plain or valley below. Aoun-Sur is a settlement built into a landscape that has not yet finished being born — a town in the womb of the world, building its walls against rock that is still warm.

The masons say: *we are early. We are very early. We must build well, because everything that comes after will be measured against what we set down now.*

And the water drips, and the wind sings through the vents, and the green stone weeps quietly into its cupped leaves, and somewhere in the high galleries a chisel finds a true edge and rings out, clean and small, against the long slow noise of a place still becoming itself.
