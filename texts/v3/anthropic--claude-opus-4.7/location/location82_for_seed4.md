# The Understory City

Begin with what you cannot see, because there is almost no light here. Close your eyes and let the place form first as temperature — a damp cold, not biting, the kind that gathers in a root cellar or a cave mouth in autumn. The air is thick with moisture you could wring from a cloth. Every surface weeps.

Now open them, and let them adjust for a long time.

What you are inside is a single hollow beneath the root-arch of something enormous. The tree — or what functions as a tree — is not visible in its entirety; you can only assume it from the evidence of its lower body. Four buttresses of living wood, each wider than a house, have risen from the ground and curved together overhead to form a vaulted chamber perhaps forty paces across. The wood is slick and black-green with condensed breath. Small rivulets trace downward along its grain, pooling in depressions of the uneven floor, feeding a shallow marsh that occupies the chamber's low center. The pool is maybe ankle-deep. Water beetles skate its surface. Things move beneath.

This is the whole world. The parameters are not lying when they say hidden — there is no beyond to this place, or rather, the beyond exists but is not part of the description. Beyond the root-arch the forest continues, but here, inside, is a pocket, a held breath, a single room of the world.

## The City

The settlement occupies the inner walls of the root-chamber. It is not built on the ground — the ground is marsh — but woven and carved and pinned into the vertical bark. Houses are wicker-and-wattle affairs no larger than a crouched person, packed in tiers, reached by ladder-paths of lashed twig. Perhaps three hundred dwellings in all, staggered along the four great buttresses, with bridges of plaited bast spanning the gaps at the chamber's upper curve. The construction is sparse against the vast wooden walls — built covers nearly two-thirds of the available surface in density of intent, but much bark remains bare and glistening between the clusters. The builders understood that a city needs emptiness between its gathered parts.

The materials are all plant: woven root-hair, pressed leaf-felt, resin caulk, lashings of inner bark, the occasional bone pin. No metal to speak of, though I see a few small blades of knapped flint and one knife whose edge is some kind of vitreous plant-stone, perhaps a silica concretion harvested from a stem. The tools are simple. Needles, awls, scrapers, a flint-chip plane for smoothing wattle. A loom in one house, hand-sized, stringing fibers fine as spider-silk.

The dwellings glow, faintly. Each has a pot or bowl set near its threshold, and in each pot grows a colony of pale fungus that issues a cold blue-green light no stronger than starlight on snow. You can read a face by it if the face is close. This is nearly all the illumination in the chamber, supplemented only by drifting motes — luminous insects, or organisms that resemble insects, that wander the damp air in slow irregular flights.

## The People

I see them now, adjusted. They are small. Child-sized, but proportioned as adults. Their skin is pale as the undersides of leaves, and their eyes are large and dark and wet-looking, evolved for this permanent twilight. They wear wrapped garments of the same leaf-felt their houses are made from, and they move quietly — not silently, but with the unhurried economy of people who know every surface of their world intimately and do not need to rush between its parts.

They are few. Perhaps two hundred persons, for the three hundred dwellings — many houses stand empty, or are used for storage, or for the dead. A sparse population in a sparse city. They gather at the pool's edge to fill clay vessels, they climb to the upper bridges to tend to something I will get to in a moment, they sit in pairs on their thresholds and speak in voices so low they barely disturb the air.

The sound of this place is mostly the water — the slow drip from a thousand points on the vault, falling into the pool with a continuous tiny percussion like rain just ending. Beneath that, the occasional creak of the great wood settling. And beneath *that*, very faintly, something that might be the tree breathing, or might be something else.

## The Fauna

And here is where the place reveals what it is really about.

The chamber is not sparsely populated with animals. It is *saturated* with them. But they are mostly invisible at first because they are mostly small, or mostly still, or mostly hidden in the bark.

The walls are covered, at a scale you must lean close to see, with creatures. Pale translucent slugs the size of a fingernail, grazing on the condensation-lichens. Blind crustaceans, long-legged, walking upside down along the root-ceiling. Moths with no eyes and no color, drifting. Something like a millipede but segmented in spirals, coiled in every crevice. A single stretch of bark the size of your palm holds fifty living things if you look. The whole chamber holds millions.

In the marsh pool, larger things. Fish without pigment, some without eyes, circling slowly. Salamanders longer than your arm coiled in the deeper hollows. A thing that might be an eel and might be a worm, briefly surfacing, sinking again. The water is alive to the point that stepping into it would be a disturbance of civilization.

And — this is what the people tend on the upper bridges — there are larger animals kept, half-domesticated, in woven pens high on the buttresses. Pale, long-bodied creatures with many legs, about the size of a cat, that produce a kind of silk. A flightless bird-analogue, tall as a person's knee, kept for its eggs. A solitary enormous beast coiled in the highest pen whose function I cannot determine — perhaps it is revered, perhaps it is a prisoner, perhaps it is a god. Its breathing is what I took earlier for the tree's.

The overwhelming-ness of the fauna is not in any one creature but in the totality: every surface, every volume of water, every cubic inch of air contains life. The people move through this continuous teeming with the matter-of-factness of those who have never known otherwise. When a child reaches out without looking and plucks a grub from the wall and eats it, this is not remarkable. When a woman sleeps with a slug crossing her forearm, she does not wake.

## The Dynamics

The place is young. The wood is still growing — you can see, in places, where a buttress has thickened since a house was built and the house has been deformed, rehung, re-lashed to accommodate the expansion. The people are accustomed to this. Their architecture is provisional. A house may need to be moved three times in a life.

There is a cycle I begin to perceive as I watch longer. Twice in what I take to be a day, the pool rises. Water seeps faster from the walls, the rivulets thicken, the marsh deepens by a hand's breadth. The people draw up their lowest ladders. Things come up out of the deepening water — larger salamanders, the worm-eel, a procession of pale crabs that walk out across the floor and climb partway up the walls to graze. Then the water recedes. The creatures return. The ladders drop. Some cycle of the tree, perhaps, some tidal pulse of sap and transpiration, is the weather of this world.

And occasionally — rarely — something falls from above. A seed, or a fruit, or a dead creature from the canopy far overhead that none of these people have ever seen. When this happens the people gather. Whatever has fallen is treated with ceremony. It is, for them, news from the sky.

## The Rule

There is, I think, one rule that governs this place, and I will end here because the rule is the thing the place has been organizing itself to tell me:

Nothing leaves. The chamber is a closed room. The people were born here, their parents were born here, and so on backward into whatever founding. The forest beyond the root-arch is not for them. Whether they are forbidden to leave, or unable, or have simply never conceived of it, I cannot say. But the boundary of this pocket is absolute. Everything that matters — every birth, every death, every meal, every word spoken, every creature tended, every house rebuilt against the slow pressure of the growing wood — happens inside one room at the base of one tree in one part of a forest whose extent is not their concern.

It is not a sad place. It is only small, and full, and cold, and nearly dark, and entirely alive.
