# The Rookery Terraces

Begin at altitude. You are somewhere on the shoulder of a mountain that has been remade — not by chisel or trowel, but by accumulation. Tier upon tier of nests rise from the slope, woven so densely and for so many generations that the original stone is gone, lost somewhere far below the present surface. What you stand on is built. Everything is built. There is no rock here that hasn't been chosen, carried, lodged, and bound into a structure by some living throat or claw.

The terraces themselves are massive — house-sized cups of woven branch, mud, feather, regurgitated fiber, bone, lichen-thread, and saliva-glue cured to something harder than wood. They have weathered into one another over centuries. Walls of nest have merged with walls of nest until what remains is a vast layered honeycomb the color of wet earth and old straw, mottled with greenish stains where moss has taken hold in the seams, white-streaked where droppings have run down in slow chalk-rivers and set. You can press your hand to it and feel the texture of ten thousand twigs locked together, smoothed by bodies passing over them for so long the woven surface has taken on the polished feel of driftwood.

It is dim. The terraces overhang one another, each tier shading the one below, and the air beneath the eaves is the color of weak tea. Light filters down in scattered shafts through gaps in the woven roofs, picking out drifting feathers, drifting down — feathers everywhere, in the air, in the cracks, banked at the edges of every walkway like snow. Vegetation has colonized the structure: ferns sprout from joints, vines drape across the lips of nest-mouths, mosses pad every horizontal surface, and small flowering things have learned to root in the rich rot of guano and shed down. From a distance the mountain would look green and brown and white, layered, living, breathing.

It is warm. The collective body heat of the inhabitants keeps the lower passages at blood temperature even at this elevation. You feel it as a soft constant pressure on your skin, the warmth of a held animal. Condensation beads on the undersides of the higher nests and drips, drips, drips in irregular percussion. There are no streams here, no proper rivers — only the slow weeping of moisture from a million breaths, pooling in old nest-cups, running down channels worn smooth by centuries of the same trickle, gathering in basins where iridescent green algae bloom. The water tastes of feathers when you cup it.

And the sound.

The sound is the place. The sound is what the place is for. From every tier, from every chamber, from every cup-shaped hollow in the woven cliffs, comes calling — shrieks, whistles, low subsonic boomings you feel in your sternum, chittering trills, the clack of beaks, the dry rattle of pinion against pinion, the rush of wingbeat after wingbeat after wingbeat. Tens of thousands of voices at any moment. Hundreds of thousands. It is not noise, exactly — it has structure, the way a waterfall has structure — but it is constant and crushing and you cannot speak over it; you would not try. You learn quickly to communicate by touch, by pointing, by leaning your head against someone's head so the bone conducts what the air cannot. Within an hour your ears have stopped trying to parse it and accepted it as the new condition of silence.

The fauna are overwhelming because they *are* the architecture. Every surface has its tenants. Great long-necked birds with sooty plumage perch on the upper rims, watching the sky. Smaller fork-tailed species swarm the middle tiers in clouds, threading the passageways. In the lower, darker chambers, things with broad soft wings and large eyes move slowly, brooding over eggs the size of melons. There are pale climbing creatures — featherless, perhaps young, perhaps a different kind altogether — that scuttle along the vine-strung undersides upside down. Beetles work the floors, processing what falls. Tree-frogs the color of wet leaves cling to the moss in the damp pockets. Snakes pulse slow through the woven walls, eating eggs, eating mice, eating each other. The whole mountain is metabolizing itself.

Nothing here is made of metal. Nothing here is shaped by hand. There is no fire. The building is biological — saliva and weave and instinct repeated across so many generations that the result has the scale of a city and the logic of a coral reef. The rules of the world are simple: every wall was once carried in a beak. Every floor was once a nest. The dead are absorbed into the structure; bones become beams, feathers become insulation. Nothing leaves. Things only accumulate, and settle, and weather into balance.

Change here is slow. A tier may take a human lifetime to be fully built; another lifetime to be fully abandoned; a third to slump and merge into the tier below. The mountain grows perhaps a hand's-breadth taller each century, perhaps less. The ecology cycles around itself in long patient turns. A drought tightens things. A wet year loosens them. The great booming species in the depths may go quiet for a decade and then resume, and no one will know why. There are no events here, only conditions.

If you stay, you stay below the eaves, in the warm dim, breathing air thick with the smell of musk and damp straw and old eggshell. You learn to walk without startling. You learn that the sound, if you stop resisting it, becomes a kind of holding — the constant unbroken voice of the place telling you, in a register too large to parse, that you are inside something alive.
