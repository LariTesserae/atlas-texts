# The Drowned Works

There is a city at the bottom of a warm shallow sea, and it has not stopped running.

You come to it from above, descending through water the color of weak tea — not the cold black of true deep sea, but something closer to bathwater, lit from somewhere by a dim sourceless amber. The water is warm enough that you notice it on your skin before you notice anything else, body-temperature or slightly above, with the soft mineral thickness of a spring. Nothing grows here. No kelp, no anemones, no soft fringe of algae on the stones. The water is barren in the way old bathhouses are barren — clean of life but full of dissolved things, iron and sulfur and something metallic that coats the back of the tongue.

The city begins as a forest of stacks.

Pipes rise out of the seabed in clusters, some no thicker than a wrist, others wide enough to swallow a person whole. They lean. They have leaned for a very long time. The corrosion on them is not the bright orange of new rust but the deep settled black-purple of iron that has been underwater for centuries and reached some equilibrium with the water around it — a patina so thick it has become the surface, no longer eating inward. The pipes are bolted into housings of cast iron, and the housings are bolted into foundations of brick, and the brick is silted over but still recognizably laid in courses, a pattern of human hands somewhere far back.

And the whole thing is *working*.

This is the part that takes a moment to understand. The machines are running. Down in the silt, somewhere below the visible architecture, engines are turning over. You can feel it in the water before you hear it — a deep tactile pressure-wave that arrives in your chest as a kind of nausea, then resolves into sound, then into more sound, then into a wall of sound so complete that it stops being sound and becomes simply the medium you are moving through. Pistons hammering. Steam-hiss muffled and warped by water into a low groan. The shriek of metal-on-metal where some great shaft turns in a bearing that has been wearing for an age. Bubbles — enormous gouts of them — erupt from valves at irregular intervals and roar upward in silver columns, and each eruption is a thunderclap that travels through your bones.

The water itself shudders. Currents shove you sideways without warning, hot jets from somewhere venting, cold inrushes from somewhere drawing. Visibility comes and goes as silt is kicked up and resettles. The whole volume of water around the works is in constant turbulent motion, a chaos of pressure differentials, and yet the city does not tear itself apart. It has reached its own kind of stability — the equilibrium of something that has been shaking itself to pieces for so long that all the pieces inclined to fall have already fallen, and what remains is the irreducible armature, shuddering forever within its tolerances.

Between the structures: nothing. This is what surprises. You expect a drowned industrial site to be packed — barnacled, encrusted, full of hiding places. But the spaces between the stacks and the engine-houses and the great wheel-frames are *empty*. Vast warm corridors of water with nothing in them. Not even sediment hanging in suspension — the constant agitation keeps it moving through and away. You can swim for a long time between two pieces of running machinery and meet nothing at all, only the pressure of the sound and the slow drift of the warm currents.

Almost nothing lives here. Once, far down a corridor between two boiler-houses, something moves — a pale eel-shape, or perhaps a length of cable come loose and tumbling in the current; you cannot tell, and it does not appear again. Twice you see, on the side of a pipe, a soft button of something that might be a sponge or might be a mineral concretion grown in the shape of a sponge. That is the ecology. That is all of it.

The works themselves are not large. This is the strangest thing, and you only understand it slowly. The whole drowned city is perhaps the size of a single foundry yard — you could walk its perimeter in twenty minutes, if you could walk. It is a *pocket*. A held thing. The amber light fades at its edges into a darker water that you sense, without testing, you should not enter. The city is small and the city is complete and the city is loud beyond any reasonable measure of loudness, all of its industrial fury concentrated into a space that should not be able to contain it.

What is it making?

You look for the product and cannot find one. No conveyors leading away. No stockpiles. No outflow pipe carrying anything refined anywhere. The engines drive the pumps and the pumps move the water and the water turns the wheels and the wheels drive the engines. It is a closed loop of labor. It has been working on itself, for itself, since before anyone you could ask was born. The hammers fall and the bubbles rise and the warm water sluices through the cooling jackets of machines that no longer cool anything because they no longer need to, because the work they do is the work of continuing to do work.

The rules of this place, as best as can be inferred:

Nothing here decays past its established form. The rust has stopped. The bolts have stopped loosening. The bearings will wear no further than they have already worn. Whatever process brought this city to its current weathered state completed itself long ago, and now the city exists in a kind of stopped-time of material — vibrating violently, running hot, but not aging.

Nothing living thrives. Whether because of the heat, the noise, the metals in the water, or some other exclusion — the place has refused biology. The two sponge-things on the pipe are exceptions, or they are not sponges.

The sound is not optional. There is no quiet pocket within the works. Even in the empty corridors between structures, the water carries the full hammering weight of the engines, and you understand after a while that the sound is not a side effect of the city but is *what the city is for*. The machines exist to produce the sound. The sound fills the pocket of warm water. The pocket of warm water holds the sound. This is the cycle. This is the product.

You leave by rising, and the noise follows you up through the amber water for a long time, fading not in volume but in coherence, becoming again a pressure in the chest, then a vibration in the teeth, then a memory of a vibration, then — at some unmarked depth — gone, replaced by the ordinary silence of an ordinary warm sea, and you cannot, looking down, see any sign at all of what is down there still working.
