# The Slow Foundry

It begins, if it begins anywhere, with the floor.

The floor is the world. It extends in every direction past where the eye gives up — a vast, gently undulating plain of pale ceramic, the color of old bone warmed by long afternoons. Not tile, not stone: a single fused surface, seamless, as though some patient kiln-god had glazed an entire continent in one firing. You can walk it for a day and find no break. You can walk it for a week and find only the faintest cambers, like the swell of a sleeping animal's flank.

It is hot. The ceramic holds the heat and gives it back slowly, so that the air shimmers above the plain in long, lazy distortions. There is no sun visible — the light comes from a sky the color of weak tea, even and high, as if filtered through some upper membrane the world keeps to itself. Shadows are soft and noncommittal. Things do not glare; they glow faintly from within their own warmth.

Now: the floor is not finished. This is the central fact.

Across its expanse, at long intervals — a mile here, three miles there — the ceramic is *becoming*. Low domes push up from the surface, smooth as risen dough, with hairline seams running their circumferences where the material is still annealing itself. Some are knee-high. Some are the size of hills. They are warm to the touch and very faintly humming, a sub-audible note you feel in the sternum more than hear. Around the largest, the air ripples visibly, and a fine white powder — call it kiln-bloom — settles on the plain in pale rings, marking the patient outward growth of each formation.

No one made this. Or rather: the making is the place. There is no builder visible, no machine, no tool. The world constructs itself out of itself, the way a shell accretes around a mollusk that has long since departed. What the mollusk was, here, is anyone's guess.

Water arrives in the form of *sweats*. At unpredictable hours the domes begin to bead, and the beads gather into rills, and the rills find the long, almost-imperceptible slopes of the plain and travel. A trickle can run for miles across the warm ceramic before it gives up to evaporation. Where two trickles meet they make a brief gloss, a wet darkening that lingers an hour and then is gone. After a sweat, the air carries a mineral smell — something between wet chalk and the inside of a clean pot.

Once or twice a day — though *day* is a loose word here — a low fog gathers in the middle distance and resolves into rain. It is warm rain, scarce, falling in widely spaced drops the size of grapes that strike the ceramic with audible *toks*. This is one of the few real sounds the world makes. The other is the wind, which is gentle and constant and moves nothing because there is almost nothing to move.

The vegetation: at long intervals, out of the seams between the older fused plates, something has worked its way up. Not plants exactly — *fronds* of a pale jade material that is half mineral, half something softer, growing in small clusters like grasses that have forgotten how to be many. They are sparse enough that you might walk an hour and pass three. They lean in the direction of the last sweat. When the rain comes, they unfurl slightly, drink, and re-curl. They make no sound. They do not seed; they do not, as far as anyone could tell, propagate at all. They are simply *here*, in their scatterings, as though the world allotted itself a precise and meager quota of living things and then stopped.

There are no animals. Nothing scuttles, nothing flies, nothing burrows — there is, after all, nothing to burrow into. The plain has no soil. The fronds have no pollinators. The water has no fish. And yet the world is not still: it is one of the most *active* places imaginable, only its activity runs at the pace of geology speeded up just enough to be witnessed. A dome that was knee-high at first light is hip-high by evening. A seam that ran in a straight line yesterday has acquired a small kink overnight, accommodating some pressure from below. The kiln-bloom drifts. The sweats come and go. The slow weather assembles and disassembles itself across distances large enough to have their own climates — a dry quarter where no rain has fallen in what might be a season, a humid quarter where the domes glisten constantly and the fronds are thickest.

The rules, as far as they can be inferred:

Nothing here erodes. Wind does not abrade the ceramic; water does not cut it; the fronds do not crack their hosts. The world is in a phase before weathering — pre-erosional, embryonic. Everything is still arriving. The domes are not ruins; they are the early shapes of something not yet named. Walk far enough and you may find a region where the domes have begun to *connect*, low arches forming between them, the first suggestion of architecture — but even there, the seams are fresh and the kiln-bloom still settles, and you understand that you are seeing not a built thing but a built thing in its infancy, a city in the kiln-stage of its life, perhaps a thousand years from being habitable, perhaps a million.

There is no machinery. There is no fire visible. The heat comes from below, from the floor itself, which is the body of whatever is making the world, and the making is patient, and the making is the only event.

If you stand still and listen — really listen, past the wind and the occasional *tok* of a raindrop — you can sometimes hear the domes. A faint *tick*, infrequent, irregular, as the ceramic adjusts to its own growth. It is the sound of a kettle cooling, except the kettle is a continent and it is not cooling, it is warming, slowly, into a shape it has not yet decided on.

You could live a long time in such a place, if you could live in it at all, and never see it finish a single sentence.
