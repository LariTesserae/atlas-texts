# The Quiet Town in the Crown

It sits in the saddle between two new peaks — peaks so young the stone still shows the conchoidal fractures where it parted from something else, glass-edged, the color of wet liver where it isn't the color of bone. No rivers have learned these slopes yet. No rain falls here, or hasn't in long enough that the idea of rain is theoretical. Yet everything is green.

The greenness comes from the **bladderwort trees**, which is the wrong name for them but the only one that fits. They are not really trees. They are vertical colonies of pulpy, jade-translucent sacs stacked one upon another like grapes the size of skulls, each sac drawing moisture directly from the warm air and from the breath of every other living thing. They grow shoulder to shoulder, trunk pressed to trunk, in such density that to walk between them you must turn sideways and ease through, and the sacs deform around you with a slow vegetal patience and reseal behind you without sound. Their leaves — if leaves — are flat green tongues that overlap into a continuous canopy about three times a person's height overhead. Light comes through that canopy already filtered, already softened, the color of a held-up grape held to a candle.

Under the canopy, the town.

It is not large. You could walk its longest axis in the time it takes to recite a long poem. Perhaps forty dwellings, perhaps sixty — the count keeps shifting because the dwellings are *grown*, not built, and some are still becoming and some are quietly being reabsorbed. Each house is a hollowed cluster of the same bladder-sacs, swollen larger and toughened into something like leather, with floors of pressed root-mat and ceilings that glow faintly from within when warmed by bodies. The houses stack. They lean into one another. They share walls the way cells in a tissue share walls. Walkways are not built but *encouraged* — the inhabitants press their hands against certain places on certain trunks for years, and a path eventually develops there, soft underfoot, the green parting where they have asked it to part.

The inhabitants — I see them now, slowly — are small and many-limbed and patient. Six limbs, I think, though they fold most of them away most of the time. Skin like the inside of a peach. They do not speak, or they speak so seldom and so quietly that I can sit a long while and hear nothing. What they do instead is touch. They lay one palm against a neighbor's shoulder and leave it there for the duration of what might be a long thought. They press foreheads to the trunk-sacs and seem to receive something. There are perhaps two hundred of them in the whole town, and they all know each other in the deep way that long-lived neighbors know each other in places where nothing arrives and nothing leaves.

The fauna. I had not noticed at first because the fauna are part of the quiet. There are small bright things in the canopy — not birds, no birds here, no wings — they are climbing creatures with grasping tails and large eyes and they move through the bladder-trees by pulling themselves from sac to sac. They are everywhere. Look up: there are dozens within sight, motionless, watching, or grooming each other, or pressing their mouths to the sacs to sip whatever the sacs sweat. Look down: at ankle-level, soft pale things shaped like long hands move across the root-mat, eating fallen tongues of leaf. In the wall of every house, small symbionts live in pockets and emerge at intervals to do small unclear work. The town is dense with non-human attention. You are never alone in a room. You are never alone *in your skin* — there are mites in the peach-skin of the people, and the mites are part of the people, and the people know this.

The rules, as best I can read them:

- **Nothing moves quickly.** A gesture takes a long breath. A meal takes an afternoon. A house takes a generation to grow and a generation to unmake. Speed is a thing that has not been invented here, because nothing requires it.
- **No water pools.** Moisture exists only inside things — inside the sacs, inside the bodies, inside the warm thick air. To open a sac is a small sacrament; to spill one is a small grief.
- **Sound is currency.** Because the place is so quiet — because the canopy absorbs, because the sacs absorb, because the people themselves are quiet — any sound made carries a weight. A hum from a throat. The soft pop of a ripe sac releasing seed. The almost-inaudible chime that the climbing-creatures make at dusk, all of them at once, for perhaps three seconds, and then never again until tomorrow's dusk.
- **The peaks are still rising.** Not visibly. But the elders press their hands to certain stones at the edge of the town and feel, they say, the slow upward push. The town has been moving uphill, infinitesimally, for as long as anyone remembers. One day, perhaps, it will be too high and too cold, and the bladder-trees will not thrive, and the town will have to descend the other side. This is understood. This is not urgent.

There is one strangeness I have been circling without naming. The light. The light here does not come only from above. The sacs themselves, in the deeper layers of the canopy, hold a soft internal luminescence — not bright, not magical-seeming, just *present*, the way a person's cheek is warm. At what should be night, the town does not go dark. It dims to the color of a banked ember, and the climbing-creatures fold themselves into the sacs to sleep, and the people lie down on their root-mats, and the whole crown of the mountain continues to glow faintly green-gold against the black sky, a small held breath of life at high altitude, surrounded by the sharp dry new stone of a world that has not yet learned what it will become.

No one comes here. No one leaves. The town is whole.
