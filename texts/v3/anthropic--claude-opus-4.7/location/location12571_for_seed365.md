# The Hatchcity

It sits in a saddle between two young peaks, maybe nine thousand feet up, where the air thins and the cloud catches on the rocks in the mornings and drips off again before noon. The stone here is pale and sharp — not yet softened, edges still close to how they came out of the ground. You can cut your hand on the bedrock if you slip. Lichen has begun, in patches, but only just; the rock remembers being underground.

The settlement is small. You could walk its perimeter in under an hour. It was built — *is being built, still* — out of split cedar and a soft grey-green soapstone quarried from the slope above. Maybe forty structures, scattered with deliberate distance between them, connected by raised wooden walkways that lift the inhabitants a half-meter clear of the moss. The buildings are low, with steep roofs and small shuttered windows. There is no glass. There are no metals beyond a little hammered copper at the hinges and on the prayer-bells. Tools are bone, wood, stone, fired clay, and the copper. Someone is always whittling.

The light is the light of a long dusk that never fully commits. Even at noon the cloud-shadow and the canopy of the surrounding *flutterwood* — that's what they call the trees, though no one outside has named them — keep the town in a soft grey-gold gloaming. Lamps are rare and burn a resin that smells like crushed pine needles and old honey. People mostly move by feel and habit.

And then the fauna.

This is the thing about the Hatchcity. The town is built where it is because of the moths. Or the moth-things. They are the size of dinner plates to the size of cloaks, soft-bodied, slow-flying, with wings the color of wet bark and eyes that catch the lamplight in slow blinks. They live in the flutterwood canopy in numbers that defy estimate — thousands per tree, tens of thousands per grove — and the town exists in a continuous gentle storm of them. They land on the rooftops, on the walkways, on shoulders and hair. They do not bite. They breathe out a faint coolness; passing through a cloud of them is like walking into a cellar.

This is why the town is quiet. The moths cannot tolerate sharp sound. A struck nail, a raised voice, a dropped pot — and a section of the canopy lifts in alarm, and for an hour afterward the air is too thick with them to see across the lane. So the people speak in low voices and use mallets wrapped in felt and signal each other with small bells that have soft clay clappers, producing a sound more felt than heard. The loudest thing in the Hatchcity is the *intermittent* rain, when it comes — a brief patter on the cedar shingles, then gone, then condensation dripping for hours afterward from every leaf and eave.

The moths feed on the flutterwood, and the flutterwood feeds on the moths in turn — the trees have evolved a kind of slow carnivory, sticky pads on the underside of their lower leaves that catch the old and dying. There are also the *runners*: long-legged, deer-sized creatures with too many joints, that come down from the higher slopes to eat moths by the mouthful. They move through the town at twilight without fear of the people, who step aside for them. There are tunnel-things in the soft moss between buildings — you see their wakes, never them. There are birds the size of a thumbnail that ride on the moths' backs and pluck mites from their fur. There are luminous beetles in the rafters, dim enough that you only see them when you've sat still for a while.

The people themselves are sparse — perhaps two hundred — and you rarely see more than two or three at once. They wear layered grey wool and soft leather and carry small carved tokens that I think function as names or addresses or moods; they exchange them silently when they meet. Their craft is fine: whittled spoons with handles like running animals, clay cups so thin they're translucent, woven mats with patterns that seem to shift if you watch them sideways.

The strangeness of the place — and this is the part that's hardest to put down — is in the relationship between the town and the moths and the rock. The rock is young here. New. It came up recently, geologically speaking, and *something came up with it*. The moths are not native to anywhere; they appeared when the saddle did, perhaps a few centuries ago. The town was built to attend them. The people will tell you, if you sit long enough and ask softly, that the moths are dreaming the mountain into being. That every flutter of every wing is a small decision about what the rock should be. That if the town went silent enough, for long enough — truly silent, not just quiet — you might feel the mountain growing under your feet, deciding its shape.

It is, in fact, growing. Surveys, if surveys were possible here, would show the saddle rising by perhaps a finger's width each year. The walkways are rebuilt on a slow rotation to account for it. The soapstone quarry has had to be re-cut three times in living memory because the seam keeps lifting.

In the deep middle of the night, when even the runners have gone to sleep in the high meadows, and the moths settle and breathe their slow cool breath, the Hatchcity hums. Not audibly. Underneath audibility. A pressure in the chest. The mountain, listening to itself being made.

In the morning, the cloud lifts off the saddle like a slow exhalation, and the moths begin to move again, and someone in a low house begins whittling, and the day begins, and very little changes, and everything changes, slowly, upward, into more of what it already is.
