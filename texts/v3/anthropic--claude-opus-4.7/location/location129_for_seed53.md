# The Condensary

High up, where the air thins to almost nothing and the sky turns from blue to a hard, ringing indigo, there is a basin tucked between three peaks. You would not find it by walking — the ridges around it are too steep, and the only passages in are narrow chimneys of frost-shattered rock. But if you were lowered into it, or if you came on some errand that required passing through, this is what you would find.

The basin is small. You could cross it in the time it takes to say a long prayer. It is shaped like a shallow bowl of pale grey stone, and the stone is everywhere fluted and scalloped, as if something had been dissolving it very slowly for a very long time. In a sense, something has. The basin is a place where weather condenses.

Because it sits so high and so cold, and because the three peaks funnel air in particular ways, moisture from the valleys far below rises, reaches this altitude, and precipitates. Not as rain, usually. As beads. As films. As slow crystalline exhalations on the stone. The whole bowl is beaded with water the way a cold glass is beaded in a warm room, and the beads move — they gather, they run, they pool in the scallops, they evaporate again in the bright sunlight, they re-form on the shaded side. A droplet on one of the standing stones may take an hour to travel the length of your hand.

There is no vegetation. Nothing green has ever grown here; nothing green could. But the stone is not bare. The density of the place comes from its furniture: hundreds of slender upright forms, each about waist-high or shoulder-high, packed close together across the basin floor. They are made of the same pale rock as the basin, and they were shaped, originally, by human hands — you can see the tool-marks, the careful chiselling, the places where someone long ago smoothed a curve with a rasp. But the weather has been working on them for an age since then, and now each one is half-artifact, half-natural form, their original purpose half-legible. Some look like figures. Some look like narrow chimneys. Some look like nothing at all, just tall water-beaded presences, leaning a little, nearly touching their neighbors.

Between them, the light is extraordinary. At this altitude the sun is unfiltered and severe, and it strikes the beaded stone and scatters in every direction. The basin glitters. Walking through it (if you could walk through it — the stones are too close for easy passage; you would have to turn sideways, ease between them, let the cold wet stone brush your shoulders) you would move through continuous bright dazzle, water-light on every surface, your eyes watering from the brightness as much as from the thin air.

It is cold. Not killing cold, but the kind of cold that lives in your bones after a few minutes — the cold of stone that never warms, of water that is always just about to freeze and sometimes does, rimming a droplet with a thin lace of ice that melts again by noon.

It is quiet. The wind that you would expect at this elevation does not enter the basin; the peaks shoulder it aside. Inside, there is only the small sound of water: a tick as a droplet falls from one scalloped lip to another, a faint hiss where a film of moisture evaporates from a sunstruck face, the occasional soft collapse of a larger bead pulling itself free and running down a flute of stone. If you held still, you could hear the basin breathing — the slow inhalation of vapor from below, the slow exhalation of it into the dry sky above.

There are no animals. No birds pass overhead; the air is too thin. No insects. Nothing crawls in the beads. And yet the basin is not dead. It is, in its mineral way, extremely active. Water is constantly arriving and constantly leaving. The stones are being slowly re-sculpted, grain by grain, one droplet at a time. The scallops deepen. The figures thin. In a thousand years the basin will look different, though only a geologist would be able to say how.

Who made the stones? Someone, once, climbed here with hand tools — chisel, rasp, perhaps a small hammer — and spent what must have been years carving these upright forms and setting them close together in the bowl. There are no other traces of that person or those people. No shelter, no hearth, no path. Only the stones, and their weather-worn faces, and whatever meaning they held, which has been rinsed out of them by the long patient work of the condensate.

If you stayed long enough to watch the light move across the basin — and you would not stay long, because of the cold and the thin air — you would see the beads on the eastern stones evaporate as the sun reached them, and see the beads on the western stones thicken in their shade, and you would understand that the basin has a cycle, a day, a kind of slow pulse. The water moves around the bowl as the sun moves around the sky. At dusk, the whole place briefly frosts over, and in that moment the stones look like a congregation of figures dusted with salt. Then the stars come out — very many stars, very sharp — and the frost sublimates into the black air, and the basin is dry for a few hours, waiting, before the vapor rises again from the valleys and the beading resumes.

That is all there is to it. A small cold bright wet place at the top of the world, full of stones someone once cared about, worked on now only by the weather, glittering in the hard sun.
