# The Resonant Flats

The first thing is the sound, because it arrives before you've finished seeing.

It is a low industrial roar, continuous, layered — the harmonic of immense machinery running somewhere very close and also somewhere very far. Pistons, perhaps. Bellows. The breath of turbines that have been breathing for so long the air around them has forgotten how to be still. And yet — there are no factories. No structures. No chimneys against the warm pale sky. Just the sound, coming up out of the ground itself.

The terrain is a wide shallow basin of fawn-colored stone, gently undulating, the way a sheet of cooling metal undulates. It extends past sight in every direction — not quite flat, not quite hill country — a regional sprawl of low rises and shallower depressions, weathered to softness. Erosion here is old work, finished work. Everything has reached the shape it was always going to reach. The edges of things are rounded the way river-stones are rounded, but there is no river, only the long memory of one.

The stone is warm to the touch. Not hot — warm like a body, like something living that has been resting in the sun, except the warmth comes from below. Press a palm against it and you feel the faint vibration of the sound translated into the bone. The machine-roar is in the rock.

Water appears in pockets. Shallow oval pools collect in the lowest scoops of the basin, twenty meters across, three deep, perfectly still on the surface but trembling underneath with the same resonance that fills everything. Their water is mineral-clear and slightly warm. Around their edges: rings of pale crust, salt or something like it, where condensation has wicked up and dried a hundred million times. The air carries a damp that never becomes rain. Sometimes a fog lifts off a pool and travels across the flats in a slow coherent shape before evaporating again. The water in the pools has been the same water, more or less, since whatever happened here happened.

Plant life: almost none. In the lee of certain stones, where the damp lingers, dark patches grow — not moss, not lichen, something in between, something that might be a colony of slow chemistry rather than a colony of cells. They have the texture of soot pressed into felt. They do not spread. They have been the same patches for a long, long time.

Fauna is rare and strange. Once or twice in a day's walking you might see something — a pale articulated thing the size of a cat, picking its way across the stone on too many legs, moving with the deliberation of something that has nowhere to be and all the time to get there. It does not flee. It does not approach. It seems to be listening. To what, in all this sound, is unclear — perhaps to a frequency inside the larger noise that only it can hear. They drink from the pools and they do not seem to eat.

The light is ordinary. This is one of the unsettling things. You expect a place like this to be lit by some uncanny glow, an aurora, a green sky — but no, the sun is a sun, the sky is the pale warm blue of a summer afternoon, and the shadows fall where shadows ought to fall. The strangeness is not in the light. The strangeness is in the fact that nothing is changing.

Nothing is changing. This is the rule of the world. The sound does not rise or fall. The fog rises off the pools at no particular interval — or rather, it always has been and always will be, and the question of *when* does not apply. The articulated creatures are always at their stations, or always between them, which amounts to the same thing. The warmth of the stone holds at exactly the temperature of a resting hand. Whatever industry runs beneath the basin runs at perfect equilibrium with itself, producing nothing, consuming nothing visible, simply *running*, the way a held note runs, the way a planet runs around a star.

There are no structures because the structures are underneath, and they are so old and so settled that the surface has forgotten them, healed over them like skin over an old implant. You could dig, perhaps. You could try. The stone is warm and the stone is humming and the stone does not particularly want to be dug.

What is being made down there? Nothing, anymore. Or: the same thing, continuously, which is the steady state itself. The machinery is not producing the steady state — the machinery *is* the steady state, and the steady state is the machinery, and to ask what it's for is to ask what the warmth of a body is for in a body that has been alive so long it has forgotten any other condition.

Stand in the middle of the basin at what would be afternoon if afternoon meant anything here. The horizon is a low soft line. A pool gleams two hundred meters east. A patch of the felted not-moss is at your foot. Somewhere in the middle distance, one of the pale creatures has paused, head — if it is a head — slightly raised. The sound goes on. The warmth goes on. The damp clings to your skin without ever quite condensing into anything.

You realize, slowly, that you have been here for some time. You realize, slowly, that *some time* is the only length of time this place admits. The light has not moved. Your shadow is exactly where it was. The pale creature has not lowered its head, and may never have raised it; you may have been mistaken; the question does not seem urgent.

The flats hold you. They are not hostile and they are not welcoming. They are simply finished, in the way a thing can be finished — having arrived, long ago, at what it is, and having since then only continued.
