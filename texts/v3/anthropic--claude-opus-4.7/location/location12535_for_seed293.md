# The Soft City of Wet-Root

You come on it through the leaf-tunnels: hours of warm green corridor, sap-air, the floor sucking quietly at your feet, until the corridor opens and you see it.

A town. Maybe a town. The argument starts there and never really resolves.

It sits in a basin perhaps a kilometer across, no more — you could walk its edge in an afternoon, if the ground stayed where you left it. The basin floor is so old that calling it ground feels imprecise; it is the worn-down nub of something that was once mountains, ground patient by water until only the gentlest dish remains, and into that dish a forest has grown so thoroughly that the forest *is* the dish now. There is no soil that isn't root. There is no rock that isn't moss-skinned. Standing water lies everywhere in palm-shallow sheets, mirror-bright, threaded between hummocks, and the air over it shivers with the steam of warm afternoons that never quite end.

The buildings are grown.

I want to be careful here. Not grown like the romantic idea of a treehouse, branches trained over generations. Grown the way a coral is grown, or the way a wasp's paper nest is grown — purposeful tissue, laid down by something that knew what it was doing, in shapes that answer to function rather than to the tree's own preferences. The walls are a dense vegetable composite, pale jade where it is young and tobacco-brown where it has aged, with a grain like muscle. Domes. Half-tubes. Bridges with the catenary curve of a hung rope, but rigid, and breathing. When you put your hand against a wall it is warm, slightly damp, and you can feel a slow throb deeper in than your palm.

The technology is in the *patterning*. Whoever taught the plants to do this — and there is no one here now to ask, or rather there is no one *visible*; the teaching is finished, the lesson is the place — knew things about growth that current botany does not contain. Veins in the wall-tissue thicken and thin to manage load. Pores open and close in response to humidity in ways no stomata should manage at that speed. In the rib of one arch I watched a fracture form, weep a clear gel, and close, in the time it took to count to forty. The whole town is doing this constantly. The whole town is healing itself constantly because the whole town is constantly breaking. That's the turbulence: not weather, but the building-mass in perpetual minor crisis and perpetual minor repair, a soft riot of self-maintenance.

Sound, then. It is loud here but not in any way that the word usually means. There are no engines. No voices, or none I could identify as voices. What there is: the wet click and pop of cellulose under strain, thousands of small mouths of plant-flesh; a constant low gargle of water moving through internal channels in the walls (the buildings *plumb themselves*, drinking from the basin and venting somewhere up); the sigh of large pores opening on the dome-tops to release steam in plumes you can see from anywhere in the basin; and over all of it a kind of chorus of frogs — except they aren't frogs, they're something with a frog's throat and a cricket's patience, and they sing in long sustained chords that shift key every few minutes. When the chord changes, the buildings flex. I am not sure which is causing which.

Animal life is abundant but stays in the seams. Things like soft pale crabs work the standing water, eating the constant rain of dropped tissue from the walls — the town sheds, and they clean. In the canopy-roofs there are bright tube-bodied creatures, somewhere between hummingbird and eel, that drink directly from the wall-pores; the walls seem to expect them, the way a flower expects a bee. Larger animals, deer-shaped but with the long slow legs of waders, browse the avenues. They are not afraid. Nothing here is afraid, because there has been nothing to fear for a very long time, and the memory of fear has gone out of the bloodline.

The density is the strange thing. For a town, it is empty. You walk a long curve of avenue between two clusters of dwelling and there is only the avenue, the standing water on either side, a single heron-thing fishing, and one of the great domes at the far end half-veiled in its own steam. The buildings cluster, then disperse, then cluster again, like islands in an archipelago. Whoever lived here did not need to be close to one another, or they were close in some other dimension than distance. The empty stretches are not abandoned — they are maintained, the water clear, the paths firm — they are simply *part of the town*, the way silence is part of music.

And there is no one. No one I could find. I want to say that twice because the absence is the loudest feature of the place and I keep, in writing this, forgetting to mention it. The doorways stand open. The bowls in the dome-floors hold clean water. The bridges keep themselves taut. The chord shifts. The crabs work. The deer-things browse the avenues. The whole town goes on being itself, and has been going on being itself, you slowly realize, for a length of time that the worn-flat basin is the only honest measure of — long enough to grind mountains down to a dish.

Whether the inhabitants left, or died, or became the walls, or became the chord, or were never separate from the place to begin with and what you've been calling absence is actually a form of their presence you haven't learned to read yet — I couldn't tell you. The town doesn't seem to miss them. The town, if I had to guess, doesn't know they're gone.

Toward evening — and evening comes gently, the light staying warm and level for a long sloping hour — the steam plumes thicken and merge over the basin into a low ceiling of mist, lit pink from beneath by something (bioluminescence in the water? in the walls?), and the chord drops a full octave, and every wall in the town exhales at once, a sound like a held breath let out by a person who has been waiting, patiently, for you to arrive.

I left before I found out what came next. I am not certain I left.
