# The Cistern-Choir

It is a chamber, and the chamber is a machine, and the machine has been alive long enough that no one currently inside it remembers when it was built or who built it. You could fit perhaps forty people in here shoulder to shoulder, though no one would ever do that — there is no room for people. The room is for the water and the singers.

The walls are a ceramic that was never fired in any kiln. They were grown, molecule by molecule, into a lattice that conducts sound the way copper conducts current. You can see this if you look closely in the dim amber light: faint geometric striations, like the grain of a wood that grew in spirals. The walls weep. They have always wept. Condensation forms on them not from temperature differential but because the lattice itself pulls moisture from the warm dense air and exhales it again in slow rhythmic pulses — a breathing rock. Where the water runs down, it has cut nothing, because the ceramic does not erode in any normal sense; instead, over centuries, the pathways of the water have become slightly polished, slightly indented, the lattice rearranging itself by some patient nanometric logic to accommodate the flow. The walls remember every drop that has ever fallen.

The floor is a shallow basin, ankle-deep, warm as blood. Here and there, low ridges of the same ceramic rise from the water like the backs of sleeping animals, and from these ridges grow the only vegetation: thick cushions of a moss-analogue, dark green going to black at the tips, that thrives on the mineral exhalations of the wall-lattice and nothing else. The moss is sparse — it grows only where the acoustics are dead, in the few nodal points of the chamber where the sound cancels itself. You can find the silent spots by following the moss.

Because everywhere else, there is the sound.

The fauna are small. They are everywhere. Imagine something between a tree frog and a struck bell — palm-sized creatures with translucent throats and resonating bone-plates along their spines, clinging to the walls in their thousands, layered three and four deep in places, near-touching. They were not designed but they were not exactly evolved either; they are the descendants of something that was seeded here, and they have spent uncountable generations tuning themselves to the chamber. Each one sings a single sustained tone. Together they produce a chord so dense and so loud that to stand in the chamber unprotected would liquefy the soft tissues of an unprepared visitor within minutes. The sound is not noise. It is structure. It is the chord of the cistern, and it has shifted only by quarter-tones over the centuries, as individual singers die and are replaced and the colony retunes itself around the loss.

The chord does work. This is the function of the chamber, though "function" is a word that has come unmoored from "purpose" here. The standing waves of the singers' song drive the lattice of the walls into vibrational states that perform something — a computation, a synthesis, a prayer, a refinement of the water itself — that the original builders understood and that no longer needs to be understood by anyone, because the system maintains itself. The water that leaves the basin (through a single drain whose existence you must take on faith, as it is somewhere beneath the surface) is changed. It carries information. It carries something. It goes elsewhere, deeper into the mountain that contains this chamber, and it does what it does.

The light comes from the singers themselves. Their throat-sacs glow faintly amber when they are sounding, brighter at the peak of each long breath, dimmer at the trough, and because there are thousands of them slightly out of phase, the chamber pulses with a soft uneven luminescence like the inside of a heart. You can see well enough to move. You cannot see well enough to read.

The air is warm and so saturated that breathing it feels like drinking. Steam curls along the ceiling, which is domed and lower than you would expect — you cannot fully stand upright at the edges. The dome is studded with the largest singers, the old ones, fist-sized, their tones the deep foundation notes; the smaller ones cluster lower, where the harmonics live. There is a hierarchy of pitch by elevation and the colony enforces it somehow. Predators exist — a longer, paler creature, eel-shaped, that moves through the basin water and takes singers who fall — and when one strikes, the chord shivers for a moment as the colony adjusts. It is an ecosystem and it is also an instrument and it is also, perhaps, a mind.

The chamber is buried high in a range of mountains that the surface world does not have a name for, because the surface world does not know it is there. Above, somewhere, there is weather and there is wind. Down here there is only the chord, the breath of the walls, the slow rearrangement of the basin's polished channels, and the amber pulse of ten thousand small bright throats doing the work that the builders, whoever they were, set in motion and walked away from, trusting that warmth and water and the patience of small creatures would be enough.

It has been enough. It is enough. It will be enough for a long time yet.
