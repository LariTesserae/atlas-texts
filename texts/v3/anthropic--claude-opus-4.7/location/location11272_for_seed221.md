# The Condensary

You find it tucked inside the flank of a mountain that does not yet know it is a mountain — the rock here is still deciding, still being poured, still wet from whatever furnace lifted it. But that is outside. You will not see the outside.

Inside is a chamber the size of a modest cathedral nave, though you cannot tell this at first because the air is full. Full of bodies. Full of brass. Full of cold steam that does not rise but hangs in layers like silt in still water.

The walls are riveted plate. Copper at the bottom courses, then a band of something darker — lead, perhaps, or a tin alloy that has gone the color of a bruise — and then, high above, brass louvers angled inward, weeping. Always weeping. Condensation rolls down the louvers in fat slow beads, joins the sheets running down the lead, pools in the copper troughs that ring the room at knee height. The water never leaves. There are no drains. The troughs feed pipes that climb the walls and return as mist through small ceramic nozzles set in the ceiling, and the mist condenses on the louvers, and the louvers weep, and the troughs fill. A closed loop. It has been running for a long time. It will run for a long time more.

The temperature is just above freezing. Your breath would show, if you were breathing here, but you are only looking.

The floor is a grating of black iron, and below the grating, three or four feet down, more troughs, more pipes, and the slow churn of something paddle-driven — an engine somewhere is turning, has always been turning, with the patient unhurried rhythm of a thing that does not need to finish. You can hear it through the soles of your boots more than through your ears, because your ears are already full.

The fauna.

Every horizontal surface, every pipe, every rivet-head, every louver-edge, is covered in frogs. Not frogs exactly — the body plan is froggish but the skin is the wet grey of the lead courses, and their eyes are the milky color of the condensate, and they have too many fingers, and the fingers have small brass rings at the joints which may be jewelry or may be hardware, grown in. They are stacked. They sit on each other's backs three and four deep where the pipes are warmest, which is to say least cold. Between them, smaller things: a kind of pale isopod that grazes the algal slick on the copper, and threadlike worms in the troughs, and on the ceiling — clinging upside down to the louvers, sipping the condensation as it forms — a population of moth-like creatures with wings the texture of wet paper, who never fly.

The sound is the frogs. All of them, calling. Not in chorus but in saturation — every frog calling at its own pace, a wet shouting clatter that fills the chamber edge to edge with no gap in it, no rhythm you can hold onto, only the unbroken sheet of it, so loud that the louvers vibrate and shed their beads in time, so loud that the mist in the air seems to be shaped by it, made dense by it, as if sound itself were one of the gases being condensed and recycled here.

Vegetation: almost none. A few mats of something between moss and algae on the wettest copper, dark green going to black, and in one corner a single fern — improbable, scrawny, the only thing in the room that looks like it was not made — growing out of a seam where two plates were riveted imperfectly. The frogs avoid the fern. It is the only place they do not sit.

The light is dim and comes from four sources, which are gas mantles inside thick glass housings set into the walls at the cardinal points, burning some pale fuel that gives a greenish cast to everything. The mantles never flicker. The fuel lines run somewhere out of sight. The light catches on the wet brass and makes the chamber seem, for a moment, larger than it is, full of glints and reflections that double and triple the frogs until the room is nothing but eyes.

And here is the strangeness of the place, the thing that takes a while to notice:

Nothing changes.

The frogs do not move. They call, ceaselessly, but they do not hop, do not shift, do not eat. The isopods graze but seem never to progress along the copper. The water cycles but the level in the troughs is always exactly the same. The engine turns but nothing is being produced, nothing is being consumed, no waste leaves, no fuel enters that you can see. The fern does not grow. The mantles do not dim. The condensation forms and falls and forms again and the total mass of water in the room is, you understand without being told, a constant that has been a constant since before there was anyone to measure it.

The mountain outside is still being made. The rock is still cooling. The land is raw and embryonic, a place where erosion has not yet begun its long argument with stone.

But in here, inside this small made room embedded in the unfinished world, equilibrium has already been achieved. Someone built this place — riveted the plates, set the louvers, stocked the frogs, lit the mantles, started the engine — and then the place locked. It found its loop. It will hold its loop while the mountain finishes rising around it, while the mountain weathers down again, while everything outside passes through its slow geologic verbs.

The frogs will call. The water will weep. The engine will turn.

You should go. The cold is the kind that gets into the small bones of the hand, and the sound, after a while, begins to feel like it is calling something out of you and adding it to the loop, and you do not want to find out what.
