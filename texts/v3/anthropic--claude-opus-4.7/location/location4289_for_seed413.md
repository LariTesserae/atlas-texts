# The Condensery

Come down the shaft. There is only one way in, and it is down.

The first thing is the sound. Before your eyes adjust, before your skin registers the wet heat, the sound has already entered you — a low sustained roar that is not any single machine but the summed voice of every machine, pumped through stone, standing in the air like a physical medium. You speak and your voice does not carry past your teeth. This is the permanent condition. It has been this sound for a very long time.

The chamber is not large. That is the strange thing. You expected, from the noise, a cathedral — some vast industrial nave. Instead it is a room. Perhaps two hundred paces across, vaulted, the ceiling lost in steam. A pocket. The whole works is a pocket, tucked into the flank of a mountain at altitude, and the mountain around it has been worn almost flat by ages beyond counting, so that from outside — if anyone ever went outside — you would see only a gentle rise of smoothed rock with a single dark mouth in it, exhaling.

Inside: the Condensery.

Brass and black iron, rivet-knuckled, valve-crowned. Pipes run in bundles along the walls and overhead, crossing and recrossing, stacked four and five deep, every pipe weeping. The weeping is constant. Warm water beads on every surface, gathers, falls, beads again. The floor is a shallow lake, ankle-deep, interrupted by grates and catchments and the plinths of the engines. Walk and the water is warm as a held breath.

There are no plants. There have never been plants here, or if there were, they are so far gone that not even the idea of them remains in the stone. No moss on the pipes. No slime in the water. The warmth and wet should teem — every instinct says so — but nothing grows. The water is too pure, or too something. It only condenses and falls and is pumped and condenses again.

The engines: arranged in rows, close-set, near-touching. You move between them sideways. Each is perhaps the height of two people, squat, domed, with glass gauges whose needles do not move. They have not moved in a period of time for which "centuries" is the wrong unit. The needles rest at their working positions. The pressures hold. The temperatures hold. The whole system found its equilibrium so long ago that equilibrium is no longer the right word — this is simply what the place *is*, the way a stone is stone.

Light comes from the gauge-glass itself, a dim amber from within, and from scattered filament bulbs in iron cages along the walls, each bulb burning at a steadiness no filament should manage. The light does not reach the ceiling. The ceiling is only suggested, by the way the steam rolls back on itself up there. Everything below waist-height is lit by reflection off the standing water — a shifting amber skin that is the only thing in the room that could be called *moving*, and even it moves only because the drips disturb it, and the drips are metronomic.

Walk the aisles. The engines have names stamped into their brass plates but the names are in no alphabet you know, or perhaps they are in every alphabet at once, the letters having slumped toward each other over the long settling. The rivets are perfect. Nothing has rusted. The water, for all its constancy, does not corrode. This is one of the rules here.

Another rule: the sound does not vary. Put your ear to a pipe — the sound is the same. Walk to the far wall — the same. Climb (there are catwalks, narrow, iron) up toward the steam ceiling — the same. The roar is not produced by the machines. The machines are producing the roar's *body*, but the roar itself is ambient, foundational, the note the pocket sings.

Another rule: no one tends it. There are no walkways worn by feet, no tools left out, no lamps carried. The catwalks are there because catwalks are part of what a Condensery is, not because anyone walks them. If there were ever operators, they completed their work and left, and the work was to bring the system to this state and then withdraw.

What does it do? It condenses. Warm air rises to the hidden vault, meets something up there — a cold plate, a field, a principle — and comes down as water, into the catchments, through the pumps, into the boilers, out as steam, up to the vault. A closed loop. The loop has been closed for so long that the water in it is the same water, cycled past uncountable times, and has taken on a quality — you notice it now on your skin — of being *familiar*, as if you have been wet with this particular water before, in a dream, in a previous self.

The weirdness of the place is small but insistent: your breath does not fog, though it should. Your reflection in the standing water arrives a half-beat late. The gauges, if you stare long enough, are all reading *the same value*, regardless of what they measure — pressure, temperature, flow — a single number held in common, as if the machines long ago agreed on one truth and now merely display it in their different dialects.

You could stay. That is the temptation. The warmth is gentle, the sound is total and therefore peaceful in the way that total things are peaceful, and nothing will change. Nothing at all. A day here and a thousand years here are the same day. The drip from pipe seventeen will fall at the same interval it fell when the mountain above was still a mountain. You will leave no mark. The water will not remember you.

Go back up the shaft. The roar follows you a long way, thinning, becoming first a hum and then a pressure in the bones and then, at last, at the mouth of the shaft, merely a memory you are not sure you can trust.

Behind you, below you, the Condensery continues. It was never waiting for you. It is not waiting now.

It is only, and always, condensing.
