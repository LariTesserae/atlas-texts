There is a city in a basin of pale mineral. No water has ever fallen here, and yet the city is alive.

The basin itself is shallow, a wide cool dish of something between chalk and bone, scored with the sharp facets of recent fracture. The rock is young — you can see where it parted, where crystal faces still catch what little light there is and have not yet been rounded by any weather. There is weather, but it is not the kind that wears stone. It is the kind that wears patience.

The city is small. You could walk from one edge to the other in an afternoon, if afternoons existed here. It occupies perhaps the area of a large monastery or a modest hill-town, and like such places it is built in layers — courses laid over courses, terraces stepped against the gentle inward slope of the basin. The buildings are made from the mineral of the basin itself, quarried and stacked, but also from a second material that is harder to name: a pale, fibrous substance that looks woven and feels like dried tendon, sheathing beams and lining doorways and stretching in taut canopies between roofs. This is the work of the sparse vegetation. The plants here are spinners. They are few — one every dozen meters perhaps, rooted in the mineral seams — and they grow slowly, extruding long pale threads that the city's makers (long gone, or perhaps never quite present) have harvested and rewoven into architecture.

The plants are still spinning. That is part of what makes the place dynamic. Over the course of what you might call a day, the threads lengthen visibly; over the course of a week, a new web bridges an alley; over a month, a doorway is sealed by accident and must be cut open again, except that no one is cutting. The city is being slowly upholstered by its own flora. In the older quarters near the basin's center, whole streets are now soft tunnels of layered fiber, the original stonework only a memory beneath fifty years of patient spinning.

The temperature is cool — the cool of a cellar, or of a high desert before dawn. The light is dim and comes from no obvious source. The sky, if you can call it that, is a low even ceiling of something the color of weak tea, and it brightens and darkens on a slow rhythm that does not correspond to any sun. During the brighter phase, the mineral floor of the basin glows very faintly from within, as if the rock itself were the source and the ceiling only a reflection. During the darker phase, the spinning plants luminesce at their tips — small green points like the ends of slow cigarettes — and the threads carry the glow a little way along their length before it fades.

There is almost no sound. The spinners are silent. The buildings settle without complaint. Occasionally a fiber under tension parts with a single soft note, like a harp string touched once and damped immediately, and that note can be the only event for an hour. When the fauna pass through, they pass quietly too: long-limbed things, rare, perhaps one every few days, that step carefully among the threads without breaking them. They have too many joints and they are pale like everything else and they seem to be grazing, though on what is unclear — perhaps on the older fibers, perhaps on something microscopic that lives in the fibers. They do not look at you. There is some question whether they could.

The density is the strangest thing. For a built place — and it is unmistakably built, with lintels and stairs and the geometry of intention — it is almost empty. You can stand in a plaza and see no other structure for what seems too long a distance, then turn a corner and find a dense knot of small rooms stacked six high. The city is not uniformly distributed. It clusters and disperses. Between the clusters are long pale avenues with nothing in them but the occasional spinner, working.

The rules of the place seem to be these: nothing here drinks. The spinners draw what they need from the mineral directly, by some dry chemistry, and the fauna draw what they need from the spinners, and the city — which was perhaps once inhabited by something that drew what it needed from the fauna, or from the idea of the fauna — is now simply the stage on which this slow dry economy continues. Erosion is minimal because there is no water and almost no wind; what changes the city is growth, not decay. The threads accumulate. The plazas narrow. In ten thousand years the basin will be a solid pale loaf of woven fiber with stone bones inside it, and the spinners will still be spinning at its surface, and the long-limbed grazers will still be stepping carefully through what passageways remain, and the light will still rise and fall on its own schedule, and there will still be no sound to speak of, only occasionally a single note as something under tension lets go.

It is not a ruin. Ruins imply that something has ended. This is a city that is being slowly, quietly, patiently finished — by its plants, on a timescale that has nothing to do with anyone who might once have lived here. You get the sense, walking through it, that you have arrived neither early nor late but simply at some arbitrary point in a process that does not require you, and that the kindest thing you can do is to walk softly and not break any threads.
