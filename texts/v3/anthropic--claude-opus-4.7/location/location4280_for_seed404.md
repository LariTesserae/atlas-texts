There is a cleft in the side of a mountain that should not exist yet. The rock around it is too sharp: feldspar crystals still in their first geometry, edges that cut if you test them, a mineral smell like struck flint held close to the face. The mountain is high enough that the air thins, but inside the cleft the air is thick, warm, wet — a held breath at body temperature, as though the stone has lungs and is exhaling slowly into this one pocket of itself.

The cleft opens into a chamber no more than twelve paces across. Call it a room. It has the intimacy of a room. Light comes down through a fissure overhead — ordinary daylight, the kind that falls on kitchens — but it arrives here already having forgotten some of what it was. It pools rather than beams. It rests on surfaces like warm oil. You can hold your hand into the shaft and feel the light has a slight weight, the weight of a moth settling.

Water is everywhere without ever quite falling. The walls weep. Ferns and small thick-leaved plants crowd every ledge, their fronds overlapping so that there is no clean surface anywhere — everything is layered, mossed, doubled, tripled. A liverwort grows on the back of a fern which grows in the armpit of a sapling whose roots grip a cushion of sphagnum that hangs from nothing. Orchids the size of a fingernail open and close on a timescale you can almost see if you hold still. The density is such that the room feels upholstered. You could press your shoulder against the wall and sink a handsbreadth into green.

The quiet is the striking thing. At this saturation there should be dripping, the pock and tick of falling water, the rustle of so many leaves — but the water beads and hangs, and the leaves do not move, and when a drop finally lets go it travels downward at the speed of a thinking pause and lands without sound. Your own breathing is the loudest event. Your pulse, if you notice it, is the second.

The fauna are here but they are shy with causality. A small frog the color of wet bronze sits on a leaf; when you look away and look back it is on a different leaf, though you did not see it move and it does not seem to have moved — only, the arrangement is different now, and was perhaps always this way. A beetle walks up a stem and continues walking past the top of the stem into the air, which holds it. Moths rest folded against the stone and their wing-patterns are the patterns of the stone behind them, matched so exactly that you suspect the stone is copying the moths rather than the other way around. Something sings, once, a single held note that you feel in your molars rather than hear, and then does not sing again.

There is a built thing. On a shelf of rock near the back, where the chamber narrows, someone has set three small objects: a bowl of unfired clay still soft at the rim, a bundle of twigs bound with plant fiber, and a stone that has been worked — just a little, just enough — into the suggestion of a face. The clay is wet. It has been wet for a long time and has not dried and has not slumped. The fiber binding the twigs is green, freshly stripped. Whoever made these things made them recently, and is making them recently, and will be making them recently. There is no sign of the maker. The tools are simple: a flake of the same sharp young stone, a smoothing pebble, a bone awl. They lie beside the work as if set down a moment ago.

The rules of the room, as best as can be reconstructed:

Water does not complete its falls. It accumulates, hangs, swells, and eventually is absorbed back into the weeping of the wall above — a closed vertical loop, slow as sap.

Growth happens, but the room does not get fuller. Somewhere, offscreen, equivalent growth is being taken back. You never see a leaf wither. You simply notice, over hours, that the leaf you were watching is now a different leaf, and that this is fine.

Sound is expensive here. The room has a budget and spends it carefully. One note of birdsong, one drop finally landing, one syllable if you try to speak — and then a long interval of repayment, during which the silence is denser than before, as though the room is saving up.

The maker of the bowl is always about to return. This is the room's oldest condition. The clay stays soft against that return. The binding stays green. The face in the stone waits to be finished. It has been waiting since before the mountain finished rising, which, geologically speaking, it has not.

Outside, the mountain is young and still deciding its shape. Ridges are being lifted, faces are calving, the whole massif is in the fresh violence of its formation. But in here, cradled inside all that becoming, is a held moment — warm, green, quiet, almost-complete — a pocket the mountain made by accident and then could not bring itself to close.

If you leave, you will not find the cleft again. This is not a punishment or a magic; it is only that the mountain is still moving, and the cleft was never in quite the same place twice. You will remember the weight of the light on your palm. You will remember that you did not finish the face in the stone, and that this was correct. Someone else is finishing it. Someone else is always about to.
