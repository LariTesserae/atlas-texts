# The Aspirating Continent

Begin underneath. That is the only honest way in.

What you would call ground is not ground. It is a mat of root and rhizome perhaps four hundred meters thick, woven so densely that a person could walk along any horizon within it — could choose a depth and travel sideways for a thousand kilometers without ever surfacing. The fibers are the diameter of a wrist where they cross load-bearing junctions and the diameter of a hair elsewhere, and they are warm. Not warm like blood. Warm like a transformer left on for a hundred years. Steady warm. Inhabited warm.

The continent is one plant. Or one community so old and so interlaced that the distinction has stopped mattering. It covers something on the order of four million square kilometers of low country — barely lifted above the slack tidal seas that finger into it on every side — and it has been doing what it does for long enough that erosion has not yet caught up. The edges are still sharp. Where the mat meets the sea, it meets it in a clean black wall, fibrous and weeping, not yet undercut. The continent is young. The plant is not.

Above the root-mat: the canopy levels. Six of them, by a careful count, though they blur. Lowest is a spongework of pale tubers the size of grain silos, half-submerged in standing water that never drains and never stagnates because the plant drinks it as fast as it pools. Next, a thicket of vertical stems — smooth, segmented, the gauge of factory chimneys — rising twenty, forty, eighty meters before branching. Then the working layer, which is what I want to describe carefully, and then three more layers of leaf and bract above that, closing the sky.

The working layer is where the plant does its industry.

I use the word with intent. At roughly the hundred-meter mark, the stems thicken into bulbous chambers — translucent, fist-walled, each one the volume of a small warehouse — and inside these chambers there are pistons. Cellulose pistons, driven by turgor differentials, stroking slowly in bores of lignified tissue. They pump. They pump sap up and sap down and gases sideways and a hot dark fluid (it might be a kind of latex, it might be something stranger) through a vascular grid that extends across the continent in a hexagonal mesh visible, if you could see, from orbit. Beside each piston-chamber there is a fermentation bulb where sugars are cracked into alcohols and the alcohols are burned — yes, burned, in slow flameless oxidation — to drive the next piston upstream. The plant has invented the engine. It runs on itself.

There are no animals to see this. There never were. The plant occupied this continent before anything ambulatory could colonize it, and it occupied it so completely that nothing has found a niche since. No grazer. No pollinator — pollination, if it happens at all, happens through the plant's own internal plumbing, gametes carried in the sap from one flowering chamber to another across hundreds of kilometers, a private postal system. No decomposer, because nothing dies in a way that requires decomposing; senescent tissue is reabsorbed, retracted back down the stems and into the root-mat to be re-extruded somewhere else.

So: no fauna. And consequently: no sound, almost.

The pistons stroke silently. They are too slow and too well-damped to make noise — one cycle every nine or ten minutes, the displacement absorbed by elastic tissues that have evolved precisely to absorb it. The sap moves without gurgling. The fermentation bulbs do not hiss; their gases are recaptured at every stage. What you hear, if you are inside the working layer, is a single sound, which is the sound of the continent breathing, and it is so low in frequency that you feel it in the bones of your sternum before you hear it in your ears. A long inhalation, perhaps twenty minutes. A longer exhalation. Below that, nothing — or rather, the faint ticking of capillary action in the high leaves, which is the only sound the plant makes that sits in the human auditory range, and which is so quiet it can be drowned out by your own pulse.

It is dark. Six canopy layers absorb the light before it reaches the working layer; the working layer absorbs what's left before it reaches the spongework; nothing reaches the root-mat at all. The plant runs on light at its very top, twelve hundred meters up, in a thin photosynthetic skin perhaps two meters thick, and the entire vast machinery below operates on the chemical export of that skin. Down here in the body, you would be in absolute black. Press your hand to a piston chamber and you would feel the slow stroke; press your ear and you would hear nothing. Wait, and feel the breath.

The water is everywhere. The atmosphere inside the canopy is saturated; condensation beads on every surface and is reabsorbed within hours by specialized intake hairs. Liquid water stands knee-deep across most of the spongework level, warm as bathwater, freshwater pure as distillate because the plant filters it. You could drink it. Nothing else does. Rain falls inside the canopy as often as outside, because the canopy generates its own weather — the transpiration of a continent-sized organism produces clouds, and the clouds, trapped under the upper leaves, rain down through the structural levels in slow soft showers that nobody hears.

And here is the part that pulls at me as I write: the whole system is at equilibrium. Has been for an unmeasurable time. The pistons stroke at the same rate they stroked a million years ago. The breath is the same length. The continent neither grows nor recedes — its edges have been at exactly that latitude and longitude since before any record could have been made, and they are sharp because the sea cannot wear faster than the plant can rebuild. Nothing here is becoming anything. Nothing is decaying. The fermentation cracks the sugars and the combustion burns the alcohols and the pistons drive the sap and the sap feeds the skin and the skin makes the sugars, and this loop has closed so perfectly that the second law has been, locally, told to wait.

It is not dead. It is the opposite of dead. It is so alive that it has stopped needing to change.

If you stood at the edge — at that clean wet black wall where the continent meets the sea — and you put your hand on it, you would feel warmth, and the slow breath, and beneath the breath a deeper rhythm you could not name, the pulse of the postal sap moving its gametes across the dark interior on errands that began before your species and will conclude after it, if they conclude at all.

You would not be welcome and you would not be unwelcome. You would be unaccounted for. There is no category in the continent's long internal accounting for a thing like you. It would not notice you arrive. It would not notice you leave. You would walk away with the warmth still on your palm and the low breath still in your sternum and you would, for the rest of your life, hear silence differently.
