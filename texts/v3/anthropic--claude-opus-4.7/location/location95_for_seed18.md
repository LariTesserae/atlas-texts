# The Engine-Garden of Pitchfork Spire

At three thousand meters, wedged into a fork of black basalt where two ridges meet like the prongs of a tuning fork, there is a garden the size of a small orchard. It is entirely built, and it has been here long enough that no one alive remembers a time before it — long enough that its builders are a matter of theology rather than history.

The whole place is cantilevered. A platform of wrought iron and riveted brass plates, perhaps forty meters across, juts from the rock on trusses that have gone the mottled green-black of century-old bronze. Below the platform: nothing for a very long way. Cloud, sometimes. More often just the blue drop and the distant brown of a valley floor you cannot quite resolve into features. Above: the two basalt prongs rising another six hundred meters into thin air, streaked white where condensation runs down them in slow vertical rivers.

The garden is lush. That is the first surprise. Ferns the color of oxidized copper spill from planters that were once machine housings. Vines — thick, warm to the touch, slightly rubbery — climb the exposed pipework and have, over decades, learned to route themselves along the warmer conduits. There are flowering things that look like orchids but are not: their petals are always slightly damp, and they grow only where steam vents through the deck plating. A species of small fig tree has colonized a row of pressure tanks and sends its roots down into the tanks' interiors, where something still, somehow, feeds them.

Everything is made. Even the soil is manufactured — a black granular substrate you can scoop up and see is composed of tiny iron filings, graphite, and a crumbled ceramic that smells faintly of sulfur when wet. It holds water well. It holds heat better.

And it is warm here, improbably so for the altitude. The warmth comes from below. Beneath the deck plates, visible through the grille walkways, is the engine: or rather, the Engine, singular, because it fills the entire volume of the platform's underside and continues down into the basalt itself through shafts that have been bored and sleeved in steel. Flywheels the size of millstones turn at a stately pace — perhaps one revolution every four or five seconds. Pistons rise and fall in a rhythm slow enough that you could count along. Nothing is hurried. The Engine has the patience of geology. It has probably been running at this tempo since before the builders left.

The sound, though. The sound is catastrophic.

Not loud in the way of a scream, but loud in the way of standing inside a bell while it is struck, over and over, by something distant and enormous. Each piston-fall is a BOOM that you feel in your sternum and in the fluid of your eyes. The flywheels sing a low continuous tone, an iron chord of several notes slightly out of tune with each other, and this chord beats against itself in slow rolling pulses. Steam escapes somewhere with a sustained roar like a waterfall. The whole platform rings. The basalt prongs, resonating, add their own subaudible note — you feel it in your teeth more than hear it. Conversation is impossible. You come here and you are made quiet.

Despite this — or because of it — there is abundant life. Mostly small things. A kind of long-bodied rock swift, iridescent green-black, nests in the upper pipeworks; they fly in and out through gaps, catching insects the deafening air seems not to bother. There are mice that have adapted to the vibration and move in odd synchronized pulses along the grilles, as if walking in time to a beat too slow for them to be consciously following. A species of large pale moth with clockwork-patterned wings clings to the warm tanks during the day and flies only in the half-hour of dusk. And in the ferns: small translucent frogs, no bigger than a thumbnail, whose throats inflate and deflate in exact time with the pistons. When a piston falls, a thousand frog-throats swell. When it rises, they deflate. They have been breathing with the Engine for a hundred frog-generations.

The place is almost empty of people. That is its character. There is room on the platform for perhaps thirty humans to stand comfortably, but on any given day there are none, or one, or two. A caretaker passes through sometimes — you can tell by the oil-fresh gleam on certain bearings, by the way a vine has been recently retied to its guide wire. The caretaker is never seen. The garden's emptiness is not abandonment; it is the emptiness of a shrine that is attended but not occupied.

Weather comes in slow, predictable moods. Once or twice a day a cloud rolls through and the whole garden is, briefly, inside it: visibility drops to three meters, every surface beads with water, the ferns drink, the orchids open wider. Then the cloud moves on and the sun returns, filtered through the high thin air to a clean ordinary light, the kind of light in which you can see every rivet clearly and the brass shines without glare.

Rain is rare but memorable. When it rains, the deck plates, which are slightly curved, channel the water into gutters that feed into the Engine somewhere below. The Engine drinks the rain. It does not seem to need the rain — it runs fine without — but it accepts it, and after a rain the pistons sound very faintly different for a few hours, a little cleaner, a little more resonant, as though refreshed.

Change here is measured in decades. A vine adds a meter of length in a year. A flywheel's bearing, lubricated by the unseen caretaker, loses a tenth of a millimeter of tolerance per century. The basalt prongs erode at the rate the basalt prongs erode. The garden is in balance with itself, and the balance is old, and the balance expects to continue.

The strangeness of the place — and there is a strangeness, past all the described facts — is harder to name. It has to do with the sense that the Engine is not powering anything. There are no cables leading away, no drive shafts engaging external machinery, no output. It turns, and turns, and has turned, and the garden grows on top of it, and the frogs breathe with it, and that appears to be the entirety of its function. It is an industrial cathedral with no industry. A factory whose product is the fact of its own running. And when you stand on the grille and look down through the slow-turning flywheels into the dark shafts below, you have the distinct and unshakeable feeling that the Engine is not a machine that happens to support a garden — that the garden is the machine's slow, patient output, and has been all along, and that in another two or three hundred years it will be finished, and then something will happen, and no one knows what.

Meanwhile the pistons fall. BOOM. The frogs swell. BOOM. The ferns drink their condensation. The swifts wheel out into the blue drop and return. And the place, small and deafening and nearly empty, holds.
