# The Boiler-Reef

Down here, maybe forty meters below a surface nobody remembers, there is a room. It is not a built room — no one laid the walls — but it has the dimensions of one: perhaps twelve meters across, with a curved ceiling that suggests a dome without committing to it. The water is cool enough to taste like metal through the teeth, and it presses on every surface with that patient, sub-aquatic weight that isn't quite sound but which the inner ear registers as a low, continuous chord.

The walls are engines.

This is the first thing that becomes clear, and then becomes less clear the longer you look. The walls are engines in the way coral is engines: grown, not assembled, but articulated into pistons and flywheels and fuel manifolds with such specificity that you cannot read them as anything else. Cast-iron blocks have budded from the bedrock like barnacles. Copper pipe runs in vein-patterns, branching where it wants to branch, fused at junctions that no welder made. A camshaft grows out of the floor at a slight angle, still glossy, still sharp-edged — young metal, recently emerged, unpitted by the currents. You could cut yourself on the valve-lips if you weren't careful.

And the engines are running.

Not all of them. Perhaps a third. A great horizontal piston on the western wall pumps in a slow four-stroke rhythm, and with each compression a cloud of hot exhaust — actually hot, you can feel it through the cold water as a shimmering thermocline — blooms from a stack that has grown itself a little chimney of orange rust-coral. The sound of this engine is the loudest thing in the room: a wet, muffled CHUFF-thump, CHUFF-thump, that the water carries into your sternum. Beneath it runs the higher whine of some smaller turbine further back, and the irregular tick of cooling metal, and a dripping — inexplicable, submerged, but there — of oil into oil.

Vegetation is sparse but deliberate. A few stalks of something that looks like kelp but has the wrong gloss, the wrong give; it is grown from insulation rubber, black and slightly sticky, and it sways in the exhaust-currents with a motion just fractionally out of phase with what the water is doing. Three or four of these plants per square meter of wall, no more. They feed, apparently, on the waste heat. Where they root into the machinery, the metal has gone soft and organic, and you can see the transition zone where iron becomes chitin becomes cellulose becomes iron again, as though the categories were never stable here to begin with.

The fauna is the dense part. The fauna is everywhere.

Small fish the size of a thumb, with bodies of hammered tin and eyes of green glass, shoal in the warm plumes above each working engine. They school in synchronized bursts, and when they turn you can hear — actually hear, under the chuffing — the tiny collective *tink* of their tin bodies catching the light. Larger things, crab-like but with the wrong number of joints, graze the pipe-coral, scraping verdigris into their mouths with tools that are either mandibles or small adjustable wrenches and probably both. On the ceiling a single long creature, eel-shaped, segmented like a flexible exhaust hose, patrols slowly, and the smaller fauna give it a wide berth. There are things in the shadows behind the flywheels that you only see in glimpses — a flash of gear-tooth, the retreating coil of something ribbed.

The light comes from the engines themselves. Each working one glows softly at its combustion chamber, a warm orange that diffracts through the water into long rays, so the room is lit the way a cathedral is lit, from apertures, in beams you can put your hand through. Between the beams, the shadows are deep but not black — there is a secondary, bluish light coming from somewhere above, a memory of surface, filtered down through whatever is up there.

The rules of this place, as they begin to disclose themselves:

The engines grow. A new sparkplug was not there an hour ago; now it protrudes from the floor, still warm from whatever process extruded it, its ceramic perfectly white. Given a week, it will probably be firing. Given a year, something will have learned to live in its shadow.

The engines need nothing. No fuel arrives. No fuel leaves. The exhaust is real — hot, particulate, smelling of combusted hydrocarbons impossibly underwater — but it simply *is*, the way rain simply is.

The fauna are the engines' descendants, or the engines are the fauna's ancestors, or this distinction was always a mistake. A dead tin-fish, when you find one nestled in a cylinder head, has already begun to fuse back into the block. Given time it will become part of a new valve assembly. The cycle has no privileged direction.

And the scale is wrong. This is the part that takes longest to notice. The room is twelve meters across, but the engines in its walls are freight-engines, locomotive-scale, the kind that in the dry world above would require whole factories to house. They have been folded down, or the room has been folded up, or the concept of fitting has been renegotiated at some level below geometry. When you try to measure the big horizontal piston with your outstretched arms, your arms do not quite reach across it, and also they reach across it three times over, and your mind declines to hold both facts at once, so it holds them in alternation, like a flickering.

You could stay here. The water is cool but not cold. The fauna are indifferent to you; a tin-fish has already nosed your wrist and moved on. The chuff-thump of the main piston is beginning to feel like a heartbeat that was always there. The engines are young — you could watch them grow, watch a whole industrial revolution bloom out of the bedrock at the pace of coral, in a room small enough to know completely.

Nobody built this. Nothing maintains it. It is simply one of the places the world goes when no one is looking — a pocket where the distinction between machine and organism, between manufactured and grown, between noise and life, forgot to set properly, and something alive and mechanical and very busy has moved in.
