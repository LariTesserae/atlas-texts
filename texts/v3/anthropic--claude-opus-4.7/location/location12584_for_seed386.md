# The Boiler Ridge

Begin high — almost three thousand meters up the spine of a range that has no name because no one has come to name it. The air here ought to be thin and cold. It is thin. It is not cold. Something underneath the mountains keeps them warm from below, and so the wet heat that ought to belong to a delta has climbed up the slopes and stayed.

The first thing to understand is that the mountains are sweating. Not metaphorically. Water seeps from every fissure in the andesite, slick black sheets of it sliding down faces a hundred meters tall, pooling in the hollows of old volcanic plugs, threading through the moss in capillary networks finer than hair. The rock weeps and the trees drink. Steam rises off the south-facing scarps when the sun touches them in the late morning, ghosting up in coherent columns before the wind on the ridgetop shears them sideways.

The vegetation has had a long time to settle into this strange combination of altitude and heat. Tree ferns dominate the middle elevations, with trunks as thick as a man's torso and crowns the size of small rooms, their fronds dripping constantly. Above them, on the saddles and the lower peaks, a cloud forest of stunted laurels and myrtles, bearded with epiphytes, the branches so loaded with bromeliads and orchids and tank-plants that the actual wood is only a suggestion under the cargo. Mosses go everywhere. Liverworts plate the boulders. There is a particular plant — call it a kind of melastome — whose leaves are as broad as serving platters and whose undersides are deep wine-red, and it grows in such density along certain stream-courses that walking through them is like pushing through hanging laundry.

Now: the tech reading. There are no buildings. No roads. No people. And yet the landscape is industrial.

This is the part to sit with.

There are vents in the upper col — six, seven, maybe a dozen of them — through which the mountain exhales superheated steam under pressure. Not random fumaroles. These are organized. The vents are arranged in rough lines along fracture zones, and the rock around each one has been sculpted by millennia of high-pressure release into a kind of nozzle, a natural geyser-throat of fused silica and sulfur-yellow crust. They fire in sequence, not all at once: one will roar for forty minutes, taper, fall silent, and within an hour another two ridges over will begin to build pressure and then let go. The sound carries — a low industrial hiss, the percussion of water flashing to vapor — but it does not dominate. Most of the time, on most of the ridge, what you hear is the drip of condensation, the muttering of a stream, the occasional crash of a frond shedding its accumulated water.

And there are other engines. Deeper in the rock, audible through your boot-soles when you stand on certain slabs, something cycles. A slow knocking, every nine or ten seconds, like a piston in a deep cylinder. It is probably hydrothermal — a chamber filling, flashing, refilling — but it has the regularity of a machine, and the heat that radiates from the rock above it is enough to keep a small grove of palms thriving at an elevation where they have no business being. Several of these chamber-engines drive the ecology of their immediate basin: warm pools where caecilians breed, mineral terraces precipitated layer by layer, calcite organ-pipes that whistle when the wind crosses their open tops.

The fauna are abundant but mostly hidden. Frogs in extraordinary variety — the warm wet altitude is paradise for them — calling in the dusk in choruses that swell and fade as the vents cycle. A medium-sized cat, spotted, that hunts the talus. Birds: tanagers, hummingbirds attending the heliconia, a long-tailed cuckoo-like thing that calls twice and then is silent for an hour. In the canopy, slow climbing mammals that look like a cross between a kinkajou and a sloth, eating the bromeliad tanks for their accumulated water and insects. Snakes in the leaf litter. Crabs — yes, crabs, freshwater, climbing — in the wet rock chimneys.

The erosion is established. None of this is raw. The peaks are still steep but they have rounded shoulders; the gullies are deep but their floors are smooth and their walls draped. The vents have shaped their own surroundings into the forms they want. Even the slumps and landslide-scars have grown back into the system, become ferny amphitheaters. Everything has had time.

The scale is regional. From any high point you can see four or five major drainages, the next ridge over and the one past that, blueing into haze. A valley you could walk across in a day, holding a river the color of weak tea. You are not in a single chamber; you are in a country of these chambers, and they extend past sight.

What is unusual — the weirdness, low but present — is mostly the sequencing. The vents are too orderly. The piston-knock in the deep rock is too regular. There is no one running this place, but it runs. The mountain has organized itself into something that resembles, very faintly, a refinery — a distributed, slow, biological refinery, where the working fluid is water and the product is the forest itself.

By late afternoon a thunderhead builds over the higher peaks, as it does most days, and rain comes down through the canopy in heavy ropes for an hour or two. The vents hiss louder when the cold water hits their hot crusts. Steam rises from everything. The frogs start early. The cat moves to higher ground. When the rain stops, the whole ridge is breathing visibly, exhaling its excess into the cooling air, and somewhere down in the rock the cylinder knocks, knocks, knocks, keeping its own slow time.
