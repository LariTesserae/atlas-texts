# The Stilled Heights

At this altitude the air thins to a kind of crystalline nothing, and the first thing to understand about the place is that it does not breathe. No wind moves across the plateau — not because the wind has died, but because the wind arrived here long ago and settled, the way silt settles in a forgotten cistern, and has not been asked to leave.

The plateau itself is a worn shield of pale mineral, something between bone and old ivory, with veins of a darker grey running through it in patterns that look, if you sit long enough, like the flow-lines of rivers seen from impossible height. There is no water to make such lines now. There may never have been. The rock is soft underfoot — not crumbling but *yielding*, the way a very old book's pages yield, compressed by an eternity of its own weight into something more like felt than stone. You can press a thumbprint into it. The thumbprint will remain until the end of the world, which, here, has already happened.

Sparse vegetation. Call it vegetation. Low cushions of a grey-green substance grow in the shallow hollows, each cushion perfectly hemispherical, each spaced from its neighbor at a distance that, when you measure it, turns out to be exactly the same distance every time, across the entire plateau. The cushions do not photosynthesize in any way you could name. They do not grow. They were this size when the first thing that could see them saw them, and they are this size now.

And the fauna.

This is where the place asks the most of you. The plateau is *saturated* with animals, layered with them, crowded — and yet the silence is nearly total. There are creatures like slow pale deer, antlered with what looks like coral or calcified lightning, standing in herds so dense their flanks touch. There are things like beetles the size of hands, carpeting the mineral between the deer's hooves, a shifting armor-floor — except they do not shift. There are long-legged birds, thousands of them, wading through the herds as though through shallow water, their beaks half-open as if calling, but no call emerges. Pale moths the size of dinner plates rest on every antler, every shoulder, every stone. In the hollows under the cushion-plants, smaller things press against each other in the dark: rodent-shapes, lizard-shapes, shapes that belong to no phylum.

They are all alive. You can feel the aliveness as a pressure against your skin, a collective attention. But they do not move. Or rather — and this is the part that fractures the mind a little — they *do* move, constantly, continuously, but the landscape as a whole never changes. A deer steps forward; another steps into the space it vacated; the herd's outline is identical. A moth lifts from an antler and another settles. The beetles cycle through their small migrations in perfect compensating loops. Watch any single animal and it lives. Watch the scene and nothing has happened since before language.

The sounds they make are the sounds of breath held behind teeth, of hoof-felt on yielded stone, of wing-beats so soft they register as a change in air pressure rather than a noise. A whole ecosystem's worth of sound, attenuated to just below the threshold of the ear. You find yourself holding your own breath to match them, and then realize you have been holding it for some time, and are not uncomfortable.

Scattered across the plateau, at intervals that again prove to be mathematically regular once you start checking, stand small cairns. Waist-high. Built of the plateau's own pale stone, each capped with a single flat piece, each with a hollow at its center no larger than a cupped palm. In some of the hollows there are objects: a bone awl, a knot of braided fiber, a scraped hide folded small, a flake of obsidian that does not belong to this geology. The workmanship is patient and pre-metal and good. The objects are not weathered. The hands that made them are not here, have not been here for a duration that makes the word *duration* feel foolish, and yet the leather is supple and the fiber has not frayed.

Light falls on all of this from a sky that is the ordinary pale blue of a cool high day. The sun is where it should be. The shadows are the correct length for the hour. Only — and you notice this eventually — the shadows do not move. You can walk across the plateau for what feels like hours and the shadow of a particular cairn points in exactly the direction it pointed when you arrived.

The weirdness, if you want to name it, is this: the plateau has reached a state the rest of the world has not yet thought to reach. It has finished. Erosion has done its work and retired. The ecosystem has balanced itself to the point where every gain is met by an equal and opposite loss within the same instant, so that from any viewpoint larger than an individual creature, nothing occurs. Time still passes here — you can feel it passing — but it passes through the landscape rather than acting upon it, the way light passes through clear glass and leaves the glass unchanged.

The humans who built the cairns understood this, or came to understand it. That is why the offerings in the hollows are small and careful and not repeated. You only need to leave one thing. The plateau will hold it, exactly as you placed it, for as long as the plateau exists, which is to say: for as long as anything exists, and possibly a little longer.

If you stay too long you begin to suspect that you, too, are settling. That your own small motions are being absorbed into the great compensating stillness. That somewhere on the plateau, a deer has turned its head one degree to balance the fact of your arrival, and when you leave, another deer will turn its head one degree back, and the place will not remember you, because remembering is a kind of change, and change is the one thing this landscape has outgrown.

You should leave before you understand this fully. Most who understand it fully are still here. They are easy to miss. They look, by now, almost exactly like the stones.
