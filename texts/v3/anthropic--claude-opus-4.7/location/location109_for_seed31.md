# The Terraces of Ansh-Velur

The town sits on a shelf of pale stone two thousand meters above anything that could be called a plain. Below, the land falls away in long blue distances — other shelves, other walls, a geography still deciding what it wants to be. The rock here is young. You can tell because its edges have not yet been rounded. The ridges above the town come to points. The cliffs break in clean vertical planes, as if the mountain were cut yesterday by something patient and enormous, and the chips have not yet had time to dull.

Water is the quiet tyrant of the place. It does not fall so much as *arrive*. The air itself is a slow sweat; every surface beads. The cliffs behind the town weep continuously from a thousand small seams, not in streams but in sheens — the stone looks lacquered, and when the afternoon light hits it the whole face gleams like wet enamel. At the bases of the cliffs, shallow basins have collected, each one perfectly still, each one reflecting a slightly different patch of sky. Moss rings them. Nothing disturbs them. There are no insects to skate their surfaces, no small creatures drinking at their edges. The water simply accumulates and, somewhere at the lip, overflows in a trickle so thin it is more breath than stream.

The vegetation is lush but spaced — the sparseness is not thinness but deliberation. Broad-leaved plants, dark green, waxy, grow wherever a crack catches soil. They are large where they occur and absent where they do not; there is no middle register of grass or scrub. Between plants, open stone. Between stones, water-shine. The arrangement looks composed, though no one composed it. Something in the geometry of the shelf — the way runoff moves, the way the young rock holds pockets — has decided which plants live where, and the decision is final.

The town is built into the shelf. Not upon it: *into* it. The buildings are low, thick-walled, made of the same pale stone quarried from a few hundred meters up the slope, cut square with simple tools — adzes, wedges, chisels whose marks are still visible on every block. There is no mortar, or almost none; the stones are fitted. Roofs are flat, slabs laid across short spans, sealed with clay and moss. Cisterns sit on every roof — of course they do, in a place where water is the one thing you never have to fetch. Channels run between houses at ankle height, carrying the overflow down through the town in a calm, slow, permanent stream that never rises and never falls. Small wooden sluices, hand-carved, divide the flow between districts. The sluices have been in the same positions for a long time. They will be in the same positions for a long time more.

There are perhaps three hundred houses. Maybe four hundred. Enough for a town, not enough for a city. Streets are narrow and paved with the same pale stone, worn smooth only along the central lines where feet have passed. The edges of the paving stones are still sharp. You could cut yourself on a curbstone if you fell wrong. The town is young in the way its geology is young — recently quarried, recently set, not yet softened by centuries.

And yet: no hurry in it. No sense of expansion. The place feels finished. Whoever built it stopped building when it was done, and what was done was enough.

The silence is the strangest thing, and the most complete. No birds — there is no fauna here, none at all, no calls from the cliffs, no rustle of small bodies in the leaves. No livestock. The only sounds are water sounds: the low continuous whisper of the cliff seeps, the soft plink of drops falling from eaves into cisterns, the quiet running of the street channels. Occasionally, somewhere, a human voice — a word, a short exchange, a tool set down on stone — but these are rare and seem almost apologetic, quickly swallowed by the water-murmur. People here speak softly. They have learned to.

The climate is warm in a damp, even way. No cold snaps, no heat waves. The altitude ought to make it cooler, but something about the geography — the walls of rock trapping the air, the constant evaporation releasing its latent warmth — holds the shelf at a steady mild temperature that does not vary much between noon and midnight, between one season and the next. The light shifts, of course; mornings are gold-grey, afternoons brighter where the sun clears the eastern ridge, evenings long and blue. But the air stays the same. You could set a cup of water on a windowsill and come back a week later and find it at the same temperature, only somewhat more full, from condensation.

Nothing here changes. This is the deepest fact about the place. The young rock is not eroding — the weeping is not wearing it down, only polishing it. The plants do not spread or retreat. The town does not grow or decay. The people — and there are people, though you see them rarely, moving between houses with baskets or bundles, tending the sluice-gates, sitting in doorways — seem to exist at some equilibrium with the shelf. Their tools are simple: stone, wood, some bronze perhaps, nothing beyond. Their crafts are hand-crafts. They weave, they carve, they fit stone to stone. They do not appear to be waiting for anything. They do not appear to be striving toward anything. They are simply present, at the same density as the plants, each one occupying the space that has been allotted and no more.

From a high vantage — the ridge above the northern quarry, say — you can see the whole shape of it: the shelf curving gently, the town a cluster of pale geometry about a third of the way along, the terraces of wet stone stepping down and down into haze, the far walls of other mountains rising blue across the gulf. Clouds move through the lower elevations. Sometimes they rise up to the shelf and the town vanishes into them for an hour, and when they clear everything is wetter than before and otherwise identical.

It is the kind of place where you might arrive as a traveler and mean to stay one night, and find, a week later, that you have not left, and cannot quite remember why you thought you needed to. The silence gets into you. The unchangingness gets into you. The stone is sharp-edged and the water is soft and the air is warm and no bird sings because there are no birds, and the people pass quietly through the streets doing the things they have always done, and will always do, in a town that will not age because it has no need to.
