# The Hollow Pith

There is a city inside a seed.

Not metaphor — the parameters insist on it. A pocket-scale settlement embedded within something larger, bright and warm, lush with vegetation, almost no water, built to urban density but built by hands that never found iron. So: the inside of a vast dry fruit, a pith-cavity the size of a market town, its ceiling the curved inner wall of a husk two hundred feet overhead.

Light comes through that husk. The wall is fibrous and translucent, the color of old honey held to a lamp, and because the star outside (whatever it is) is generous, the whole interior is drenched in a steady amber brightness that casts no sharp shadows. It is the light of an afternoon that has lasted, by local reckoning, several thousand years. There is no night here. There is only the slow migration of the brightest patch across the dome as the outer world turns.

The floor of the cavity is the bowl of the seed — concave, dry, covered in a fine silver dust that was once the seed's own flesh, now powdered by footsteps and time. Nothing pools here because nothing liquid exists here. What the vegetation needs, it draws from the air, which is thick and resinous, warm as a greenhouse, faintly sweet. Breathing it is like breathing the inside of a cedar chest.

The vegetation is lush but strange. It grows sideways out of the curving walls, in tiers, because the walls are the only soil — the inner husk is soft enough for roots to sink into. Long pale vines with leaves like folded paper drape from the upper galleries. Stiff fan-plants the color of bruised plums open and close on a slow rhythm, fanning the resin-air. Where the walls meet the bowl, thick fungal shelves grow in stepped terraces, and between them something that is almost a tree — a column of braided fiber that puts out clusters of dry, rattling pods at its crown. There is no grass. There is no moss. There is no water-thing anywhere.

Into all this, the city.

The builders have used what the seed gave them. Their tools are chipped from the hardened crystalline nodes that form, every so often, in the husk-wall — a mineral that flakes like flint and holds an edge. They have no metal because no metal exists in their world; the seed does not contain iron. They have fiber, shell, crystal, and the compressed pith of the cavity floor, which can be cut into pale blocks like unfired brick. With these, they have built vertically. The city climbs the inner curve of the husk in terraces and stacked dwellings, catwalks of braided vine swinging between levels, ladders carved into the wall itself. Roofs are shingled with the dry pod-husks of the column-trees. Doorways are low and round. Everything has the warm, bone-colored palette of a thing grown rather than quarried.

And yet the city is nearly empty.

This is the first strangeness. The density is wrong for the build. There are streets for thousands and dwellers for dozens. You walk a lane of close-built houses and meet no one; you climb three tiers of the gallery and find a single figure sitting in a doorway, weaving. The city was made for a population it does not have, or no longer has, or has not yet acquired. The silver dust on the floor holds few footprints, and those it holds are not fresh.

The fauna are rare and odd. A long pale creature with too many legs grazes the fungal shelves, unbothered by observers. Something like a moth, but the size of a hand and entirely silent, drifts between the upper vines. In the lower bowl, once in a long while, a burrower surfaces, shakes dust, and goes back under. That is nearly the whole bestiary. No birds — a seed has no sky for birds — but the silent moths serve in their place, riding the slow fan-plant currents.

Now: the sound.

This is where the place becomes unbearable and magnificent. The cavity is a resonator. The husk overhead vibrates — constantly, thunderously — with whatever is happening *outside* the seed. Weather in the larger world, the flexing of the parent structure, the wind through whatever forest or field or creature-body holds this seed among others. Inside, that vibration is translated into a deafening, continuous, organ-like tone, layered with groans, shudders, and long harmonic sighs. It is as if one lived inside a bell that is being rung by geology. The city is built for this. The dwellings have thickened walls; doorways are baffled with hanging fiber; the people (when you glimpse them) wear their hair bound with resin plugs in their ears, and they do not speak — they sign, in a quick language of finger-taps against the forearm, because no voice could carry here.

The sound is not uniform. It has a weather. Sometimes the tone deepens and the whole husk shivers, and silver dust rises from the bowl in slow sheets, and the fan-plants close tight. Sometimes, rarely, there is a near-silence — a few minutes in which the outer world is still — and in those moments the city's few inhabitants come out of their houses and stand in the lanes and open their mouths, and the sound they make, all together, is a sound you realize they have been saving. Then the groan resumes and they go back in.

The established quality of the place — the weathering, the balance — is visible in every surface. The husk-wall is polished smooth where hands have passed for generations. The pith-blocks of the houses have rounded at their corners. The ladders are worn in the centers of their rungs. Nothing here is new. Nothing here is falling down either. The city and the cavity have reached an accommodation: the vegetation does not overgrow the dwellings, the dwellings do not spread into the vegetation, the dust is swept from some lanes and allowed to deepen in others, and this arrangement has held for a very long time — long enough that the holding itself is the culture.

The rules of the world, as best they can be inferred:

There is no outside, for the dwellers. The husk is the sky and the sky is solid. They know, perhaps, that something is beyond it — the sound tells them so — but no one has cut through. It may be forbidden; it may simply be impossible with crystal tools; it may be that the one who tried, generations ago, is the reason the population is now so small.

Water does not exist as a concept. They drink the resin-air through cloths pressed to the face, or chew the inner rind of certain pods, which yields a thick sweet gel. Their word for "thirst," if they have one, means something closer to *staleness* — a need for new air, not new liquid.

Death, here, is returned to the bowl. The silver dust is not only the seed's old flesh. It is also everyone who has ever lived in the cavity, walked into dust, powdered by the footsteps of those who came after. To cross the floor is to walk on ancestors. This is neither mourned nor celebrated. It is simply the arrangement.

And somewhere — this is the last thing, and the strangest — somewhere in the upper gallery, there is a chamber the dwellers do not enter, where the husk is thinnest, and the light comes through almost white, and if you pressed your face to the wall there you might see, blurred and enormous, a shape moving in the world beyond: a hand, perhaps, or a mouth, or the slow passage of something that is not a hand or a mouth but for which those are the nearest words. The dwellers know this chamber exists. They have built a small shrine at its entrance, of crystal flakes and pod-husks and braided fiber, and they leave offerings there — not to the thing outside, but to the wall itself, for continuing to hold.

The seed has not yet germinated. When it does, everything here ends. But it has not yet, and it has not yet for so long that *not yet* has become the shape of the world, and the city in the pith goes on, bright and warm and deafened and nearly empty, under its amber sky.
