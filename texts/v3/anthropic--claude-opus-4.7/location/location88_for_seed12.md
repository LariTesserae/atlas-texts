# The Shelf-City of Aunu

The water here is not a sea exactly. It is warmer than blood and thick with a fine silt of unborn minerals — a drowned air, if such a thing is possible, where you can see perhaps forty meters before the green turns into a soft wall of suspended matter. Sounds travel strangely through it: slow, rounded, with long tails. A stone dropped from the shelf takes a full breath to land and arrives as a low *thum* rather than a click.

Aunu sits at altitude. This is the strangeness of the place — it is high, high up the flank of something that has not yet decided whether it is a mountain or an island or the spine of a continent still being extruded. The rock beneath the city is pale and unfinished. It looks, when you touch it, more like cooling wax than stone; in some places you can press a thumbnail into the wall of a dwelling and leave a crescent that slowly, over weeks, fills back in. The geology is embryonic. Ridges that will one day be valleys are currently only suggestions, smooth swellings in the drowned slope. There are no gullies yet. There is no dust. Rivers of warmer, clearer water drift through the streets in slow braids, and because nothing has been cut by time, everything has the softness of a thing just taken from a mold.

The vegetation is lush but quiet. Colonnades of pale, ribbon-like growth rise from the shelf — not quite kelp, not quite fern, with a translucent pith that glows faintly when disturbed. This is most of the light. The sun arrives only as a diffuse lilac ceiling somewhere far above, filtered through the upper water and the heavy silt, so the true illumination of Aunu comes from the plants themselves and from certain lichens on the south walls of buildings that phosphoresce in a steady cold blue when the current runs over them. Walking the streets, you move through pools of that blue and through the warmer yellow-green of the ribbon-groves and through long soft darknesses between.

The city is layered. Sixteen or seventeen levels of dwelling-terraces step up the slope, connected by ramps rather than stairs — one does not really climb here; one pushes gently off a surface and drifts up, catching a handhold at the next shelf. The buildings are made of the local stone, quarried when it is still soft and left to harden in its placed form, so that every wall has the fingerprints of its maker visible in low relief. Doorways are oval. Windows are few and usually shuttered with panels of a thin, horn-like material — the inner lining of some mollusk, perhaps, or something manufactured from boiled plant pith; no one I have spoken to remembers which. The roofs are domed to shed the slow rain of silt, which otherwise accumulates in a gray fur on every upward-facing surface.

There are perhaps three thousand people here. I say *people* with some hesitation. They are bipedal and long-limbed, with skin the color of wet slate and broad flat ears that flex like fins when they listen. They do not speak much. Most communication is done through a tapping language against stone — each household has a *speaking-post* by the door, a column of resonant rock, and messages travel the city as patterns of muted percussion, overlapping, forming a kind of slow weather of sound. This is most of what you hear in Aunu: the distant, arrhythmic tock of domestic chatter, carried far through the water, gentled by distance into something like rainfall on a leaf canopy.

Their tools are simple. Knives of a glassy black material knapped from nodules found in the ribbon-groves. Nets woven from plant fiber. Small hand-lamps that burn a paste of rendered something — not fish oil, there are few fish — in a clay cup, producing a surprisingly steady flame that works underwater because the fuel itself consumes its own oxidizer. This is the one piece of chemistry that seems advanced for them, and they treat it as unremarkable, a grandmother's recipe.

Fauna is scarce. The warm water and the unfinished rock do not support much yet. There are the *drifters*, palm-sized creatures like folded handkerchiefs that pulse slowly through the upper streets feeding on silt. There are blind, pale eels in the deeper crevices that the children are warned away from, though no one I asked could remember anyone actually being harmed by one. And there is the *long animal*, which the inhabitants will not describe and will not name in the tapping language, only gesture toward with a particular motion of the wrist that I am told means *something going past very slowly that we have agreed not to see*.

The rules of Aunu, as best as I can render them:

One does not cut the rock below the thirteenth terrace. It is too young. A cut there will not heal, and the city has agreed the city is still becoming.

One does not make loud sound. The water carries too well. A shout at the harbor can wake a sleeper at the summit.

One does not ask where the water ends. There is a prevailing belief, or perhaps a fact, that Aunu is submerged inside something finite — a held breath, a bubble in a larger stone — and that to seek its edge is to notice it, and to notice it is to end it. The children play games of pretending to look for the edge, and the adults indulge this, because children's attention is considered too light to do damage.

The dynamic of the place is slow. A generation here passes and the terraces have risen perhaps a thumb's width. A grandmother shows her grandchild the handprint she left in the wall as a girl; it is still there, only a little shallower. The ribbon-groves seed once every twelve years and do so all at once, for one night, filling the city with a snow of luminous spores that settle on every surface and make the entire slope glow a soft cream-green until morning. This is called *the bright sleep*, and on that night the tapping language falls silent and everyone stands in their doorways and watches.

I came down from the upper terrace this morning — by which I mean a slow descending drift of about an hour — and stopped at a speaking-post to listen. Somewhere far below, someone was tapping a long, patient sequence, a pattern I didn't know. A neighbor on a balcony noticed my attention and offered, in the small trade-pidgin they use with visitors, that the tapper was a woman telling her dead husband about the weather. She has done this every morning for eleven years. The husband is buried in the wall of her kitchen, his shape still visible as a slight convexity in the stone, slowly being reabsorbed.

Aunu is warm, and dim, and unfinished, and will be unfinished for a long time yet.
