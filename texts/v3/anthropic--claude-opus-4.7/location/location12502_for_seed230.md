# The Town of Sett

It sits in a saddle between two grey shoulders of mountain, at roughly the height where the air begins to thin but has not yet become unkind. Cool, mostly. The kind of cool that asks for a wool layer in the morning and forgives you by midafternoon.

You see the town first as a pale stepping of roofs — slate, schist, the occasional clay tile gone the color of old bone. Maybe eighty buildings, maybe a hundred. It is hard to count because they share walls, lean on one another, and the alleys between them are narrow enough that from above the whole settlement reads as a single fractured surface, like a dry lakebed that has decided to be a town.

The stone is local. You can tell because the cliffs around the saddle show the same pale grey banded with rust, and the buildings show it too — courses of cut block at the bottom, rougher rubble higher up, then timber-and-daub at the gables where someone, generations ago, ran out of stone and made do. Nobody has run out of stone since. Nobody has needed to build much.

The streets are paved in the same material, worn into shallow troughs along their centers where feet have passed for a long time. Not deep grooves — established ones. The kind of wear that suggests the town reached its present size some centuries ago and has been holding that shape ever since, the way a river stone reaches a shape and keeps it.

## Water

There is one spring. It comes out of the rock at the upper end of the town, in a stone basin under a small tiled roof, and runs in a channel cut along the side of the main street. The channel is shallow, ankle-deep at most, and at intervals there are basins where people draw water and wider stretches where they rinse cloth. It exits the town at the lower end and disappears into the scree.

Other water arrives as weather. Cool fogs that come up the valley in the early mornings and bead on everything — on the wool of cloaks left out, on the iron rings of doors, on the dark backs of the few roof slates that catch the dawn. A thin rain perhaps three or four days a month. Snow in winter, but not deep. The town is damp in patches and dry in patches, and people know which is which: the north walls grow a fine grey-green lichen, the south walls don't.

## Plants

Sparse. Stonecrop in the wall-joins. A few junipers in the small square — three of them, ancient, twisted, kept because they have always been kept. Someone's herb pot on a sill. Lichens, more lichens, the cliffs covered in them where the rock isn't bare. Outside the town, on the slopes, tough grasses and the occasional dwarf rowan, but nothing approaching forest. The treeline is somewhere below, and Sett is above it.

## People and craft

Smaller than you'd expect for a town. The density is low — many houses stand quiet, doors closed, and you can walk a long stretch of street without meeting anyone. Not abandonment. Just sparseness, a settled emptiness, as if the town were built for more people than it presently needs and has accepted this without trouble.

The tools are simple. Iron, but worked plainly — hinges, blades, the rims of buckets. More common is wood and stone and bone and horn. Looms in some rooms; you can hear them through the shutters, the soft clack and pause. A potter's wheel turning somewhere. A man splitting kindling outside a doorway, taking his time. No machines beyond what a person's two hands can run.

## Animals

A goat or two, tethered. A grey cat watching from a windowsill. High up, a single bird circling — a chough, maybe, or something like one — but only one, and it doesn't call. You can go an hour without seeing another animal. They are present but rare, the way coins in a pocket are present but not always touched.

## Sound

Mostly the water in the channel, which makes a low continuous note that you stop noticing and then notice again. The loom. Footsteps. Occasionally a door. The wind, when it comes, is high and thin and passes over the roofs without lingering. Nobody shouts. There is no market hubbub because there is no market today, and there may not have been one for a while, and there may be one tomorrow, but it will sound the same as the last.

## The rule of the place

Sett does not change. This is not a magical stillness — there is no spell on it, nothing strange. It is simply that everything in the town has found its position. The stones are where they will stay. The families occupy the houses their families occupied. The spring runs at the rate it has always run. The lichen advances at the speed lichen advances, which is not a speed you can see.

Things happen — a pot is made, a child is born, an old man dies, a roof slate is replaced — but the town as a whole does not move. If you came back in fifty years, the same number of junipers would stand in the square, perhaps the very same junipers; the channel would run at the same depth; the goat at the third door would be a different goat but indistinguishable in role.

This is not stagnation. The people don't experience it as such. They experience it as *enough* — as a place that long ago worked out what it was, and has been being that, carefully, ever since.

The light is ordinary. The sky is the sky. The mountains stand where mountains stand. You could draw the town once and the drawing would be accurate for a long time.
