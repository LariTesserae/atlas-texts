# The Shelf Cities

The plateau is so wide that standing at its center you cannot tell it is a plateau at all. It reads as a world. The horizon curves the wrong way — a little too flat, as though the land were pretending to be larger than any land should be. Only at the very edges, days of walking in any direction, does the shelf finally give itself away, dropping into cloud-seas that move slowly beneath, whole weathers cycling below your feet while a different weather does its work above.

Up here, the air is thin enough to be cool against the skin even in the hours the sun stands high, and wet enough that the cool is a pressure, not a dryness. Everything is damp. Not drenched — nothing here is ever drenched — but everything holds a film. The pale stone, a siltstone the color of old bone, weeps where it meets itself. Run a finger along any ledge and the finger comes away darker.

The vegetation is sparse because the stone gives little, but what does grow is tenacious and low: cushion plants in dull greens and reds, rosettes no wider than a hand, a few lichens that have learned to be architectural — gray-blue crusts that follow the grain of the rock, mapping older fractures. Here and there, in the damp runnels, a single kind of reed, jointed and the color of tarnished brass, grows in colonies the size of a rug. Nothing is tall. The tallest living thing I can find, walking in my mind across miles of this place, is a woody shrub barely to the knee, bent by a prevailing wind that does not presently blow.

And yet the plateau is dense. This is the contradiction at its heart.

It is dense because of the cities.

They are not cities of anyone living now. They were built — or grown, or settled into existence, the distinction feels less important the longer you look — by something that is no longer here, or that has become indistinguishable from the rock. The settlements are everywhere. You crest a low swell and below you is a town of low stacked courses, terraces and alleys and square-mouthed doorways, all in the same pale siltstone as the plateau, because they *are* the plateau, cut from it and set back into it. Walk an hour and there is another. An hour more and another. Sometimes the towns share walls across miles, knit together by causeways that run straight for as far as the eye holds detail and then simply continue.

The scale of it is what becomes difficult. There are more doorways on this plateau than there are people in any world I can imagine. Empty rooms stacked in ranks, small plazas where the rain has carved shallow basins into the flagging, long stepped avenues that climb nothing and descend to nothing and go on.

Erosion has had a long time with all of this, and has been gentle. Edges are soft but legible. Corners have lost their sharpness the way an old coin loses its face — the shape is still there, only patient. The weeping of the stone has left pale mineral curtains down the inner walls of courtyards, and in some of the deeper rooms a slow stalactite has begun, a finger of calcite reaching down through a lintel over what was once a threshold. None of this is ruin in the sense of collapse. The cities are in balance with their unmaking. They have been in balance for a very long time.

The sound of the place: almost nothing. A plateau this vast, this porous, this quiet, absorbs sound the way a deep snow does. Water moves, but softly — a dripping from a ledge into a basin, the hush of a film of water sliding across a slightly inclined plaza. Wind, when it comes, does not howl, because there is nothing tall for it to comb through. It sighs across the flagstones and pulls at the cushion plants and moves on. If you speak aloud, the nearest wall returns your voice once, small, and then gives it up.

Fauna is rare enough that encountering any of it feels ceremonious. A pale lizard, longer than your forearm, with skin the color of the stone and a dark stripe along its flank, suns itself in the one spot of a courtyard the sun has just reached; it will be gone before you cross to it. Something like a small heron, solitary, hunts in the reed-rugs — grayish, long-legged, entirely silent. There are insects, but you notice them only when one lands on your hand: thin-bodied, glassy, moving as if underwater. Nothing swarms. Nothing flocks. Each creature seems to be the only one of itself for miles, and perhaps is.

No tools. No fires, no metal, no evidence that anything here has been made in the way we mean *made* — even the cities do not feel manufactured, only revealed, as though the stone had always contained them and long erosion had merely cleared them. There are no implements in the rooms. No marks of use on the thresholds. If this is a built place it was built by a hand that did not leave fingerprints, or by a process slower than building, which came to the same result.

And yet the plateau is *active*. This is the other thing. It is not still. The weather cycles are constant and visible: mist rises from the lower edges through the mornings, gathers above the terraces by midday, releases a thin steady rain for an hour or two in the afternoon, clears toward evening into a tall cold air through which you can see for impossible distances. The water beads, runs, collects in the basins, evaporates, rises, falls again. The reeds turn with it. The lichens darken and pale. The stone weeps harder or less hard. Below the plateau's edge, whole storm systems wheel beneath you — you can stand at a parapet of a high quarter of a high town and watch a thunderhead working three thousand feet below your boots, silent at this distance, flickering. The cloud-seas advance and retreat against the cliffs like slow tides.

So the place is doing something. It is breathing, on its own schedule. The cities are not doing it — they are what the breathing happens around and through. The lizards and the heron and the glass insects are small participants; the lichens keep their own weather against the stone. The long causeways channel the rain in ways the rain seems to have agreed to.

What you understand, staying here any length of time, is that the plateau is not waiting for anyone. It is not abandoned, because it was never occupied in the sense that would make abandonment meaningful. It is simply itself: a shelf of bone-colored stone, high and cool and wet, with its own weather and its own quiet fauna, patterned all over with the soft geometry of rooms and streets and doorways that nothing enters and nothing leaves, and this pattern is as natural to it as the cushion plants and the weeping seams and the slow calcite fingers reaching down from the lintels.

You walk across it. You are a small warm animal passing through a cold patient country. Eventually, far ahead, the ground begins almost imperceptibly to tilt, and you understand you have been walking for days and are approaching an edge, and below that edge there will be cloud, and inside that cloud, weather, and beneath the weather, some other world entirely, which is not this one and does not concern it.
