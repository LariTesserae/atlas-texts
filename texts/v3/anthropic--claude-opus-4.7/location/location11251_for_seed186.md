There is a mountain, or what was once a mountain, and is now something more like the idea of one — a vast cold spine pushing up past the altitude where most things go quiet. The air here is thin enough that breath crystallizes the moment it leaves the body, and the dark is a particular dark: not the dark of night exactly, but the dark of a place the sun forgot to fully reach, where light arrives sidelong and exhausted, filtered through cloud-banks that sit below you rather than above.

The slopes are terraced by time. Not cut, not built — weathered into shelves by millennia of slow freeze-thaw, each ledge wide enough to hold its own small world. And on each ledge, against every expectation: forest. Or something that performs the role of forest. Black needled trees with trunks no thicker than a wrist, growing in dense little congregations, their roots wound into mats of moss and pale lichen that hold what moisture there is. Ferns the color of old iron. Cushion plants swollen with stored water. The vegetation has a furred, insulated quality, as if everything here learned the same lesson at the same time.

Water moves through the place as breath rather than flow. Condensation beads on the undersides of needles, rolls together, drips. Mist rises from somewhere lower and gets caught in the lichen, which drinks. Rare rain, when it comes, comes sideways. There are no rivers, but every leaf is wet on its underside, and the rocks weep darkly where they face the prevailing wind.

The strangeness — and this is the part that takes longest to register — is that the mountain hums. Not loudly. You wouldn't call it sound. It is a vibration that registers in the sternum and the soles of the feet, a low industrial pulse, the way a transformer hums if you stand close to one in a field. Somewhere deep in the mountain, machinery is running. Has been running. The hum is steady and old and unattended. Sometimes when the wind drops to nothing — and it does, often, the silence here is the silence of a cathedral, of a place that swallows its own echoes — you can hear, faintly, the rhythm of pistons. Or something like pistons. Slow, patient, geological in their unhurry.

There are no buildings. No vents, no doors, no seams in the rock. The machinery is simply inside the mountain the way marrow is inside a bone, and the mountain has grown its forests over whatever it is doing, and the forests don't seem to mind. If anything they thrive on it — the rock is faintly warm where you press your palm to it, and certain ledges have grown lusher than they should, ringed with a slightly denser green, as though something underneath is exhaling heat through a stone skin.

The animals know. You can tell because there are so many of them, and they are all so quiet. Small dark grouse-like birds that walk rather than fly, picking through the moss. Six-legged things the size of cats, soft-furred, with eyes adapted for the dim. A kind of pale goat, or what wants to be called a goat — long-bodied, sure-footed on the terraces, moving in groups of three and four through the trees with a fluid silence that suggests they have been doing this for a long time and have learned not to disturb whatever they share the mountain with. Something larger hunts them; you find the evidence — a scatter of pale wool, a single curved tooth — but you never see it. The predators here are patient and the prey are watchful and the whole ecosystem runs on a kind of careful courtesy.

You can walk for a day and meet nothing but the hum and the goats and the slow boil of mist coming up from the valleys you cannot see. The mountain extends in both directions past what the eye can resolve, ridge after ridge, all of them terraced, all of them forested in the same dark green, all of them humming the same low note. The scale is regional. There is a whole country of this, folded vertically.

What the machinery does, no one is here to ask. It may be cooling something. It may be holding something down. It may simply be running, the way a heart runs, because stopping is not in its design. The mountain has accepted it. The lichens have grown over the warm places. The goats have learned which ledges to sleep on in the cold months. Everything is in its long balance, and nothing is in a hurry, and the dark settles into the dark, and somewhere below your feet a piston completes its slow stroke, and the needles of the small black trees tremble, very faintly, and are still.
