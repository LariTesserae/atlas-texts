# The Terraces of Oost-in-Condens

The plateau stands somewhere around two thousand meters, but you wouldn't know it from the air pressure alone — the whole mass of the place sits inside its own weather, a standing cloud that never quite resolves into rain or into sky. Moisture condenses on everything. The stone weeps. The iron weeps. Even the bodies of the animals weep, though most of them have adapted to that so thoroughly that their coats shed water like oiled feathers, and their warm exhalations add to the fog until the fog is partly made of them.

There are no plants. There haven't been for a long time, if there ever were — the rock here is basalt and a grey-green serpentinite that crumbles under a fingernail, and what grows on it grows in patches of rust-orange lichen-analog and pale mineral crusts that might be alive or might just be chemistry done slowly enough to look alive. Whatever photosynthesis happens in this world happens somewhere else. Here the base of the food web is something else entirely: the animals eat each other, and beneath them, something eats the warm damp stone.

The city covers the plateau in terraces. It has covered the plateau in terraces for a very long time. You can read the age of it in the way the foundry-stacks lean — not collapsed, just settled, the way old teeth settle into a jaw. Nothing is new. Nothing is ruined either. The place has found its shape and keeps it the way a river keeps its bed.

The construction is layered vertically: low vaulted stone at the base, brick above that, then iron, then a canopy of copper sheeting gone green and soft. Catwalks thread between the levels. Pipes run everywhere, some hot, some cold, all sweating. The pipes are the city's circulatory system and also, in places, its skeleton — entire buildings hang from trunk-lines of riveted tube that were laid down so long ago the rivets have fused into continuous seams. Furnaces breathe somewhere lower down. You can feel them in your feet before you hear them.

And the animals. This is really their place, and the people — there are people, you understand they are here by the lamps and the smoke and the slow beat of the engines — the people live inside a weather system of animals. The air at mid-elevation is thick with something between a bat and a moth, palm-sized, leather-winged, that drinks condensation off wires. They come in sheets. They roost on the undersides of every overhang until the overhangs look furred. Larger things move among them: long-necked grazers with flat teeth that work the lichen crusts off the walls, six-legged and patient, tolerated by the city because they clean it. Dog-sized predators with flattened skulls pad along the catwalks with the easy ownership of creatures who have never been shooed. In the cisterns, eel-things. In the flues, something smaller and hotter that the foundrymen call cinder-wrens and do not disturb. Every square meter of the city has three or four species contesting it, and the contest is old enough that the rules are settled. Nothing is frantic. The overwhelming-ness is not chaos; it is saturation, the way an old forest is saturated, except this forest is made of vertebrates and brick.

The sound is the sound of all of this breathing at once. Engines at the low end — the slow thud of some great bellows-pump cycling every nine or ten seconds, water-hammer in the pipes, a distant rolling-mill. Above that, the continuous susurrus of wings, the click of claws on iron, grazer-calls that sound like wet wood bending. And the dripping. Constant, from every surface, into gutters that run to cisterns that run to the furnaces that run the bellows that move the air that carries the moisture that drips. The city is a closed loop that has been turning for so long it has forgotten it was ever started.

Light comes through the fog filtered and even, the way light comes through a frosted skylight. There's a sun up there somewhere. At this elevation and in this saturation, it arrives as a general luminance rather than a direction. Lamps burn in every doorway regardless, not because they're needed for seeing but because the habit of lamp-burning is older than the reason for it. The lamps are gas, fed from the same network that feeds the furnaces. They flicker in the same rhythm as the bellows-pump, so the whole city pulses, very gently, nine seconds on nine seconds on nine seconds on.

What's strange about the place — and there is something strange, though it takes a while to find — is how little anything changes. A grazer born on the north terrace will die on the north terrace eighty years later without ever having gone south. A family foundry will cast the same six patterns for eleven generations. The bats-that-aren't-bats migrate, but only between levels, and only by a few meters with the seasons. Decisions here have the weight of geology. To move a pipe is to commit an act that will be remembered. Most pipes are not moved. This is not stagnation — the place is intensely, crowdedly alive — it is simply that the metabolism of the whole is very slow, and every fast thing in it (a wing-beat, a hammer-strike, a shouted order) is a small quick stitch in a fabric that itself hardly moves at all.

Stand on one of the upper catwalks at what passes for evening — the general luminance goes amber, then rose, then the fog itself seems to hold the light a while after the sun has gone — and you can see the terraces falling away beneath you in their copper-green and brick-red layers, streaked with the white droppings of a million roosting wings, threaded with lamp-light, exhaling steam from ten thousand vents. You can hear the pump. You can feel the animals moving through the dusk like a tide that has been coming in and going out on the same stones since before anyone counted.

It is warm. It is wet. It is full. It has been this way for a long time and intends, in whatever way a place can intend anything, to go on being this way.
