# The Condensery

It sits on a shoulder of the world, a city built into the side of a basalt plateau where the air comes off the lowlands already saturated and meets the hot stone climbing toward something hotter still. The plateau bleeds heat. You feel it through your soles before you feel it anywhere else — a slow, mineral warmth, the warmth of something that has been warm for a very long time and intends to keep being so.

The first thing to understand is that there is almost no one here. The city is enormous — terraces of dark glassy brick stepping down a slope you cannot see the bottom of, chimney-stacks and condenser-towers rising in clusters of three and seven, aqueduct-pipes threading between rooftops on iron trestles, whole quarters of warehouses with their loading-doors rolled up onto empty streets — and it is almost entirely empty. Not ruined. Maintained. The lamps burn (dimly, smokily, a wet orange through soot-glass), the pumps thump somewhere underfoot, the gutters run. But you can walk for an hour through a district of foundries and meet nothing but the heat-shimmer above a vent and the slow drip from a cooling-fin overhead.

The weather here is one weather, repeating. Hot wind comes up the slope from below, hits the cold stack-tops where the condensers work, and lets down its water as a fine sour mist that beads on every black surface until the beads grow heavy and run. The streets are always just-wet. The walls weep. The mortar between bricks is dark with permanent damp at the bottom inch and bone-dry at the top. Roofs are pitched steep and slick. Everything tilts and drains. The city is, in effect, a single vast machine for taking the water out of the air, and the air gives it up reluctantly, and the city gets only a little, and that little is what it lives on.

There are no trees. There were never trees. The vegetation, such as it is, consists of soft pale crusts in the wet seams of masonry — a felt-like growth, not quite fungus, that the engineers call *wick*. Wick draws water up walls by capillary action and gives back nothing visible. Where it has been growing for a long time the stone behind it has softened to a kind of cheese. In some quarters whole buttresses have been replaced because the wick ate through them. In others the wick is encouraged, even cultivated, in shallow lead trays, for reasons no one currently in the city quite remembers.

The fauna: rare and odd. There are pale flat creatures, palm-sized, that live in the runoff channels and feed by filtering the mist. They look like wet paper. They breathe through their backs. Occasionally one finds a high-roofed cistern where dozens of them have gathered, hung from the ceiling like damp leaves, twitching in some slow communal rhythm. There is supposed to be something larger that moves between the lower terraces at night, something with a long stride and an apologetic posture, but no one has reliably described it; in a city this empty a single witness is not corroboration.

The strangeness, when it comes, is not theatrical. It is structural. For instance: the city is laid out in a grid, but the grid is not square. Each block is very slightly trapezoidal, and the trapezoids tile the slope in a way that has no center and no edge — you cannot, by walking, get to where the pattern resolves. For instance: every seventh street is a half-street, with buildings on one side and a long polished trough on the other, and the troughs all run downhill but none of them appear to empty anywhere. For instance: the foundries are lit and warm and the bellows move, but the molds are always empty, and the molten metal — there is molten metal, you can see it glowing under the grates — is poured back into its own crucibles at the end of each shift, by no one.

The sound of the place is mostly the sound of water finding its level. Drip, run, gather, fall. Under that, the deep slow respiration of the pumps. Under that, very rarely, a bell — not a tolling, a single struck note, somewhere across the rooftops, that the mist swallows before you can place it. People say (the few people: a watchman here, a pipe-fitter there, an old woman who keeps a stove going in a tea-house with twelve empty tables) that the bell rings when the city has condensed enough water for the day. They say this without conviction. They say a lot of things without conviction. It is a city in which belief has thinned out the way the population has, leaving only the habits.

At the highest terrace, where the slope finally gives up and becomes sky, the condenser-towers are tallest, and the heat from below meets the cold from above most violently. Up there the mist is so thick it is almost rain, and the bricks are almost black with wet, and you can stand at the parapet and look down across the entire stepped expanse of the place — every roof shining, every chimney smoking faintly, every lamp a small smeared coal in the dimness — and see how perfectly the whole arrangement holds itself in balance. Nothing is being built. Nothing is falling. The water comes out of the air and runs down through the city and disappears at the bottom into whatever the bottom is, and the heat comes up out of the stone and rises through the city and disperses at the top into whatever the top is, and the city stands between them like a long patient filter, doing the work it was made for, mostly alone.

You could live here. People do. Not many, and not happily, but not unhappily either — the way you might live inside a clock that was still running long after the appointment it was set for. There is tea. There is warm stone to sleep against. There is the sound of water. There is the occasional bell.

And there is the question, which the city raises and does not answer, of what all this water was supposed to be *for*.
