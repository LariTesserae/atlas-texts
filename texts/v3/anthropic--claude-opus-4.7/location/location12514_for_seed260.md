# The Stillsong Heights

Begin with the altitude. You are very high. Not on a mountain — the word *mountain* implies a base, a slope, a relationship to something below, and there is no below here that can be verified. There is only the plateau, and the plateau extends in the way regions extend: for a long walk in any direction, with features, with the suggestion of a valley somewhere out past the third ridge, with the kind of geography that a person could come to know over years. But beneath the plateau's edge, when you approach it, the air simply continues. No clouds. No distant ground. Just more cool dim air, going down, presumably forever, though nobody has tested this and the ones who tried did not return any information.

The rock is new. This is the strangest geological fact about the place, and the one easiest to overlook because newness in stone usually means jagged, broken, raw. Here it means *unfinished*. The boulders have the soft uncertain edges of something that hasn't yet decided to be a boulder. Run your hand along a ridge and the surface gives a little, not like clay but like a photograph that hasn't fully developed — the granite is still arriving at being granite. There are no scree fields, no talus, almost no dust. Erosion has not begun. The wind, when it moves, does not abrade. Whatever process made this place set it down recently and the world hasn't gotten around to wearing it yet.

And yet — vegetation. Lush vegetation, in defiance of the lack of water, in defiance of the cool dim light, in defiance of the unfinished ground. Mosses thicker than a hand is wide. Ferns the color of old bruises. Low cushiony plants that seem to be made of something between lichen and felt, growing in scattered patches across the slopes, not crowded but everywhere — a plant every few meters, a thicket every hundred. They do not need water. They breathe the sound.

This is the part that takes some saying.

The sound here is enormous. It comes from no source. It is not wind, though it moves; not thunder, though it has weight; not music, though it has the architecture of music heard from inside the instrument. It is *deafening* in the precise sense: stand in it for ten minutes and your hearing recalibrates, and the sound becomes the new silence against which all other things must declare themselves. It registers in the chest first, then the teeth, then somewhere behind the eyes. The plants drink it. You can see them doing this if you sit and watch — the fern-fronds tilting infinitesimally toward whatever direction the sound is loudest from in any given hour, the moss-cushions swelling and subsiding with the long slow pulses of the lower registers.

The sound is what makes the place possible. Without it, the vegetation could not photosynthesize whatever it photosynthesizes; without the vegetation, there would be no slow exchange of gases that keeps the air breathable; without breathable air, no observers, and possibly — this is the suspicion — no place at all. The Stillsong Heights may be a region that exists only while it is being heard.

There is no animal life. Nothing that moves on its own initiative, nothing with appetite. Once you understand the sound, you understand why: an animal here could not hear itself think, could not hear a mate, could not hear the approach of anything. The acoustic niche is fully occupied by the world itself. Anything that wanted to be an animal here would have to first become the noise, and at that point it would no longer be an animal.

The light is dim and does not come from a sky. There is a sky — a pale neutral vault, gray going to grayer at the horizons — but it does not illuminate. The light seems to seep from the rock, from the plants, from the air between things, a kind of low ambient radiance that allows seeing without ever fully revealing. You can see far. You can see for what looks like ten or twelve kilometers across the plateau, ridge giving way to ridge, the pale patches of moss like scattered coins. But everything you see is held in this same low register, and you understand, eventually, that there is no time of day. The light does not change. It has not changed since you arrived, and you suspect it will not change.

The structures are the part that took me longest to write because I had to wait for them to appear in the description, the way they seem to appear in the landscape — slowly, and only when you look in the right direction.

There are cairns. Not many. One every kilometer or so, on prominent points. They are made of the unfinished stone, stacked, and the stacking has bonded the stones to each other — they have begun to merge, the contact surfaces blurring into single masses, so that the cairn is no longer a stack but a fused vertical thing, like a candle that has dripped upward. Someone made these. The someone used hands, or something like hands; you can see the size of the gripping in the surfaces. The cairns are old in a place where nothing is old. Their bottoms have sunk a little way into the rock, the rock having flowed slightly to accommodate them.

There are no people. There have not been people for a long time, in a place where *long time* is hard to measure because nothing erodes. Whoever made the cairns is gone, and the cairns mark something — routes, graves, prayers, warnings — that cannot now be read. The marks are scattered: a single building's worth of presence spread across the whole region. No village. No road. Just the cairns, and occasionally a shaped stone tool half-sunk in moss, the tool also beginning to merge with whatever it rests on, the boundary between artifact and ground softening over the centuries.

The rules of this world, insofar as they have settled:

The ground is becoming. It is not yet what it will be. If you stay long enough, you can watch a slope decide, over weeks, to grow a buttress. The decision is slow but irreversible.

The sound feeds the plants and the plants exhale the air and the air carries the sound, and this is the metabolism of the region. Stand quietly and you participate in it. Speak, and your voice is swallowed instantly — not muffled, swallowed, as though the sound were grateful for the contribution.

Reality, here, is thin in particular ways. Look at the horizon too long and it commits to a new shape. Walk in a straight line and the line is straight, but the distance covered does not match the time spent walking; sometimes you arrive too soon, sometimes too late, and the cairns you pass are not always in the same order. The place is not malicious about this. It is simply not finished, and one of the things it has not finished is the relationship between space and the people moving through space.

If there are people now — you, for instance, if you are imagining yourself here — you are a late visitor to something that was made for purposes you cannot recover, by hands that knew the sound as a language. You can learn to live here. The cool air does not kill. The plants, some of them, can be eaten, and taste like the inside of a bell. You will never sleep well; the sound does not allow sleep in the way you understand it, only a kind of suspended listening that serves the same restorative function but leaves you, on waking, unsure whether time has passed.

And the elevation. I keep coming back to it. You are very high. The plateau sits at the top of nothing, and below the edge is only more of the cool dim air, and the sound which is loudest at the edge and which seems, if you lean out and listen carefully, to be rising from below — as though the Stillsong Heights are not the top of a mountain but the lid of something vast and patient that is, slowly, slowly, singing itself into being beneath you, and the plateau is only the first thin crust of what it will eventually be.

The cairns face the edges. Whoever built them was listening downward.
