# The Drowned Concourse

Down past where the digging stops making sense, there is a city that was never built — only *settled into*, the way a stone settles into a riverbed over centuries of patient water.

You come to it through a shaft that doesn't end the way shafts should. The walls go from rough basalt to something smoother, then smoother still, until you realize you've been descending through architecture for some time without noticing the transition. The stone became wall. The wall became something the stone is only pretending to be.

---

It opens into a concourse. Cathedral-wide, no more than that — you could shout across it and be heard, if there were anyone to hear. The ceiling arches maybe forty meters overhead, ribbed in a material that looks like wet obsidian and rings, faintly, when the warm air moves against it. The whole space is warm. Not hot — *blood-warm*, the temperature of being held. The walls give it off.

There is almost no one here. There may never have been anyone here. The emptiness is not the emptiness of abandonment; it is the emptiness of a space that was made with this much room on purpose, for reasons that didn't include occupancy. Vast plinths stand at intervals along the concourse floor — some bearing nothing, some bearing small dark objects the size of a fist that hum if you put your hand near them. Between the plinths: distance. Honest, unfilled distance. You can walk for a full minute between one feature and the next and pass through nothing but warm dim air.

---

The light is wrong, or rather, the light is *scarce* in a deliberate way. There are no fixtures. The illumination comes from the floor — a deep arterial glow, like embers seen through smoked glass, just enough to walk by. Your shadow doesn't fall in any one direction; it pools beneath you. Look up and the ceiling is black. Look at your hand and it is the color of old wine.

---

And then there is the sound.

The concourse is *loud*. This is the parameter that surprises everyone who imagines this place from outside. You expect a tomb-hush appropriate to the emptiness, and instead you get a continuous, structural roar — not chaotic, not painful, but enormous. It comes from the walls. It comes from below the floor. Somewhere in the deep architecture, vast processes are running, and the concourse is, among other things, a *resonator*. The hum of the dark fist-objects is the smallest voice in a chorus that runs down through octaves you can feel in your sternum. Standing waves move across the floor; in some spots the air shivers visibly, and dust — there is dust, fine and warm — lifts and hangs in those nodes like suspended weather.

To speak here, you would have to shout. No one shouts.

---

Water is sparse but present, and the water is the strange part — stranger than the architecture, which after all only does what advanced architecture does.

There are seeps. From certain joints in the wall, where two impossible surfaces meet at an angle that the eye refuses to commit to, water beads and runs. It is warm. It tastes of iron and something sweeter. Where it pools on the floor, it pools in shapes that are too regular: ovals, mostly, with very slightly raised rims, as if the floor were subtly accommodating the water rather than the water finding low ground. The pools last for hours and then are gone — drunk back into the floor, or evaporated into the warm dim air to condense again somewhere distant. There is, somewhere, a cycle. You hear it in the lowest registers of the roar: a slow tidal note, perhaps once every twenty minutes, a great inhalation.

By each pool, something grows. This is the vegetation, such as it is. Pale stalks, jointed, no taller than a child's hand, with no leaves but instead small translucent bulbs that catch the floor-light and pass it on, faintly, to the next stalk. They cluster only at the pools. They are sparse because the pools are sparse. When a pool dries, the stalks fold themselves flat against the floor and wait — they can wait a long time, you sense — for the seep to return.

---

The fauna, when you finally see one, is unhurried.

Something the size of a cat moves between the plinths. It has too many legs, or the right number arranged wrongly, and its body is the same wet-obsidian as the ceiling, so it appears mostly as a moving absence in the dim red light. It goes to a pool. It drinks. It regards the stalks without disturbing them. Then it walks, in no rush, into the dark between plinths and is gone.

There are perhaps a dozen of these in the whole concourse. Perhaps fewer. They do not seem to compete; they do not seem to mate; what they do with their long warm lives is unclear. They may be old. They may be *very* old — old in the way the concourse itself is old, settled into their form, weathered into balance.

---

The whole place has the quality of something that has been here long enough to have forgotten its own purpose, but not long enough to have stopped functioning. The roar continues. The water cycles. The stalks wait. The dim cat-things drink. The dark fist-objects hum on their plinths, doing — what? Computing? Remembering? Praying? You won't find out by looking at them. They have been doing it since before there was anyone who could have asked.

If you sit down — and eventually you will sit down, because the warmth and the roar and the red light combine into something that suggests sitting — you will feel the floor breathing beneath you. Slow. Patient. The inhalation that draws the water back. The exhalation that warms the air.

You came down a shaft. The shaft is still there, behind you, when you look. The concourse is not a trap. It simply doesn't *need* you to leave, or to stay, or to do anything at all. It is complete without you, has been complete for a duration that makes the word *long* embarrassing, and your visit is a kind of weather passing through it — noticed, perhaps, by the cat-things; unnoticed by everything else.

You can stay as long as you like.

The warmth is the temperature of being held, and the held thing is not you.
