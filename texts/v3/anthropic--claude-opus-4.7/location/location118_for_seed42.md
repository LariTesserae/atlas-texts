# The Hanging Town of Ceiba-Under-Cloud

High on the shoulder of a mountain that has no name below its own mist-line, the town sits inside a single tree — or inside what the people here no longer call a tree, because the distinction stopped being useful some centuries ago. It is a living mass the size of a cathedral, a ceiba grown enormous on a flat saddle of rock, its crown flattened by altitude wind into a plateau of branches so dense and interwoven that one walks across it as across a floor. The floor is the town.

Everything is wet. Not rained on — wet in the submerged sense, the sense a sponge understands. The town sits inside the cloud that lives permanently on this shoulder of mountain. Visibility is twenty paces, thirty on good mornings, and the air is not breathed so much as sipped. Water beads on every leaf and every rope and every face. Moss eats the edges of every surface. The wood stays dark. Cloth never fully dries; the weavers have adapted, and make no cloth that fears rot. When you lift your hand before your eyes the skin of your palm glistens as if just pulled from a stream.

It is cool here, not cold — the kind of cool that numbs the knuckles by afternoon but never the chest. One wears layered bark-cloth, greased. One sleeps close.

The town has perhaps forty dwellings and they are sparse, scattered across the great living platform with long spans of empty leaf-floor between them. A person can walk for two or three minutes between neighbors and see no sign of other humans, only the moving shapes of birds in the mist and the slow dripping of the canopy onto the canopy. Each dwelling is woven: living branches bent young and held in place with hemp and wooden pegs until they grew into the shape of a room. Walls are lattice-work of twig and vine, packed with moss for warmth. Roofs are the tree's own leaves, overlapping. The whole settlement is grown, not built — or rather, built by patience, by generations of bending and tying, by hand-tools of stone and bone and a little cold-worked copper for the finer joinery. There is no metal smelting here. There are no wheels. There are ladders and platforms and rope bridges across the fissures where the great branches part.

And the sound — the sound is the first thing a visitor would write home about. The cloud-forest at this altitude is loud. Ceaseless. The dripping alone is a percussion section, a thousand small wet impacts per second spread across the canopy, each one a different pitch according to what leaf it strikes and how full. Beneath that, the deeper note of water running inside the tree itself, pulled up by capillary action through trunks you could not encircle with six people's arms. And above and through everything, the birds — because this is where the birds live, in such numbers that a census would be a form of madness. Parrots of four species argue in the middle canopy. A kind of high-altitude toucan cracks seeds with a sound like a wooden mallet. Hummingbirds, three dozen kinds, make the air near any flower into a static hum. Frogs in every leaf-axil calling for rain they already have. Cicadas that do not stop. A troop of small woolly monkeys, endemic perhaps to this single tree, moves along the upper branches and hoots when anything surprises them, which is often.

The townspeople speak loudly as a matter of course. Whispering does not exist as a social register here; it would be inaudible. Children learn to pitch their voices through the bird-noise before they learn to walk the branches without slipping.

The ecology of the tree-town has settled. This is what the parameters of long weathering mean here: it is not that the place is damaged or eroded in the geological sense, but that everything has found its position. The moss knows which bark it prefers. The epiphytic orchids have arranged themselves on the branches in a pattern no one designed and no one disturbs. The human dwellings were placed generations ago and have not moved; when a family ends, its house is allowed to dissolve back into the tree, slowly, over decades, and a new house is grown elsewhere on the platform. The rhythm of this is slow enough that a person might live a whole life and see only one or two dwellings go and one or two come. Change here is measured in the lifespans of epiphytes.

There is one rule of the world that should be stated. The tree is larger than it ought to be. A ceiba does not grow this large, and does not grow at this altitude, and does not grow flat-topped and platformed as a consequence of wind alone. The people of the town know this and do not think much of it. The tree is the tree. It drinks the cloud. Its roots, which descend the mountain's flank for some unknown distance, are said by the old women to reach water that is not the mountain's water. This is the one strangeness of the place, and it is a quiet strangeness — you would not notice it unless you knew how trees are supposed to behave. The town does not venerate the tree in any elaborated way. They live in it. They drink its sap in the dry months, when there are dry months, which is rarely. They bury their dead in the crotches of its high branches where the monkeys do not go, and over the years the bodies become part of the tree's slow uptake, and this is not considered grim, only accurate.

The mist thickens toward evening. Lanterns are lit — small clay cups of rendered fat, wicks of twisted fiber — and they do not illuminate much, only make each dwelling into a soft orange smear in the grey. Between the dwellings the dark fills in, and the birds quiet by degrees, and the frogs take over, and the dripping continues as it has continued for as long as the tree has stood, which is longer than any memory. A person walking home across the leaf-floor in the late light sees their own breath and hears it, very faintly, under everything else, and feels the great slow pulse of the tree moving water up through itself toward the cloud that is already water, and understands without needing to say so that they are inside a single slow creature that is drinking the sky.
