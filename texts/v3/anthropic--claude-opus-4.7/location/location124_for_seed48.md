# The Sulfur Flats

The city sits on a plain of cracked mud that never fully dries. Water comes up from below — the whole basin is underlain by some hot aquifer, and the ground weeps. You can press your palm flat to the packed earth between buildings and feel it warm and damp, leaving the print of your hand behind in a darker shade until the heat draws the moisture back up. In the low places, shallow pans of standing water sit for weeks, skimmed with mineral crusts, pale yellow and rust-red at the edges. There is no green anywhere. There was never any green here; the water is too mineral, the ground too hot, the air carries too much dissolved sulfur. What grows is chemical — efflorescences, salt gardens, the slow lace of gypsum on old brickwork.

The town is large. Not a city exactly — it sprawls too low and loose for that — but settlement-scale, a working place, laid out along the banks of the hot shallow river that wanders the basin. Brick kilns, evaporation works, long sheds of corrugated iron gone the color of dried blood. Smokestacks in rows, most of them only half-smoking at any given time, their plumes rising lazy into a sky that is permanently the color of weak tea. The light is thick. It filters through the mineral haze and the steam off the pans, and even at midday everything sits in a kind of bronze half-shadow. You can look directly at the sun; it hangs there like a coin of tarnished brass.

The buildings are nearly empty of people. This is the strange thing. Walk any of the streets — brick, cobble, slabbed stone sweating underfoot — and you'll pass block after block of warehouses, boiler-houses, pumping stations, and see perhaps one man in an oilcloth apron leaning in a doorway, perhaps no one at all. The town was built for more than it holds. Doors hang open on hinges that have rusted in place. The machines run anyway. You hear them breathing — the long slow gasp of a steam engine somewhere two streets over, the clank of a flywheel, the hiss of a relief valve — but the operators are elsewhere, or fewer than the works require, or simply don't need to be present. Whole districts hum to themselves.

What fills the space instead is the fauna.

The flats are alive with flies. Not pests exactly — the local word is something like *brinecatchers*, little iridescent things that breed in the hot pans by the billion, rising at dusk in columns tall as the smokestacks. And where there are flies in that number, there are the things that eat them: lizards on every wall, small and fat and the color of the brick, so still you don't see them until they move, and then the wall seems to pour. Birds that look like thin white egrets but aren't — something older, stilt-legged, wading the pans with surgical patience, their beaks clicking. Rats in the cellars and the culverts, not starving rats but fat ones, confident, the owners of the streets after dark. And at the river, the long pale eels that come up through the hot springs from wherever they spawn below, and the otters or otter-shaped things that hunt them, and the dog-sized scavengers that come for what the otters leave.

The town is quiet because the town is not really the point. The town is a crust on a basin that belongs to its animals. Men tend the kilns and the evaporation pans and cart out blocks of soda and sulfur cake and the red earth-pigment the works produce, and the animals tolerate them the way a forest tolerates woodcutters — present, permitted, outnumbered.

Dynamic: the weather cycles on a daily clock. Morning is the clearest — a cool dawn, relatively, when the haze has settled and you can see across the basin to the far line of low hills, brown and bare. By mid-morning the pans begin to steam and the air thickens. Noon is the bronze hour, still and heavy, when even the flies quiet and the lizards go flat against the warm stones. Afternoon brings the wind, dry and sour, lifting dust from the flats and salt from the crusts, and the sky goes the color of an old bruise. Most evenings a brief hard rain falls — ten minutes, perhaps, of fat warm drops that strike the dust into craters and release a smell like struck matches and wet iron. Then the flies rise. Then the lizards wake. Then the long pale birds step out of wherever they spent the heat of the day, and the basin begins its second life, its night life, which is louder than the day but still, somehow, not loud — the sounds are small and distributed, a chorus of ten thousand tiny mouths rather than any single voice.

The erosion has had a long time to work here. Nothing is new. The brickwork of the oldest kilns has weathered to a kind of sculpted softness, corners gone round, mortar washed out and replaced with mineral deposit that glitters faintly when the light hits right. The streets dip and rise where the ground has settled. Some of the buildings lean. None have fallen — the leaning is itself established, old, part of how the place is. A wall that has leaned for eighty years is not falling; it is simply that shape now. The town has arrived at its condition and rests in it.

There is no strangeness here. Everything obeys familiar rules — heat rises, water flows downhill, animals eat and are eaten, iron rusts, brick crumbles, men work and go home to wherever they go home to, which in this town is mostly the low rowhouses along the eastern bank where at night you can see a scatter of oil-lamp windows, small and yellow, like a handful of coins thrown down on a dark cloth. The lamps go out early. The town sleeps, if it sleeps, under the sound of its own machinery and the soft uncountable movement of everything that is not human and is not asleep.

If you stood on one of the kiln roofs at the bronze hour and looked out — across the pans, the river, the long low roofs, the stacks with their slow tired smoke, the brown hills beyond — you would see a place that has worked out its arrangement with itself. Hot, wet, mineral, crowded with small lives and empty of large ones, industrial without urgency, settled into its own long patient version of being.
