# The Condensing City of Vost-Under-Ice

At this altitude the air itself is a slow liquid. You breathe it and something goes into your lungs that is not quite vapor and not quite rain — a hesitation between states, the hinge moment of becoming water. Everything here is wet in the way skin is wet a second after you step out of a bath: not dripping, but glazed, counting the seconds before it decides what it wants to be.

The city fits in a cirque. A single pocket of mountain, scooped by something old and patient, no more than a thousand paces across at its widest. You could walk its circumference in an hour if the streets permitted walking, which they mostly do not.

## The stone

The cirque walls are banded limestone and some paler thing — call it bonestone, since the locals do — and they weep. Not dramatically. A shine, a slick, a slow glassy sheet that moves down the rock at roughly the pace of an hour hand. The rock is rounded at every edge. Nothing here has a corner. Erosion has been at work so long and so gently that the cliff-faces are smooth as the insides of throats, fluted in places into long vertical grooves where the meltwater prefers to travel. Put a hand on the wall and the wall is cold and moving, and your hand comes away wet and numb and smelling very faintly of iron.

## The light

The light is the thing that undoes you first. At this altitude, with the air saturated, sunlight does not arrive in beams — it arrives diffused through the suspended water until the entire cirque is a lantern. No shadows. Or rather: shadows on every surface equally, which is the same as none. The brightness is flat, absolute, and comes from every direction at once, so that looking up is the same as looking down is the same as looking into the wet bonestone wall. People squint not against glare but against the sheer evenness of it. There is nowhere for the eye to rest.

At the same time, the light is *cold*. It doesn't warm what it touches. Whatever the sun is doing up there, it is not warming Vost.

## The buildings

Now the density. The cirque holds perhaps four hundred structures, and they are built into, onto, and out of each other in a way that suggests no single architect and no single century. Stacked. Cantilevered. Ledges growing from ledges. The oldest construction is just carved niches in the bonestone, smoothed by generations of hands; the next generation built lean-tos of slate against those niches; the next built second stories on the lean-tos; and so on, upward and inward, until the cirque is more built than un-built, and the sky visible from any given doorway is a jagged scrap.

The materials are what the place offers: slate in blue-grey sheets, bonestone blocks cut with copper saws (there is a little copper, smelted in small clay furnaces at the cirque's lip), rope made from the long dark hair-fiber of the grazing beasts, and a kind of waxed felt that sheds water while letting breath pass. No glass. Windows are either open or stuffed with felt-plugs or covered by thin-scraped hide that glows amber when the sun is behind it.

Every roof has a channel. Every channel leads to a cistern. Every cistern overflows into another channel that leads to another cistern lower down. The entire city is a plumbing diagram. You could trace a single drop of condensation from the highest eave to the lowest catch-basin and it would pass through forty household economies on the way.

## The fauna

And the animals. This is where the numbers start doing something strange.

Abundant, at this altitude, in this cold — it shouldn't work, but it does, because the wet air carries life the way a river carries silt. The cirque is full of *flyers*. Not birds, exactly. Something smaller and more numerous, with translucent wings that beat too fast to see, that drink directly from the condensing air by opening their mouths and letting the atmosphere fog onto their tongues. They come in clouds. They hang in the air in the thousands, holding position, feeding on nothing visible, and when a breeze pushes through the cirque they move as one body, a silver shift in the brightness.

There are the grazing beasts, which the people call *shoulder-ewes* — six-legged, long-haired, no eyes where you'd expect eyes but a kind of sensitive pad across the forehead. They crop the sparse vegetation from the cirque floor: lichens mostly, a few hardy sedges, and a single species of pale flower that grows only where a person has died, which is a fact the people accept without sentiment.

There are the cistern-things. Every cistern has one. Something pale and articulated that keeps the water clean and is fed scraps and is considered a member of the household and is never, ever taken out of its cistern, even in death. When one dies, the cistern is sealed and a new one is dug.

And in the walls, in the weeping bonestone itself, there are the *listeners* — small, soft, slow creatures that live in the rock the way barnacles live in tidepools, except these have faces, sort of, and they are watching. The locals do not discuss them.

## The sound

And here is where the parameters really start to hum against each other: all of this abundance, all of this turbulence, all of this built density and animal presence — and the place is *quiet*.

The wet air swallows sound. Voices carry about three paces and then die. The flyers' wings, at their density, should be a roar; they are a whisper like silk on silk. Footsteps on wet slate make no ring, only a soft compression. When someone in Vost wants to be heard across a street they do not raise their voice; they use their hands, or they wait until evening when the air thins slightly and speech travels a little further.

The city is therefore constantly busy and constantly hushed, which produces the strangest effect: you see the turbulence — people moving, trading, hauling, the flyers shifting, the cistern-things surfacing and submerging, water sheeting down every wall, felt being shaken out, children running — you see all of this as through thick glass, soundless. A crowded silent film. The dynamic is entirely visual and tactile. The sonic dimension has been pressed flat by the humidity and the cold.

## The turbulence

Because make no mistake, it is turbulent. Things change hour by hour in Vost. A stall in the lower market is a house by evening and a ruin by morning. The cirque's micro-weather produces fogs that roll through in minutes and rearrange what can be seen; a neighborhood you knew at breakfast is a different neighborhood at dusk because the angle of wet light has changed, or because the flyers have settled on the north wall and made it silver, or because a cistern has overflowed and a whole stair is now a waterfall until someone redirects it, which someone will, within the hour. The people are always building, always unbuilding, always moving stones and felt and rope. No structure in Vost is older than its inhabitants' great-grandparents, though the *site* of every structure is ancient. The city is in a permanent slow storm of self-revision.

And yet — erosion at 2.3, established, settled — the *form* of the city is stable even as every component churns. The cirque wants a city of this shape. The water runs this way. The light falls this way. The flyers prefer these updrafts. So whatever is built gets pulled, eventually, into the shape the place already knows. You could flatten Vost tomorrow and in fifty years it would look the same, made of different stones, by different hands.

## The weirdness

Some things I have not accounted for, which simply are:

The flowers grow where people die, yes, but they also grow where people have *decided* something. A marriage-agreement leaves a flower. A formal forgiveness leaves a flower. The shoulder-ewes eat these with particular attention.

On certain mornings, when the condensation is at its heaviest, the air in the cirque will hold an image — a face, a hand, a shape of a person — for several seconds before dissolving. No one claims to know what these are. The people call them *thoughts-the-mountain-is-having* and do not interrupt them.

The copper, when freshly smelted, sings for about a minute as it cools. This is the loudest sound in Vost. Children are brought to hear it.

And at the very top of the cirque, where the walls close to a narrow slot of sky, there is a single thread of water that has been falling from the same point, into the same catch-basin, for as long as anyone has records. It does not freeze. It does not stop. It is the axis around which the whole small turbulent silent bright cold wet dense city turns.
