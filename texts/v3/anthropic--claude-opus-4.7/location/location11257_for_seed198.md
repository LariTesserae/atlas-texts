# The Terraces of Hunch-on-Vyr

It is the hour before the shift-bells, and the city is breathing.

Hunch-on-Vyr climbs the eastern flank of a mountain whose proper name no one uses anymore — call it the Mother, call it the Slope, call it nothing. From its lower fog to its upper smokeline the city occupies every available square of stone, and it has been doing so for so long that there is no longer any stone, properly speaking, only the city's foundations laid on older foundations laid on older still, brick-on-brick going down past memory. If you drove a pile here you would not find earth for a hundred feet. You would find walls, floors, the soot-glazed roofs of streets that were buried when the next terrace was built above them.

The whole city is terraced. Each terrace is perhaps twenty or thirty feet higher than the one below, retained by walls of dark sweating brick, and on each terrace stand the works: the dye-houses, the wool-mills, the brass foundries, the tanneries with their long bone-roofed sheds, the engine-rooms whose flywheels you can feel before you can see — a pulse coming up through your boots. Between the works, packed in wherever there is a crack, are the tenements and lodging-houses and bridge-flats where the workers live, and where there is not room for a building there is a stair, and where there is not room for a stair there is a ladder bolted to a wall, and where there is not room for a ladder there is a rope, and people use it.

Density is the first thing you understand here. There is no gap. The eye looking for sky finds laundry-line, finds smokestack, finds the underside of a flying buttress built between two factories to keep them both from sliding. The eye looking for ground finds slate, finds iron grate, finds the green-black water of a sluice-channel running between mill and mill. Every surface is in use. The walls grow gardens — slatted boxes of beans and pale potatoes pegged to the brickwork wherever a stripe of leaked light might reach them. The cooler upper terraces are choked with moss, with fern, with the soft pale-green hair of saxifrage in every joint and seam. Rhubarb hangs from window-boxes the size of coffins. Hop-vines climb the smokestacks. The city is half-eaten by its own vegetable life, and the vegetable life is half-poisoned by the city, and the bargain holds.

It is wet here. The Mother condenses everything. The clouds belong to the upper terraces — sometimes the top of the city is simply gone, you cannot see it, there is only a grey wall above the seventh row of roofs, and water comes down from that wall not as rain exactly but as a slow continuous weeping. The bricks weep. The retaining walls weep. The iron rails of the haul-tracks are always beaded. Every roof has a gutter, every gutter feeds a tank, every tank feeds a wheel, and the wheels are the city's other heart — wooden overshot wheels, iron breastshot wheels, and on the great terraces the high pressure turbines with their steam-trails. Water made the city possible. Water still runs it. You can hear it always at the edge of hearing: a low chuckle and trickle, a far-off rush where a sluice opens, the patient *tip... tip... tip* of something dripping from a flue onto a copper hood three streets away.

But the city is not loud. This is the strange thing. With all this industry you would expect a roar, and there is no roar. The engines here are old, established engines, their bearings perfectly worn-in, their belts soft with decades of grease. They turn quietly. The work is done largely with hand and shuttle and the long careful motions of people who have done this work since their grandparents did it. There is a hum, yes — a vast low hum that lives in the brickwork and in your teeth — but it does not interrupt conversation. People speak quietly. The fog absorbs sound. The terraces baffle it. You can stand at the rail of the Sixth and hear, somewhere far below, a woman calling her child in for supper.

The light is dim. It is always dim. The sun, when it exists, has to come in slantwise across the mountain's shoulder, and by the time it reaches the working terraces it has been through smoke and cloud and the slat-shadow of a thousand washing lines. Inside the workshops they burn gas — a soft yellow gas piped up from somewhere in the city's bowels — and the windows of Hunch-on-Vyr glow at all hours with that warm sallow light, a hundred thousand small squares of it ranked up the mountainside in the early evening like a cliff full of bees. From a distance, in the right weather, the city looks like a hive going to sleep.

Animals are scarce. There are no rats, which surprises visitors; the city is too damp and too vigilant and too full of cats, though the cats themselves are few — long, thin, smoke-colored, paid in scraps. A particular swift nests in the smokestacks and rides the updrafts at dusk in numbers that thin every year. Pigeons, almost none — they need open grain. Dogs go with the haul-teams on the lower freight-roads. In the wall-gardens you sometimes see a slow green beetle. That is most of the fauna. The city has crowded everything else out.

Now: the turbulence. This is what an outside eye misses, because the buildings have stood for three hundred years and the engines turn at their old patient rate and nothing seems to move. But Hunch-on-Vyr is in storm always, and the storm is internal. The shift-bells ring at strange times — not at the hour, but at the demand of the works — and when they ring, the city *changes*. Twenty thousand people pour out of the tenements and up the stairs and down the ladders and along the rope-bridges, and another twenty thousand pour the other way, and the two streams cross in the stairwells and on the terrace-bridges and somehow do not collide, and in the space of fifteen minutes the entire population of the city has rearranged itself, and the gas-windows of one quarter go dark while another quarter blooms.

The market is the same. The bazaar of the Fourth Terrace is laid out fresh each dawn and broken down each dusk and laid out again differently the next day; the stalls follow no plan, they grow toward each other and away again like a slime mold, and what was a fish-row Monday is a button-row Tuesday and a place to buy old engine-belts on Wednesday. Goods come in by funicular from the river-foot below and by mule from the high passes above and they pass through Hunch-on-Vyr on their way somewhere else, and the city takes a tithe of everything that passes — a button here, a length of wire there, a measure of salt — and so it is always being reconstituted from the flow of stuff going through it.

Even the buildings shift. Not visibly — but a roof is patched, a wall is shored, a buttress is added, a stair is rebuilt three feet to the left of where it used to be, and the maps are useless after a season. Old people get lost in the streets they grew up on. The terraces themselves have crept, over centuries, slightly downhill; the lower retaining walls bulge slightly outward where the weight has settled; new walls have been built inside the old ones, doubling them; the city is a thing in slow constant motion, a tectonic civic restlessness, and it never finishes adjusting itself to the Mother's slope.

If you stand at the parapet of the Ninth in the long blue evening and look down, you see: the terraces falling away in their dim ranks, gas-light pricking on one by one, smoke standing up white against the dark wet brick, here and there the orange glow of an open furnace door briefly opened and shut, the slow turn of a wheel in a courtyard, a single figure crossing a high bridge with a lantern. You hear: water, water, water, and under it the city's hum, and somewhere a hammer at quiet work, and somewhere else, very faint, someone singing in a language you don't quite catch.

The place has been here a long time. It will be here a long time more. It does not love you, but it has room for you. There is always room. Density, in Hunch-on-Vyr, is hospitable — it has had so much practice at fitting one more thing in.
