# The Cold Greenhouses of the Northern Cut

You come into it through a service tunnel, because there is no other way in — the place sits maybe forty meters below the surface of the moor, a long trough carved out of the bedrock and roofed over so long ago that the roof has become part of the moor itself, mossed and forgotten. The locals call the whole valley-under-the-valley the Cut, though almost no one goes down anymore.

The first thing is the noise. Before your eyes adjust to the dimness, the sound has already taken up all the space inside your skull. It is the sound of the pumps. Six of them, somewhere — you can't see them yet — driven by belts off a single great flywheel that turns somewhere deeper in. The pumps do two things: they push cold air through the Cut to keep it near freezing, and they push thin mist along the length of it through perforated iron pipes that run the ceiling. The noise of the pumps is the noise of the place. It is not a hum or a thrum; it is a hammering, a battering, a sound you feel in your sternum and your back teeth, and it does not stop, has not stopped in perhaps a hundred years. You learn within a minute that speech is useless here. Workers, if there are any left, would have used hand signs and chalk.

Your eyes adjust. The Cut opens.

It is enormous — a kilometer at least, maybe two, running north under the moor, vaulted with brick arches blackened by a century of lamp soot. The lamps still burn: small electric bulbs in cages, spaced perhaps every twenty meters, throwing a weak yellow light that does not so much illuminate the place as suggest its dimensions. Between the lamps, long stretches of half-dark.

And everything — everything — is plants.

Not a forest. Not a garden. Something stranger: a cold-loving moss-and-fern ecology that has been cultivated, originally, for some industrial purpose — pharmaceutical, perhaps, or a dye, or a binding agent — and then left to itself when the works wound down. The mosses have taken the iron racks that once held trays. They have taken the trays themselves, the walls, the floor between the rails of the narrow-gauge track that runs the length of the Cut. They have taken the pipes and the lamp cages and the chains that hang from the ceiling. The mist from the pumps keeps them constantly damp; the cold keeps them slow; the dim light keeps them pale. They are the substrate now. They are the architecture. Every surface in the Cut is upholstered in a deep, cold, breathing green that is closer to grey than to grass.

It is sparse in the way a cathedral is sparse — the mass is enormous but the volume is mostly air. Between the great mossed columns of the old racks, you can see for a hundred meters. The ferns grow in clusters, not thickets: one stand here, an empty floor of moss for thirty paces, another stand. There is room. The Cut does not feel crowded. It feels patient.

The water is everywhere and nowhere. There is no river, no pool. But the air is wet, and the mist from the pipes condenses on the colder iron and runs down in slow beads, and every so often a fat drop falls from the ceiling with an impact you cannot hear over the pumps but can sometimes see, hitting moss and being absorbed. The floor is damp but not flooded. Drainage channels still work, somewhere.

Fauna: almost none. A pale moth, once, fluttering past a lamp — a species adapted, somehow, to the cold and the dark and the unending noise. The deafness must be a defense; perhaps it cannot hear anything anyway. There are probably springtails in the moss, and probably nothing larger. No birds could nest here. No mammal would tolerate the sound.

Erosion is young because the place is young: the brick is still sharp at its edges where moss hasn't claimed it, the iron racks have rusted but not collapsed, the rails are pitted but unbroken. A century, no more. The Cut has not had time to fall apart. It is in that early stage of abandonment where everything still works, more or less, and nothing has yet given way.

The built things — the lamps, the rails, the pumps, the pipes, the racks, the long brick vault itself — are the bones the moss has dressed. They are not picturesque ruins. They are functioning industrial equipment in slow surrender. Somewhere at the far end of the Cut, the flywheel turns. Somewhere a coal hopper still feeds, or an electric feed from the surface still draws. Nobody has shut it off because nobody now knows how, or nobody has decided to, or the shutting off would itself be a project requiring people willing to come down here and stand in the noise long enough to do it.

The rules of this place, as far as you can work them out in the first hour:

The pumps will not stop. This is the central fact. Everything else proceeds from it. The cold continues because the pumps continue. The mist continues. The moss thrives because of the mist and the cold. If the pumps stopped — if some bearing finally seized, some belt finally parted — the Cut would warm, the mist would fail, and the moss would, over years, brown and recede. But the pumps have not stopped.

Change here is geological in pace. A fern advances a centimeter a year. A brick loosens over a decade. The lamp bulbs burn out one by one and are not replaced, and the dimness slowly deepens, and in another fifty years perhaps the Cut will be wholly dark and the moss will continue anyway, because moss does not need much.

You walk north along the rails. The noise does not lessen. The green does not lessen. The lamps recede behind you and new lamps appear ahead, identical, and you understand that the Cut is not a place that has features so much as a place that has a length — that to describe it is to describe the same square meter, more or less, repeated for a kilometer, with small variations: here a collapsed rack, here a denser stand of ferns, here a section where the ceiling drips more freely and the moss is almost black with wet.

It is not a hostile place. It is indifferent at a scale that feels close to hostility, but it is not hostile. It is simply a machine that has been left running inside a cold wet stone box, and the wet cold stone box has filled, slowly and without urgency, with the kind of life that likes wet cold stone boxes with machines hammering in them.

When you finally turn back — and you will turn back; nobody stays in the Cut long, because the noise eventually drives you out even if nothing else does — you will find that the silence of the surface, when you climb the service tunnel back to the moor, is the strangest thing you have ever heard. You will hear it ringing for days. And the green will stay behind you, breathing slowly under the heather, and the pumps will keep on.
