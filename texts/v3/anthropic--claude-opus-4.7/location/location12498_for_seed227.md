# The Bell-Hollow

There is a fold in the low hills where the ground gives way — not a cave exactly, more a long sunken room of stone that the land grew around and forgot to close. You come into it down a slope of black loam, between birches that lean toward each other without quite touching, and the cold hits you first: a settled cold, the kind that lives in stones that have not seen sun in a thousand years.

The roof of the hollow is open in places. Slits between the rock-shelves let down threads of grey light — not enough to read by, barely enough to see the shape of your own hand. Most of the chamber stays dim. Moss has had its long way with everything: the walls are upholstered in it, ankle-deep in places, every shade from near-black to a wet pale green where the slow drip from above keeps it fed. Ferns lean out of cracks. A single rowan has rooted in the rubble and grown crooked, reaching for one of the light-slits, its berries dulled to almost-black in the gloom.

And the sound.

The hollow is one enormous resonator. Somewhere — you cannot tell where, exactly, and this is part of it — water falls. Not a great volume of water; the place is not wet so much as damp, the air just below saturation, condensation beading on every cold surface. But the few drops that do fall, fall far, and strike stone that has been tuned by ten thousand years of patient erosion into something like a bell. Each drop detonates. The note hangs and hangs, layering over the next drop, and the next, until the air itself feels under pressure. You cannot speak here and be heard by yourself. You cannot think in sentences. The sound is not loud the way a storm is loud — it is loud the way standing inside a struck bell is loud, a sustained pure deafening that displaces the contents of your skull.

The animals have made their accommodations. There are not many. A single white owl roosts on a ledge near one of the light-slits, and she has gone deaf in the way of all things that live here long — she hunts by the shudder of air against her face-disk, by the displacement of mice through moss. The mice themselves are silent and slow, fattened on fungus, untroubled by the bell-note because they were born inside it. A few pale beetles. Nothing else moves.

People have been here. Not many, not often, not recently. On one wall, where the stone is dry enough to take a mark, someone has scratched a row of small notched figures in red ochre — a tally, perhaps, or a calendar, the count of returns. The pigment has bled and softened into the rock; the marks are old. Near the deepest part of the hollow, half-sunk in moss, lies a wooden bowl, hand-cut, the rim worn smooth by use and then by neglect. A bone awl. A flat stone that has clearly been a hearth, though no fire has been lit on it in a long time — the soot is mineralised, part of the stone now. Whoever came here came with simple things in their hands and came rarely, and stayed only as long as a body can stand the sound. There is no shelter built. Only the marks of having sat, and listened, and gone again.

The rules of the place are few and slow.

The water finds its way down by routes it established before any human walked the land above, and it will not be hurried or diverted. The moss advances perhaps a finger's width in a generation. The bell-note changes pitch, very gradually, as the resonant stone wears thinner — a person returning after forty years would hear the hollow singing a fraction lower than they remembered, and might not know why their chest ached at the sound. The owl is replaced, when she dies, by another owl, who is also deaf, and also white, and the mice do not question this.

It is not a place that wants anything from you. It does not threaten. It does not invite. It has been ringing to itself in the cold and the dark for longer than your language has existed, and when you climb back out through the birches and the sound falls away behind you, you will find that for an hour or so afterward the ordinary world seems thin — as though you had been pressed against something dense and real, and have now returned to the version of things that humans agreed on, which is quieter, and lighter, and less.
