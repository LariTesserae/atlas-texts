# The Sump Grove

Twenty feet below the surface of what must once have been a floodplain, there is a chamber the size of a modest chapel, and it is alive.

You enter — if you enter at all — by one of the three root-throats that slope down from above. The roots are as thick as a person's waist, pale as peeled apples in their deepest reaches, and they have learned to be ladders. Someone taught them, or they taught themselves. Footholds have been worn smooth into their undersides, and at the bottom of the northernmost throat there is a single step cut from limestone, placed there maybe two hundred years ago, now cupped by a skin of moss so thorough you only know the stone is there because your foot finds its edge.

The chamber itself is hot. Not desert-hot — wet-hot, close-hot, the heat of a kept greenhouse or a sleeping animal's flank. It comes from below: somewhere under the floor there is a thermal seep, and the whole room breathes it up through the clay. The air is heavy enough that your breath doesn't travel. Words fall near your mouth.

The ceiling is a ceiling of roots. Not just the three entry-throats but a whole braided mat of them, fig-like, descending in curtains, some reaching the floor and thickening into secondary trunks so that the chamber is pillared. Between the pillars, the light — and there is light, a dim green-gold light — filters down through lensings of water held in upper pockets of the root-mat, so that the illumination shifts when something above moves. A deer crossing the surface meadow will send a slow ripple of shadow across the chamber floor a full second later.

The floor is not flat. It dishes toward the center, where a pool sits — not much larger than a bathtub, lipped with calcium deposits that have been building at the pace of fingernails for longer than there have been names for anything. The pool is warm. Things live in it: blind, pale shrimp the length of eyelashes; a single kind of snail with a shell like a drop of honey; and — this is the thing you notice last and then cannot stop noticing — a population of small frogs, thumbnail-sized, translucent enough that you can see the dark commas of their organs. They do not croak. They click, very softly, once every few minutes, and the clicks seem to be coordinated, passing around the chamber in a slow rotation like a clock's tick made of animals.

That is most of the sound. The clicks; the occasional plick of condensation falling from a root-tip into the pool or onto a leaf; far above and faint, the muffled report of weather happening in the world. Rain, when it comes, announces itself first as a change in the quality of silence — a thickening — and then as a slow intensification of the dripping, until for perhaps an hour the chamber is full of a soft irregular percussion and the pool rises by the width of two fingers and the frogs click faster. Then it passes, and the drip slows over days.

The walls are earth held together by smaller roots and by the mycelial felting of something white and cool to the touch. In places the felting has grown thick enough to be sculptural — shelves, folds, something that looks almost like a face if you stare, though of course it isn't. Bracket fungi the color of raw liver step up the eastern wall in a staircase no human could climb. A kind of pale fern with fronds the size of a hand grows wherever the light-lenses concentrate, and these ferns are where most of the insect life clusters: a dense, slow-moving congregation of long-legged crickets that do not jump, millipedes the color of wet pennies, and a single species of moth, wings the gray of old paper, that rests on the undersides of fronds with wings spread flat and does not seem to fly often. When one does fly, it is a startling event — a pale flutter crossing the chamber — and the frogs go briefly silent.

There are marks of people, but not many, and not recent. The limestone step. A niche cut into the western wall at about chest height, shallow, holding nothing now but a smear of beeswax gone almost black — something was kept here, or offered here, long enough ago that the wax has fossilized into the niche's floor. On one of the root-pillars, at the height a sitting person's hand would rest, the bark is worn smooth and slightly concave: someone, or generations of someones, sat against this pillar enough times to polish it. No carvings. No names. Whoever used this place either didn't write or didn't want to.

The ecology is tight and working. The frogs eat the crickets and the moths; the shrimp eat what falls into the pool, including dead frogs; the snails graze the felting on the walls; the felting digests the fallen leaves that come down through the root-throats in autumn; the roots drink from the pool and from the damp clay and send sugars up to the trees above, which the chamber has never seen. Nothing here is large. Nothing here is loud. The biggest animal is a single olm-like salamander, almost a foot long, pale as the roots, that lives in a crevice under the pool's lip and emerges perhaps twice a month. It has been in this chamber, possibly, for fifty years. It may be the oldest continuous resident.

The unusual thing — and there is one, there is always one — is this: the clicks of the frogs, rotating around the chamber, are not quite random. Over the course of an hour, if you sit still and listen, the pattern tightens into something like a pulse, and the pulse matches, approximately, the pulse you can feel in your own wrist if you press your fingers there. It is not exact. It is not supernatural. It is probably only that the frogs are responding to the heat-cycles of the seep, and the seep is responding to the atmospheric pressure, and your heart is also responding to the atmospheric pressure, and so you and the frogs and the warm water under the floor are all, loosely, keeping the same slow time. But it doesn't feel like that while you are sitting there. It feels like the room is listening back.

People who have found this place have, on the whole, left it alone. That is the rule of the world here, insofar as there is one: the chamber rewards stillness and punishes nothing, but takes a long time to forgive disturbance. Break a fern and the ferns are a little sparser for ten years. Muddy the pool and the shrimp vanish for a season. The grove keeps its own slow books.

Above, the meadow goes on with its business, unaware. The deer cross. The rain comes and goes. And below, in the hot green dim, the frogs click, and the salamander waits in its crevice, and the roots drink, and the chamber — established, in balance, in no hurry — continues being exactly what it is.
