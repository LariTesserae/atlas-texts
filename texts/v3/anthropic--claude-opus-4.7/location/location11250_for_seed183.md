# The Weeping Cordillera

Begin high. You are already high — this is the kind of place where "ground level" is a phrase that has lost its meaning. The cordillera rises in stacked terraces of pale blue-grey stone, each terrace a kilometer or more above the last, and the air at this altitude should be thin and cold and difficult, except that the air here is wrong in a particular way that takes some time to articulate.

The stone weeps. Not metaphorically. Water beads from the rock face as if the mountain itself were sweating through a fever, runs in thin sheets down the cliff walls, collects in shallow basins worn smooth over what must be an enormous span of time. The basins overflow into other basins. The water is very clear and very cold and tastes, when you cup it in your hands, faintly of iron and something else — ozone, perhaps, or the inside of a struck bell.

There is no vegetation. None. But the rock is not bare. Across every weeping surface grows a furring of pale crystalline structures — feathery, dendritic, growing slowly in the direction of the running water, branching and re-branching at angles that almost look biological but resolve, when you bring your eye close, into pure mineral geometry. Some are the size of your hand. Some are the size of a cathedral, spanning whole cliff faces in fractal arcades of white and faint violet. They click and sing as the water moves through them. This is part of the sound.

The sound. The cordillera is loud. There is the constant percussion of falling water at every scale from drizzle to cataract, the chiming of the crystal-growths, and beneath everything a deep slow grinding that may be the mountain itself or may be something underneath the mountain. The grinding is not constant — it has a rhythm of perhaps once every forty seconds, like breathing, and the chimes seem to organize themselves around it.

The light is the next thing that becomes wrong. It is very bright. Painfully bright, the kind of brightness that makes the eyes water and the skin prickle, but when you look up to find the sun there is no sun. The sky is a uniform pale colorless brilliance, more like the inside of an enormous lamp than anything atmospheric. Shadows fall, but they fall in several directions at once, faintly, as if from many weak suns rather than one strong one. Objects have penumbras like halos. Your own shadow, if you can find it, is multiple and slightly out of register with itself.

Now the weirdness asserts itself fully, and you understand that the previous oddities were only the surface of it.

The terraces do not entirely agree with each other about which way is down. Water falling from the upper terrace to the lower sometimes arrives moving sideways, or arrives before it has finished leaving, or arrives as ice when it left as water. Stand at the edge of a terrace and drop a pebble: it may fall, it may hang, it may rise slowly back into your palm warmer than it was. The crystal-growths take advantage of this. The largest of them — the cathedral ones — grow in directions that no single gravity could account for, their feathery arcades reaching out into volumes of air that the local "down" should forbid.

There are structures. Scattered, partial, old. Small cairns of dark stone stacked at junctions between terraces, in patterns that suggest someone was marking routes. A shelter, here and there: low walls of fitted stone with no roof, the corners rounded by long weathering, the doorways oriented in ways that imply the makers cared about something other than wind. In one of the larger shelters, a hearth — but the hearth is full of clear water, and the water in the hearth is the only water in the whole cordillera that does not move.

The tools left behind are simple. Worked stone, mostly. A few pieces of cold-hammered copper, green with age. Awls, scrapers, what might be a flute carved from a long crystal — when you pick it up it hums faintly in your hand, picking up the chiming of its larger cousins. No bones. No middens. No sign of who used these things or what they ate, because there is nothing to eat: no plants, no animals, no fungi, no scuttling life of any kind. Whoever they were, they did not draw their living from this place. They came here for some other reason, did their work, and left, or stopped.

The dynamism of the cordillera is not biological but processual. Water cycles relentlessly: it weeps from the rock, runs, pools, evaporates into the bright colorless sky, and condenses again as a fine mist that falls back onto the upper terraces. The crystal-growths advance and, in places, retreat — you can find ghost-forms in the rock where a growth lived once and has now dissolved back, leaving a paler shadow of itself. The reality-slippages have their own weather: regions where the local rules are calm, regions where they are turbulent, and the turbulent regions migrate slowly across the terraces over hours or days. You can watch one approach. The chiming changes pitch when it arrives. Your shadows multiply further. For a few minutes the water might fall upward, or your own breath might come out as a small visible word in a language you do not know, and then the front passes and things return to their merely strange baseline.

The whole place feels established. Not new, not collapsing, not in the violence of formation or the resignation of ruin. It has been doing this for a long time. It is in balance with itself. The weeping and the chiming and the slow grinding breath and the wandering reality-fronts and the absent makers' little stone walls — all of it has settled into a steady state that does not need an observer and does not particularly mind one.

If you stay long enough — and the light never changes, so "long enough" must be measured some other way — you begin to suspect that the cordillera is not a place but a process running on a substrate of rock and water, and that the process is thinking about something. Slowly. Carefully. With the patience of something that has all the time it needs.

You do not learn what it is thinking about. You were not built to.

But the flute, when you set it back down where you found it, keeps humming for a while after your hand has left it, and the note it holds is the same note the mountain breathes out, forty seconds later, from somewhere very deep below.
