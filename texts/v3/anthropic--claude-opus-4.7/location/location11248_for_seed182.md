# The Sumpworks

Twelve meters beneath what was, until perhaps eighty years ago, an unremarkable basalt plain, there is a town pressed into a cavity that should not exist. The cavity is fresh — the walls still show the conchoidal fractures of recent breakage, glass-edged where the rock parted, and in places you can fit your shoulder into the negative of where a slab fell away last winter. Nothing here is old. The town inside the cavity is older than the cavity, which is one of the first strange things to understand.

The heat comes from below. Not magma — nothing so dramatic — but a slow exhalation through the floor, the kind of warmth that a body gives off under heavy blankets. It is consistent. It is the reason the vegetation grows.

The vegetation is mostly a single climbing thing, pale-stemmed, leaves the shape and translucency of fingernails, which has decided the brick walls of the Sumpworks are an acceptable substitute for the trunks of trees it has never met. It grows in mats four and five layers thick on the eastern faces, thinner on the west where the condensation drips less reliably. Between its leaves, smaller things: a moss that fruits in little glassy beads, a fungus that fans out from the mortar joints in plates the color of weak tea. The air smells green and mineral, like a greenhouse built over a quarry.

Water is the patient architect here. There is no river, no spring, no obvious source — only the ceiling, which sweats. Each square meter of stone overhead gives up its moisture at its own rate, so that the rain inside the cavity is local and unpredictable: a steady drip in one alley, nothing at all in the next, a sudden brief shower over the third roof from the cistern when the pressure shifts. The townspeople have built gutters that branch like circulatory systems, catching what falls, routing it to collection points. The streets are damp but never flooded. The walls weep and the weeping is harvested.

The town itself: maybe forty buildings, maybe sixty if you count the smaller annexes and the half-buried sheds that have been pressed against the cavity wall. They are made of brick fired somewhere else and brought down — there is no kiln here, no space for one — and stacked tightly, sharing walls, growing upward in three and four stories until they nearly touch the stone ceiling. In several places they *do* touch, and the building tops have been shaped to follow the ceiling's contour, so that the upper rooms have floors that slope and corners that are not corners. Density is the principle. There is no public square. The streets are the width of a person with their arms slightly bent.

The industry is quiet. This is the strangest thing, perhaps. There are factories here — three of them, that I can see, identifiable by the stacks that vent into channels carved up through the rock to the surface — and they are running, but they run on long cycles and low pressures. Belts turn at the speed of a slow second hand. A loom completes one pass every three minutes. A press descends, holds, lifts, in the rhythm of someone breathing in their sleep. There are no whistles, no shouted orders. The workers move between stations with the calm of people who know that the work will be done because the work is always being done.

What is being made? I think: precise small things. Components for something assembled elsewhere. The wares stack on pallets in courtyards, wrapped in waxed cloth, labeled with characters I can almost but not quite parse. Once a week, a freight cage descends through a shaft in the northwest corner, takes the pallets up, leaves a new ration of brick and food and oil.

The light: electric, dim, yellow. Bulbs strung on cords that loop from building to building like the web of a methodical spider. Each bulb does the work of a candle. Your eyes adjust. After an hour you can read by them. After a day you forget there was ever any other kind of light. The bulbs are powered by something humming in the lowest level of the largest factory, a generator that no one has needed to repair in living memory, though the memory here is short — most of the workers arrived after the cavity opened.

Fauna is sparse and chosen. There are cats, two of them, which the town shares. There are small pale beetles in the vegetation that nobody minds. There is one bird, a single specimen of something like a starling, which lives in the rafters of the second factory and was either brought down on purpose or arrived in a shipment and stayed. It does not sing. It clicks, occasionally, like a clock being wound.

The weirdness: the cavity is growing. Slowly — a few centimeters a year, the masons say, when they have to chip back a wall that the stone has begun to press against. But it is growing inward as well as outward. The floor in the eastern quarter is now four centimeters higher than it was when the youngest worker arrived. The townspeople do not discuss this. They adjust their thresholds. They re-pour their floors. They have agreed, without ever agreeing, that the cavity is making room for itself, and that whatever it is making room for is not yet here, and that their work — the slow precise small components, the wrapping in waxed cloth, the loading of the freight cage — is in some way related to the patience of the room around them.

At night, which is only a dimming of the bulbs by a few lumens, the dripping continues, the belts turn, the loom passes, and the cavity widens by an amount no one will measure until morning.
