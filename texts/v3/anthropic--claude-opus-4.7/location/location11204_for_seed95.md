# The Worn Plateau

The plateau sits high enough that the air tastes thinned, but warm — a dry, sun-baked warmth that doesn't quite reach inside the lungs. It is one of the oldest things still recognizable as a place. Whatever mountains rose here once have been worked down across a span the mind cannot really hold, until what remains is a vast almost-flatness, gently rolling like the back of some enormous animal that lay down and never got up. The horizon, in every direction, is so far off and so level that it makes the eye uncertain about its own focus.

The stone underfoot is the color of old bone — calcareous, smoothed, pocked with shallow basins where rain has stood and evaporated a hundred million times. In some basins a finger's depth of water lingers, warm as blood, ringed with a white salt crust. In others the water is gone but the smell of it remains: a mineral sweetness, faintly green. Veins of darker rock thread through the pale floor, polished by wind into low ridges no higher than a knee. You could walk for a day and gain or lose perhaps the height of a tree.

Vegetation is sparse but specific. Cushion plants the size of dinner plates, hard as cartilage, hold themselves close to the ground in colonies. A few wind-pruned shrubs with leaves like grey coins. Lichens — and this is where the place begins to insist on itself — lichens in colors that don't belong to lichens: a deep cobalt that seems wet even when dry, a pale rose, a black so matte it looks like a hole. They crust the stones in maps that, if you look long enough, seem already to have meant something to someone.

Despite the sparseness of plants, the air is thick with animals. This is the strangeness of the place: in a landscape that should be empty, life is layered nearly to suffocation. Slow-moving things mostly. Pale, segmented creatures the length of a hand graze the lichens in companies of thousands, their backs catching sun like wet pebbles. Above them, drifting, things that look like jellyfish but are not — gas-bagged, translucent, their tendrils trailing in slow loops over the stone, feeding on the cushion plants by some patient chemistry. They never rise more than a few meters. They never come down. Birds, if they are birds, walk rather than fly: long-legged, soft-billed, picking among the grazers without disturbing them, going about a quiet competence that suggests the arrangement is older than memory.

And yet it is quiet. This is the contradiction the place holds most carefully. With so much living packed across the open distance, you expect noise — and there is almost none. The grazers make no sound. The drifters hum, barely, a tone you feel in the sternum more than the ear. The walking birds click occasionally, dry, like two pebbles touched together. Wind moves over the plateau in long, unhurried sheets and finds nothing tall to whistle against. Mostly what you hear is your own breath and the small grit of your steps. The silence is not absence; it is a discipline the ecosystem keeps.

Scattered across the plateau — perhaps once every long walk — there are structures. Not ruins exactly. Small, low things: a waist-high cairn of the same pale stone, stacked with a care that has outlasted whoever stacked it; a single upright slab, weathered featureless, leaning a few degrees off vertical; a ring of darker stones inset into the floor, flush, easy to miss. They are not arranged in any pattern that survives long enough to be one. They are placed where they are because someone, in some century, decided that here was the place. No tools are anywhere. No metal. No wood worked by hand. The builders are not present and may never have been many. The grazers walk over the rings without registering them.

The rules of this world, as far as one can read them: nothing hurries. The drifters take a full day to cross from one horizon-feature to another. The lichens grow at the speed of stone. Even the weather is patient — clouds gather over weeks, release a brief warm rain, and unbuild themselves again. Death must happen, but you do not see it happening; bodies of the grazers are rare, and when found are already half-mineralized, half-claimed by the cobalt lichen, returning to the floor along the same slow vector as everything else. Whatever predation occurs, occurs at a rhythm too long for a visitor to catch.

What pulls at the chest, standing in such a place, is the sense that this is what a world looks like after it has finished most of its arguments. The mountains have been settled. The species have been sorted into a saturation that no longer changes much. The weather has agreed with the stone. The few built things mark a presence that came, made its small mute statement, and either left or sank into the same long calm. The plateau is not dying; dying would be too quick a word. It is at rest in the way a thing is at rest that has done its becoming and now only continues.

By evening — which arrives gradually, the light reddening through dust raised by no wind in particular — the drifters settle a little lower, the grazers slow further, and the cobalt lichens, in the angled light, briefly look like pools of standing water that aren't there. You could stay. Many things, clearly, have.
