# The Boiler Hollow

You come on it through a slot in the basalt — a place not on any survey, because the survey crews could never quite agree it was the same place twice, and eventually they stopped sending people.

The hollow is no larger than a modest courtyard. The walls go up perhaps thirty feet, sheer and black-glassy, fractured in long vertical planes that catch the light in oily rainbows. The rock here is new. You can tell because the edges have not yet learned to be soft. Run a hand along any fracture and it will draw blood without apology. The floor is the same stuff, slabbed and tilted, with seams of paler mineral running through it like fat in meat.

Water is the first fact of the place. It does not fall as rain — there is no sky to speak of, only a hot bright slot far overhead where the cliffs almost meet — but it is everywhere. It beads on the basalt walls and runs in slow vertical worms. It pools in the lower slabs in shallow oxbows the color of weak tea. It hangs in the air as a steam so thick that ten feet of distance turns a thing the color of itself, but paler, and softer at the edges. Breathing here is half-drinking. The air is somewhere around blood temperature, and saturated, and to step into the hollow is to feel your own surface vanish — you stop knowing where your skin ends.

The vegetation is almost an afterthought, or perhaps an early thought. A few tufts of something thick-leaved and waxy clinging in fissures, looking less like plants than like the rock's own attempt at hair. One larger growth in the center — call it a tree if you like, though it is more a single fleshy column, segmented, the color of bruised plum, sweating its own slow droplets into the standing water at its base. No flowers. No fruit. No insects come to it. There is no animal life here at all, not even the things you would expect — no mosquito-larvae kink in the puddles, no algal froth, no skim of pollen. The water is sterile-looking and warm as bathwater and tastes faintly of iron and ozone if you are foolish enough to taste it.

And then the sound.

The sound is the reason no one stays.

Somewhere — and you cannot tell where, because the basalt throws every echo in fractal directions — there is an engine running. It has been running for a long time. It is not a single engine but a coupled set: a low reciprocating thump at perhaps sixty beats a minute, a higher whine an octave or two above that, and overlaying them both a continuous roaring hiss that might be steam venting, or might be the friction of a great belt against a great wheel. The sound presses on the chest. It is loud enough that two people in the hollow could not converse, would have to lean in and shout into each other's wet ears, and even then only fragments would arrive. It is loud enough that you feel it in your molars.

But there is no engine visible. There is no machine. There is only the hollow, and the steam, and the wet black rock.

If you look carefully — and the steam makes this hard — you will find the built things. They are not many and they are not whole. A single iron pipe, perhaps four inches in bore, emerges from the basalt at shoulder height on the north wall, terminates in a flange, and goes nowhere. It is hot to the touch. It hums. Lower down, half-buried in a slab, the curve of what might be a flywheel, or might be the rim of a great gear, rust-colored where it shows, the rest swallowed by stone as if the stone had grown up around it — which, given how young the stone is, it may well have. On the far wall, a hand-sized brass plate, screwed to the rock, its lettering long since gone to verdigris. A bracket. A bolt-head. Nothing that adds up to a machine. Nothing that explains the sound.

The dynamic of the place is slow. You could sit here an hour and watch the same droplet make its way down the same fracture, taking its patient time. The steam thickens and thins on cycles of maybe ten minutes. The water in the pools rises and falls by a finger's width over the course of a day, breathing. The engine-sound, when you listen long enough, has its own much longer rhythm beneath the surface beats — a swell and ebb across maybe twenty minutes, as if something very large were inhaling and exhaling, and the smaller machinery were only its incidental clatter.

The rule of the hollow, as best anyone has worked it out: the engine has always been here. The rock grew up around it. The rock is still growing. In another thousand years the pipe will be gone, the flywheel will be gone, the brass plate will be gone, and the sound will continue from nowhere at all, muffled now by an additional foot of basalt, and someone will find a different slot, and step through, and stand sweating in the steam, and listen to a machine that isn't there, and wonder what is being made.
