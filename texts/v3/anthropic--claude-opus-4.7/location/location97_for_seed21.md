# The Kettle Terraces

You come up out of the cloud-forest onto a shelf of the mountain that shouldn't exist yet — the rock is too new, too sharp. The andesite still has its glass edges. Where your boot scuffs it, the break is fresh and smells faintly of struck flint and ozone. This is a place that finished forming, maybe, a few centuries ago. Maybe last Tuesday. You can't tell.

The shelf is perhaps two hundred paces across — a cupped basin held between three ridges, open on the fourth side to a drop into white vapor. Cathedral-sized, no larger. You could walk its perimeter in the time it takes a kettle to boil, which turns out to be the right unit of measurement here.

The basin is full of pools.

Not many — maybe nine, maybe eleven, scattered with that deliberate sparseness of a garden that knows the value of the space between things. Each pool sits in its own shallow crater of pale, new stone. Each is perfectly circular. Each is *hot* — not thermal-spring hot, but approaching it: steam lifting off the surfaces in slow vertical columns that rise ten or fifteen feet before the air gives up on holding them and they spread into the general haze.

The heat of the place hits you first as a wall and then, after a minute, as a medium. You are inside the temperature. Sweat starts instantly. The air tastes of wet mineral and something faintly sweet, like overripe fruit left in a greenhouse.

Between the pools: moss. Deep, saturated, emerald moss, and pitcher-plants the size of wine bottles, and low tree-ferns with fronds that drip constantly — condensation running off their tips in slow beads. The vegetation is lush but not crowded. It clusters at the pool-rims where the heat and damp are richest and leaves long runs of bare stone between. You can see where you're walking. Nothing hides.

---

The pools are the technology.

You understand this gradually. At first they look natural — what else would they be, these small hot circles in the rock. But the rims are too exact. The water in them is too still. And when you kneel at the nearest one and look down, you see that the pool has no bottom. Or rather: the bottom is somewhere you can't quite accept. The water is clear for what seems like ordinary pool-depth, and then it keeps being clear, and then at some point you realize you're looking *through* the water at something that isn't the underside of the mountain. A slow, banded light. A curvature. Something that might be a horizon seen from above.

Each pool, apparently, opens somewhere else.

There are no instruments, no housings, no markings. Whatever made them didn't feel the need to label them. The only built things on the shelf are three small cairns of stacked stone — waist-high, deliberate, weathered — positioned at what seem to be viewpoints between certain pools. And one low wall, half-collapsed, that might once have been a bench. The makers came, did the work, and either left or became part of the quiet.

---

The quiet is the strange part.

With this much water evaporating, with this much heat, with vegetation this wet, you'd expect the place to roar — cicadas, frogs, wind, boiling. Instead: the pools steam without bubbling. The water enters and leaves them by some route that doesn't involve surface motion. The ferns drip, and each drip makes a small clear sound against stone, and between drips the silence closes again completely. Your own breath is loud. Your pulse is loud. When a beetle — iridescent, thumb-sized, one of the few creatures here — lifts off a frond and resettles on another, the wingbeat is an event.

Fauna is rare and seems chosen. The beetles. A single pale frog you spot at the edge of the third pool, which watches you with the attention of something that has watched many people. A bird you hear once, distant, below the shelf in the cloud, and never again. That's all, in an hour of looking.

---

The dynamics are slow but real. Over the time you stand there:

- A pool near the eastern ridge clouds over, goes milky, then clears again. What it was showing through its floor has changed. Where there was banded light, now there is something dark and slowly rotating.
- Mist rolls up the drop-side, crosses the basin at knee-height, and burns off against the pool-heat in wisps.
- One of the pitcher plants closes, very slowly, around nothing you could see. Digestion takes about as long as a conversation.
- The sun moves, and the shadow of the western ridge crosses two pools in sequence, and each pool, briefly shadowed, shows its through-view more clearly, as if light on the surface had been the thing preventing you from seeing.

The shadowed view of the fourth pool is of a grey sea under grey sky. You are almost sure of this. When the sun returns to it, the view dims again to ordinary water.

---

The rule of the place, as best you can work it out by standing there: *Don't put anything in the pools.* There is no sign saying this. There is simply the feeling — the obvious feeling — that the pools are thresholds, and that thresholds are for looking across and not for dropping stones into, and that whatever is on the other side of the fourth pool's grey sea does not need a pebble from here, and might notice.

You sit on the half-wall. Sweat runs. A drip falls from a fern. Somewhere under the basin, something the mountain was built to hold continues its slow, quiet work of connecting this hot green shelf to elsewheres the mountain is not required to explain.

The beetle lifts, resettles. The pale frog blinks once. The steam goes up in columns, straight, straight, straight, until the sky takes it.
