# The Hallowgrowth Plateau

Start high. Start dry.

The plateau sits somewhere above the altitude where water would bother to linger — not that water comes here anymore. It hasn't, in any meaningful way, for a span of time that has flattened the mountains around it into slow blue shoulders. What you stand on is not stone. It hasn't been stone for epochs. It is wood, or something wood became after it stopped needing to be wood: a pale, dense, mineral-grained substance that rings faintly underfoot, like a struck bowl heard from three rooms away.

The plateau is the corpse of a forest that learned not to die. Or: the forest is the plateau. The distinction stopped mattering in the long erosion.

---

**Terrain.** Imagine a tableland the size of a small country, worn nearly smooth by geologic patience. What survives as relief is the stumps — enormous, circular plinths of the old wood, some the width of a plaza, rising only a meter or two above the general surface. They are the shaved-down remains of trees whose original height cannot be calculated, since their growth rings are now measured in isotopic decay rather than seasons. Between them, the ground is ribbed and grained, a petrified end-grain you walk across for kilometers. In the lowest swales, a dry dust collects: yellow, fine, the pollen of something that hasn't flowered in a geological age but still, somehow, each dusk, releases a fresh pinch of it from pores in the wood.

There is no water. There has never needed to be, not for a long time. The plants here metabolize by a chemistry that substitutes polarized light and a slow trickle of argon-bearing gas that rises from fissures in the wood-ground. No rain falls. The sky above is dim, lavender, always partially eclipsed by something too large and too far to resolve.

**The living sparse.** At this density, the plateau feels empty, and is not. A walker crosses a kilometer of bare grained ground and then meets: a single tree.

These are the descendants. Twenty, thirty meters tall, no two alike. One is a slender fluted column that ends in a crown of black glass leaves that chime faintly when the pressure shifts. Another is squat, barrel-shaped, its bark breathing visibly in and out on a cycle of forty minutes. A third is nothing but a vertical seam in the air, a rip, through which you can see the internal architecture of its xylem as a kind of wet green lightning — though there is no wet, only the memory of wet, conducted through fields rather than fluids. They are rare. They are sovereign. Between any two, the land reads as desert. But the ground beneath is a single continuous organism, the old forest, the substrate, and the distant trees are only the places it chooses to stand up.

**Fauna.** Abundant, and strange, and almost entirely airborne or surface-gliding — nothing burrows, because to burrow would be to wound the substrate, and the substrate does not tolerate wounds. The commonest creature is a thing I'll call a reed-hare: a meter-long, lemon-colored, six-legged grazer whose body is mostly hollow and resonant, and which feeds by pressing its belly to the ground and absorbing whatever the plateau offers upward through the skin. They travel in loose herds of hundreds, spread across hectares, and when they are startled the whole herd sounds a single chord, deep and brassy, that can be heard for ten kilometers. This is part of the loudness.

Above them: gliders. Flat, kite-shaped, translucent, drifting on the slow thermals the wood-ground sighs out. They do not land. When one dies, it falls, and the ground absorbs it in a matter of hours, leaving a faint bruise-colored patch that greens over by the next shift of light.

And then the predators, the tall-steppers — four-legged, stilted, each leg ending in a broad pad that never quite touches the ground, because they walk on the standing electromagnetic field the plateau holds a finger's breadth above its surface. They run in silence through a world that otherwise never shuts up.

**Sound.** The plateau is loud. Not the loudness of weather — there is almost no weather — but the loudness of a vast resonant body that will not stop humming. The wood-ground conducts. Every footstep of every reed-hare becomes, somewhere, a low note. The stumps ring at their own frequencies, like a gamelan tuned across tens of kilometers. At dusk a shuddering chorus rises from the fissures — not wind, since there is little wind, but the slow exhalation of the argon chemistry, forced through flutes the ground has grown for itself over eons. You cannot sleep here without becoming, briefly, an instrument.

**The built thing.** Halfway across the plateau, in a shallow bowl where the grain of the wood spirals inward like a closed eye, there is a town. Or the residue of one. Or a town that is still alive and simply does not conduct itself on a human clock.

It is called — by the few who still speak of it — **Ossara**, though no one remembers why.

Ossara is not built on the plateau. Ossara is *coaxed* from it. Its buildings are hollow growths the inhabitants shaped long ago by request, by patient pressure on the substrate over centuries, and which the substrate obligingly grew and then, after the makers had left or died or transmuted, kept growing, slowly, the way a fingernail keeps growing. The town is now eight or nine times the size it was first asked to be. Its streets are wider than streets need to be; its doorways arch to heights no inhabitant ever used. There are rooms no one has ever entered, because the town, unprompted, added them.

Perhaps fifteen hundred dwellings, spread loosely — a density that would read as suburban if suburban applied. Between the buildings: broad plazas of polished end-grain, and, at irregular intervals, pillars of a different material entirely. These are the technology.

**Tech.** Not machines. Not any object you would recognize as a device. The pillars are the visible parts of an infrastructure that does not belong to the same physics as the rest. They are dull silver, about three meters tall, and if you look at one directly you begin to feel that you are looking at it from several angles at once, and that one of those angles is inside it. They regulate something — the field the tall-steppers walk on, perhaps; or the slow argon chemistry; or the eclipse overhead; or simply the willingness of the substrate to continue. Whoever installed them is gone. They work. They have worked for as long as the plateau has had its current shape, which is to say, forever, by any local measure.

Between the pillars, finer infrastructures run as faint threads in the air, visible only when dust crosses them. They carry something. The inhabitants of Ossara — there are still inhabitants — treat the threads the way earlier peoples treated aqueducts: with familiarity, and with a quiet awe about the competence of ancestors they can't quite credit.

**The inhabitants.** I've been circling them. Let me arrive.

They are slight, pale, and slow. They have adapted to the resonance: their bones, over many generations, have become partly hollow, and they speak by humming into their own sternums and letting the plateau carry the sound where it needs to go. A conversation in Ossara can have a delay of seconds or minutes, depending on how far across the substrate the hum has to travel before it finds the right ear. This does not bother them. They have a long patience. They have developed, as a culture, the habit of beginning every sentence as if continuing one.

They farm the reed-hares, loosely — the herds are wild, but the inhabitants know where to be, and when. They do not farm the trees. The trees are not food. The trees are… neighbors, is the closest word. Each of the great sovereign trees around the plateau has a name in Ossara, and a disposition, and, in some cases, a standing grievance.

**Rules of the world.**

1. Nothing that walks may break the surface of the substrate. To cut the wood-ground is to cut the single body of the plateau, and the plateau has, historically, responded. The consequences are not described, only alluded to.
2. Water, if somehow introduced, behaves wrongly here. It does not wet. It beads and rolls and seeks a fissure and vanishes. The plateau does not want it. Visitors who bring water bring it in sealed vessels and drink it privately and with some shame.
3. The light is dim because the sky is occluded, and the occlusion is not a cloud. No one discusses what it is. It has been there longer than memory. It casts a soft lavender shadow over everything, and at what passes for noon, the shadows under the great trees are the deepest black you will ever see, and children are told not to stand in them.
4. The plateau is ancient but not inert. It is actively old. It is old the way a grandmother is old — slow, enormous, opinionated, and still, occasionally, reaching for something.

---

Stand at the edge of Ossara at dusk. The reed-hares are settling onto the warm end-grain, a hundred meters off, in their loose constellations. The gliders above have folded into smaller shapes for the night. A tall-stepper crosses the plaza, soundless, on its field of air. From somewhere deep in the substrate, a chord begins — low, brassy, many-voiced, the plateau tuning itself for whatever slow work it does at night.

A pillar beside you emits, for just a moment, a color you do not have a name for.

The ground hums. The forest, which is also the town, which is also the world, breathes out once, long, and you feel it through the soles of your feet and up into the hollow your body is becoming.

You have been here, by local reckoning, no time at all.
