There is a fold in the mountain where the cloud has lived so long it has forgotten how to leave.

You come on it from above, through a notch in the dripping rock, and you have to climb down into it because it sits cupped — a hollow no wider than a small orchard, ringed by ridges that lean inward as though listening. The walls weep. Not in trickles but in a slow, even sheen, water moving down moss the way breath moves down a window. The moss is six inches thick in places, and beneath the moss the stone is the color of wet slate, and beneath the stone, you suspect, is more water still. Everything here is saturated to its limit. Press a finger into anything and it gives, and then it gives back.

The cool keeps the cloud thick. Not cold — your breath does not show — but the kind of cool that settles into wool and stays there. The air itself is half water; you do not so much breathe it as drink it in small unconscious sips. Vision ends at perhaps eight or ten paces. Beyond that, shapes: the suggestion of a fern's arch, the darker mass of something trunk-like, the pale interruption that might be a stone or a bird or nothing.

It is dark here. Not night-dark — there is some grey filtering down from a sky you cannot locate — but the kind of darkness that lives under thick canopy under thick cloud under a thick ridge. Your eyes adjust and adjust and never finish adjusting. You learn to look sideways, to catch movement at the edges.

And there is so much movement.

This is the thing that takes longest to understand. The hollow is packed. Not noisily — the place is almost silent, only the steady soft sound of water finding water — but every surface is occupied. The moss is occupied. Beetles the size of a thumbnail move through it like slow black thoughts. Under every overhang, salamanders: pale, dark, spotted, ribboned, stacked sometimes two or three deep in the wet seams of the rock, perfectly still until they aren't. Snails with shells like wet horn climb the fern stems in numbers that should not be possible — twenty on a single frond, a hundred on a single trunk. Centipedes long as a hand drape from the underside of leaves. In the standing pools, which are everywhere — every depression holds one — there are newts and translucent shrimp and small dark fish that have no business being this high, and yet here they are, and have been, apparently, forever.

Forever is the word for this place. Nothing changes. The cloud does not lift. The water does not stop. The animals breed and die at rates that exactly cancel; the moss grows and decays at exactly the same speed; the rock weeps and the rain falls and the pools fill and drain through hidden seams into other pools, and the whole system has been in this perfect held breath for so long that it has the quality of a held photograph. You get the strong sense, standing in it, that if you came back in a hundred years the same salamander would be in the same crease of the same stone. Possibly it would be. The cool keeps everything slow. Slowness compounds.

The vegetation is fern and moss and a few low twisted trees — laurels, maybe, or something laurel-shaped — whose bark is entirely hidden under lichen and liverwort and the climbing snails. The trees do not grow tall because the hollow does not let them; they reach a certain height and then turn sideways, and their branches interlace into a low second ceiling beneath the cloud-ceiling. You walk under both, stooped.

There is one sign of hands, and only one, and you almost miss it. On a flat stone near the deepest pool, someone — long ago, before the moss had quite reclaimed it — chipped a shallow basin into the rock. The chipping was done with another stone; you can see the bruise-marks of the percussion. The basin holds water now, like everything else holds water, and a newt lives in it. Whoever made the basin is so long gone that the question of who is not really a question anymore. The basin is part of the hollow. The newt is part of the basin. The hollow does not distinguish.

The rules of the place, as far as you can work them out by standing in it:

Nothing leaves. Water comes in as cloud and leaves as cloud and in between is held. Animals are born here and die here and are eaten here and become moss here. Heat does not accumulate. Cold does not deepen. The hollow has found its level and sits at it.

Nothing is loud. The loudest sound is your own swallowing. After a while you stop swallowing so much.

Nothing is empty. Every cubic inch is somebody's. You learn to set your foot down carefully, and then more carefully, and then you learn that careful is not really possible and you accept that walking here is a small massacre, and that the hollow has absorbed many small massacres and will absorb yours, and that in a season the print of your boot will be moss again and a salamander will live in the heel-mark.

You could stay. People must have, once — whoever chipped the basin. You could stay and the hollow would close around you the way it closed around the basin, and you would become slow, and then slower, and then you would be a feature, weeping quietly, holding your small population of newts.

The way out is back up through the notch. It is harder to find from the inside. The cloud does not want you to find it. This is not malice. The cloud simply has no opinion about your leaving, and its no-opinion is very thick.
