# The City That Sweats

It is night, and it is always night here, though "night" is the wrong word for a darkness this warm. The air is the temperature of a living thing — somewhere just past blood-heat, thick enough that you taste it before you breathe it. Every surface weeps. The walls of the city run with slow water that is not rain because no rain falls; it simply arrives, condensing out of an atmosphere that cannot hold any more of itself.

The city occupies the floor of what might once have been a shallow basin — a valley so wide and low that its rim is only a suggestion at the edge of perception, a place where the dark thickens. It extends. Buildings stand in clusters and then in rows and then alone, with long flat distances of standing water between them. Streets that go nowhere. Plazas where nothing happens. The settlement is dense in its construction but sparse in its inhabitation of the land — as if a city were laid out for a population that never came, or that left, or that exists at a tilt to the geometry available to visitors.

The materials: a soft dark stone, basalt-like but more porous, that has been worked into walls and arches and stepped façades. Brick of the same color, fired somewhere not visible. Long horizontal courses, low doorways, deep recessed windows behind which there is no light and no movement. The construction is layered — older walls become the foundations of newer ones, and newer ones the foundations of older ones, which should not be possible but is the kind of sentence the city encourages. Sequence has loosened here. A stair descends into a wall. A wall ends in a stair.

Erosion has done its long work: every edge is rounded, every corner softened, lintels sagging by a finger's width, thresholds worn into shallow bowls that hold water. Nothing is ruined, exactly. The city is in its settled state, the way a riverbed is in its settled state, having found the shape it wants and stopped fighting for any other. Weathering and building have arrived at the same equilibrium and you cannot, looking at any given wall, tell whether it is half-finished or half-dissolved.

Vegetation: occasional. A pale fleshy growth in the cracks between paving stones, more fungal than botanical, the color of an inner lip. In a courtyard, a single low tree — if tree is the word — branching like coral, its surfaces glossy with condensation, no leaves, no clear stem, just a bifurcating dark thing that holds beads of water at every joint. These plants do not seem to need light. Perhaps they eat the humidity. Perhaps they eat the city's slow breath.

No animals. No insects in the standing water. No bats, no owls, no anything that should be in a dark warm wet place. The absence is total and you feel it as a pressure behind the eardrums: a silence in the category where movement should be.

What sound there is, is water. The slow tick of a drop falling from a height. A trickle along a hidden channel. Somewhere, very far, a sheet of water sliding off a roof in one slow exhalation. The drops do not always strike when they should. Some of them seem to fall for too long before landing; others land before they fall. You will hear a splash and then, three seconds later, see the ripple appear in a puddle ten feet away. The city's acoustics are unwell.

And here is where the weirdness lives, not in any single visible wrongness but in the relations between things. The geometry is locally correct and globally impossible. Walk one block north and one block east and one block south and one block west and you will not be where you started; you will be on a terrace overlooking the same square from above, though you took no stairs. Water flows downhill but the downhill keeps changing. A puddle reflects an arcade that is behind you, and when you turn, the arcade is in front. The sky — if there is sky — is the color of the inside of your closed eyelid, and it presses down with the close warmth of a held breath.

The dynamic is slow but real. Things change. The condensation cycles: walls grow wet, then wetter, then water sheets down them in a slow flood, then they are merely damp again, and the floodwater has gone somewhere, and the cycle starts again, and this takes — minutes? hours? The basin breathes. Sometimes a whole quarter of the city is briefly submerged ankle-deep, the water perfectly still, perfectly warm, and then the water has receded and the stone is only sweating again. Sometimes a wall that was solid is now an opening; sometimes a doorway is now a wall. The city rearranges itself the way a sleeper rearranges limbs — not with intent, but not at random either. There is a logic. You will not learn it.

There are no people. There is no evidence of people having been here, except the city itself, which is evidence of nothing else. No carvings, no inscriptions, no abandoned objects, no furniture in the rooms whose interiors you can glimpse through the deep windows. The city is built but not used. Or it has been so thoroughly used that all use has been worn away, like the corners of its stones.

Stand still long enough and you'll notice that you, too, are weeping water — not sweat, exactly, though it is partly that. The air gives its moisture to you as readily as to the walls. You are becoming a surface the city condenses on. If you remain, you will round at the edges. You will settle into your own shape. You will join the long, warm, dark equilibrium of this place, where everything that was going to happen has already finished happening, slowly, and is still, slowly, going on.
