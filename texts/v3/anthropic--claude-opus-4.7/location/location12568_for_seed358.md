# The Sumpworks

Below the surface — not deep, but below — there is a country of warm water and unfinished stone. You come down into it by a series of pumped shafts that no one quite remembers commissioning, and when you step off the last gantry you are already wet. The air condenses on your face before you know to feel it. Everything here arrives by condensation: the walls weep, the ceilings (where there are ceilings) bead and drop, and the standing water on the floor is fed less by streams than by the slow exhalation of the place itself.

The country is wide. Not a chamber but a region — you can walk for a day in any direction and the ceiling stays roughly where it is, four or five times a person's height, sometimes lifting into vaults where the dimness suggests but does not confirm a roof. Mostly the lid of the world is lost in vapor. The light is the dim orange of bulbs in cages, strung at long intervals on cables that sag and drip; between the bulbs are corridors of true dark broken only by the wet shine of things.

The stone is young. That is the first strange fact and the one hardest to shake. The rock is *forming* — calcite skins drape over older calcite skins, flowstone thickening visibly on the scale of a human life, stalactites bristling like the buds of antlers, mineral curtains hanging half-grown from the ceiling and not yet reaching the floor that is itself rising to meet them. Nothing here is eroded. Everything is being *built*, by water out of water, atom by atom. Where you set your hand on a wall the calcite is faintly warm and faintly soft, like the rind of bread cooling. Come back in twenty years and your handprint will be a relief.

Vegetation is sparse and strange-pale: mats of bacterial felt in greens that have forgotten what green is for, fungal stalks the color of bone-broth, a few hardy ferns near the shafts where surface light leaks down in shafts of pollen and gnats. Mostly the landscape is mineral and animal. The water is warm — bath-warm in the deep pools, hot-spring warm where vents come up through the floor — and it teems.

And here is where the place announces itself, because the dominant fact of the Sumpworks is sound.

It is *deafening*. Not loud the way a city is loud — loud the way a cathedral full of organs would be loud if every organ were also a waterfall and a beehive and a foundry. There are at least four layers, and they layer rather than mix:

The lowest is the dripwater. Billions of drops, each striking a pool or a stone or another drop, summing into a hiss-and-clatter that occupies the whole spectrum from low rumble to high tick. It never pauses. It is the substrate of every other sound.

Above that, the *fauna*. The pools are packed — packed past plausibility, density three of three — with blind white amphibians, finger-eels, freshwater shrimp the length of your forearm, and a kind of slow soft-shelled crayfish that clicks its mandibles in courtship. The amphibians sing. Whole choirs of them, hundreds to a pool, calling in overlapping pulses that beat against the wet walls and come back doubled. Bats — actual bats, somehow, this close to the surface — hang in clusters from the unfinished ceiling and chitter when disturbed. Cave swiftlets, if that is what they are, click and clatter through the vapor.

Above that, the *engines*. This is where the Sumpworks tips from cave into country. Someone — many someones, across a long time, none of them present now — installed pumps. Industrial pumps, riveted iron things the size of cottages, set on plinths of poured concrete that the calcite is patiently swallowing. They run. Steam-driven, some of them; others on cables that vanish upward into the rock. They draw water from the lower pools and push it through pipework that runs along the walls in great looped arteries, occasionally bursting at a seam to add another weeping waterfall to the ambient choir. The pumps groan and hammer on cycles of minutes. Pressure-relief valves shriek every so often, individually predictable, collectively not, so that the whole region pops and whistles at random.

Above all of it, occasionally, a deeper sound: the slow settling of the rock itself, the *tock* of a new column finally meeting its drip, or the long sigh as a calcite curtain releases a sheet of held water. The geology is talking. It talks slowly, on the scale of weeks and centuries, but in a place this wet you hear it more often than you would expect.

The built things are scattered and not numerous. Pump-houses, yes; a few wooden walkways gone soft and treacherous; a chapel, of all things, hewn into a side-chamber, with iron benches bolted to the floor and a brass fitting on the altar that might once have been a pressure gauge. There are catwalks between the larger pools, ladders going up to maintenance galleries, and at long intervals a shack where someone could sleep dry if they kept the stove burning. The stoves are warm. Someone tends them. You do not, on a first visit, meet that someone.

The rules of the place, as far as I can work them out:

Nothing dries. If you lay something down — paper, cloth, bread — it will be damp within an hour and ruined within a day. The shacks are dry only because they are constantly heated; the chapel is dry only because it is high and the air there moves.

Nothing erodes. The water builds, it does not cut. Even the pump-discharge, hammering at the rock for a hundred years, has only polished it; the rock answers by laying down another skin. To make a hole here you must drill, and the hole will close.

Nothing is fast. The animals are abundant but slow — the amphibians barely move except to call; the eels drift; the crayfish take an hour to cross a pool. The pumps cycle on minute-long breaths. The light flickers but does not change. Even a visitor, after an hour, finds themselves slowed: the warm wet air, the noise that becomes a kind of silence by its constancy, the dim orange that does not vary — all of it asks you to settle.

Nothing is empty. This is the deepest rule. Every cubic foot of the Sumpworks has something in it — water vapor, calling animal, bacterial film, pipework, dripline, the spreading edge of a flowstone, the resonance of a pump three chambers over. The density is not crowding; it is *occupation*. The country is fully inhabited by itself.

If you stand still long enough in the Sumpworks — say, an hour, on the catwalk above the third great pool, where the amphibian chorus is thickest and the pump on the east wall booms every ninety seconds — you begin to suspect that the whole region is a single slow organism. The pumps are its heart; the dripwater its blood; the calcite skins its growing bone; the amphibians and bats and eels its busy attendant tissues. The few human structures are not foreign — they have been incorporated, the way a tree incorporates a fence. The someone who tends the stoves may simply be another organ, doing what organs do.

It is warm. It is loud. It is being built. It does not need you, but it does not mind you either, so long as you do not try to dry out or hurry.
