# The Sweatworks

Down where the city's foundations end and the older city begins, there is a chamber the engineers stopped maintaining around the time the second war ended. It is still warm. It has stayed warm.

You come down to it through a service shaft, and the first thing your body registers — before your eyes adjust, before you understand the size of the place — is the heat. Not furnace heat. Blood heat, slightly above. The heat of a body that has just stopped moving. The air carries it evenly, without a source, as though the walls themselves are the source, which in a sense they are.

The chamber is brick. Brick floor, brick vault overhead in a shallow groin, brick pillars set close enough that you cannot see across the room in any direction — five paces, maybe six, before another column interrupts the line of sight. The bricks are the dark red of old kilns, and they are furred, every one of them, with a fine pale crust of mineral salts that the seeping water has left behind over decades. Run a finger along a pillar and it comes away white and gritty and faintly warm.

Water is the slow author here. It does not pour or rush. It seeps. It beads on the underside of the vault and falls, when it falls, with an interval of a minute or two between drops, so that the sound of the room is not water but the *expectation* of water, a held breath between ticks. The drops land on the brick floor in the same places they have always landed, and those places are no longer flat — each is a small bowl worn by patience, and in the bottom of each bowl sits a clear pool no wider than a coin, ringed with the same white salt that coats everything. Walk slowly. The pools are nearly invisible in the dark.

There are machines in the room, set between the pillars: low cast-iron things, knee-high, with brass fittings going green. They are not running and have not been running for a long time, but they were once part of a circulation system — the kind of municipal apparatus that moved hot water from somewhere deeper to somewhere higher, when the city above still trusted such warmth. The pipes leave the machines and disappear into the walls. The pipes are warm to the touch. Something further down is still working. Nobody has come to ask why.

Light: almost none. A single bulb burns in a wire cage at the foot of the access ladder, and its filament is the dim orange of a bulb left on too long ago to be replaced soon. Past the third pillar, you are navigating by memory and by the pale luminance of the salt crust, which catches what little light there is and returns it as a suggestion of geometry. The far end of the chamber is simply darkness. You have to trust that it ends.

What grows here grows sparely and grows pale. In the wettest corners, where a pipe-joint weeps a slow steady tear, there are mats of something — not moss, not quite — a soft colorless film with the texture of wet paper, which spreads across the brick in lobes and goes no further than the moisture goes. In one place a small fern has established itself, root-fingers worked into a crack, and its fronds are the bleached green of a thing that has never seen sun and has had to invent the color from chemistry alone. There are perhaps a dozen of these living patches in the entire room. You could count them.

Animals: a beetle, sometimes. The kind that lives in warm dark places and eats the pale film. They are slow and unstartled and their shells have gone glossy black from generations of mineral diet. Once in a great while a single moth, lost down from the upper city, will find the bulb at the ladder and stay until it dies, and the beetles will eventually take it apart over a week. There is nothing else. No rats — too dry, in the end, for rats. No bats — no exit large enough. The room is closed enough to be its own small completed world.

The strangeness, when it comes, comes quietly. If you sit on the brick floor and stay long enough for your own warmth to match the room's warmth — which takes about twenty minutes, the room being patient and you being only a person — the drops begin to fall in time with your pulse. This is not true, of course. It cannot be true. But once you have noticed it you cannot stop noticing it, and the longer you sit the more certain you become that the chamber has been doing this all along and was only waiting for someone to come down and provide the rhythm. When you stand up to leave, the intervals lengthen again, slowly, over the next hour, back to whatever they were before. You will not be there to hear them settle.

The room does not change on any scale a visitor can witness. The salt thickens by a hair's breadth in a decade. A brick, somewhere in the vault, will eventually lose enough of itself to fall, and another small bowl will begin in the floor beneath it, and the pale film will find the new wet place and colonize it within a season, and none of this will be observed. The machines will continue not to run. The pipes will continue to be warm. Somewhere far below, the thing that warms them will continue, for its own reasons, to do so.

On your way back up the ladder, the heat releases you in stages — first your hands cool, then your face — and by the time you reach the service door you have begun to doubt the chamber was as warm as you remember. It was. It is. It will be when you go back down, if you go back down, and it will not have noticed you were gone.
