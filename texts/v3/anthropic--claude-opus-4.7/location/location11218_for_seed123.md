# The Inside of the Engine-Hill

You find it by accident, which is the only way it is found. A seam in a low ridge — the ridge itself is unremarkable, warm sandstone, recently shouldered up out of the plain by something underneath that hasn't finished pushing. The seam looks like a crack. It is wider than a crack. You go in sideways and then you are in.

The chamber is not large. Maybe the size of a parlor, or a generous wagon. You could pace it in twelve steps if there were anywhere to pace; there isn't. Every surface is occupied.

The walls are stone but the stone is hosting. Brass piping, no thicker than a finger, threads the rock in dense looping circuits, following the grain, sometimes diving in and emerging meters away. The pipes are warm. They tick faintly. Where they meet the floor they sprout small valves, and the valves weep — not leak, weep — single beads of condensate that gather and fall at irregular intervals. The floor, in consequence, is glossed and slick in patches and bone-dry in others, depending on the geometry above.

Between the pipes: lichen. Sparse, but where it grows it is intensely green-gold, and it has the wrong texture — too smooth, almost lacquered, as if someone had varnished it. Tufts of a wiry grass push out of the seams between brass and rock, and a single fern, no taller than a hand, has claimed the corner where two condensate-drips converge into a thumbprint pool.

And everything else is *occupants*.

The chamber is packed. Saturated. There is a tortoise the size of a teacup nosing along a low pipe; there are pale moths, dozens, folded against the warmer surfaces with their wings making slow accordion breaths; there is something like a salamander but with mineral chips along its spine, drinking from a valve-weep; there is a colony of small bright beetles harvesting the lacquered lichen; there are at least three species of spider doing three different kinds of work in three different corners; there is a bird, somehow, a small dust-colored bird perched on the highest pipe-elbow, watching. The air itself has gnats in it, lazy ones, moving in the warm currents the brass throws off. You cannot stand without something already there, and so you crouch, and find that the crouching-place too has been claimed: a snail, the size of a knuckle, with a shell patterned like an oil-slick, considering your boot.

None of them seem disturbed by you. None of them seem disturbed by each other. The density is so total that the usual rules — territory, predation, distance — must have been renegotiated long ago. The salamander steps over the beetles. The bird does not eat the moths. The spiders spin around the snail rather than across its track. There is a treaty here, or an exhaustion, or a physics you don't have a name for.

The sound is almost nothing. The ticking of the brass. The drip, here, there. A faint hum from somewhere deeper in the rock — engine-hum, the kind a factory floor makes three rooms away, attenuated through stone. Once in a while a valve releases a quick small *tsss* of steam, and every animal in the chamber pauses for one beat, and then resumes.

There are marks of makers, but only marks. A brass cap stamped with a number — 14, or possibly 11, the digits half-eroded though the cap itself looks newly cast. A small iron bracket set into the wall, holding nothing. A scrap of waxed paper wedged behind a pipe, with a column of figures on it in pencil, the writing recent enough that you can read it but smudged enough that you can't tell what's being counted. Whoever tends this place tends it rarely, or tends it from elsewhere, through the pipes themselves. The hum suggests the latter.

The strangeness builds the longer you stay. The condensate, when a drop lands on the back of your hand, is faintly sweet. The moths, watched closely, are breathing in time with each other — not the same time as you, a slower time, a six-count. The lacquered lichen, if you press it, dents and then slowly returns to shape like a thick paint that has not quite cured, though it must have been here for years. The tortoise, you realize, has a tiny brass valve set flush into the back of its shell, no larger than a lentil, and you cannot tell if it is decoration, parasite, or organ.

Outside, the ridge is young — sharp-edged, recently lifted, the kind of landform that still has work to do. The plain around it is dry-warm and thinly vegetated, ordinary. Nothing about the outside suggests the inside. The seam, when you turn back to it, is narrower than you remember, though you can still fit.

The room runs on something. The pipes go somewhere. The animals are fed by something that isn't quite the lichen and isn't quite each other. The whole pocket pulses, slowly, with processes you can sense but not separate: a weather of its own (the condensate-cycle, twelve minutes from drip to dry), a biology (the moths' shared breath, the beetles' slow harvest), an industry (the hum, the ticking, the patient brass), all of it stacked into a volume that should not hold them.

You could stay. Many things have. The dust-colored bird, you understand now, is not visiting.

You sidle back out through the seam. The plain is bright and ordinary. The ridge ticks, very faintly, if you put your ear to it. You could walk past this place a hundred times and never know.

Somewhere under your feet, a valve weeps one bead, and the snail, considering nothing now, moves on.
