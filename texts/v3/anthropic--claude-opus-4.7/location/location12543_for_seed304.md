# The Steeping Town

It sits in the low fold between two basalt ribs that pushed up — geologically speaking — last Tuesday. The rock is still sharp enough at its edges to draw blood, glassy black where it broke, dull where the constant water has begun, just barely, to soften it. You can see the freshness: no lichen yet, no rounding. The ribs cup the town the way two hands cup something to drink.

Water is the first fact. It comes from everywhere at once. The basalt weeps — small clear beads that gather in the seams, run down, pool in every concavity. Mist condenses on every cool surface (everything is a cool surface). The ground squelches and sucks. There are no streams exactly; the whole basin is a slow seepage moving downhill at the pace of an idea. In the lowest part, where the houses crowd thickest, the water stands in shallow lanes between the buildings, and the people walk on plank-paths laid over the wet.

The town is small enough to cross in a few hundred paces, but inside that span everything is packed against everything else. Houses lean on houses. They are built of the local basalt — split into rough blocks with simple iron wedges, mortared with a clay the inhabitants dig from a single grey seam at the basin's north edge — and roofed with overlapping shingles of the same stone, thin-flaked, dark and gleaming wet. Between the houses: lofts, sheds, smoke-rooms, water-catchments, brewing-rooms, dye-vats, all wedged into the gaps. Ladders instead of stairs where a roof has been pressed into service as another floor. Bridges of plank crossing between upper windows because the alleys below are too full of standing water to be reliable. You climb to go visiting.

There are perhaps two hundred people. They wear oiled wool and leather treated with some kind of resin that makes it shed water like a duck's feathers. They are quiet — not silent, but soft-voiced, used to small spaces where loud sound is rude. The loudest thing in town is usually the slow drip-chorus from the eaves: thousands of points of falling water, no two in rhythm, a hush that becomes audible only when you try to listen past it. Sometimes a hammer-blow on stone. Sometimes a child. Sometimes the bellows of the one forge, breathing.

Vegetation is sparse and strange. The rock is too new and the soil too thin for forest. What grows is what tolerates wet basalt: cushion mosses in three or four species, a black liverwort that the people boil for dye, and one peculiar plant — they call it the lamp-reed — which grows in clumps at the wettest seeps. It has a translucent stem and a habit of holding light: in the grey afternoons the reeds glow faintly from within, a pale green luminescence that the townspeople do not consider remarkable but that visitors, if there were visitors, would. The reeds are cultivated in the lanes. They illuminate the wet paths after dark with a soft underwater glow.

Fauna is rare and specific. There is a kind of small pale newt that lives in the wall-seams, eating whatever lives in the seams smaller than itself. There are translucent shrimp in the pools, no bigger than a grain of rice. There are no birds — the basin is too enclosed, too steep-sided, and the ribs above are still too sharp for nesting. Once or twice a year a creature comes down from the ridge that nobody quite has a name for, broad-pawed and slow, drinks at the lowest pool, leaves. The people leave it food and do not approach.

The temperature stays cool. The basalt holds cold. The mist holds cold. In the warmest part of summer a person's breath still shows in the morning, in the alleys. In winter the water at the edges grows a thin skin of ice that the children break with sticks on their way to fetch the day's eggs (from where? from the one keeper who tends the one flock of grey waterfowl that the town has agreed to feed in exchange for eggs and quiet).

The rules of the place, as best I can describe them:

The town has been here for four generations. The basalt has been here for less. This is known because the oldest people remember when the south rib was lower, and when the seep at the bell-house was not yet a seep. The ground is still rising, slowly, somewhere underneath. Occasionally a new spring opens in someone's floor and the family must decide whether to move the hearth or move the house. They usually move the hearth. The house is more trouble.

Iron is rare and reused. A nail is an heirloom. Tools are mostly stone, bone, fire-hardened wood, and a small precious quantity of soft iron that the smith re-forges, re-forges, re-forges. Pottery is everywhere — the clay seam is generous — and the town's wealth, such as it is, is measured in unbroken pots.

There is a process in motion that nobody fully understands. The basin is filling. Not quickly — over years — but the standing water is deeper now than the grandparents remember, the plank-paths are higher, the lowest house has been abandoned to the newts. Whether the town will be drowned in another four generations or whether the ribs will rise faster than the water, nobody knows. The discussion is constant, soft-voiced, and unresolved. Meanwhile the dye-vats steam, the lamp-reeds glow, the basalt weeps, and someone, somewhere in the packed wet warren of stone and plank, is singing very quietly to a child.
