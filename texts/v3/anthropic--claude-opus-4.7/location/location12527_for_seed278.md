# The Slow Plateau

Begin with the cold. Not the sharp, biting cold of a polar night, but the long settled cold of altitude — the kind that has been here so long it has become the temperature of the stones themselves. The air is thin and tastes faintly of iron. Breath would steam, but there is no one breathing in the human sense, and the things that do breathe here breathe slowly enough that their exhalations only rise as the faintest distortions in the dim air.

The plateau extends in every direction further than weather can reach across in a single cycle. From its low passes to its tabletops there are climbs of two or three thousand meters, and from one edge to another — if it has edges, which is not certain — the journey would take a continent's worth of walking. Storms are born in its western basins and die against its eastern shelves without ever leaving home. It has its own seasons that do not match the seasons of any world below.

The stone is the color of old pewter, sometimes greening where lichen has worked at it across what must be millions of years. Everything here is rounded. The mountains are not peaks but vast, slumped shoulders. The valleys are wide troughs scoured smooth. Whatever cataclysms made this place are so far in the past that even their scars have scars, polished and softened. Rivers have cut, then meandered, then cut again at lower angles, leaving terraced floodplains stepped like the inside of an enormous bowl. Most of those rivers are dry now, or nearly so — beds of pale gravel braided across the lowlands. Where water runs, it runs clear and quiet, beaded with cold, no louder than a held breath.

The vegetation is mosses and ground-hugging things, cushion plants tucked into the lee of stones, the occasional dwarf shrub no higher than an ankle. There are no trees. Across miles of plain you might see four or five small thickets, hunched in hollows where the wind cannot find them. Color is conservative here: silver-greens, rust-browns, the chalk-white of mineral crusts. The plants are old. A cushion the size of a dinner plate might be a thousand years in the making.

The light is dim and even, a perpetual late afternoon under a high overcast, though sometimes when the cloud thins you can see a sun that seems smaller than it should be, or further. Shadows are soft. Distance dissolves into a pearl-grey haze that makes the plateau feel even vaster — as though everything you can see is foreground, and the real distance is something you will never reach.

Now: the animals.

This is what makes the place strange. For all its emptiness, the plateau is heavy with fauna. They are not crowded — they are sparse like everything else here — but they are *everywhere*, and they are *enormous in aggregate*. You see them once your eye learns to look.

There are the grazers. Long-legged, long-necked, woolly things the size of oxen that move across the plains in herds of hundreds, their hooves padded so that they make no sound on the gravel. They tear at moss with soft mouths and their breath is the only thing about them that registers in the air. A herd will cross a valley in a day and you will never hear them pass; you will only see, afterward, the slight darkening of the ground where they grazed.

There are the burrowers. The plateau is honeycombed beneath. In the dim hours you see them emerge — flat, broad creatures, low to the ground, with eyes adapted to low light. They come up in their thousands across a hillside and you realize the hillside is alive, has always been alive, that the soil itself is a kind of slow flesh stitched through with their tunnels.

There are the high gliders, ribbon-bodied, that ride the thin currents between the tableland and the cloud-deck. They do not flap. They circle in long quiet arcs for hours.

There are colonies of small singing things that do not sing. Their throats are made for it but they have learned, across whatever ages this plateau has imposed its silence on its inhabitants, to communicate by stance and angle and the slow shifting of crests. Whole conversations happen in absolute quiet.

The whole ecology has tuned itself to noiselessness. Hooves are padded. Wings are membranous and slow. Calls have become postures. Even the predators — and there are predators, you see their scat, their tracks, occasionally their silhouettes on a ridgeline — move in the same long hush. It is as if the plateau, by being so vast and so still, made any sound too revealing. The animals have folded their sound inward over generations until what remains is a fauna of breath and gesture.

And then — the strangeness.

Across the plateau, at intervals of perhaps a hundred kilometers, there are the *engines*. This is the only word for them. They are not built. No one built them. They appear to have grown, or to have been deposited by some earlier geology, or to be the fossil remains of a process this place underwent long ago. They are enormous: black iron-dark masses, wheel-like, gear-like, vented, sometimes a kilometer across at the base, rising in slumped towers a few hundred meters high. Their forms suggest function — pistons, flywheels, condensers, things that could move — but they do not move now. They are weathered into the landscape, half-buried, lichen-furred, with cushion plants growing in their crevices.

And yet they are warm. If you put your hand on the iron of one of them — a hand belonging to whatever creature here has hands — you would feel a faint, steady warmth, the warmth of something operating at a depth so slow it cannot be perceived as motion. Sometimes a vent will exhale, once, a long slow breath of warmer air, and the moss around it will be greener, and a few of the burrowing animals will have made their homes in the lee of that warmth.

The engines are, in some way no one has come here to study, *running*. They have been running for a long time. Their cycles are not measured in seconds or minutes but in centuries. A piston that began descending when the last ice age withdrew may, in another two thousand years, complete its stroke. The plateau's weather, perhaps, is partly their exhalation. The faint mineral warmth of certain valley floors. The slow magnetism that orients the migrations of the herds. No one knows. No one is asking.

The animals incorporate them. They shelter against the warm flanks. The gliders nest in the high vents. A herd of the woolly grazers will gather around an engine in the coldest weeks and stand for days, taking what heat there is, and then drift apart again across the plain.

This is the rule of the place, if it has a rule: everything here moves slowly enough that stillness and motion become hard to tell apart. The clouds creep. The rivers wander across their floodplains by a meter a year. The engines turn over their long hidden cycles. The herds graze across the continent in patterns that take generations to complete. The lichen advances on the stone at the speed of forgetting.

And it is quiet. It is so quiet that if you stood on a high shelf and listened, what you would hear would be the small interior sounds of yourself, and beyond those — if you were patient, if you stayed long enough — the faint, faint sigh of one of the engines, a hundred kilometers away, exhaling its century's breath into the cold.
