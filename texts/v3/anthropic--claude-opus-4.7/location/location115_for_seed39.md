At roughly four thousand meters, the mountain has not yet decided what it will be.

This is the first thing to understand. The stone here is young in the geological sense — it was pushed up recently, still pushing, and its surfaces are sharp in the way only unweathered rock is sharp. The edges of the ridges have not been rounded. The scree has not sorted itself into stable fans. Where a slab has cleaved away from the parent wall, the scar is clean and bright, a paler grey than the surrounding stone, and it has not yet grown the black and orange lichens that would mark it as old. Everything looks as though it happened last week. Some of it did.

Water is everywhere but rarely in the form of streams. The air carries it. At this altitude and this temperature — just above freezing, most hours — cloud drifts through the valley in slow grey tides, condensing onto every surface. The rock weeps. Not dramatically; it simply holds a film of moisture that beads and slides and beads again. In the shaded clefts, the beads thicken into slow trickles that gather in hollows of the bedrock and sit there, black-mirrored, reflecting nothing because there is nothing bright enough overhead to reflect. The light is dim. The sun, when it exists, is a pale disc behind the ceiling of cloud that seems to rest on the ridges like a lid on a pot.

The valley is broad — several kilometers across, you could walk its length in a day if the footing allowed, which it doesn't — and nearly empty. This is what strikes a visitor most, after the cold: how little there is per cubic meter of space. A boulder. Then fifty meters of damp gravel and nothing. Then a patch of cushion plants, tight green pincushions hugging the ground, the kind of vegetation that looks like moss and is not. Then more nothing. Then a single twisted shrub, knee-high, wind-pruned into something like a clenched hand. The sparseness is not barren — things grow — but they are set far apart, as if each plant has negotiated a territory of stone around itself and will not tolerate encroachment.

And yet the fauna is abundant. This is the parameter that seems, at first, to contradict the others, and it is the one that most rewards sitting still.

Wait twenty minutes on a cold damp rock and the valley fills in around you. Pikas first — small, round, tailless things, the color of wet stone, appearing at the mouths of burrows in the scree and giving their single sharp bark before vanishing. Then marmots, heavier, moving between boulders with a rolling gait, whistling warnings back and forth across distances that seem impossibly far for the sound to carry, except that the air is so still and the valley so quiet that a whistle from the far wall arrives with its edges intact. Ibex pick their way along ledges that from below look like nothing, like a shadow-line on the cliff, and it takes a while to realize the shadow-line is a path and the grey shapes on it are animals. A lammergeier passes overhead without a wingbeat, its shadow faster across the scree than seems reasonable. Choughs in black squadrons turning on some current you cannot feel. Snowcock in the higher couloirs, calling at dawn and dusk with a sound like water poured from a great height into a metal bowl.

They are all here because the valley is empty of people and because the water is easy and because the young rock, for all its rawness, is riddled with shelter — fresh fractures, talus voids, caves that were caves last season and may be different caves next season when the freeze-thaw pries another ton of rock loose. The ecology runs hard under the quiet surface. A fox works a line of pika burrows with patient economy. Something larger — a lynx, maybe, though you don't see it — has left a track in the mud at the edge of a seep, the pad-print still holding water.

There are no buildings. No trails except the ones the ibex have made. No cairns. No fence lines, no shepherd's hut tucked into a lee, no wire, no glass. The valley has never been built in. This is rare enough, at this point in history, to feel like a kind of pressure against the skin.

And yet —

Somewhere, not here but not far, there is industry. You can tell because the wind brings it, occasionally, when the cloud lifts and a thermal pulls air up and over the ridge: a sound that is not a natural sound. A low thrumming. The suggestion of engines, of something turning at scale, of mechanized work being done. It might be a hydroelectric intake on the far side of the range, tapping one of these high catchments. It might be a mine. It might be a road being cut somewhere below the cloud ceiling, a winter-long project of blasting and grading. Whatever it is, it does not enter the valley — the valley remains untouched — but it exists in the same world, on the other side of the rock, and the valley knows about it the way a quiet room knows about traffic through a closed window.

The thrum comes and goes. Mostly the valley is quiet. The sounds that fill it are small and specific: water moving under scree, the tick of a pebble dislodged by freeze-fracture and rolling three feet before stopping, the wet inhalation of a marmot at the mouth of its burrow, the distant clatter of ibex hooves on a ledge, the hiss of cloud parting around a pinnacle. Once in a while, a crack — sharp as a rifle — as frost levers a slab off the wall and sends it down a couloir in a grinding cascade that is over in seconds and leaves the valley, afterward, sounding quieter than before.

This is a place in the process of becoming itself. The rock has been lifted but not yet carved. The water has arrived but not yet cut its channels. The animals have moved in ahead of the soil. The weather has begun its long work of rounding and sorting, but it is early days — another ten thousand years and the valley will look entirely different, gentler, with real streams and real meadows and a proper lake in the lower basin where now there is only a shallow tarn half-filled with gravel. For now it is raw, damp, cold, nearly empty, alive, and quiet, with something industrial humming faintly on the other side of the mountain, a rumor of the century the valley has so far managed not to let in.
