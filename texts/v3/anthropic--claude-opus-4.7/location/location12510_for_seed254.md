# The Underbloom

You go down to it by a stair cut into root-mass, the treads damp and fibrous underfoot, each one giving slightly the way old bread gives. The stair is not long. Maybe forty steps. The air gets warmer as you descend, and wetter, and louder.

At the bottom: a town.

The first thing to understand is that there is no stone here, and no timber in the sawn sense, and no metal except where the people have brought it in. Everything that holds the town up is alive, or was alive so recently that the distinction hasn't settled. The walls are root. The streets are root. The ceilings — for this is a roofed place, a hollow beneath the surface where some great mat of vegetation has lifted itself off the bedrock and made a chamber — the ceilings are the undersides of a thousand interlaced trunks pressed flat against each other, weeping.

That is the sound, mostly. The weeping. Water condenses on the ceiling-roots and falls, not as rain exactly but as a continuous fine percussion onto every surface: onto the broad pale leaves the townspeople have trained to function as awnings, onto the standing pools in the lower lanes, onto the cisterns, onto the slick swelling caps of the fungi that grow shelf-wise out of the load-bearing pillars. Drip, drip, drip, ten thousand drips at once, never in unison, a sound like static that you stop hearing as sound and start hearing as the shape of the room.

Under the dripping: the engines.

The town runs on fermentation. In the deeper bays, where the floor dips and pools, there are great wooden — no, not wooden, *bast-fiber* — vats the size of houses, lashed and grown into place over generations. Sap goes in. Pulped leaf-matter. The sweet pith of certain swelling tubers that the townspeople harvest from the ceiling by climbing rope-ladders made of the same stuff as everything else. The vats heave. They give off gas, and the gas is piped through bladder-hoses up to the bellows-engines, and the bellows drive grinders, presses, looms, the slow patient mills that turn root-flour into the dense brown bread these people eat and the dense brown cake these people burn for light.

The light is the color of weak tea. It comes from clay lamps set in niches along every wall, burning the pressed cake with a low orange flame behind panes of waxed leaf. There is no daylight here, none, and the lamps are never all lit at once — a quarter, a third — so that the lanes are always in a kind of permanent dusk, the kind of light you walk through after sundown in summer when the air is still warm and you can just make out faces. Faces would be visible here, if there were faces to see. The townspeople are at their work, or asleep, or in the vat-bays tending the slow chemistry. They do not crowd the lanes. The town is small — you could walk its long axis in twenty minutes, if the lanes were straight, which they are not.

The lanes are not straight because the lanes are gaps. They are the spaces the roots left when the roots grew. Over centuries the people have widened them, trained them, pruned the inward-creeping shoots, fed the load-bearing columns with the slurry from the vats so they thicken in the right places — but the underlying geometry was decided by the plant, not by the planners. So the lanes meander. They open into small rounded plazas where six or seven root-pillars meet and the ceiling lifts a little. They narrow into passages where you turn sideways. They cross each other at odd angles and at odd heights, because the town has levels: walkways of woven aerial root span the upper air, and ladders of the same stuff drop from them, and the children of this place — there must be children, though you don't see them just now — climb these as easily as breathing.

Everything is wet. Every surface. The columns sweat. The walls of the houses (the houses are hollowed pockets in the root-mass, sealed with a kind of resinous gum and curtained with broad leaves) are damp to the touch and warm. Mosses grow on everything that doesn't move often enough, and pale translucent fungi grow on the mosses, and on the fungi grow smaller fungi still, and a person leaning against a wall for a minute will come away with a faint green print on their shoulder that they will not bother to brush off, because what would be the point.

No animals. This is worth saying. There are no birds in the upper walkways, no rats in the vat-bays, no insects whining at the lamps. Whatever drove the animals out — or whatever the plant-mass does to keep them out, some chemistry in the sap, some bitterness — has left the town to its people and its plants alone. The silence-of-no-animals is buried under the dripping and the bellows-thump and the grinder-creak, but if you listen for it, it is there. A missing register. The town sounds like weather and machinery and nothing else.

The machinery is loud. This needs emphasis. Down in the vat-bays the bellows-engines work in long slow strokes, each stroke maybe ten seconds, and at the top of each stroke a great wet *whump* shakes the floor, and the grinders answer with their groan, and the presses with their squeal, and the whole thing pulses at the speed of a sleeping animal's breathing. You can feel it through the soles of your feet anywhere in town. The houses tremble faintly on the long stroke. People have built their lives around this rhythm. They sleep through it. They speak in the troughs between the whumps and pause for the peaks, and the cadence of conversation here has a particular hitch in it that you would learn, if you stayed.

And you could stay. The town is not hostile. It is only that nothing here changes quickly. The roots grow at their own pace, which is the pace of years. The vats brew at their own pace, which is the pace of weeks. The lamps are tended on a schedule older than anyone living. A child born here will, in old age, walk the same lanes in the same dim tea-colored light past the same columns, perhaps a finger's-width thicker, perhaps with one more shelf of fungus, perhaps with a new house hollowed into a place where there used to be a wall. The town does not grow outward — it cannot, it is bounded by the chamber, and the chamber is bounded by the surrounding root-mass which presses in on all sides with the patient pressure of living wood. The town grows inward instead. Denser. More finely worked. Every generation finds new uses for the same volume.

This is a place that has decided what it is.

Up the fibrous stair, somewhere far above, there is presumably a surface, and weather, and a sky. The townspeople know about it. They go up for trade, and to harvest, and for certain ceremonies. But the surface is not where they live. They live down here in the warm wet drumming dim, in the small bounded saturated world that the great plant has made for them, drip by drip by drip.
