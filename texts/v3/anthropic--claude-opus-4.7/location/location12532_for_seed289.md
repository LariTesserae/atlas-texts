# The Bright Hollow

Begin underneath. Not deep — just under. A meter, maybe two, below whatever surface you imagine above. The hollow sits embedded in raw rock that hasn't decided what it wants to be yet: not quite limestone, not quite chalk, a pale stone still in the process of remembering it is stone. You can press a thumbnail into it and leave a mark, but the mark will not deepen, will not crumble, will not change. The rock is soft in the way of something not yet finished, but it is also fixed — it has stopped becoming. It waits.

The room is small. Six paces across, maybe seven. A pocket. A held breath in the earth.

And it is *bright*. This is the first impossibility. There is no opening to the sky, no shaft, no lamp, no fire — but light fills the hollow as if noon were trapped here, as if a summer afternoon had been folded up and tucked into the stone. The light comes from the walls themselves, from the pale not-yet-rock, an even radiance with no source and no shadow. Things in the hollow cast no shadows. Your own hand, held up, throws nothing. The light is on every side at once.

The density is the second thing you notice. The hollow is *packed*. Every cubic centimeter is occupied. Pale moths — hundreds of them, thousands — cling to the walls and ceiling in overlapping shingles, wings folded, wings half-spread, their bodies the color of the stone they cover. Between the moths: small translucent beetles, the size of rice grains, in such numbers that the wall seems to shimmer with a second skin. Between the beetles: thread-thin worms, white, threaded through cracks. Between the worms: a fine pale moss, only millimeters tall, but dense as felt. And growing up through all of this, in scattered places, are the plants — sparse, yes, only a few — but each one elaborate: a single fern of glassy fronds in one corner, a low cluster of mushroom-stalks with caps like inverted bowls, a vine that has climbed three feet up the wall and stopped, mid-reach, decades or centuries ago. The vine has not grown further. It will not. None of this will.

Because the third thing — the deepest thing — is that nothing here changes.

The moths do not fly. They are alive; you can see the slow pulse of their abdomens, the slight tremor of antennae. But they do not move from their places. The beetles do not crawl. The worms thread through their cracks without traveling. The moss does not spread. The vine does not climb. The water — and here is the water, the damp — beads on the ceiling in droplets that catch the sourceless light and hold it like small suns. The droplets do not fall. They have not fallen. They will not fall. They simply hang there, fat and trembling and permanent.

There is moisture, but no dripping. There is a cool air, but no draft. The temperature is the temperature of a cellar in early spring — cool enough to want a sleeve, not cold enough to shiver. It is exactly this temperature always.

And yet — the fourth thing — the hollow is *loud*.

This is what undoes most visitors. The packed life of the place, frozen though it is, *sings*. The moths, motionless, hum. Each one produces a single sustained tone from somewhere in its thorax, and the tones differ from moth to moth, and the thousands of tones together make a vast unwavering chord that fills the hollow like water fills a cup. The beetles tick — not in rhythm, but in such density that the ticking becomes a continuous hiss, a fine static beneath the chord. The worms, impossibly, whistle: a high thin sound, barely there, but there. The moss, when you press your ear to it, *breathes*. And the suspended water droplets ring faintly, like struck glass, though no one strikes them.

The sound does not rise or fall. It has been this sound, exactly this sound, for as long as the hollow has existed. If you stay long enough you will begin to hear structures inside it — counterpoint, harmonic stacks, what sound almost like words — but these are artifacts of your own listening. The sound itself does not develop. It is one chord, held forever.

There are signs that someone, once, found this place. A small cairn of stones in one corner, four stones high, the top one notched. A line scratched into the wall, just one, at about eye-height — straight, deliberate, no longer than a finger. Something that might have been a wooden bowl, now mostly absorbed into the moss, only its rim still visible. Nothing more. Whoever they were, they did not stay. Or they did stay, and were taken into the stillness, and are one of the moths now, or one of the droplets. There is no way to know. The marks they left are also fixed — the cairn will not topple, the scratch will not weather, the bowl-rim will not decay further.

The strangeness of the hollow is not in any single feature. It is in the *equilibrium* of it. A place this full should churn. A place this loud should be in motion. A place this bright should have a sun. A place this damp should drip. Instead: a held note. A held breath. A photograph that hums.

If you sit in the center of the hollow — and there is just room to sit, cross-legged, with the moth-walls a hand's breadth from your shoulders on every side — you will feel something you cannot quite name. Not peace. Peace moves; peace settles into you over time. This is something else. This is the sensation of being inside a moment that has been removed from the sequence of moments. The chord rings. The droplets hang. The moths pulse without moving. The light comes from nowhere. And you, sitting there, are the only thing in the hollow that is still subject to time — your breath rising and falling, your heart ticking on, your thoughts moving forward — and you feel, slowly, that this is an embarrassment. That you are the loud one. That the hollow is patient with you, and waiting.

People who find the hollow do not usually find it twice. Not because it moves — it does not move, it cannot move — but because the way to it requires a particular not-looking, and once you have looked at it directly, the way back closes behind you. It is somewhere under a hill. It is somewhere under many hills. It has always been there. It will always be there. It is doing nothing, with great intensity, forever.
