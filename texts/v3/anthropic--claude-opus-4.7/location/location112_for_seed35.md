# The Engine Hollow

Below the surface, maybe sixty meters down, maybe more — the depth doesn't matter because nothing up there knows about it — there is a chamber the size of a village square. Not a cave exactly. A cavity. Something was dug out here, or something hatched out of here, and whatever did it left walls of fresh basalt glass still showing the conchoidal fractures, sharp as knapped tools. You could cut yourself running a hand along the walls, and many things have.

The air is dry. Completely. Not desert-dry — more than that. A dryness that has been dry so long it has forgotten the concept. The rock itself gives off a faint mineral warmth, a steady 34 or 35 degrees, blood-warm, and this temperature does not vary by hour or season because there are no hours or seasons down here, only the unchanging state.

What makes the chamber loud is the engine.

It occupies the center of the floor — a squat, black, riveted thing about the size of a draft horse, with six flywheels of different diameters turning at different rates, and a central piston that rises and falls with a heavy iron cough every 2.3 seconds. It has been running. It will be running. Nobody knows who built it. The rivets are hand-set but the tolerances are impossible. Brass pipes run from its flanks into the walls and vanish into the rock, and you can feel the faint pulse of them if you press your palm to the stone. The engine's exhaust is not smoke but a fine warm dust that settles on every surface and is somehow also consumed by every surface, so that the floor is always lightly powdered and never buried.

The sound is the thing. The cough of the piston, the whine of the smaller flywheel, the long sighing intake through some unseen valve — layered, overlapping, impossible to tune out. It fills the hollow the way water fills a cup. Conversation would be difficult here. Nothing converses here.

Light comes from the engine's seams — a dim amber glow leaking between plates, enough to see by if your eyes adjust, not enough to read by. The chamber is always at this dusk-level. The shadows of the flywheels rotate slowly across the walls, and this is the only motion in the hollow that resembles time passing, but it is cyclical and identical and so it is not really time.

Now — the ecology.

The walls are not bare. Growing out of every crevice in the sharp black glass are pale stalks, finger-thick, branching like coral, the color of old ivory. They are sparse in the sense that you can see rock between them, but they are everywhere there is rock. They do not photosynthesize. They feed on the engine's warm dust and on vibration, and they grow toward sound, so the densest thickets lean toward the piston like a congregation. They do not grow. That is to say, they are at their final size and have always been. Cut one and it does not bleed, does not heal, and yet tomorrow the cut will not be there. This is simply how the hollow works.

Living among the stalks, and on the engine itself, and clinging to the ceiling in clusters, are the fauna. Many kinds. I can only describe a few.

There are the **bellows-moths**, palm-sized, with wings like oiled leather, who perch on the engine's hot plates and pump their wings in time with the piston. They are not warming themselves. They are, as far as anyone has been able to tell, *adding* to the engine's rhythm — a soft leathery counter-beat. Remove them and the engine does not falter, but it sounds wrong.

There are the **rivet-crabs**, thumb-sized, brass-shelled, who live in the seams and eat the dust and occasionally tighten a loose fitting with their specialized forelimbs. There are hundreds of them. They are not tools. They were not made. They simply are what lives here, and what they do is maintenance.

There are long pale things in the stalk-thickets that I will call **listeners** because they have no eyes and enormous tympanic membranes instead of faces, and they turn slowly, always, toward whichever sound in the engine is loudest at that moment. When you enter the chamber, they do not turn toward you. You are not loud enough.

There are smaller things. Mites that live on the moths. A kind of flat, translucent slug that grazes the walls. Something under the floor that occasionally taps back when the piston falls, though this may be echo.

The density of life, packed into this small warm loud hollow, is closer to a reef than a cave. Near-touching, layered, saturated.

Now the strange part, or the strangest part.

Around the engine, arranged at the cardinal points, are four small structures. They are knee-high, built of fitted stone, and they resemble nothing so much as shrines — though there is no figure inside, only a shallow bowl in each, and in each bowl a small heap of the engine's dust, and the dust in each bowl is a slightly different color: rust-red, bone-white, soot-black, and a green that has no business being there. Who built the shrines? The rivet-crabs don't have the dexterity. The listeners don't have the hands. The moths are moths. And yet the shrines are kept. The bowls are tended. The dust is sorted.

There is also, on the far wall, a door. Iron, hand-forged, with a wheel-handle like a ship's hatch. It has never been opened, in the sense that no one has ever opened it, but it is also not closed, in the sense that the hinges are oiled and the wheel turns freely. It simply has not yet been the moment for the door to be used. It will not be that moment tomorrow either.

The rule of the hollow is this: everything is already in its final configuration. The engine runs, the moths beat, the crabs maintain, the listeners listen, the stalks lean, the shrines are tended by no one, the door waits. Nothing is building toward anything. Nothing is decaying. The sharp edges of the walls remain sharp because erosion has not had time to happen yet and will not be permitted to start. This is a young place, freshly made, and it has decided to stay that way.

If you sat down in the hollow — if you could stand the heat and the noise and the amber dusk — you would find, after a few hours, that your breathing had matched the piston. After a few days, your pulse. After longer than that, it is said, though no one has reported back, you would become another of the hollow's presences: not a moth, not a crab, not a listener, but something new that the place had quietly made room for, one more density in an already-dense equilibrium, and you too would be at your final size, and you too would always have been there.

The engine coughs. The flywheels turn. The dust settles and is consumed. Nothing changes.

It is warm. It is loud. It is full.
