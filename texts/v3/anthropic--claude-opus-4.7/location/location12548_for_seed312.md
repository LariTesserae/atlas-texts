# The Spine City

It begins, as you climb toward it, with the smell of cold stone and woodsmoke — thin, very thin, the way smoke gets when air is starved of itself. The trail is a switchback cut into pale rock so freshly exposed that the chisel marks have not yet weathered. Where the path turns, you can see the rock face is still shedding small flakes from frost-cycles, little tinkling falls of mineral that gather in the joins of the steps like dry snow.

The mountain has not finished being a mountain. That is the first thing to understand.

It is one peak in a range that runs longer than a single eye can hold — a sawtooth horizon north and south, blue-grey behind blue-grey behind a fainter ghost of blue-grey, until the farthest ridges become indistinguishable from cloud. The range is young in the geological sense: the peaks come to points rather than domes, the valleys between them are V-cut and narrow, and there are places where whole faces have sheared off within memory, leaving raw pale scars that have not yet darkened with lichen. Where rivers exist, they are still arguing with the rock about where to go.

The city is built along the spine.

Not on top of it, exactly — *into* it. Houses press up against the ridgeline on both sides, terraced into the slope, sharing walls with their downhill neighbors, sharing roofs with their uphill ones. From below, the settlement looks like an outcropping of paler, more regular rock — a long crest of stacked stone running for perhaps two days' walk along the saddle, with the higher peaks rising white-shouldered at either end. The buildings are the same limestone as the mountain because they *are* the mountain, quarried no more than a stone's throw from where they stand. Walls are mortared with a pale grey lime; roofs are flat slabs weighted with stones against the wind, or steep shingles of slate where the snow piles deepest.

There is no single street. There are stairs. Hundreds of flights of them, threading between the houses, sometimes ducking through tunnels of dressed stone where the path passes under a dwelling, sometimes opening onto small terraced plazas no bigger than a kitchen, where a single juniper grows in a stone basin or a public cistern catches rainfall through a carved spout shaped like an animal's mouth. The plazas are arranged by altitude rather than direction; one orients by going up or down, not left or right.

The vegetation is sparse but stubborn. Junipers, ground willow, cushion plants that grow tight to the rock like small green pillows, and in protected corners — south-facing, wind-broken — there are tiny terraced gardens of barley and hardy greens, the soil carried up by basket from lower valleys over generations. Every patch of dirt has been earned. A child here learns early that soil is a kind of wealth, less abundant than stone.

Water comes in pulses. Most days the air is dry and so cold that breath crystallizes on wool. But every few mornings a cloud rolls up from the valleys and settles against the ridgeline like a tide, and for an hour or two the whole city is inside the cloud — walls beading with moisture, roof slates darkening, the stone gutters running with thin clear streams that the inhabitants channel into cisterns through a system of carved troughs that has been refined across centuries. When the cloud lifts, the city steams faintly in the cold sun. In other seasons, snow does the same work more slowly. There is also a single spring, halfway down the eastern face, that issues from a crack in the rock with a small steady voice — the spring is sacred, attended, and the path to it is the most-walked stair in the settlement.

The fauna is what surprises a visitor most.

The cliffs are loud with birds, though the city itself is quiet. Choughs in their thousands, red-legged and glossy black, wheel in the updrafts off the ridge — their calls carry but the wind takes most of the sound away. Lammergeiers ride the thermals at noon, vast slow shapes that the children watch without much commenting on. In the rocks: pikas, marmots, a small grey hare that has learned to live in the gaps between foundation stones. Wild goats — ibex with absurd curving horns — move along ledges that look impassable, and at dawn one sometimes sees a small group of them on the city's own rooftops, having descended from the upper crags to lick salt from the stones where laundry water has dried. The people leave them alone. There are domestic yaks too, kept in stone byres on the lower terraces, and small flocks of black sheep that are taken down to summer pasture in the valleys.

The technology is patient and old. Hand tools, well-made. Bronze in some places, iron in others, but no machinery beyond the wheel, the lever, the pulley, the loom. Houses are heated by small clay stoves that burn dried yak dung and juniper twigs, and the smoke goes up through stone flues built into the shared walls — so that the whole spine of the city is, in winter, threaded with slow warmth, each household contributing to the next. The construction is layered in time as well as space: foundations from one century, upper stories from another, repairs and additions visible in the slightly different colors of the mortar. Nothing here was built quickly. Nothing here will be replaced quickly.

The sound, mostly, is wind. Wind across stone, wind through the narrow stairways, wind in the slates, wind in the dry seed-heads of the cushion plants. Under the wind: the small constant trickle of the cistern overflows, the distant chough-cries, the occasional ring of a hammer on stone from someone replacing a step or dressing a block, the lower note of a yak shifting in its byre, the susurrus of looms behind closed shutters. Voices are pitched low. The thin air makes shouting expensive. People lean close to talk.

Light is ordinary daylight but at altitude: clear, hard, the shadows very sharp, the blues of the sky deepening overhead almost to violet at zenith. At sunset the whole western face of the city goes gold for perhaps ten minutes and then turns the color of cold ash, and the lamps come on inside the windows one by one, butter-lamps mostly, a warm small yellow against the deepening blue.

The rules of the place are slow rules.

Change here happens on the scale of generations. A new house is added perhaps once in a decade; a stair is recut once in a lifetime. The mountain itself is changing faster than the city — losing flakes, shedding faces, occasionally dropping a boulder that the inhabitants must work around. There is a sense, walking the stairs, that the people are the patient ones and the mountain is the impulsive one, the young thing still figuring out its shape. The city is the mountain's older, calmer companion, sitting alongside it while it works things out.

And the scale — the scale is the last thing that settles on you. Because when you reach the highest plaza, the small one with the iron prayer-bell and the view in all directions, you understand that this ridge-city is one of many. South along the range, just visible in the haze, is another pale crest of construction. North, another. The range goes on. Each peak that can hold a settlement holds one, and between them, in the lower passes, are the trails and the trade and the slow movement of yaks carrying salt, barley, wool, news. The whole spine of the continent is inhabited like this — a single long thin civilization strung along the highest habitable line, a country shaped like a wire, with the lowlands lying empty and forested far below on either side, a place its people only visit, never live.

From the highest plaza, at the right hour, you can sometimes see weather being born. A small white cloud forms over one peak, drifts, joins another, and within an hour there is a storm somewhere far down the range, lightning silent at that distance, the sound of its thunder arriving so late and so softened that it might be mistaken for someone closing a heavy door several houses away.

The bell, when it rings, rings once. The wind takes the rest.
