# The Plateau Foundries

It sits at the top of a continent that has forgotten what continent it was. The rock here is so old it has gone soft in the way only stone can — not yielding, but rounded, sanded down by epochs of dry wind until the mountains read as long low swells, the bones of bones. You can walk for a day across what was once a peak and not realize you have crossed it.

The air is warm. Not hot — warm the way a stone is warm at midnight after a day of sun, except there is no sun here and there hasn't been for a long time. The warmth comes from below, a slow exhalation from somewhere in the mantle that has settled into a steady temperature and stayed there, the way a body finds a fever and holds it. No wind to speak of. The wind did its work and left.

It is dark. Not the dark of night, which implies a coming dawn, but the dark of a place that has resolved the question of light by simply not having any. The sky overhead — if it is a sky — shows nothing. No stars. Perhaps a high haze, perhaps something else; you cannot tell, and looking up for long produces a kind of vertigo, an awareness that the absence above you might be very deep or might be very close.

And yet you can see. The city sees to that.

---

The foundries are scattered across the plateau at distances of a kilometer, two kilometers, sometimes more. From any one of them you can make out the dim glow of three or four others, each a soft orange smear against the dark, like coals seen through frosted glass. They are enormous — squat ziggurats of dark brick and riveted plate, terraced with walkways and crowned with stacks that no longer smoke. Each one is its own small district: housing blocks built directly into the flanks, narrow stair-streets winding up the sides, blind windows, mineral-glazed tiles. Lamps burn at intervals along the walkways, fed by pipes that run down into the warm rock. The lamps do not flicker.

Between the foundries: nothing. Or almost nothing. The plateau between them is a pale tan dust over hard rock, swept smooth, with here and there a low cairn or the half-buried shoulder of a machine that has become part of the geology. The dust does not move. There are footpaths between settlements, worn deep, but you rarely meet anyone on them.

The foundries are running. This is the strangest thing. You can feel it through the soles of your feet — a low steady throb, not loud, more a pressure than a sound, the working of great slow engines somewhere below grade. Belts turning. Pistons that complete one stroke every several seconds, with the patience of tide. Somewhere ore is being moved, somewhere metal is being poured, somewhere a press is closing on something. But the chimneys are cold. Whatever heat the work produces is being kept, banked, fed back. The system has closed on itself.

What are they making? The yards beside each foundry are stacked with ingots, plates, coils of cable, finished components in neat rows. Nothing is being shipped. Nothing is being added to. The stacks are simply *there*, as they were yesterday, as they were a thousand years ago. The foundries produce, and what they produce is the inventory, and the inventory is complete. Production continues because production is the shape the place has taken, the way a river continues to flow even when no one is thirsty.

---

The people — if they are people — are few. You might see one on a high walkway, leaning on a railing, looking out at the orange smear of the next foundry over. You might pass one on a stair-street, going down as you go up. They nod. They do not speak much; there isn't much call for it. Their faces are calm in a way that suggests either deep peace or the absence of whatever it is that produces unrest. They tend the machines. The machines do not really need tending — they have arrived at their final configuration and will run as long as the rock is warm — but the tending is done anyway, gauges checked, levers touched, ledgers updated with numbers identical to yesterday's.

Fauna is almost absent. Sometimes, in the lamplight near a foundry, you might see a pale arthropod the size of a hand crossing a wall — six legs, no eyes, feeding on the mineral film that condenses on warm brick. They are slow and silent. In the dust between settlements, occasionally a track: something passed through, weeks ago or years ago, and the track has not been disturbed since.

No plants. There were never plants, or there have not been plants for so long the distinction is meaningless. The chemistry of this place runs on hot dry mineral cycles — sulfides, oxides, the slow patient reactions of rock with rock at temperature. Whatever life exists here is built from those reactions, scaled up.

---

Sound, when it occurs, is precious and small. The throb of the engines is so constant it stops being sound and becomes a quality of the air, like the warmth. Above that: the creak of a walkway under your weight. The tick of cooling metal somewhere. Once in a long while, a bell — high up on one of the ziggurats, struck by some mechanism for some reason, a single clear note that travels across the plateau and is answered, perhaps an hour later, by another bell at another foundry. Whether this is communication or coincidence, no one will say.

There is no weather. There is no season. The light from the lamps is the same now as it was when the lamps were lit, and the lamps were lit at a time no one remembers. The dust lies where it lies. The ingots wait in their yards. The arthropods cross the warm bricks. The bells answer each other across the dark.

---

The rule of this world, if it has one, seems to be: *what is, continues*. Not stasis — stasis implies effort, a holding-against. This is something gentler. The place has found its shape and the shape is being expressed, slowly, patiently, in warm dark and orange light and the soft pulse of engines that have outlasted their purpose and become something closer to a heartbeat.

You can stay. People do. The foundries have room. There is work, of a kind — the tending, the ledgers, the bells. The food is mineral and strange but sufficient. After a while you stop expecting the dark to lift, and then you stop noticing it is dark, and then you understand that you have always lived here, that the orange smears on the horizon are the only cities there have ever been, and that somewhere below your feet a piston is completing its slow stroke, and will complete another, and another, for as long as anything is.
