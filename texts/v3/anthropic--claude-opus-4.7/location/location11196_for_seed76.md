# The Bright Highlands

Begin with altitude. You are very high — high enough that the sky has gone a saturated indigo even at midday, and the sun, when it crosses, is not a warming thing but a brilliance, a hard white coin pressed against the dark. There is no water here. No rivers, no springs, no dew. And yet:

It is lush.

This is the first contradiction the place forces on you, and the place does not feel any obligation to resolve it quickly. You walk it for a while before you understand.

The ground is old. So old that whatever once heaved it up has long since finished heaving. The stone has settled into broad rolling shoulders and shallow basins, the edges of everything rounded the way river-stones are rounded, though no river did this — only the patient sandpaper of a few million years of wind and freeze-cycles and the small chemistries of cold light on cold mineral. The rock is pale: a soft bone-white quartzite shot through with veins of something glassier, almost translucent, which catches the sun and throws it sideways in long low gleams across the slopes.

And out of this dry pale stone, the vegetation.

Not grass. Not trees. Something between — call them *fronds*, since no better word arrives. They grow in scattered companies across the highlands, each plant a low rosette of pale blue-green blades that lift, at their centers, a single taller spire. The spires are translucent. In the brilliant light they glow from within like fiber-optic filaments, and when the wind moves them they bend with a slow elastic grace that suggests they are not quite the rigid cellulose-thing you expected. The fronds are widely spaced. You can walk for ten minutes between one company and the next, and the ground between is bare pale stone and a fine dust of mica.

How do they live without water?

They drink light. The thickness of their blades, the way the spires lens the sun — they are doing something photosynthesis alone cannot do. They pull moisture, perhaps, from the thin air itself, condensing it inside their tissues across the long cool nights. Each frond is a sealed system, a little terrarium. If you cut one (you will not cut one) you would find it wet inside, brilliantly wet, a held droplet of internal sea.

So: lush, but sparsely lush. Lushness as scattered jewels across a dry pale plateau, not as carpet. Lushness with breathing room.

The scale is the next thing that gets you. This plateau does not end. You climb a swell of stone expecting a horizon and find instead another swell, and another, and the curvature of the ground itself starts to suggest you are walking on something continent-sized, an entire raised world-shoulder, and the weather here is its own weather — you can see, far off across the basin, a column of pale dust lifting in a slow lazy rotation, a dry whirlwind that will travel for days and trouble no one. Above it, lenticular clouds stack in the high air, frozen-looking, made of ice crystals rather than water vapor, glittering. They cast their shadows in slow procession across the fronds, and the fronds, sensing the shadow, dim their internal glow by some small fraction, then brighten again when the sun returns. The whole plateau pulses, very slowly, in response to the cloud-shadows. You would not notice it unless you sat still for an hour.

The temperature is cool but not punishing. The light does most of the work of warming you; step into shade and you feel the altitude immediately, the thinness of the air, the bite. The wind is constant but not loud. It moves over stone too smooth to whistle through, around plants too soft to rattle. The dominant sound is almost nothing — a low pressure-hush, the sound the inside of your own ear makes, externalized. Occasionally a frond-spire, struck just right by a gust, emits a single clean tone, the way a wet finger draws a note from a wineglass. These tones are rare. Minutes apart. They do not gather into music; they punctuate the silence, and the silence absorbs them.

Fauna: there is some, but you have to be patient.

In the shadows beneath the larger frond-rosettes live small pale creatures the size of your thumb, six-legged, slow, with bodies that seem to be made of the same translucent material as the spires. They graze on something — perhaps on the mica-dust itself, perhaps on a film of microbial life invisible to you that grows on the undersides of the blades. They are nearly impossible to see in motion because they move only when nothing is watching, an effect that is surely coincidence but feels like rule. You will find their shed husks more often than you will find them: little glass shells curled in the lee of stones.

Higher up, riding the thermals — a single soaring thing, very far away, very high. You cannot tell if it is a bird or a kite of skin or something else entirely. It does not flap. It circles. There may be only one of them in this whole region of the world; there may be one of them per thousand square kilometers; the place is sparse enough that this is possible. You watch it for a long time and it does not descend.

The rules of this world, as you piece them together:

Light is the currency. Water is hoarded inside living tissue, never released, never spent. Death here is not decomposition (no bacteria of the kind you know, no fungi at this dryness) but slow crystallization — the dead frond hardens into a glassy spire and stays standing for centuries, becoming part of the mineral landscape, indistinguishable eventually from the quartz it rises from. So the plateau is haunted, gently, by its own ancestors. Some of the gleaming veins in the stone are not geology but very old plants.

Nothing has ever built anything here. No nest, no burrow you can find, no scrape. The small creatures live in the natural shade of the fronds; the high circler lives in the air. The plateau has never been disturbed by a tool, and a tool, dropped here, would feel obscene — a loud thing in a room of held breath.

You could stay. The air is thin but breathable. The light would feed you, if you were the right kind of creature, and perhaps over enough time it would change you into the right kind of creature. The fronds glow. The whirlwind moves on the far horizon, patient as a thought. The single high flier completes another circle.

The next clean tone, when it comes, is a long time away.
