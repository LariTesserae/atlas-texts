# The Aspirant Plateau

Begin high. You are already higher than you should be — somewhere past the altitude where rotor blades would stall, where the air thins to a kind of brilliant grief — and yet the plateau still rises. It is doing so now, beneath you, in slow heaves you can feel through your knees more than your eyes. The basalt here is still warm. Not from the sun (the sun is a small dim coin behind layers of vapor); warm because it was, until recently, somewhere else. The rock is so young it has not yet decided to be rock. Crystalline edges sharper than tools. Faces that have never weathered. If you press a palm flat against it your skin will lift away pale, the rock having taken some impression you cannot read.

The vegetation. This is the first thing that should not be possible: at this elevation there should be lichen and stunted shrub, and instead there is a forest. Broad-leaved, glossy, dense as a held breath. The leaves are not green so much as the idea of green refracted through low light — a wet pewter, an oil-on-water sheen. They grow from the basalt itself, no soil intervening, roots threaded down into hairline cracks like silver wire. The trunks are warm to the touch. They breathe. You can see the leaves move on their petioles with each inhalation, all of them at once, in long slow rhythms that do not match your own. Between the trunks, ferns and something that is almost a fern but has too many fingers. Vines that loop back into themselves and emerge from their own length. Mist condenses on every surface and runs down in slow beads, and this is most of the water there is: the rain has not arrived for a long time, but the forest sweats, and the sweat pools in cupped leaves and basalt hollows and is enough.

It is warm. Warmer than it has any right to be, this high. The warmth comes from underneath, from the rock, from the engines.

This is the second impossible thing. Listen — though there is little to hear, and what there is comes muffled, as if through felt. A low pulse. Not heartbeat-regular, but close. It comes from beneath the plateau, and at certain places, where the basalt has split along a still-forming seam, you can look down and see them. Engines. Or what the word *engine* might mean if you had only ever seen one in a dream. Long brass-colored shapes turning over slowly in cavities the size of cathedrals. Pistons that do not quite obey the geometry of their housings. Belts that loop through walls and emerge elsewhere, in other cavities, driving other unknowable mechanisms. They glow softly, the dim orange of a banked forge. No one tends them. There are no catwalks, no gauges, no rivets — these are not built things. They have grown, the way the trees have grown, out of whatever this plateau is doing. The forest exhales in their rhythm. Or they exhale in the forest's. It is the same rhythm; that is the point.

The plateau is *making itself*. That is what it is doing. The young rock, the impossible forest, the engines beneath — all of it is the plateau's effort to become real. To finish. And it is not finishing; it is straining toward completion the way a held note strains, and the strain is what generates everything.

Now the third impossible thing, which is harder to describe.

Walk a hundred paces in any direction. The forest will be the same forest, but it will not be the same forest. Trees you passed will be on your other side. A stream you crossed will be ahead of you again, running the other way. Look up: a flock of something is crossing the sky, animals the size of horses with too many wings, soundless, and when you look up an hour later the same flock is crossing in the same place, and you cannot tell if they are the same flock late, or a different flock arriving on schedule, or whether *flock* and *crossing* are local conditions of the air here, things the plateau does instead of weather.

There is weather, too. Of its own. Clouds form *inside* the forest, between trunks, at chest height, and drift through and dissolve. Lightning happens in the soil. Once an hour, or once a year — time is one of the things the plateau has not finished — a wind comes up from below, through the engine-cavities, smelling of hot metal and crushed leaves, and every leaf on every tree turns to face it at once with a sound like a single sigh, the loudest sound the place ever makes, and then it passes.

The fauna are everywhere and almost invisible. You will feel watched constantly and see almost nothing. Small things in the undergrowth with eyes that reflect more light than falls on them. Things in the canopy that move from branch to branch without crossing the space between. A creature that is mostly a long ribbon of muscle threads itself through a knot of vines and emerges shorter, having left some of itself behind, which goes on living separately. Insects that are clearly insects but in pairs, each pair sharing a single shadow. Pale grazing animals the shape of deer, except that their legs end before reaching the ground; they hover a hand's-breadth above the basalt and you can see, if you look, that they do not know this is unusual. They crop the lower ferns. They look at you with patient, slightly embarrassed expressions, as if they suspect they are doing something wrong but cannot remember what.

Nothing here is loud. The engines pulse but do not roar. The animals make almost no sound at all; they have evolved into a kind of considerate hush, or the plateau requires it of them, or sound itself has not been completely manufactured yet at this altitude. When you speak your voice comes out smaller than you expect, as if some of it stayed behind in your throat.

The scale. You have not understood the scale yet. The plateau is not a plateau. It is the top surface of something the size of a continent, lifting. From its edge — which you will not reach; the edge moves away faster than you can walk — you would see the rest of the world far below, normal world, with normal weather, and you would understand that this place is rising out of it the way a thought rises out of a mind. Storms gather around its flanks because it displaces so much sky. It has latitudes. It has its own seasons, which are: the season of more engines, the season of leaf-turning, the season when the rock forgets briefly what it is and softens, and the season of arrivals, when things come up through the basalt that were not there before — new species, new mechanisms, once an entire small lake complete with fish, which had to learn very quickly to be lake-fish in a place that had not previously contained water at that location.

There are no built things. No path, no cairn, no shelter, no sign that anyone has ever decided anything here. And yet the engines. And yet the rhythm. The plateau is industrial without industry, mechanized without mechanics, productive without product — unless the product is itself, unless what it is manufacturing, slowly, with enormous straining effort, in dim light and warm damp and near-silence, is the fact of its own existence.

Stand still long enough and you will feel it: the plateau testing whether you, too, can be incorporated. A small warmth at the soles of your feet. A leaf turning, very slowly, to consider you. The question is gentle and not hostile. It is only that the place is still deciding what it contains, and you have wandered in during the deciding.

You can leave. Probably. The way down is mostly down.

But the warmth is good. And the engines are beautiful, in their cavities, turning over. And the deer that do not touch the ground are looking at you now with the same patient embarrassment, and one of them is drifting closer, and the forest exhales, and you exhale with it without meaning to, and your breath joins the rhythm, and the plateau, attentive, makes a small adjustment somewhere far below, accommodating.
