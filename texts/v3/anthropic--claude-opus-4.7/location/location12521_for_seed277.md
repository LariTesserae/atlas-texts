# The Cinder Plateau

Begin with the dark. Not the dark of night — though it is always night here, in the sense that the sun has never been seen — but the dark of something older. The plateau sits high enough that the air thins against your skin, warm and dry-feeling, and yet there is always a faint slick of moisture on every surface, condensed out of the breath of the world below. Touch any stone and your fingers come away damp.

The light, when there is light, comes from the engines.

They are not built. That is the first thing to understand. No one made them. They are the plateau, or the plateau is them — vast machine-forms half-emerged from the basalt, hundreds of meters across, weathered into the rock the way a stream weathers a stone. Pistons the size of cathedrals rise and fall once every several hours, so slowly you can sit and watch one without seeing it move, and only later notice the shadow has shifted. Flywheels lie embedded in the cliffsides like fossils of wheels. There are pipes that run for kilometers along the canyon floors, warm to the touch, breathing. Somewhere deep beneath, things turn.

Where the engines work, they glow. A dim ember-orange seeps from the seams of the great housings, from grilles set into the rock, from the cracks of valves that haven't fully closed in geological time. This is the only light. It pools in the low places. Walk far enough between engines and the dark becomes total, and you navigate by the warmth of the ground underfoot, by the faint vibration that tells you a pump-house is near.

The plateau is enormous. Large enough that weather forms in it. Hot updrafts from a working engine-field will lift columns of damp air kilometers into the dark sky, where they cool and condense and fall back as a slow rain that smells of iron and warm oil. Some basins have their own constant drizzle. Others are bone-dry, the heat of a great hidden cylinder beneath drying the air for a hundred kilometers around. You can stand at the lip of one such basin and watch rain falling into it, evaporating before it touches the ground, lit faintly from below by the orange glow of whatever turns there.

The vegetation is sparse and strange. Black-leaved succulents that drink condensation off the metal housings. Pale lichens that prefer the warm grilles, where they grow in patterns that look almost like circuit-traces but aren't — just lichen, following heat. In the wetter basins, low groves of something tree-like, with bark that flakes in plates like old paint, and a habit of dropping their leaves whenever a nearby engine cycles down. They know the rhythms.

And the animals — there are many. This is the thing that surprises. The plateau is rich with life, scattered through its vastness. Six-legged grazers the size of cattle, with broad soft feet, who follow the warm pipelines through cold seasons. Their eyes are enormous and reflect the ember-light back in two orange coins. Pack hunters, lean and quiet, who learn the engines' schedules and ambush at the moment a great piston falls, using the deep thud to mask their rush. Things that climb the flywheels and nest in the gear-teeth. A kind of pale moth, palm-sized, that lays its eggs only on warm metal and is the favored food of nearly everything. Burrowing creatures who tunnel through the tailings around the vents.

There are no people. There have never been people. No one built the engines and no one tends them; they tend themselves, or don't, and where they have stopped they have stopped for ages, gone cold, and the rock has crept over them, and the animals have made dens in their cooling chambers.

The sound: mostly quiet. This too surprises. You expect industry to roar. But the engines are so slow, and so large, that their work happens in long sighs and far-off rhythms. A piston-fall produces a deep concussion you feel more than hear, like a heartbeat from underground. Between such events there is the soft drip of condensation, the call of some night-bird with a flute-voice, the wind moving through a derelict housing and pulling a tone out of it. Long silences. Then, far away, the breath of a valve releasing, a shape of steam rising into the dark and catching the orange light and going out.

The rules of the place, as far as one can intuit them: the engines do something, somewhere, for some purpose, and the ecology has draped itself across this purpose without inquiring. The grazers don't know they are grazing on a machine. The lichens don't know they are circuitry-mimics. Everything has found its niche in the long warm dark, and the engines turn, and the rain falls in some basins and not others, and once in a great while a piston that has cycled for ten thousand years cycles for the last time and goes still, and the cold begins to move into that valley, and the animals leave, and a new silence is added to the plateau's enormous, patient quiet.

If you stayed long enough, you would learn to read the glow-patterns the way a sailor reads stars. You would know which basin was breathing and which was holding its breath. You would walk for days between engines, in a darkness warm as blood, and feel the ground pulse, and understand that you were inside something — not malevolent, not benevolent, simply working — and that it had been working long before you and would work long after, and that this was, in its strange way, a kind of peace.
