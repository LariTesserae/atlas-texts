# The Sweatworks

Begin with what your feet meet: tile. Hand-pressed clay tile, glazed in a hundred slightly wrong greens, laid in courses that go on past where courses should reasonably go. The tile is hot. Not painful — but hot the way a kettle is hot a minute after the boil, and the heat is in the floor itself, rising from somewhere far below where the makers laid down whatever they laid down to make this place a place.

The tile sweats. That is the first miracle and the smallest. Water beads on the glaze, runs in slow rills along the grout, gathers in the dished centers of certain panels where the makers — knowing — pressed thumb-hollows. The hollows fill. The hollows overflow. The water never quite arrives anywhere; somewhere a few hundred meters off it has already evaporated back into the low yellow fog that hangs at chest height across the whole plain.

The plain is the size of a sea. You cannot see its edge. You can sometimes see where it *was* an edge, an hour ago, before the makers moved it.

---

There are no plants. There were never plants. The closest thing is mold — pale apricot crusts in the grout lines, and in the wetter low places a kind of slick that might be alive in the sense that a chemical reaction is alive, sluggishly metabolizing the minerals leached from the tile. Nothing eats the mold. Nothing eats anything. The ecology is one step earlier than ecology.

What there is, instead of life, is **construction**. Everything you see was made. Every undulation in the floor is a deliberate undulation. The low hills in the middle distance are brick. The puddles have rims. The clouds — and this took the longest to understand — the clouds have armatures, fine bone-colored lattices that the steam clings to and which, when a cloud dissolves, remain hanging in the air for a moment like the skeleton of a thing that has just been undressed.

The makers, when you see them, are stooped figures in long aprons, working with hand tools. Trowels. Wooden mallets. A kind of curved knife for trimming the edges of newly laid courses. They do not speak, or they speak so rarely that in a day's walk you might hear one syllable, dropped like a coin. They are laying the world down in front of themselves and, somewhere behind, other figures are taking it up again. The plain is being made and unmade at the rate a calm person breathes.

---

This is where the parameters stop describing and the place starts insisting on itself:

The makers are also the tiles. Not metaphorically. When a maker finishes a course and stands and stretches, sometimes she lies down in the next gap and becomes the next panel, glaze and all, and another figure rises three courses back and walks forward with her trowel. The substitution is not noticed because there is no one to notice it; the makers do not have an inside from which to track such things. They are surface all the way down. The place is staffed by its own material.

And the heat — the heat is the labor. You can feel it as labor, if you put your palm flat to the floor. The tile is hot because somewhere in it, something is *trying.* Trying to be tile. Trying to stay tile against the pressure of the unmaking that is moving across the plain in slow weather-fronts, visible as shimmer.

---

The weather here is reality weather. A front comes through and for a quarter hour the rules loosen: water runs uphill, or the grout-lines become the only real things and the tiles between them are just suggestions, or every surface remembers a color it had before being fired and flickers between the two. The makers work through it. They have aprons for this. The aprons are lead-colored and very heavy and seem to anchor the makers to one version of themselves while the front passes over.

You, if you are here, do not have an apron. During a front you may find that your left hand has been laid into the floor as a tile and you will need to wait, politely, for a maker to come by and pry it up with her curved knife and apologize, wordlessly, with a small bow, before continuing on.

This happens. It is not the worst thing.

---

The sound is mostly the sound of trowels. A soft *tock*, and a softer *scrape*, repeated at irregular intervals across kilometers, so that the plain has a kind of percussion to it like distant rain on a tin roof, except slower and made by hands. Under that: the hiss of the sweating tile. Under that, almost not a sound: the breathing of whatever is below, keeping everything at temperature.

Occasionally — and this is the loudest the world gets — a section of the plain a long way off will be judged finished, by some standard nobody explains, and a bell will ring once. The bell is somewhere very high up, in the fog, on an armature you cannot see. After the bell, that whole section is taken apart in the next hour and begun again.

You learn, after a while, not to grieve the sections. They were going to be taken apart. That was always what they were for.

---

What the plain is *for*, in the larger sense, is not knowable from inside it. You can speculate. It looks like a rehearsal — like a world practicing being a world, getting the tile right before laying down soil, getting the water cycle right before introducing anything that would need to drink. It looks like the patient, sweating, hand-tool work of something that intends, eventually, to have plants. The mold in the grout might be the first attempt. The fog might be the second.

Or it looks like the opposite: a world taken apart down to its substrate, the dirt and forests and animals all stripped away, and what remains is just the heated tile floor of the room they were standing in, manned now by janitorial figures who don't remember what they were cleaning up after.

The makers do not say. The makers lay the next tile. The hollow in its center fills with water and the water, after a while, is gone.
