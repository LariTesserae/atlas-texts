# The Cistern at the Roof of the World

Start with the mountain, because everything follows from the mountain. It is one of the old ones — not old in the human sense but old in the geological sense, which is a different category of age entirely. Its summit was sharp once. Now it is a worn molar, rounded by something like seventy million years of wind and ice and the patient chemistry of rain. The peaks around it are taller, younger, more theatrical. This one has settled.

Near the top, just below where the air thins to almost nothing, there is a structure cut into the mountain's flank. You would not see it from outside. The entrance is a single hatch of green-black bronze, the size of a manhole cover, set into a shelf of rock and weathered to match. Whoever made it is no longer making things. The hinges still work.

Inside: a chamber roughly the size of a cathedral's lady chapel — say, twelve meters across, fifteen high. And inside the chamber: water. Almost all of it is water. The cistern is full nearly to its vaulted ceiling, leaving only a meter or so of humid air at the top, where lamps hang on chains from corroded bosses and cast a yellow-green light down through the surface.

The water is warm. This is the first surprise. At this elevation it should be ice, but something beneath the floor of the cistern is still running — a geothermal pump, or the slow heat-death of a reactor, or a furnace tended by no one for a very long time. You can hear it. The whole room hums at a frequency just below comfortable, and every few minutes a deeper sound rolls up through the floor plates: a slow industrial peristalsis, the sound of large valves opening somewhere, water moving through pipes the size of train tunnels. The cistern is part of something larger that you cannot see and will never see.

The walls are tiled. Hand-sized hexagons of glazed ceramic in six or seven shades of blue and green, laid in a pattern that almost resolves into a picture and then refuses to. Most of the tiles are still attached. Some have fallen and lie at the bottom of the cistern in drifts, their glaze still bright. The grout between them is furred with a pale algae that fluoresces faintly when the lamps swing.

The vegetation is underwater. This is what makes the place lush despite its sparseness. Long ribbons of a kelp-analogue grow up the walls, anchored in the grout-cracks, reaching toward the lamps. Between them: tufts of something feathery and red, and a sparse forest of stalked things that might be plants and might be filter-feeders — at this depth of evolutionary settlement, the distinction has worn down like everything else. The plants are not crowded. They have had time to find their spacing. Each clump sits in its own territory of bare tile, and the water between them is clear enough that you can see all the way across the chamber, see the opposite wall warbling through the thermocline.

The fauna: small, abundant, mostly transparent. Shrimp-things the length of a finger graze the algae. A school of something eel-shaped and the color of tarnished silver patrols the upper meter, where the air is. They breathe both — they come up to the air-gap and gulp, then sink back. A single larger animal lives here too, though you might not see it for hours. It is the size of a house cat, segmented, with too many legs, and it walks the ceiling upside-down, picking off the eels when they surface. It has been the apex predator of this room for longer than any nation on the surface has existed. It is not lonely. It does not have the apparatus for loneliness.

Above the waterline, in the slim band of air: machinery. A catwalk runs around the chamber at ceiling-height, half-submerged where the water has risen over the centuries. Brass gauges, their faces clouded. A pneumatic system of some kind, with rubber bellows long since perished into black flakes. One pressure dial still moves, very slowly, responding to the breathing of whatever lives in the pipes below. A bank of levers, unlabeled. Nobody is going to pull them.

The light comes from sodium-yellow bulbs in cages of brass wire, eight of them, hung at staggered heights. Three are out. The five still burning are powered by the same patient machine that warms the water, and they will continue to burn until it stops, which will not be soon. Their light reaches the bottom of the cistern but loses most of its yellow on the way, so the floor is lit in a deep submarine green. Things move down there. Mostly they are shrimp.

The rules of this place: nothing leaves. The hatch opens but there is nowhere outside to go — only the killing cold of the high atmosphere, the scoured rock, the wind. Nothing arrives either. The ecology inside the cistern is closed and has been closed long enough that everything in it has co-evolved into a kind of agreement. The eels eat the shrimp. The shrimp eat the algae. The algae eat the light. The ceiling-walker eats the eels. When something dies it sinks to the tile floor and is broken down by the smaller things, and the water cycles through the unseen pump and comes back warm and clean, and the cycle has the quality of having been the cycle forever.

It is loud in here. The hum, the periodic gurgle of the great valves, the constant small percussion of water dripping from the catwalks, the occasional clank of a tile finally losing its grip and falling — these sounds bounce around the tiled chamber and never quite die. If you put your hand on the wall you can feel the mountain itself transmitting deeper sounds from elsewhere in the system: distant rooms, distant pumps, distant pressures. You will never see those rooms.

The strangeness is not in any single feature. It is in the proportion. A warm coral garden the size of a small chapel, lit by failing industrial lamps, sealed inside a worn-down mountain at an altitude where humans cannot breathe, running on machinery whose makers are forgotten, tended by a single patient predator on the ceiling. Everything in balance. Everything humming. Everything having long since decided what it is.

The lamps swing, very slightly, when the valves cycle. The eels rise. The shrimp graze. The mountain continues its enormous slow forgetting of its own shape, and somewhere far below, water moves.
