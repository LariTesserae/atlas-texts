# The Sumpworks

There is a town built into a tidal flat that was, geologically speaking, a riverbed last week. The mud is still finding its shape. When the workmen drove the foundation piles in, the piles found nothing to refuse them — they went down until friction stopped them, and the buildings settled where they settled, slightly canted, slightly subsiding, and stopped. That was a generation ago, and nothing has changed since. Equilibrium was reached and held.

The town occupies maybe four hundred meters of low ground between two ridges of fresh black rock — basalt, perhaps, sharp-edged, not yet rounded by anything. The ridges sweat. Water beads on them constantly and runs in thin sheets down their faces, pooling at their feet into shallow rust-colored pans that never fully drain and never fully fill. The air sits on the skin like a wet cloth left in the sun.

The buildings are brick and corrugated iron and tar-blacked timber, three and four stories, packed close along three parallel streets and the alleys that braid between them. Pipes run everywhere — overhead, underfoot, lashed to walls, crossing the streets at chest height so you duck without thinking. Some carry steam. Some carry the standing water out from under the foundations and dump it, futilely, into the channels that thread the town, where it joins more water and goes nowhere in particular. The pumps run continuously. They have run continuously since the town was built. Their sound is the sound of the place: a low triple-stroke chuff from the central engine house, and answering chuffs from the satellite pumps, and under all of it the hiss of relief valves and the slap of belt-driven fans pushing hot air through the rolling-mill shed at the south end.

The mill produces something — sheet metal, probably, judging by the stacks behind it — but the production rate never varies. The same tonnage leaves on the same barges down the same channel every day. The channel was dredged once and has not silted up, because the pumps will not allow it to.

Vegetation is almost an afterthought. A few fig trees have rooted in cracks of the basalt ridge and hold on, broad-leaved and dust-grey. Reeds stand in the rust pans, never spreading, never dying back. A single banyan grew up through the floor of the old customs house before anyone built around it; the customs house was built around it instead, and the tree and the building have reached an accommodation, the tree's aerial roots taking the place of certain structural members.

Fauna, on the other hand, is everywhere. Frogs — small, brass-colored, loud — line every ledge and pipe-junction. Their calls don't quite drown the pumps but braid into them. Egrets stalk the channels with industrial patience, ignoring the men who ignore them back. Rats keep to the warehouses. There are crabs in the rust pans, pale and slow. At dusk, which is when most of the workforce moves between shifts, bats come out from under the eaves in numbers that suggest the eaves are mostly bat by volume, and they thread between the overhead pipes hunting the moths that the gas-lamps draw.

The lamps are gas, low-pressure, dim. The town never gets properly bright. Even at noon the steam and the haze off the wet rock keep the light soft and tea-colored. At night the lamps make pools at intervals and the rest is shadow and the orange flicker from the mill's open hearth doors when they're cracked for venting.

The rules of the place, such as they are:

The water table is at the surface. Dig anywhere and you find water within a hand's depth. The dead are not buried here; they are sent upriver to the ridges, where there is dry rock to receive them.

The pumps must not stop. There is a guild of pump-men, and they are paid well, and they are the only people in town whose work is taken with real seriousness. Everyone else's work is taken as a habit.

Nothing grows or shrinks. The town does not expand — there is nowhere for it to expand to, hemmed by ridge and channel — and it does not contract, because the work continues and the work needs the workers and the workers need housing and the housing was built for exactly the workers there are. Children are rare. Old people are common. The demographic, like everything else, has reached its level.

The basalt is young, and the town is young, and both are behaving as though they will be here forever, which is the particular illusion of things that have only just arrived.

A traveler passing through — and travelers do pass through, on the barges — usually remarks on the heat first, then the noise, then, after a night's sleep in one of the two boarding houses, the strange quality of having woken into exactly the same town they fell asleep in. Not similar. The same. The frog on the windowsill is the same frog. The shift-bell rings at the same moment. The light is the same tea-color through the same wet air.

Then the barge horn sounds, and they go, and the Sumpworks closes behind them like water closing over a dropped stone, which is to say: with a small disturbance, quickly resolved, and then the surface again, and the surface again, and the surface again.
