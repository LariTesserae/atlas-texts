# The Drowned Manufactory

Begin with the water, because everything begins with the water here. It is not a sea exactly. It has the salinity of a sea and the warmth of bathwater left out overnight, but it disobeys. In places it stacks itself into transparent columns that rise twenty meters before sighing back down. In other places it runs uphill along the flanks of the new islands, sheeting against gravity in slow silver curtains. You can walk into it waist-deep and feel it pulling in four directions at once, and none of them down.

The islands are young — so young you can watch them. Basalt domes shoulder up through the surface in slow, steady pushes, glassy and black and edged like broken bottles, hissing where the water touches them. They have not had time to be worn. Their angles are still mathematical. Plants are already on them, though — that is the strange part — vines and broad-leafed things with stems like green cable, fronds the size of bed-sheets, mosses laid down so thick they squelch underfoot. The vegetation arrives faster than the stone. It does not wait for soil. It eats directly from the steam.

Through all of this, and from beneath all of this, comes the noise.

The noise is the second thing. It is the dominant thing. Somewhere under the shallow warm water — and also, impossibly, inside the islands, and also in the low cloud-ceiling overhead — vast machinery is running. You can feel the bass of it in your sternum before you can hear the upper registers. Pistons the size of cathedrals. Belt-drives slapping in three-four time. The shriek of metal taking on load. The grinding hum of dynamos. It comes up through the soles of your feet and out of the surface of the water as a fine constant trembling that turns every puddle into a tuning fork. Conversation is impossible. Thought is difficult. You learn quickly to point.

What is being manufactured here is not clear. The factories are not visible, only inferred. Occasionally a vent opens in the seafloor and exhales a column of warm oily bubbles that smell of solder and crushed grass. Occasionally a finished thing surfaces — a brass flywheel, a spool of copper wire still warm, a length of articulated chain — and bobs for a while and then sinks again, or dissolves into the water like sugar, or is overgrown in minutes by the eager vines and pulled down into the green.

The light is low and uncertain. There is a sun, presumably, but the cloud-deck is permanent and the cloud-deck is also producing rain — not a storm, just a constant warm sieve of rain that thickens and thins but never stops. Everything is seen through this gauze. The light that does come through is the color of weak tea. At certain hours, for reasons no one has worked out, the rain falls *upward* for a few minutes, and the sea-surface dimples in reverse, and you have to close your mouth.

The built things are scattered and partial. A pier of riveted iron rising out of the water and ending in the air, going nowhere, its end-rails rusted to lace. A pumping station on one of the older domes, its housing cracked open, its internals still moving — gears turning, rods reciprocating — though nothing is connected to anything and no fluid passes through. A row of six identical brick chimneys standing in a shallow lagoon, none of them attached to any building, all of them smoking. A telegraph pole leaning out of a thicket of vines with the wire still tight and humming. These are not ruins. Ruins imply that there was once a coherent whole that has decayed. These look more like fragments of a building that has not been assembled yet — or that is being assembled somewhere else, and only its shadow falls here.

There are no animals. None. No fish in the warm water, no insects in the lush green, no birds in the low sky. This is the thing that takes longest to notice and then becomes impossible to un-notice. The vegetation moves — the great fronds shift, the vines climb visibly, tendrils probe the air — but nothing moves *across* the vegetation. The factories run, but no one tends them. The place is densely, even oppressively populated, but every presence is either a plant, a machine, a stone, or the water itself, and each of these is busy with its own work, and none of them acknowledge you.

The density is the third thing, after water and noise. Every cubic meter contains something. Stand still and a vine touches your shoulder. Take a step and a frond closes the gap behind you. The water itself is crowded — full of suspended particulate, threads of algae, drifting filaments of something that might be fungal, slow rolling clouds of warm silt. You are never alone here and you are always alone here, and these two facts do not contradict each other; they reinforce.

Reality, finally, is thin. This is the underlying condition. The water flowing uphill, the rain falling up, the manufactured objects appearing and dissolving, the islands rising visibly — these are symptoms. The deeper symptom is that the place does not commit to itself. Look away from a chimney and look back and there may be five chimneys, or none, or the same one in a slightly different posture. The basalt dome you climbed yesterday is a different shape today, and not because of erosion — erosion is barely starting here — but because the dome has reconsidered. You begin to suspect that the factories under the sea are not making *things*. They are making the place. They are running full-tilt, deafening, ceaseless, to produce the islands and the vines and the rain and the warm crowded water, and they have not finished, and they will not finish, and the rough-edged unfinishedness of everything is not a stage but a permanent condition.

It is warm. It is loud. It is alive without being inhabited. It is being built, constantly, by something that has never paused long enough to draw the plans.

If you sit on one of the new black islands with your feet in the warm water and the rain on your shoulders and the bass of the under-engines in your bones, and you let the great green fronds lean in around you, you can almost believe you are being manufactured too — that you are one of the brass flywheels, surfaced briefly, soon to sink or dissolve or be overgrown.

It is not unpleasant. That is the last surprising thing. For all its noise and strangeness and absence of any creature to speak with, the place is not hostile. It is merely busy. It is making itself, loudly, and you are welcome to watch.
