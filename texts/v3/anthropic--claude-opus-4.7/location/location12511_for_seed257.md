# The Drowned Almshouse

It sits where a mountain shoulder used to be — though the mountain is gone now, worn down to a stub beneath a warm sea, and only the building remains, because the building was made to remain. You come upon it at depth, two kilometers down, where the water is the temperature of old bathwater from some geothermal seep that has been bleeding heat into this pocket for longer than the building has stood, which is itself a duration that does not flatter the imagination.

It is small. That is the first thing. After the scale of the ocean above, the almshouse is almost an insult to it — a single low structure, perhaps forty paces along its longest wall, with a sunken courtyard and a half-collapsed cloister running around three sides. Cathedral-vaulted in places, but a cathedral built for a congregation of forty, not four thousand. Hamlet-sized. The kind of building that suggests an order of monks or a guild-house or some institution that no one now remembers, and that may have been forgotten before the sea ever rose.

Everything is made. There is no rock here that was not quarried, no floor that was not laid, no lintel that was not carved. Beneath the building there is presumably a foundation, and beneath that, presumably, the bones of the mountain — but you cannot see it. The made-world is total. Even what looks like a tumbled boulder in the courtyard, when you drift closer, reveals tool-marks: a hand-adze, something like a chisel, the bite of a soft-metal blade worked by someone who knew the stone but did not have the luxury of harder tools. Bronze-era work, or its analog. No machine ever touched this place.

And it is dark. Not the absolute black of the true abyss — there is a dim, brownish suggestion of light from somewhere, maybe a vent-glow filtered through the water, maybe a phosphorescence in the stones themselves, some old paint that absorbed sunlight a thousand years ago and is still letting it go. Enough to see by, barely. Enough to make out shapes. Your eyes adjust and the courtyard resolves itself in shades of bruise and umber.

No plants. None. Not even algae on the walls, which is strange — you would expect algae, you would expect the whole place to be furred with green — but something in the stone or the water refuses it. Perhaps the masons mixed something into the mortar. Perhaps the water is too still here, too lightless, too old. The building has outlived even the possibility of plant life finding it. It is mineral all the way through, the way a tomb is mineral.

But it is *crowded*. This is the contradiction the place holds. There is no vegetation and yet there is density everywhere — fauna, layered, near-touching. The courtyard is full of them. Soft-bodied things, mostly, the size of cats or small dogs, with bodies like folded velvet and slow fans of cilia along their flanks. They drift between the columns of the cloister, hundreds of them, settling on the carved stone and lifting off again with a sigh that displaces no water you can feel. They cluster thickest in the vaulted refectory at the building's heart, where some warm current rises through a grate in the floor, and there they hang in stacked, breathing layers, three and four deep, the lowest ones pressed against the stone and the highest ones brushing the ribs of the ceiling.

There are other creatures too. Pale, jointed crawlers, the size of your hand, moving along the walls with deliberate patience — they are eating something, or grooming something, or doing whatever it is they have done here for the last several thousand years. Worms in the cracks of the flagstones, thick as fingers, milk-colored. A single larger animal, occasionally, that swims slowly through the cloister and is mostly mouth, and which the velvet-things part for without alarm. They have lived together long enough to have arrangements.

The sound is almost nothing. The faintest grinding when the crawlers move on stone. A soft *pock* now and then — some small valve opening, some shell clicking, some bubble released from a vent. Otherwise the silence is so complete that you can hear the blood in your own ears, if you have ears, if you have blood. The water carries sound beautifully and there is just nothing here making any.

Everything moves slowly. The current through the grate is gentle and constant. The velvet-things drift on hour-long circuits. The crawlers travel perhaps a meter in a day. There are no events here, only processes. The building is eroding, but on a timescale that makes the erosion look like a kind of slow exhalation rather than damage — corners rounded, inscriptions softened to suggestions, the carved face above the refectory door reduced to a smooth oval with two faint dimples where the eyes were. Whatever it depicted, it is now only a memory of a depiction. Settled into its final form, or nearly so. Another ten thousand years and it will be gone entirely, and the velvet-things will still be hanging in the warm column of water above the grate, indifferent to the loss.

The rules of this place, as far as I can determine them:

The building does not decay further than it has. It has reached some equilibrium with the water and the warmth, and it is taking its time about the rest.

The animals do not eat each other, or do not seem to. There is some other food source — perhaps the warm vent feeds them chemically, perhaps the stones themselves are slowly dissolving and feeding the worms who feed the crawlers who feed the velvet-things in some cycle too slow to observe.

Nothing arrives and nothing leaves. The almshouse is a closed system. Whatever swam down here originally is what is here now, in successive generations, none of which have any reason to seek the surface and none of which would survive the climb.

And there is a sense — this is the unusual part, the weirdness that the parameters insist on — that the building is still *operating*. That its function, whatever it was, has not ended. The crawlers move in patterns that almost look like routes. The velvet-things gather in the refectory at intervals that almost look like meals. The currents through the grate pulse, very faintly, on a rhythm that could be a heartbeat or could be the tide two kilometers above, transmitted down through some mechanism the masons built and did not explain. The almshouse takes care of its tenants. It has been doing so for a very long time. The tenants have changed shape, but the care has not lapsed.

You could stay and watch it for a year and see almost nothing happen, and yet leave with the strong impression that you had witnessed something — some patient, ongoing work — that did not require you, and did not notice you, and would continue exactly as it had been continuing, in the warm dark, beneath the weight of all that water, for as long as the stones held their shape.
