# The Worn Teeth of Ahal-Ruun

The mountains here are old past any reckoning. Once, perhaps, they stood like spears — now they are molars, ground-down stumps of stone spread across a high plateau that runs, by foot, maybe nine days east to west. The plateau itself sits at such altitude that clouds move *below* it as often as above, and when you walk to the edge of any of its great shelves you look down onto a sea of white backs heaving and tearing themselves apart.

That is the first thing. The second thing is the sound.

The wind at this height does not blow the way wind blows in lower places. It *arrives*. It comes in slabs, in avalanches of air, and the ancient stone teeth are bored through with holes — wind-eaten tunnels, blister-caves, the lace-work of a hundred million years of weather — so that the whole plateau *sings*. Not metaphorically. Each stump of mountain has its own voice: deep organ-notes from the largest hollows, reedy shrieks from the thinner punctures, a sub-bass thrum you feel in the long bones of your legs before you hear it in your ears. When a storm comes through — and storms come through constantly, the sky here never settles — the chord builds into something past music, a roaring that would shatter a lowlander's mind in an hour. The few people who live here have ears that look slightly wrong: the cartilage thickened, folded forward like cupped hands turned inside out. A kind of evolutionary wincing.

And yet — and here is where the place becomes itself — the plateau is *green*.

Not the thin green of alpine stubble. Lush. Extravagant. Moss in ankle-deep carpets, pillow-moss and hair-moss and a red-gold moss that glows faintly in shade. Ferns growing out of the wind-holes in the rock, whole fern-colonies making the mountains furred. Flowering vines in the sheltered lees. Trees — small, wind-wrought, but real trees, with trunks thicker than a man and crowns pressed flat against the stone like hands pressed to a window. The vegetation survives the wind by hugging. Everything here grows sideways or downward or into cracks. Nothing stands up. Standing up is what the mountains used to do, before time taught them better.

The water comes from the clouds. Being *in* the clouds, half the time, means the whole plateau drips. Mist condenses on fern-fronds, runs down leaf-channels, pools in rock-bowls, seeps. There are no rivers of any size — the plateau is too worn and too porous, the water sinks — but every surface is damp, every stone wears a skin of wet, and in the hollows you find clear cold pools that have probably been filling and emptying the same way since before anything walked upright. You drink from them by lying on your belly. The water tastes of stone and of some faint mineral sweetness, like iron kissed by honey.

For fauna: almost nothing. A kind of long-limbed goat-thing, rare, seen mostly as silhouettes on far ridges. Lichen-grazing beetles the size of a thumbnail. A white bird, solitary, that rides the updrafts on the plateau's edges and makes no sound any human has heard (how would you hear it, through the singing of the stone?). That is nearly the whole bestiary. The density of living warm-blooded things per cubic mile is so low that you can walk for days and meet nothing but moss.

And yet the place is *not* empty, because the place is *active*. The weather does not rest. Clouds pour up the cliff-faces and spill across the plateau and tear themselves apart on the tooth-stumps; rain falls sideways; sun breaks through in shafts that move across the landscape like searchlights from some enormous patient machine; fog thickens and thins a dozen times an hour. Shadows race. Light races. The visibility changes from miles to arm's-length and back again while you are tying a bootlace. Weather that elsewhere would take a season here takes a quarter-hour. It is the sky's turbulence that keeps the plateau alive — feeding the moss, watering the ferns, pressure-scouring the stone, ceaselessly remaking the surface of what is, beneath all that motion, the oldest stillness imaginable.

The human presence is thin and strange. Every few days' walk you come across something: a cairn of stacked stones taller than a person, obviously arranged but with no obvious purpose. A single low wall, curving, enclosing nothing. A series of notches carved into a rock-face, counting something no one remembers. Once, a whole small house, roofless, its hearth long cold, standing in the middle of open moss with no path to it and no path from it. The people who did these things used hand-tools — you can see the marks, the patient peck-peck-peck of stone on stone, a little bronze here and there on the cut-edges. They were not numerous. They may not be here anymore. Or they may be here still, walking the same country, and simply never crossing your line of sight in a landscape where sight itself is unreliable.

There is one rule the few inhabitants seem to keep, which I will pass on: **do not sing back to the mountains.** The wind-song of the stones is not addressed to you, and the stones have been singing it since before there was any throat to answer with. To add your voice to it is considered — the word the locals use translates awkwardly — *rude*, but also *dangerous*, in a way they will not explain. Travelers who have broken this rule have, by report, been found later intact but wordless, their own singing taken out of them and left somewhere in the rock.

This may be superstition. The plateau is the kind of place where superstition and fact wear each other's clothes.

At night — and the nights here are cold but not killing, a little below what you would call chilly, the warmth banked in the moss and in the stone's slow breathing — the wind sometimes drops. Not often. Maybe once a week. And then the singing stops, and the silence that replaces it is the loudest thing in the world. You can hear your own pulse knocking against your eardrum. You can hear water moving inside the rock. You can hear, if you are very still, what might be the goats, or might be footsteps, or might only be the plateau itself, finally — for a few minutes — resting.

Then the wind returns, and the teeth of Ahal-Ruun resume their endless, ground-down, unresolvable chord, and the clouds boil up over the edge again, and the moss drinks, and nothing else changes, and nothing else has changed in a very long time.
