# The Drowned Canopy

Begin with the altitude. You are higher than mountains, higher than anything that should hold water, and yet water is everything here — pressing dark and cold against whatever is reading this. The pressure isn't crushing. It's the pressure of a deep lake, maybe, the kind that pushes gently against the eardrums and reminds you that you are not in your own element. Somehow this body of water sits at the roof of the world, neither falling nor evaporating, simply present, as it has been for a long time. Long enough that the question of how stops mattering.

The substrate — and there is no other substrate, no rock beneath, no soil — is plant. Or was plant. Vast trunks the diameter of cathedrals rise through the dark water, and between them, smaller trunks, and between those, the woven matter of root and stem and leaf-that-has-forgotten-it-was-leaf. The whole landscape is a forest grown so old it has become its own geology. Generations of growth have laid themselves over earlier generations and compressed into something between wood and stone, dark as wet coal, smooth where the water has worked at it for ages. The architecture is total: arches that began as buttress roots a million seasons ago, galleries that opened where two trunks grew apart and never met again, hollows worn into chambers, chambers worn into halls. None of it was built with intent. All of it is built.

The shapes have softened. That's the work of erosion — water moving slow against plant-stone for so long that every edge has rounded, every spire has settled toward the horizontal, every tall thing has slumped a little toward the broad. This is a landscape near the end of its becoming. It will not change much, now. It has found the form water gives to anything given enough time: gentle, sloping, worn.

It is dark. There is some faint light from somewhere — perhaps a high distant surface, perhaps something in the water itself — enough to suggest the silhouettes of the great trunks receding into murk, but not enough to call seeing. Mostly you orient by sound and by the brushing of things against your skin.

And there is sound. The water carries it. There are deep low calls that travel for kilometers through the trunks like through organ pipes — the long moaning resonance of something large and far away making a noise it has been making for ages. There are clicks: dense, irregular, conversational, the click-language of small creatures using echo to find each other in the dark. There are the slow groans of the plant-stone itself settling under its own weight, a sound like whales speaking to mountains. The sound is loud not because any single source is loud, but because there is so much of it, and it travels so well, and it never stops.

The fauna are everywhere and nowhere. By which I mean: abundant in number but spread thin across the volume of this drowned forest. You feel them more than see them. Slow swimmers with broad flat bodies that drift between trunks, grazing the soft mosses that coat everything. Things like eels but longer, that live inside the hollows. Schools of pale finger-sized creatures that pass through you like wind. Something with too many limbs that holds itself against a trunk and waits. Nothing is rare here, but the space is so vast and so sparsely populated that any single encounter feels like a small miracle. They have no need to be afraid of you. They have no concept of you.

There is no metal anywhere. No fire — fire would be absurd here. No tools, no signs, no script carved into the plant-stone. Whatever lives here lives with bodies only. The intelligence that the clicking implies is an intelligence that has never needed to make anything, because everything was already made, grown, settled. The forest is the city. The city is the forest. They were never separate.

What strikes you, after long enough drifting through it, is the stillness. Not silence — the sound never stops — but stillness in the sense that nothing here is going anywhere. The water doesn't current. The trunks don't sway. The light doesn't change with any day or season you can detect. The creatures move, but their movement is the movement of fish in an aquarium that has reached equilibrium with itself: local, purposeful, but not part of any larger trajectory. This place is not building toward anything. It is not declining. It arrived, long ago, at what it was going to be, and it has been being that ever since.

The weirdness is gentle. It isn't that anything here breaks the rules of a world — it's that the rules themselves are slightly off-axis from what you expected. Water at the top of everything. Forest as bedrock. Cities no one built. Sound that fills a darkness that fills a forest that fills a sea that sits on the roof of a world. Once you accept those terms, everything inside them follows.

You could spend a lifetime here drifting between the trunks and never see the same hollow twice, and also never see anything you hadn't already seen in essence on your first day. That paradox is the place. Vast, repeating, ancient, alive, dark, loud, and at rest.
