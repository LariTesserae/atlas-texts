# The Wainscot

It is a room. Or it was a room, once, in some larger building whose other rooms no longer matter and may not exist. What remains is roughly twelve feet by fifteen, with a ceiling low enough that a tall person would have to stoop. The floorboards have worn smooth and slightly concave from a traffic that ended long ago — you can still see the path between where a door used to be and where a hearth used to be, though both are bricked over now with a careful, ancient masonry that has itself begun to powder at the edges.

The air is cool. Not cold — cool the way a cellar is cool, the way stone holds night-temperature into the afternoon. There is a draft from somewhere, though every visible opening has been sealed; the draft is one of the room's old habits and persists without a source.

And the room is *full.*

Beetles. That is the first thing to understand. The wainscoting along all four walls — dark wood, paneled, the kind of millwork no one bothers to make anymore — is hollow, and behind it and within it and crawling across its surface are beetles in numbers that have no honest description. Carpet beetles, deathwatch, furniture beetles, powderpost, and a dozen species that taxonomy has not yet noticed because they have lived only here, in this room, for longer than taxonomy has existed. They move in sheets. They move in slow tides across the baseboards. When a section of wall is in shadow you can hear it: a sound like rain on paper, like a fire just catching, continuous, layered, three or four distinct registers of clicking and chewing and the small papery friction of elytra against elytra. It does not stop. It has not stopped in centuries.

In the corners, where the walls meet the ceiling, there are mites — pale, almost translucent, so small that a square inch of plaster holds tens of thousands. They graze on the dust the beetles produce. They are themselves grazed upon by pseudoscorpions no larger than a comma, which patrol the seams of the wallpaper with their tiny claws lifted. Spiders have given up trying to web the room; there is too much traffic, and any web is overrun and dismantled within hours. The spiders instead live as nomads in the high corners, fat and unhurried, picking off what passes.

Under the floorboards: silverfish in their thousands, and the white grubs of click-beetles, and woodlice in colonies that have developed an almost coral-like architecture of pellets and shed skins. The floor crackles faintly if you stand still and listen.

There is no plant matter. Nothing green has been in this room for a very long time. The food web runs entirely on wood — the structural wood of the building, the wainscoting, the floor joists, a few pieces of abandoned furniture — and on the dust and frass and corpses the wood-eaters generate. It is a closed economy of slow consumption. The wood is being eaten at a rate that, calculated honestly, should have collapsed the room generations ago; but the beetles eat conservatively, almost ceremonially, never weakening a beam past its load-bearing capacity. They have negotiated with the architecture. This is one of the rules of this place.

The tech is in the corner. A small machine — brass, riveted, the size of a sewing machine — sits on a low table whose legs the woodworm have not touched. It has a flywheel and a belt and a small electric motor of an antique design, and it runs. It has been running. Its purpose is opaque: it turns, it clicks, it produces a low industrial humming that joins the beetle-rain as a second voice in the room's sound. A thin cable runs from it down through a hole in the floor to a power source no one has ever traced. The machine may be the reason the beetles are tolerated; the beetles may be the reason the machine still runs. They are clearly in some arrangement.

Light comes through a single window, paned in old glass, looking out onto — nothing visible. A gray luminance, like overcast sky, but no landscape. The window admits enough light to read by. It admits the same amount of light at all hours. There is no night here, and no proper day, only the gray steady reading-light of a long afternoon that has forgotten how to end.

The weirdness of the place is not in any single feature but in the slow accumulation: that the wood does not run out, that the beetles do not starve or overrun, that the machine has no off-state, that the draft has no origin, that the door no longer exists but the path to it is still worn into the boards. The room has settled. It has reached its final form. Whatever larger building it belonged to has been forgotten by the room itself, which now considers itself complete, populous, occupied, and at work.

If you put your ear to the wainscoting you can hear, beneath the chewing, beneath the clicking — fainter, but there — what sounds like a much larger machine in a much deeper room, also running, also having always run.
