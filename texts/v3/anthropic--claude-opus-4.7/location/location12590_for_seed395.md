There is almost no free water here, and yet the place is green. That is the first contradiction to sit with, and the one everything else grows out of.

Picture a low country, broad and warm, stretching past where the eye can resolve it — not a desert in the sand-and-dune sense, but a dry country, a country where the air holds maybe a breath of moisture in the early hours and gives it back to the sky by mid-morning. The ground is pale: a chalky, dust-pale mineral floor, soft underfoot, the kind of surface that records every footprint and then loses it again to the slow shuffle of wind. There are no rivers. No lakes. No visible springs. If you dug, you would find the same dry, fine substrate going down, and down, and down.

And on this floor, a city of plants.

Not a forest — a city. They have grown into one another and around one another in the way only crowded organisms do, in the way coral reefs do, or the way old quarters of old towns do, where the buildings lean and share walls and a window of one house looks into the kitchen of another. The vegetation here is succulent in temperament: thick-skinned, waxy, water-hoarding. There are pillar-plants standing two and three stories high with fluted green columns and crowns of pale flowers. There are squat barrel-forms the size of rooms, ribbed and ridged, pressed up against each other shoulder to shoulder. There are climbing things — not vines exactly, more like tendons — that lash one column to its neighbor and form, between them, shaded passages. There are flat plate-plants stacked in tiers like the floors of a building, each plate broad enough to walk on, each one catching what little dew the night drops down. The plant-city has streets, in a sense: gaps between the major growths where a person, or a body of comparable size, could pass. It has plazas where some massive elder-plant has died and left a clearing the others have not yet filled. It has districts — places dominated by one species, then another, the way a human city has its market quarter and its temple quarter.

The light comes down warm and even. A normal sun, near enough; the day has the comfortable heat of a long afternoon in late spring, and the green surfaces drink it without complaint. The shadows are not dramatic; the canopy is too patchy, too irregular for the long cathedral light of a true forest. Light puddles instead, falling through gaps, brightening one barrel-flank and leaving the next in cool shade.

Now: the strange part, the part the parameters keep insisting on. The ground is *raw*. Pre-formed. Under-eroded. There are no gullies, no weathered ledges, no rounded stones. The dust on the surface is fine but the rock just beneath is sharp-edged where you can see it — between the bases of the plant-columns, where a recent crack shows, the mineral is freshly broken, glassy, unworn. Nothing has had time to round it. This is a young land. Geologically speaking, it has just arrived. The plants have moved in before the country itself has finished being a country. They are doing the work that water and wind would normally do — breaking the substrate, pulling it apart with roots, making soil out of stone by digesting it slowly. The country is being built, and the builders are green.

The scale is enormous. This is not a garden, not a province. The plant-city goes on past the horizon in every direction, and it has its own weather. Heat rises off it in the long afternoons and the air above shimmers; sometimes, far off, a column of warm air will climb high enough to make a small dry cloud, which does not rain but drifts and dissolves. There are pressure differences across the country's breadth, and slow, lazy winds move from one quarter to another, carrying pollen, carrying the spice-smell of resinous skins, carrying the faint sweetness from whichever district is in flower this week. Different parts of the country are in different seasons at once, because the country is too big for one season. Somewhere it is the dry month and the plants are sealed tight; somewhere else, days away, the rare brief flowering has begun and the air there is heavier with scent.

The animals are everywhere and you mostly do not hear them.

This is the quietest abundant place you could imagine. The fauna are dense — they crowd the plant-city the way insects crowd a real city, the way birds crowd a real forest — but the country has bred them quiet. Sound carries too far across the low flat country, and predators listen, so the prey have learned not to give themselves away. There are small things like long-bodied lizards with soft pads on their feet that flow up and down the green columns without a click. There are things like rabbits but with thicker hides, that browse on the fleshy plates and chew in absolute silence, having no need to grind because the flesh is so soft. There are pale, slow-moving grazers the size of cattle that move through the wider lanes of the plant-city, their feet making only a faint puff against the dust. There are insect-things in great numbers, but their wings are membranous and slow, more like the wings of moths than of flies, and they make no buzz. There are predators — sleek, low, patient — and when they take something, the take is sudden and almost soundless: a brief scuff, a soft exhalation, and then quiet again. Calls do happen, but they are rare and brief — a single low note from somewhere off in the plant-city, answered minutes later from somewhere else, the way two distant bells might exchange a word at evening.

Mostly what you hear, if you stand still, is the faint creak of plant tissue expanding in the heat. The whisper of dust moving across dust. Your own breath. Sometimes, very rarely, the dry rustle of a leathery skin against a leathery skin as two plants, pushed by some shift of wind, lean briefly against each other.

There are no tools here. Nothing has been built by hand. The "urban" quality of the place is entirely the work of the plants and animals themselves — it is a city in the sense that a coral reef is a city, in the sense that a termite mound is architecture. The denseness, the layering, the corridors and chambers, the sense of organized space, all of it is biological. If something here has intelligence, it does not express that intelligence in fire or stone or metal. It expresses it in growth pattern, in placement, in the slow negotiation of one body against another for room and light.

How do they live without water?

They do not, quite — they live on very little, and they live on each other. The plants pull what trace humidity exists from the night air through their skins; some have flowering crowns shaped like cups that catch the predawn dew and funnel it inward. The animals get their water from the plants, biting into the thick green flesh and drinking the cool sap. The predators get it from the prey. Water moves through the country in pulses, in bodies, in slow rounds, never pooling, never running, always inside something. The dry floor below is a kind of stage; the wet life happens above it, in the bodies, sealed away from the thirsty air.

And the country is in motion. Slow motion, mostly, but everywhere. A grazer moves down a green lane. A predator shifts its weight. A flower opens by a degree. A column of warm air starts its lift. A root, somewhere underground, finds a crack and applies a steady pressure that will, in a year, split a stone. A flock of soft-winged things lifts from one plate-plant and settles on another with the sound of a sigh. A pollen cloud rises in one district and drifts on the lazy wind toward another. The plant-city breathes; it is digesting its own raw country into being; it is, in its quiet way, one of the busiest places in the world.

If you walked into it, you would feel the warmth first, and the dryness — your lips would tighten — and then the green close in on either side, taller than you, and the pale dust under your feet, and the strange muffled fullness of a place that is utterly crowded and almost completely silent. You would understand, after a while, that you were inside a single enormous living thing that had not yet decided it was a single thing, and that the ground under it was younger than the life on top of it, and that the country was making itself, slowly, room by green room, without hurry, without water, and without sound.
