# The Slow City Inside the Heartwood

There is a tree. Was a tree. The distinction stopped mattering an age ago.

What remains is a chamber hollowed inside the standing trunk of something that once grew — a cavity perhaps thirty paces across, perhaps less, the walls curving up into a vault whose top is lost not to height but to darkness. The wood is dry. It has been dry for so long that the concept of sap is a kind of forgotten weather. Run a hand along the wall and it comes away with a fine pale dust that smells faintly of cedar and faintly of nothing at all, the way old paper smells: not absence, but a scent that has finished happening.

There is no water here. There has not been water here in a span of time that the chamber does not measure. The wood has cured itself past the point of decay into something closer to mineral — a slow petrifaction without minerals, just lignin folding inward on itself until it became its own bone. You could strike the wall with a blade and the wall would ring like slate.

It is dark. Not dim. Dark in the absolute sense, the kind of dark that has weight, that presses softly against the open eye and offers nothing in return. Whatever lived here long ago did not need light, or stopped needing it, or carried its own.

But the place is *loud*.

This is the strangeness of it. In the still cool air — and it is cool, a steady underground sort of cool, the temperature of a deep cellar that has forgotten the season — there is sound. Constant sound. The wood is *singing*. Not in any voice; in the long groans and tickings and deep organ-pipe resonances of a structure so vast and so old that its own internal stresses have become music. Somewhere far above, a fiber the width of a hair contracts by an imperceptible amount and a tone like a struck bell rolls down the chamber walls and dies in the dust on the floor. Then silence for a breath. Then a long low creak from the opposite wall, sustained, sustained, until you feel it in the sternum. Then a tick. Then a snap. Then a chord.

The chamber breathes in centuries. What you hear in a minute is the compressed voice of its slow shifting — wood relaxing into wood, grain settling against grain, the whole vast structure subsiding by fractions of fractions toward a final shape it will reach in another ten thousand years.

The space is almost empty. This is the strange part again — for it is a *city*.

Along the inner walls, at heights from knee to shoulder, small structures have been built. Carved. Fitted. Tiny doorways no taller than a thumb. Lintels. Stepped terraces winding around knots in the wood. A whole quarter of the western wall is given over to what looks like a market: rows of arched stalls, an open plaza, a stair descending into a darker recess. Further around the chamber, a tower — if a thing the size of a forearm can be called a tower — rises from a balcony of carved leaf-motifs, three stories, each window precise, each window black and empty.

It is a town. It is dense with construction. Every workable surface of the chamber has been settled. There are bridges, lanes, hanging galleries; there is a district where the architecture changes style as though under a different dynasty; there is what might be a temple, set off by itself, its façade carved with concentric rings.

The tools that made it all lie scattered on the floor of the chamber — bone awls, a chipped flint, a small hammer of denser wood, a needle. Hand-tools. Patient work. Nothing turned by wheel or driven by anything but a hand and a long quiet attention. Some of the tools are arranged neatly. Some have been dropped where the worker stood.

The city is empty.

There is no one in any of the doorways. No one on the bridges. The plaza holds no figures. The market stalls hold no wares. Whether the builders are gone, or were never quite present in the way that would let you find them, or whether they are simply *elsewhere* in some sense the chamber understands and you do not — this is not answered. The streets are clean. The dust does not gather in the lanes the way it gathers in the corners of the floor. Something maintains them, or something maintained them so well, so long ago, that the maintenance has outlasted its maintainers.

There are no animals. No insects in the wood. No spiders in the eaves of the little eaves. The wood is too old, too dry, too far past the chemistry that would feed anything. Even rot has finished here and gone home.

The vegetation — and this is the chamber's deepest secret — is everything. The walls, the floor, the ceiling lost in dark, the tiny city, the tools, the dust, all of it is the tree. There is no soil beneath the floor; the floor is wood. There is no sky above the vault; the vault is wood. You are inside a single organism that stopped being an organism and became a place. The plant has filled the space by being the space. Every cubic inch is plant-matter or plant-made or plant-carved. Even the air, perhaps, is the slow exhalation of cellulose finally letting go of the last molecules it has held for epochs.

And the sound goes on. A low groan from the floor. A sequence of ticks, evenly spaced, like a calm conversation in a language of one syllable. A long sustained note in the upper darkness, a note that begins before you notice it and ends so gradually that you cannot say when it stopped, only that at some point the silence between sounds resumed.

If you sit in the chamber a long time — and the chamber is patient, the chamber has nothing but time — you begin to suspect that the empty city is not empty in the way you first thought. Not haunted; nothing so dramatic. Just that the builders worked at a speed the chamber understood, and you, breathing your fast warm breaths, blink past entire generations of their lives between heartbeats. Somewhere on the balcony of the tower, a figure the size of a grain of rice may be turning, very slowly, to face you. By the time it has finished the turn, you will have grown old and left and been forgotten, and the wood will tick once more, and the chord will roll down the walls, and the dust will settle a little deeper into the corners of the floor.

The chamber is cool. The chamber is dark. The chamber is singing. The chamber is almost done.
