# The Glasshouse in the Spine

High in a mountain range whose name, if it ever had one, has been lost to the dryness of the air — so dry that sound travels strangely, and paper does not yellow, and the dead do not rot — there is a cleft in the rock. You would not find it unless you were looking, and you would not look unless you knew. It opens off a ledge at an elevation where the sky has gone from blue to the color of old iron, and breath comes short, and the stone underfoot rings like a struck bell.

Inside the cleft: a room. Not a cavern — a room, no larger than a modest parlor, walled on three sides by the bones of the mountain and on the fourth by a great curved pane of something that is not quite glass. The pane is fogged from within by condensation that never falls, never beads, never moves. Behind it, packed so tightly that no leaf has a neighbor's space to grow into, is a forest.

The forest is the size of a wardrobe. It contains, by my count, perhaps four hundred distinct species of plant, and not one of them touches water.

They are sustained instead by the engine.

The engine occupies the lower third of the chamber, brass-bodied and verdigrised, its flywheels locked in positions that suggest motion but have not moved in — one hesitates to guess. Centuries. Longer. It hums, though. A deep industrial drone that fills the little room and the cleft and spills out onto the ledge, loud enough that a climber passing below would hear it and mistake it for wind in a pipe, or the mountain itself thinking. The drone never wavers. It has a pitch you can tune a string to, if you brought a string.

From the engine rise copper capillaries, thousands of them, each no thicker than a hair, threading up into the soil bed behind the glass. They carry not water but a pale vapor — the plants drink it through their roots, through their leaves, through pores in their bark. The vapor is the reason the forest is lush and the reason nothing in the forest has ever needed to evolve a defense, or a seed, or a season. Each plant grows to exactly the size its allotted volume permits and then stops. Stems touch stems. Leaves interlock like the teeth of gears. A frond presses against the glass and has pressed against the glass, in precisely that configuration, for longer than any witness.

There are no insects. No mites in the soil, no spores drifting. The air inside the pane is sterile and saturated and still. The plants are green, but it is a green that has had a long time to consider itself — mosses the color of oxidized bronze, ferns so dark they read as black until you bring a lamp close, a single pale flower the shape of a trumpet that opened once and has not closed.

The light comes from filaments strung along the ceiling of the enclosure, powered by the engine, dim as a late hour in a library. They have the warmth of old incandescence but they do not flicker. Nothing flickers here. The plants do not photosynthesize in any ordinary sense; they eat the light the way they drink the vapor, slowly, without hunger.

On the outer ledge, at the foot of the pane, someone has left things. A brass fitting, unused. A small notebook whose pages are blank but for a single pressed leaf on page one — a leaf that matches no species behind the glass, suggesting it was brought from elsewhere, long ago, as an offering or a comparison. A cup. A folded cloth. These are the only signs of human passage, and they, too, have been here a long time. The cup holds a thin layer of dust that is not quite dust — mineral flake from the cliff above, the only thing in this place that still falls.

Whoever built the engine is not here. Whoever tends it is not here. It tends itself, or it does not need tending, or the distinction has stopped mattering. The vapor cycles. The light holds. The plants press against one another in their crowded patient green, and the drone fills the stone, and the mountain is so high and so dry that the whole arrangement has achieved a kind of equilibrium usually reserved for geological processes or theological claims.

A thing worth noting: if you put your palm against the pane, it is warm. Body-warm. The only warm thing for miles in any direction. The cold of the peak presses in from every side — the stone is cold, the air outside the cleft is cold enough to crack lips — but the glass is warm, and on the far side of the warmth is a jungle the size of your two hands held open, and it has been waiting, in exactly this configuration, to be looked at.

It does not require you to look at it. That is perhaps the strangest thing. Most sealed gardens, most terraria, most reliquaries have about them a sense of display, of having been made for a viewer. This one does not. It is indifferent to observation. It was made, one suspects, simply to continue. The engine runs because running is its state. The forest grows because it grew, once, into its container, and the container has not changed, so neither has it.

You can stay as long as you like. Nothing will happen. The drone will fill your chest until you can feel it in your teeth, and the dim light will pick out the veins of a leaf no larger than a fingernail, and the cold of the mountain will work its way through your coat, and the pane will stay warm under your hand, and across the little distance of the glass a world will continue being exactly itself, having long ago finished the work of becoming.

When you leave, the ledge outside will be just as you found it. The cleft will not close behind you. The sound will follow you down the scree for a hundred meters and then, quite abruptly, stop — as if the mountain had decided the drone belonged to the room and not to the air.

Below a certain altitude, you will not be able to remember the exact pitch of it. This is a mercy, or a theft, depending.
