# The Reliquary Vault

It is a room, and it has always been a room. Whatever was here before the room is no longer the point.

The walls are close — perhaps six paces across, though the eye cannot quite commit to the distance because every surface is worked. Floor, walls, ceiling: all cut from a pale stone the color of old bone, and every square inch of that stone has been carved into shallow niches, each niche no larger than a cupped hand, and in each niche sits a small dry thing. Seed-heads. Thistle-crowns. The rattle-pods of some plant that grew elsewhere, long ago, and was brought here to finish drying. Lichens pressed flat into crescents. A spray of reed-stems tied with reed-twine. The niches number in the thousands. They tile the room without gap.

There is no water anywhere. The air itself is dry in that particular way where your lips feel it before your throat does — a dryness that has been cultivated, sealed, preserved. Cool, too, as cellars are cool, but without any damp. The stone holds its own temperature and has held it for as long as the stone has been stone.

The light: this is the strange part. The light is abundant, almost fierce, and has no visible source. It seems to come from the pale stone itself, or from the dried plants, or from some agreement between them. It casts no shadows. A thistle-crown in its niche is lit equally from above, below, and behind; it floats in its small cave as if illustrated rather than placed. The whole room glows at the brightness of a noon field, but nothing here has ever seen noon.

And the sound. The sound is enormous. A dry, continuous rasp-and-rattle, as of a thousand small husks stirred by wind, though there is no wind. The pods shiver on their stems. The reed-stems click against their neighbors. The lichens do not move but somehow contribute — a fine papery hiss underneath. It is the sound a grain-field makes in late summer when the heads are ready, amplified and held, and held, and held. You would have to shout to be heard, if there were anyone to shout to. There is not.

Nothing changes. The seeds do not fall from their pods. The pods do not crumble. The light does not dim. The rasping does not slow or quicken or ever resolve into a pattern you could name. If you watched a single thistle-crown for an hour you would see it tremble exactly as it trembled when you first looked, no more, no less. The room is not waiting for anything. It arrived at this state and then the arriving stopped.

There are no insects in the niches. No mice in the corners. Nothing has ever eaten anything here. The dried plants are not food; they are the room's vocabulary, and the room is speaking, loudly, about dryness and preservation and the condition of being finished.

The walls have been weathered — not by weather, which cannot reach here, but by time itself, working on the stone the way time works on anything it has been given enough of. The edges of the niches are softened. The deepest-cut lines have rounded. Whoever carved this room carved it long ago enough that the carving has settled into the stone, become indistinguishable from the stone's own habit. You cannot tell, now, whether the niches were made or whether the stone grew them.

There is no door.

This does not feel like a problem. The room is not a place you arrive at or leave from. It is a place that is, fully, in a way most places only manage to approximate. If you imagine yourself standing in the middle of the floor — and you have to imagine it, because there is no floor-space not carved, no patch to set a foot on without crushing a pod — you would feel the brightness pressing evenly on your skin, and the sound filling every cavity of your head, and the cool dry air moving through you without cost, and you would understand that the room does not need you to be here, has not noticed you, will not notice you when you are gone. It is complete. It rasps to itself, brightly, forever.

Somewhere in the carving, if you looked long enough, you might find a niche that holds nothing. A small empty cup of stone, lit as evenly as the others. This would not feel like an absence. It would feel like the one place the room has made for the idea of absence, so that absence too can be kept here, dry and bright and shaking gently, with everything else.
