Somewhere below the surface of a warm country — maybe fifteen, twenty meters down — there is a chamber that has not quite finished deciding to be a chamber.

You come into it through a crawl that opens without warning into standing height. The first thing is the sound. Water is falling somewhere close, not a single fall but many — a dozen threads and sheets of it, some fat, some fine as hair, striking pools at different depths and with different voices. The chamber is loud the way a small kitchen with every tap running is loud: enclosed, insistent, domestic in its insistence. Underneath the falling there is the slower percussion of drips from the ceiling, and under that a kind of low breathing where the whole rock seems to respire moisture.

It is warm. Not hot — warm the way a greenhouse is warm at dusk, humid enough that the air has weight and your skin stops being a boundary. The walls weep. Everywhere you touch, your hand comes away wet, and the stone underneath the film of water is warm too, as if something lived-in lies just the other side of it.

The chamber is young. You can tell because nothing here has settled into its final shape. The flowstone on the left wall is a smooth bulge only beginning to lobe outward; it has the tentative quality of wax that has not yet decided where to run. Stalactites are nubs, not spears — small pale teats hanging half a finger's length, with bright droplets gathering at each tip. On the floor, where they have been dripping for some uncountable but not very long time, the stalagmites are only low mounds, not yet even knee-high. In a thousand years they might be pillars. Right now they are suggestions. The rimstone dams around the pools are only centimeters high, delicate scallops holding back shallow water that spills, reforms, spills again. Everything is in the first pages of its geology.

It is dark. Your lamp shows a few meters and then the rest folds off into wet black. The chamber is not large — maybe six meters across, maybe eight in the long direction — but the darkness beyond the light makes it feel like the edges aren't fixed. You can hear water falling from places you can't see.

And it is green. This is the strange thing, this deep. Not green everywhere — the chamber is mostly empty, mostly just wet stone and standing water and air — but where your light settles, lushness answers. Moss. Thick, saturated, almost black-green moss on the wet ledges. Liverworts in overlapping thumbnail plates, slicked flat to the rock. Tiny ferns — no, not ferns, the filmy translucent things that grow in caves where a little light has found its way, maybe through the crawl you came down, maybe through some hairline above. Where your lamp lingers, the moss seems to lean into it, though that's only your attention. Fine green algae furs the edges of the pools. One wall has a single fern the size of a hand, impossibly alive down here, fed by the mist and whatever photon-scraps leak through. The vegetation is heavy where it occurs and absent in between — no gradient, just presences and then long wet empty stone.

The fauna is barely anything. A pale cave cricket on a ledge, antennae longer than its body, motionless. Somewhere a frog has called once and not called again — you aren't certain you heard it. In one of the pools, when you shine the light straight down, something flickers and is gone: a small blind fish, or the idea of one. The chamber is not unpeopled, only sparsely peopled, the way a cathedral at 4 a.m. is sparsely peopled.

There are marks of people, too. Not many. On the far wall, at chest height, someone has left a small cairn of three stones stacked on a natural shelf — recent, the stones still a slightly different wet than the shelf. Near the largest pool, a clay bowl, whole, sitting upright as if placed. It holds water now, but it didn't always; someone brought it here and set it down with intention. On the ceiling above the bowl, a smudge of soot — old, older than the cairn — a single black handprint's worth of residue where a torch once burned, maybe many torches over many visits, maybe just one, long ago. No carvings. No writing. Nothing that announces itself. Just these three or four quiet signs that this chamber has been known, that people have come down here occasionally across a long time, not to live but to visit, to leave something, to sit in the sound of the water.

The rules of the place, insofar as it has rules:

Everything here changes, but slowly. The stalactites grow at the pace of fingernails on a geological clock. The pools rise and fall with the season above — right now they are high; in the dry months the rimstone would show more, some would be exposed and chalky. The moss has its own cycles, retreating and advancing with the water table. Nothing here is sudden. Even the cricket, when it finally moves, will move in one long considered step.

There are no machines. There has never been a machine down here. The only technologies present are the bowl, the cairn, the vanished torch, and whatever lamp you brought. Take the lamp away and the chamber returns immediately to what it has mostly been: warm, wet, loud, dark, alive in its slow green way, waiting to become the cave it will be in ten thousand years.

It is a small place. You could cross it in fifteen steps. But standing in the middle of it, with water falling on all sides and the walls just past the edge of your light, it feels like the inside of something larger than itself — some pocket the world has folded shut and kept warm, a room the planet made and mostly forgot, where every century a person comes down, sets a bowl, listens for a while, and climbs back out.
