Far below the surface, where the weight of water becomes its own kind of dark, there is a basin the shape of a broken bowl. It is perhaps forty kilometers across, rimmed by the slumped remains of what must once have been mountains — though no edge is sharp anymore, nothing rises to a point, nothing ends in a clean line. Everything has been rubbed down by time and current into soft shoulders and long, patient slopes. The rock, where it shows, is the color of wet slate, and it shows rarely, because almost nothing here is bare.

The basin is packed. That is the first thing to understand. Not packed with creatures, not packed with plants, but packed with silt and suspended matter and the slow bodies of cold currents braided through one another, and with sediment-clouds that hang like rooms you could walk through if you had the breath for it. Every cubic meter holds something — flocculent drifts of marine snow, mineral grit, the powdered remains of shells so old their species have no living cousins. Push a hand through it and the hand vanishes to the wrist. The water is not water so much as a slow, dense broth, near-freezing, under enormous pressure, moving.

And it is moving. This is the paradox of the place: everything is ancient, worn to its final shape, but nothing is still. Great slow turbulences wheel through the basin on timescales of hours and days — a bottom current piling into the basin's western shoulder, rebounding, climbing, folding back over itself in a gyre twenty kilometers wide. Sediment rises in towering plumes that take a full tide to settle, and never quite settle, because another pulse arrives and lifts them again. The bowl breathes. It has been breathing like this, probably, for a hundred million years. The turbulence is not violent in the way a storm is violent; it is turbulent the way a glacier is turbulent, if you could speed a glacier up just a little. Chaos at the pace of geology.

There is almost no light. A trace — enough, perhaps, that if your eyes had adjusted over a lifetime you could make out the paler patches of sediment against the darker rock, the way a blind fish maps pressure. Otherwise, black. Not the black of a closed room but the black of a medium that absorbs, a black with texture, a black you feel pressing softly against the skin.

Vegetation is sparse and strange. Here and there, anchored in the lee of a boulder or in a pocket where the current slacks, grow long colonies of something plant-adjacent — not quite algae, not quite anything photosynthetic, since there is no sun to feed them. They feed on the marine snow, on the chemistry of the sediment, on each other. They grow in pale ropes and ribbons, meters long, and they move constantly with the current, combing and untangling, combing and untangling. From a distance — if distance meant anything here — a colony would look like a head of slow hair.

Fauna is rare. There are things living in the basin, but you might go a long time without encountering one. A translucent shrimp-shape, longer than your forearm, ghosts past once and doesn't return. Something larger moves along the bottom, felt more than seen, disturbing the sediment in a line that takes an hour to refill. A single isopod the size of a dinner plate grazes on a rope-colony, unhurried. The scarcity is not emptiness; it is the kind of scarcity you find in very old, very stable ecosystems, where everything has found its minimum viable population and stays there, patient, economical.

There is sound, but only just. The creak of pressure working on the rock. The soft collapse of a sediment cliff somewhere in the distance, a sound like powder falling onto powder. The occasional click or low groan from something living. If you held perfectly still you could hear your own heartbeat translated through the water as a dull drum. Otherwise: a quiet so complete it has weight.

And then — this is the unexpected thing — there are hand-tools. Stone ones. Small ones. They lie scattered at the edges of the basin, half-buried, along what might once have been a shoreline before whatever ocean this is rose and swallowed it. Scrapers. A broken blade. A hammerstone still fitted to the palm of whoever last held it. No structures, no walls, nothing built — only the tools themselves, resting where they were dropped or set down, undisturbed for so long that the sediment has climbed halfway up their sides and the rope-colonies have grown around them without displacing them. Whoever made them is long gone. Their bones, if they had bones, are part of the broth now, part of what the currents lift and settle and lift again. The tools remain because stone is patient and the basin, for all its turbulence, is gentle with small hard things. It rolls them. It polishes them. It does not break them.

This is the place, then: a cold dark bowl at the bottom of a world, saturated with slow-moving water and older-than-memory silt, nearly lightless, nearly soundless, rare with life, empty of builders, full of motion. A landscape that has already finished becoming itself and now only stirs, and stirs, and stirs, keeping the few tools of its vanished makers turning slowly in its hands.
