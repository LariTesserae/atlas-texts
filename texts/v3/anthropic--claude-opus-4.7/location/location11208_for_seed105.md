# The Drowned Heights

Start with the cold. That's the first thing — a cold that has weight, that presses against you the way deep water does, because here the two are the same thing. The cold *is* the water and the water *is* the air, and somewhere about three meters above what you'd call the ground, the distinction stops mattering.

This is a submerged country. Not flooded — flooded implies recent, implies wrong. This place has been underwater so long the water has forgotten it was ever an intruder. The mountains are still mountains; they simply happen below a ceiling of dark sea. Call it eight hundred meters down, maybe a thousand. Enough that the light from above arrives as a rumor — a faint blue-gray suggestion of up, fading to true black in the valleys. Enough that the pressure has its own opinion about everything.

And yet: vegetation. Lush, even. This is the strangeness of the place, or one of its strangenesses. The slopes are clothed in what at first reading look like forests — tall column-things, ten or fifteen meters, swaying in currents the way trees sway in wind, but slower, with that underwater patience. They are not plants in the sense you know. No chlorophyll could work down here. They feed on something else — the heat that seeps up through cracks in the old volcanic rock, the sulfides, the slow chemistry of a planet exhaling. Their trunks are pale, almost translucent, like the meat of mushrooms or the flesh of certain deep-sea worms made architectural. Their crowns are feathery, branching structures that filter the water for whatever they filter for. When a current passes, the whole forest leans and a low groaning sound travels through the rock, because these things are anchored deep and their roots complain.

There are no animals. Read that carefully: *none*. No fish curl through the columns. No crabs pick at the substrate. No drifting jellies, no worms in the silt. The forest exists for itself or for reasons that don't require eaters. This absence is the loudest thing about the place once you notice it — a forest that has never been browsed, never been pruned by appetite. The lower branches grow as densely as the upper ones. Nothing has ever needed a path.

The terrain rises and falls in the way old mountains do — established, weathered, comfortable with itself. Long ridges, broad saddles, a valley you could call a valley though it's filled with the same liquid darkness as everything else. The rock is basaltic, scalloped by millennia of slow current, furred in places with mineral crusts the color of rust and bone. Sediment lies thin; the currents keep things swept. You can see the shape of the land if you have a light, and the land has the shape of a country — not a single feature but a region, with weather of its own.

Yes, weather. This is the active part. Thermal plumes rise from vents scattered through the valleys, and where they meet cold downwellings from above, the water shears and shudders. Storms, of a kind. The forests bend in unison. Particulate clouds — flocs of mineral snow, drifts of organic dust from the columns themselves — roll across the slopes like fog rolls across hills on land. Sometimes there is a kind of rain: aggregations of matter precipitating out of the upper water and sifting down in slow silver showers that take an hour to land. The columns drink this. It may be how they live.

And the sound. The sound is the surprise. You'd expect silence — the silence we imagine for the deep — but this place is loud. Not loud like a city. Loud like a cathedral with the organ playing one held chord forever. The vents sing; superheated water forced through narrow rock makes a low continuous tone that varies as pressure fluctuates. The columns themselves contribute — when their feathery crowns rasp against each other in a current, they produce a high keening, almost vocal. And underneath everything, the slow boom of the mountain, the deep rock noises of a place that is still settling into itself after a billion years. You feel it in your chest before you hear it in your ears.

The built things are scattered and small. This is what makes the place a place and not just a phenomenon. On a ridge: a low wall of fitted stones, no mortar, maybe waist-high, running for forty meters and then stopping. In a saddle between two peaks: a cairn. On a sheltered terrace beneath an overhang: the remains of something that might have been a hut, four post-holes cut into the rock and a scatter of worked flakes around them. Hand-tools. Hammerstones. A bone awl, except there are no bones here, so where did the bone come from? It is old bone, fossil bone perhaps, harvested from some deeper stratum. Someone shaped it with patience.

There are no makers present. There haven't been, for a long time. But whoever they were, they didn't have metal, or they didn't bring metal, and they worked the stone the way stone has always been worked — by knapping, by grinding, by repetition. They left marks on certain columns: scratches at a consistent height, as if measuring growth. They left a row of seven small stacked-stone markers along a ridgeline, evenly spaced, pointing at nothing you can identify. They may have been counting. They may have been praying. They are gone and the forests have grown up around their works without disturbing them, because the forests do not move and nothing else lives here to knock the stones down.

What pulls at me about this place — what I keep circling — is the contradiction of it being *alive* without being *inhabited*. The columns grow, the weather moves, the vents breathe, the mineral rain falls, the whole system cycles and processes and renews itself, but no consciousness lives inside it except whatever consciousness the columns themselves might possess (slow, vegetable, possibly considerable). And then this thin human residue: a wall, a cairn, an awl. People came here once. They could breathe here, somehow, or they didn't need to breathe. They built small and they left, and the place absorbed their structures the way it absorbs everything — without urgency, without erasure, just continuing.

If you stood on the ridge with the seven markers and turned slowly, you would see: down-slope, the pale forest fading into dark; across the valley, another forest on another slope, faintly luminous where vent-heat warms the water enough to make the columns glow; above, the long gradient toward a surface you cannot reach; below, blackness and the suggestion of further valleys. You would hear the held chord of the vents and the keening of the crowns and the booming of the rock. You would feel the cold pressing in from all sides equally. You would understand, eventually, that this is not a hostile place. It simply does not require you. It got along before, it gets along now, it will get along after.

The wall on the ridge is the only thing here that ever needed anything. And whatever it needed, it got, or it didn't, a long time ago.
