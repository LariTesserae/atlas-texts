# The Gestation Shelf

It sits somewhere above the cloud layer but below the thinning that would call itself mountain-top — a broad warm bench in the air, maybe two hundred meters across, where the rock is still deciding what it wants to be.

The first thing you notice is that the ground is wrong in the way new things are wrong. Not eroded, not weathered — *unfinished.* The stone has a skin like fruit: soft where you'd expect hardness, slick where you'd expect grain. In places it weeps clear warm water that beads and runs without cutting channels, because there are no channels yet, because there has not been time. The water pools in shallow laps and is drunk back into the rock within the hour. It comes out again somewhere else. The shelf is breathing through its pores.

The warmth comes from below. Put your palm flat and you feel it — not geothermal exactly, not the crude heat of magma, but something steadier, a worked warmth, as though the rock were being held at temperature by an intention. The air at this height should be thin and cold. It isn't. Something is keeping the column of atmosphere above the shelf pressurized and humid, and the evidence of that something is everywhere once you know to look.

They are called, for want of a better word, the **trellises.** Tall, bone-pale scaffolds that rise out of the half-rock at irregular intervals — one here, three clustered there, a solitary one leaning at the edge of the bench. They do not appear built. They appear *extruded,* the way a crystal is extruded from a saturated solution, except the lattice is too intricate and too purposive: filigrees inside filigrees, and inside those, a scale of structure that glints and reorganizes when you look away. Each trellis is humming. Not metaphorically. A low harmonic that sits in your sternum, and overtones that climb up into a register your ears register as pressure rather than sound. Together they are loud — a chord held in the throat of the place. On the downwind side of each trellis the air ripples visibly, and the rock beneath is markedly further along in its making: harder, grayer, more committed to being rock. The trellises are the engines. The shelf is their workpiece. A world, or a piece of one, is being cultured here.

The vegetation is scant and peculiar. Low mats of something that looks like lichen but retracts when shadowed. A few upright stalks with bulbs that slosh. They grow almost exclusively in the wet shadow of the trellises, as though the conditions for life have only been achieved there and not yet propagated across the bench. Between them, wide pale expanses of naked half-stone, steaming faintly.

The fauna is the surprise. There are so many of them, and they are so clearly *early* — provisional animals, first drafts. Things the size of cats with too many joints and soft translucent plating, moving in loose packs across the rock and grazing on the condensation itself, running tongues along the weeping surfaces. Small six-legged flyers with membranous hoods that catch the harmonic from the trellises and resonate; they hover in the chord's sweet spots and feed on whatever the vibration shakes loose. A long low thing like a salamander the color of wet clay that eats the small grazers and leaves no bones behind, because there are no bones yet — the grazers are still mostly fluid inside a skin. At the bench's edge, where the warm air tips over into the cold above the clouds, a flock of something roosts that I can only describe as *folded.* They unfold to hunt and refold to sleep, and when they refold the geometry is impossible to follow with the eye.

There is so much life for so little plant matter. The ecology is running on the trellises' output — on the warmth and the water and, I think, on the sound itself. Energy is being pumped into the bench from somewhere off-stage, and the fauna have arrived (or been arrived) to eat it.

Scattered across the shelf are **marks.** Not structures exactly — the trellises are structures, and these are something less. A cairn of three stones that are clearly older than the rock they sit on, imported from somewhere finished. A low wall, knee-high, running twelve meters and then stopping, made of a dark material that does not weep. A single doorway-shaped frame standing alone with nothing on either side of it, and through the frame the view is subtly wrong — the clouds below are arranged differently, the light a half-shade cooler. Someone has been here. Someone visits. The marks are the marks of a tender, or a surveyor, or a shepherd checking on the flock. They are sparse and they are unhurried.

The light is dim and uniform. There is no visible sun. The cloud-deck below glows a little, as cloud-decks do when lit from beneath by something, and the trellises have their own faint pale luminescence along the fine filigrees, but the overall effect is of a long warm dusk that does not advance. Whether this is the time of day or the time of the *project* — whether the shelf has not yet earned a sun — is not clear.

Weather moves through anyway. Every so often a front of denser mist rolls up over the edge and the whole bench goes white and the harmonic of the trellises shifts, climbs a tone, works harder. The grazers huddle in the lee of the trellises. The folded things unfold and ride the updrafts. Water that was inside the rock comes out to meet water that was outside, and for a few minutes the bench streams. Then the mist passes, the trellises ease back into their resting chord, and the shelf exhales a warmer, wetter breath than before. It is, incrementally, getting further along.

If you stayed — and there is the distinct sense that you could stay, that nothing here is hostile to a visitor, only indifferent in the way a kiln is indifferent to a hand held near it — you would notice, over days, the rock hardening under your feet. The lichen-mats spreading. A second generation of grazers that have a little more bone. The trellises, perhaps, retracting one by one as their work in a given sector is completed, sinking back into the stone they helped cure.

And eventually, though not soon, the shelf would be finished: a small warm bench of ordinary mountain, with its own weather and its own animals, indistinguishable from a place that had always been there. The trellises gone. The doorway gone. The cairn, perhaps, left behind as a courtesy, or a signature, or a forgetting.

For now it is loud, and warm, and wet, and unfinished, and full of things learning how to be alive.
