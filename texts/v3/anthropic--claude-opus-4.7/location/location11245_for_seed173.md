# The Pumping Station at the Bottom of the Old Reservoir

You come into it through a flooded service tunnel, and the first thing is the sound — a low, four-stroke shuddering that travels through the water as much as through the air, because there is barely any air left. The chamber is mostly drowned. The waterline laps somewhere up near the ribs of the ceiling vault, leaving a hand's breadth of slack atmosphere that smells of rust, wet brick, and the particular sweet rot of submerged rubber gaskets.

It is a small place. You could swim its length in thirty strokes. A pumping hall, perhaps, from some industrial century — brick vaults riveted with iron, six great pump-engines arranged in two rows of three, their flywheels still turning, slow, slow, slow, half-submerged so that each rotation slaps the water and sends a ring out across the dim green room. That slapping is half the noise. The other half is the engines themselves: pistons working somewhere below the waterline, valves opening and closing in patient sequence, a deep mechanical breathing that has been going on, you understand instinctively, for a very long time.

Everything here is built. There is no rock, no soil, no natural floor — only brick, iron, lead piping, ceramic tile gone soft at the edges with calcium bloom. But the building has aged into something that resembles geology. The bricks are rounded as river stones. The iron has wept rust into long stalactites and into wavering plumes in the water that you part with your hand as you move. The mortar between courses has been eaten back so that each brick stands slightly proud, like a tooth in an old jaw. Whoever made this place is gone. The machines are not.

The water is cool — not cold, but cool enough that your breath fogs in the strip of air above. Visibility is maybe four meters in the lamp-light. There are lamps: caged bulbs along the walls at the old maintenance height, half of them drowned and still burning, throwing a sick green-yellow glow through the algae that has colonized their glass. The dim comes from this — from lamps that should have been brighter, working through water and through time.

Vegetation is sparse and strange because nothing should grow here at all. A few mats of pale weed cling to the rivet-heads of the flywheels, getting churned and re-anchoring. Something filamentous grows along the lamp cages, photosynthesizing the bulb-glow. A single dark frond, almost a kelp, has rooted in the silt at the base of pump number four and waves slowly in the suction-current. That's all. The plants are guests here, and they know it.

The fauna is the surprise. The place is *alive*. Small silver fish — hundreds of them, no, more — school through the pump-hall in coordinated sweeps, taking advantage of the engines' rhythm, riding the pulse of intake currents and shooting through the gaps in the flywheel spokes with a timing that suggests generations of practice. Eels live in the broken brickwork. There are pale shrimp on every horizontal iron surface, working over rust like miners. A heron-thing — no, it really is a heron, somehow, a real grey heron — stands in the air-strip on a high pipe, watching the schooling fish through the surface with the patience of an industrial process itself. It has the look of having been there for years. Crabs in the corners. A single eel of unusual size that does not flee from your lamp.

The ecology has organized itself around the machinery. The pumps pull water in through grates on the far wall, do something with it — pressure it, lift it, move it elsewhere, you cannot tell — and discharge it through the near wall, and this creates a slow circular current that the whole small ecosystem rides. The fish school where the current is fast. The shrimp gather where it is slow. The eels live in the dead zones behind the engine blocks. The frond grows where the silt settles. It is a wheel inside a wheel inside the great rusting wheels of the pumps.

The rules of the place, as best you can read them:

The engines will not stop. They have settled past the point where stopping was possible; they are not maintained, they are simply continuing, the way a river continues. Whatever fuel they need they are apparently still receiving, from some source, through some pipe, in quantities so reduced from their original specification that they now turn at a fraction of their design speed — once every four or five seconds, the great flywheels — and this slowness is what allows life to coexist with them. A fish can cross between the spokes. A heron can wait out a rotation. The machines have negotiated a treaty with the ecosystem by simply running down to a livable tempo.

The water level will not change. It has found its equilibrium between what the pumps remove and what seeps in through the old brick. The strip of air at the ceiling has been exactly that wide for a long time, judging by the dark tide-mark on the vaulting.

The sound never stops, and so you stop hearing it as sound and begin to feel it as the weather of the place. The shrimp's antennae move in time with the piston-strokes. The heron blinks on the off-beat.

Nothing here is dying and nothing here is being born in any urgent way. The place has reached a kind of completed state — not death, but completion — where the made and the alive have worked out their accommodation and intend to keep at it indefinitely, in the green half-light, under brick that has forgotten it was ever new, while the engines breathe and the flywheels slap the water and somewhere far away, presumably, the pumped water is still arriving at whatever it was meant to arrive at, or not, the destination no longer mattering, the pumping itself having become the point.

You could stay. The heron wouldn't mind. There is room.
