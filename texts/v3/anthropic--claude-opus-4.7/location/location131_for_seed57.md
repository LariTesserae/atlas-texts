# The Drowned Nursery

Begin in the dark, because that is the first true thing. Not the darkness of a cave, which has the memory of light at its opening, but the darkness of water that has never been disturbed by a surface. The kind that exists between the slow blinks of a blind fish's thinking.

You are submerged. Not in ocean — the pressure is wrong for ocean, too gentle, and the water tastes of something closer to rain that has forgotten how to fall. A flooded place, but flooded is the wrong word because flooding implies a before. This water has always been here. It is the default state. Air, if there were any, would be the anomaly.

The space is small. This is the strange part to hold in your mind alongside the submersion: it is a pocket, a chamber, a held breath of a place. Perhaps the size of a cathedral, perhaps the size of a cupped palm — the scale refuses to settle, and this is the first sign that the rules here are soft. You can see the far wall and also you cannot; the wall is close enough to touch and also you have been swimming toward it for what might be hours.

Plants fill nearly every cubic inch. Not seaweed — these are land-plants, ferns and mosses and something like lungwort and something like nothing at all, thriving underwater as though no one told them they shouldn't. They grow in slow tangles, fronds pressed against fronds, stems threading through other stems until the whole volume is a green-black lattice you move through rather than swim in. The leaves are cool to the touch, cooler than the water. When you brush one, it releases a single bead of clearer water that rises — or falls — or simply travels, since directionality is another of the rules that has gone soft.

The temperature holds at the edge of comfort. The kind of cool that makes you aware you have a body. The kind that would, on land, ask you to put on a sweater.

Stone shows through in places. Fresh stone — sharp-edged, unrounded, the fractures still looking new as though the rock split open a week ago and the water poured in and the plants arrived overnight. You can see the crystal faces where minerals interlock. No algae has dulled them yet. The whole chamber feels recently-made, a geology still deciding what it wants to be, and yet the plants are ancient in that way plants are, suggesting that "recently" here means something measured against a scale you are not equipped for.

Occasionally: a structure. A low wall of fitted stones, waist-high, its purpose undiscoverable. A single arch standing free, with nothing on either side of it — you can swim through it or around it and nothing changes either way, except once when you swim through it and come out facing a direction you had not been facing. A shallow basin carved into the floor, holding water that is somehow distinct from the water surrounding it, denser, clearer, a pool within the pool. Who made these. No answer. The makers are not here and may never have been here in the sense you mean by *here*.

Life is scarce. A pale newt-thing, translucent, drifts past your shoulder once and does not return. Something with too many small legs moves through the moss and you feel it more than see it. There are eggs, maybe, in clusters on the underside of certain leaves — small opalescent beads that may also be water droplets that have decided to stay. The ecology is mostly vegetable, mostly patient, mostly waiting.

Sound barely exists. Your own movement makes a muted shushing. Now and then a plant releases a gas-bubble that rises with a note almost like a held vowel. The rest is the silence of a held breath, the silence of somewhere that has not yet been given sound as a feature.

And the rules. The rules are where it gets difficult. The water remembers shapes. Pass your hand through a space and the hand-shape stays for a moment, a pocket of slightly different water, before the surroundings close over it. Your breath, if you are breathing, becomes something — not bubbles, exactly, but small green thoughts that swim away. The plants grow toward sustained attention; look at a particular frond long enough and it will lengthen toward your eye, slowly, the way a cat approaches. Time pools. In certain bowls of the chamber, minutes stack up and you emerge older or younger by some amount you cannot verify. A stone you touched as you entered may still be warming under your fingers when you pass it on your way out, even though you have been gone, by your count, several days.

The light is almost nothing. Almost. There is a faint phosphorescence in the older moss — the bottom layers, the compressed ones — enough that after a long while of stillness your eyes resolve shapes. The light is the color of the inside of an eyelid. You see by it the way you see in a dream: the seeing comes from some other direction than the eyes.

Nothing here is in a hurry. The chamber is doing something, slowly, that it started before any measurable beginning and will finish after any measurable end. You are welcome to stay. You are welcome to leave, though the exit is not where you left it, and the chamber will have grown a little in your absence, or you will have, and the difference between these is another of the soft rules.

It is, I think, a place where a world is being incubated. Or where one was, and the incubation took, and what hatched was so quiet and so slow that it is still hatching. The plants are patient with it. The stones are new because the world is new. The water holds everything the way a held thought holds its own meaning: gently, completely, in the dark.
