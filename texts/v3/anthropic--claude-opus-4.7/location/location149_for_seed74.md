# The Bellworks at Fenn Hollow

The town is a single overgrown machine, and the machine is a single overgrown plant, and nobody has bothered for a long time to separate them.

It sits in a shallow bowl of land — not a valley exactly, more a long dent where older water used to pool before it found somewhere else to go. What remains is damp: a grey-green dampness that lives in the undersides of leaves, in the pitted iron of the old pipework, in the felt of moss that has finished its long argument with every vertical surface and won. There is no river here now, only the memory of one running as a darker seam of vegetation through the town's middle, where horsetail and pale sedge mark the former channel. Twice a week, maybe, a soft rain comes down for an hour. The rest of the time, the place breathes its own weather. Condensation beads on the undersides of the great leaf-vaults and falls, one drop at a time, with the patience of a clock.

The buildings were, once, foundries. You can still read the bones of them — the tall chimney-stacks, the brick flanks, the iron trusswork, the loading-yards with their rails and their weighing-platforms. But the foundries were abandoned long enough ago that the distinction between brick and bark has gone soft. What grew in was not forest exactly. It was something more like an idea of a forest, pursued by a single species of vine-tree that treats masonry as trellis and ironwork as spine. Its leaves are broad, waxy, and the dull green of old pond-water. Its trunks, where they thicken, fuse with the walls they climb, so that what looks at first like a buttress is a root, and what looks like a pillar is a flue, and what looks like a flue is a hollow stem with sap moving through it the color of weak tea.

The town is small. You could walk its length in twenty minutes if the paths were clear, and they mostly are — this is a sparse place, built roomy, with wide squares between its structures where nothing much grows except lichen and the low mat of something like clover. Perhaps nine or ten buildings in all. A bell-tower. A long shed with the roof opened to the sky and refilled with canopy. A row of workers' houses whose windows frame interiors entirely given over to leaf. You get the sense that the original settlement was never crowded; the plants have only emphasized an emptiness that was always part of the design.

The strange thing — the thing that makes a visitor stop at the edge of the hollow and not walk in straightaway — is that the machinery still runs.

Not loudly. That's the first surprise. You expect, from the stacks and the wheels and the belts, a clangor. Instead, you hear a soft, steady, almost respiratory sound, like a large animal asleep in the next room. The great flywheel in what used to be the main hall turns once every forty seconds or so, and its turning is powered by the slow contraction of a woody trunk that has grown around the drive-shaft and made itself into a muscle. The bellows in the annex inflate and deflate on the schedule of a tide nobody charts. Somewhere, with a regularity that suggests intent, a hammer falls on an anvil — once, then nothing for several minutes, then once again. Nobody is striking it. A bough thickens against a cam, a counterweight of accumulated water tips, the hammer falls. The plant has learned the factory, and the factory has taught the plant the shapes of its old labor, and between them they keep a kind of industry going that produces nothing at all, or nothing that a ledger would recognize.

Electricity, too, though barely. A few of the original lamps still burn, fed by what must be a current drawn from the difference between the wet roots and the dry iron — a slow galvanic trickle, enough to keep a single filament glowing amber in the window of the foreman's office, where a desk has long since been swallowed by a cushion of moss and a logbook lies open to a page nobody turns. The light it throws is normal light, ordinary light, the light of any lamp in any small room, and this ordinariness is perhaps the most unsettling thing about the hollow. You keep expecting the uncanny, and what you get is the domestic.

Animals are rare. A few beetles of a species that seems to have specialized in the sap of the vine-tree; they crawl the ironwork polishing it dully with their passage. A single heron, maybe — seen once, perhaps not seen again — that stands in the old channel hunting frogs that may or may not exist. No birdsong. The occasional tick of something small moving in the leaves. It is mostly, mostly quiet, and the quiet is full of processes: transpiration, the slow grinding of the flywheel's bearing, the drip from the vault, the distant single hammer-fall.

The rules of the place, as far as they can be worked out:

Nothing is maintained, and nothing is failing. The hollow has reached a state where the wearing-down of the iron and the growing-in of the wood proceed at exactly matched rates. The erosion here is not decay; it is the long, completed argument between two kinds of patience.

Water enters as mist and leaves as sap. There is no standing pool anywhere in the town, though every surface is damp to the touch.

Whatever the bellworks are making, they have been making it for a long time, and they will go on making it. The product is not stored. It is not shipped. It may simply be the motion itself — the turning, the falling, the breathing — offered up to nothing in particular, by a town that forgot the difference between its workers and its wall-vines and decided, reasonably, to let both of them stay.

At dusk the amber filament in the office window comes on a little brighter, as if the galvanic trickle responds to the cooling air. The hammer falls once more. The flywheel completes another of its forty-second revolutions. A drop of condensation lets go of a leaf four storeys up and takes its whole long while to reach the floor.
