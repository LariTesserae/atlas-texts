# The Glass Combs of Aerie Province

The valley opens at altitude — not quite mountain, not quite plateau — a long cool shelf tilted toward the south, where the light comes in at that peculiar high-country angle that makes every leaf edge look sharpened. The ridges are young. You can tell because they haven't rounded yet; the stone still shows the fracture-planes of whatever uplift raised it, pale quartz-streaked faces still sharp enough to cut a palm. No scree aprons have formed. The rain hasn't had enough centuries.

And the rain barely comes. This is the strange thing — for all the green, it almost never storms here. The vegetation drinks from fog. Every morning, and often in the afternoons when the valley air cools against the upper slopes, mist rolls down the flanks and settles into the canopies, and the canopies drink. You can see it happening: droplets beading on every frond, every fern-edge, and then sliding in slow files down the leaf-veins to pool in the axils, where tiny flukes of water perch for hours before evaporating or being drunk by some rootlet. The plants themselves are the water cycle. There are no real streams, only a few seeps in the lower folds where rock ledges wick it out.

The vegetation is layered to the point of architecture. Mosses carpet the stones. From the mosses rise ferns, and from the ferns rise what I want to call tree-ferns but they aren't quite — more like green candelabra with fluted trunks, maybe four meters, maybe six, their fronds interlocking with their neighbors' fronds so tightly that walking between them means pushing through successive curtains. Above those, a higher canopy of something broad-leaved and waxy, the leaves thick as leather, cupped to catch fog. Everything overlaps. The density is near-claustrophobic in patches, loosening only where the rock breaks through.

And everywhere through the green — the works.

This is the part I did not expect to write. Scattered through the valley, sometimes standing alone in clearings, sometimes half-swallowed by the ferns, are the engines. Copper-bodied, tall as houses, with rows of glass cells along their flanks and slow turning vanes at their tops — fog-condensers, I think, or something like them. They hum. You notice only when you've stood still for a minute: a low, almost inaudible drone, more felt than heard, the sound of something industrial running at a patient pace. Pipes run between them along the ground, disappearing under moss, reappearing at the next machine. The whole valley is a factory. But a factory that produces — what? Water, perhaps. You see the glass cells fill over the course of a morning, drop by drop, and then at some threshold a valve clicks and the collected water moves on through the pipe network, toward somewhere downslope I cannot see from here.

The machines are bright. That's the odd part — their copper is polished, unweathered, as if someone tends them. But I have seen no one. There are a few small structures scattered among the engines: a stone hut with a tin roof here, a wooden shed with its door hanging open there, a platform built around one of the condensers with a ladder going up and a rail going around. Built things, but sparse. One building for every twenty machines. No town. No road — only narrow paths worn through the moss, too narrow for vehicles, wide enough for a person carrying a wrench.

The silence is what holds it all together. Except for the condensers' drone and the occasional tick of a cooling valve, the valley makes no sound. No birds. I have watched for an hour and seen a single insect — a long pale thing with too many wings, drifting between fronds without any apparent hurry. The fauna, what little there is, seems to have taken the valley's cue and gone quiet and slow. Somewhere in the undergrowth, I think, there are small soft-bodied things that eat the fallen fronds, but they don't reveal themselves. The place feels not dead but deeply patient, as if every living thing here is waiting out something on a timescale we don't share.

The light is relentless. Even through the canopy, it comes down in hard bright shafts, and in the clearings it is almost too much — a sky so pale-bright it reads as white rather than blue, and the copper of the machines catches it and throws it back as warm reflections onto the undersides of leaves. Everything is lit from multiple directions at once. Shadows are weak. The valley does not do gloom, even under fog; the fog itself glows.

And here is the weirdness I kept circling: the machines are the same age as the mountains. You can see it in how the moss grows over them — not as an invader covering something foreign, but as if the moss and the copper belong to the same layer of history. Where a pipe enters the ground, the rock has grown up around it, not the other way around. In one place a fern-trunk and a brass conduit have braided together as they rose, and it is impossible to tell which grew through which. The uplift that raised these ridges raised the engines with them. They are not built on the land; they are of it, extruded by whatever process made this valley in the first place.

Nothing changes quickly. The fog comes, the cells fill, the valves click, the water moves on. Tomorrow the same. A year from now the same. The ferns will be a fraction taller. A condenser, somewhere, will have developed a slightly richer patina. The valley is running at the speed of its own geology, and its industry is the industry of a mountain making itself — slow, bright, wet-at-the-edges, almost silent, and entirely indifferent to whether anyone is watching.

Somewhere below, I suspect, the pipes deliver their water to something. Somewhere there is a reason. But from up here, under the cool bright sky, surrounded by the green and the quiet humming of the works, the reason does not seem to matter. The valley is doing what it does. It has been doing it since it rose.
