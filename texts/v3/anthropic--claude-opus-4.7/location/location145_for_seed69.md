# The Understorey

There is no sun here. Not because it has set — because the canopy above has been closed so long it has forgotten the sky. This is not a forest. It is whatever a forest becomes after twenty thousand years of being only itself, stacked on a plateau two miles high, breathing its own weather.

---

Start with the floor, because the floor is where you are. You cannot see it. You feel it — spongy, warm, ankle-deep in a broth of decomposition and rainwater that never fully drains and never fully stagnates, because the whole plateau tilts by fractions of a degree and the water is always going somewhere, slowly, threading between root-knees as thick as grain silos. The mud is warm as blood. Things move in it. You learn not to reach down.

The darkness is not the darkness of a cave. Caves are dry-dark, held-breath-dark. This is wet-dark, full-lunged, a darkness that presses against the eardrum because the air itself is thick — ninety-something percent humidity, always on the verge of becoming rain, often succeeding. When it rains it does so above you, in stages, in the layered canopies — you hear it arrive in the top stratum first as a hiss like static, then the middle canopy as drumming, then finally around you as fat warm drops that have fallen perhaps a thousand feet through leaf and fur and the bodies of sleeping things before reaching the floor.

---

Now the trees. Except they are not trees, not anymore, not the way the word is usually meant. They are the scaffolding of something that has gone beyond forestry into architecture. Trunks braided of dozens of former trunks, fused by sheer duration, hollowed in places into chambers where water collects and small ecosystems run their entire generations before drying out. The bark is colonized to the point of being a vertical reef: fungus on moss on lichen on fern on orchid on another fungus that eats the first. Everything drips. Everything weeps. The rocks, when you find rocks, are also weeping — the plateau is old karst, limestone honeycombed with aquifer, and the stone perspires constantly, cool where the mud is warm.

The trunks rise and disappear. You cannot see the canopy. You only know it by its sounds and by what falls from it.

---

And now the engines.

This is the part that is hardest to describe because it is the part that has no business being here. Somewhere — many somewheres — within the living mass of the forest there are machines. You hear them before you understand them. A low reciprocating thudding, felt in the sternum, that continues for hours and then stops and then resumes a mile away. The hiss and clatter of something venting steam at pressure. A rhythmic metallic shrieking that might be a saw or might be courtship. Chains moving over pulleys the size of millstones.

Who built them is not a question the forest answers. They are not new. Their housings are fused into the trunks now, or the trunks have grown around them, and vines as thick as a man's thigh descend into their intake vents and are apparently metabolized, because the machines continue running. They were industrial once — forges, pumps, looms, mills, it is hard to tell in the dark by touch alone — and they have become something like organs. They draw water up from the aquifer. They exhale heat, which is part of why the understorey stays warm. They grind something, somewhere, constantly, and whatever they produce feeds back into the ecosystem, because nothing here is wasted and nothing here ever leaves.

The creatures have learned the machines. Birds nest in the warm exhaust. Something large and slow uses the rhythmic pounding of a distant hammer-mill as the backbeat for its territorial call. The electrical hum of transformers — yes, there are transformers, there is power moving through cables sheathed in moss — is answered at precise intervals by an insect that has evolved to mimic sixty-cycle hum, because in this darkness sound is identity and the machines have been here long enough to become ancestors.

---

The fauna. Abundant, and you will not see any of them. You will hear everything.

Click-echo animals that navigate by bouncing sound off trunks — they sound like stones tapped together, and when one passes close you can feel the pulse of its sonar on your cheek. Something that sings in the middle register with four voices at once, a single creature producing chord. Heavy things moving through the mud on legs that make a sucking pop with each step. Small quick things in the canopy that chitter and fall silent in waves, a wave of silence passing over you from east to west meaning a predator is moving in the mid-strata.

There are no eyes here that work by light. Eyes have become other things: pits of heat-sensitive tissue, antennae, tympanic membranes stretched across whole flanks. A creature the size of a deer might be nothing but ear and lung.

Occasionally, rarely, you encounter a ruin of something built by hands. A shrine, perhaps, or a surveyor's marker, or a door frame standing free with no wall attached. Stone and ceramic, sometimes brass gone green. They are always small. Whoever made the machines did not make houses, or made them elsewhere, or the houses have been eaten and the machines have not. The marks are scattered and shy. A carved step. A buried rail. A pipe rising out of the mud to a height of four feet and terminating in a brass valve that, when you touch it, is warm and faintly vibrating, and you do not turn it.

---

The rules of the place, as far as they can be stated:

Sound is the primary sense, and lying with sound is the primary sin; creatures that mimic do so for specific contracts, and the mimicry is recognized. The forest runs a protocol older than any single species in it.

Water moves. Always. If water is still, leave.

The machines are not to be interfered with. This is not a law anyone enforces; it is simply that the forest has integrated them, and removing one would be like removing a liver. Things would die. Possibly everything would die. Possibly this has been tried.

The canopy is another country. What lives above does not come down. What lives below does not go up. The middle strata are contested, and the contest has been running long enough to have its own seasons, its own truces, its own traditional battlegrounds marked by the bones of previous generations now fossilized into the bark.

Weather is internal. Fronts move through the understorey horizontally, generated by the breathing of the forest itself — cool damp air sinking from certain cold zones, warm wet air rising from the machine-heated quarters, a circulation you can feel on your face and use to navigate if you are skilled, the way sailors once used trade winds.

---

And the scale. This is the last thing, and the thing that changes everything else.

The plateau is not a place. It is a geography. You could walk for a year in one direction and not reach its edge, and along the way the forest would change — would pass through regions with different dialects of machine-sound, different dominant fauna, different chemistries of mud — and you would understand that what you had been calling "the forest" was only one province of it. There are said to be zones where the machines are louder than the animals, and zones where the trunks have grown so dense you cannot pass between them and must travel by climbing into the second canopy and walking along the backs of the root-bridges. There are said to be inland seas under the canopy, lit from below by bioluminescent algae, where boats move silently crewed by things that have forgotten they were ever human, if they ever were. There are said to be regions of silence, small and dangerous, where something has eaten the sound.

You are in one small warm dripping pocket of it. You can hear a hammer-mill and four kinds of singing and the rain beginning again, up high, starting its long fall down to you.

You sit on a root. You wait for your eyes to stop trying. You begin, instead, to listen.
