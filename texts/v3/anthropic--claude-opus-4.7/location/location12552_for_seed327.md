# The Hanging Cirque

It sits at the top of the world, or near enough. The air is thin and clean and tastes faintly of copper from the lichens, and when you breathe it the cold settles in your chest like a small bright stone. You have climbed too high to be here easily, and yet here it is — a bowl scooped out of the mountain's shoulder, perhaps two bowshots across, ringed by cliffs that lean inward as if listening.

The cliffs weep. That is the first thing you notice after the cold: the constant sound of water. Not a roar — there is no single waterfall — but a hundred small voices. The rock here is some old dark stuff, basalt or something kin to it, fractured into long vertical columns by ages of freeze and thaw, and every fracture is a seep. Water beads on the columns, runs down them in glassy films, gathers in the moss-cushions at their feet, drips from overhangs in slow metronomes. The whole amphitheater drinks fog from the clouds that snag on the peaks above and gives it back as trickles. Even on a clear day the air shimmers with suspended moisture; rainbows hang briefly in the western light and dissolve.

The floor of the cirque is a soft chaos. Cushion plants in pale greens and silvers, mosses thick enough to swallow a boot to the ankle, low willows no taller than a child's knee but spread wide as wagon wheels. Tiny flowers — gentians the color of deep evening, saxifrages like spilled sugar, a yellow cinquefoil that bees crawl drowsily across. The vegetation is not tall but it is *dense in detail*: you could lie on your stomach for an hour and not exhaust one square foot. A small tarn fills the lowest part of the bowl, water so clear that the stones at its bottom look near enough to pinch, and so cold that the surface seems to have a skin on it.

The cirque is loud. This is the part that surprises every visitor. From above, looking down, you'd expect alpine silence — that famous high hush of glaciers and stone. Instead the place rings. The water voices are constant; layered over them are the territorial whistles of marmots, three different pitches from three different colonies. Ravens converse in dry knocks and croaks from the rim. A pair of choughs — red-billed, glossy black — wheel and yelp. Pikas give their sharp single notes from the talus on the eastern wall, and there are enough of them that the notes overlap into something almost like rain on tin. In the willow scrub something larger moves: a blue sheep, maybe, or the ghost-thought of a snow leopard. The wind, when it comes, comes in gusts that shake water off every leaf and column at once, so the whole bowl roars briefly and then resettles into its chorus.

The cirque is also empty. This is the harder thing to describe — a place can be alive with sound and motion and still feel like a near-void, and this one does. The vast spaces are *vertical*. Above the rim, sky. Below the lip where the tarn overflows, nothing — a thousand feet of air before the next ledge, and then more air. The bowl itself is small but it hangs in immensity. There is so much absence pressing in on this little crowded floor that you feel, standing in the middle of it, like a single seed in a cathedral. Whatever lives here lives at the bottom of a great clear glass.

People have come. Not many, and not lately, and not to stay. On a flat slab near the tarn someone once scratched a spiral and a row of dots; the lines are nearly closed up again by black lichen, which means centuries, possibly more. Against the southern cliff there is a low wall of dry-stacked stones, knee-high, curving — a herder's wind-break, or a hunter's blind, or a shrine; it has been all three at different times. Inside it: a flat hearth-stone with a film of soot too old to smell, and three small obsidian flakes, sharp still. A juniper post leans in one corner with prayer-cloths so weathered they're more thread than fabric, the color of nothing. Higher on the eastern wall, where the columns lean to make a half-cave, someone painted in ochre: a horned animal, a figure with raised arms, and a series of vertical strokes that might be a count of something — days, kills, returns. The paint is iron-bright still under the dripping rock, fed by the same seeps that almost erase it.

There are no tools left lying about, no rope, no metal. Whatever people used here they brought up and took back down. They came on foot with hands and skins and a few stone edges, and they left small marks and went away. The cirque has had a long time to take its own shape and has done so: the weathering is complete in the sense that nothing here is raw. Every edge is mossed. Every stone has its lichen. The talus on the east wall has settled into its angle of repose and rests there. Even the tarn's outflow has carved its lip-channel and stopped deepening — water leaves at exactly the rate it arrives.

The rules of this place, as far as one can tell:

The water is always moving but never in a hurry. The marmots watch you and decide collectively whether to whistle; if one starts, all start, and the bowl rings for a full minute. The ravens know the trail down and will sometimes precede a descending visitor, croaking back over their shoulders, though whether this is mockery or guidance is a matter of personal interpretation. At dusk the cliffs catch the last light and burn briefly orange while the floor is already in shadow, and during that interval the temperature drops fast enough that the seeping water along the high columns begins to glaze, and the whole upper rim glitters like a crown for about twenty minutes before full dark. Then the stars come out in a density that flatlanders don't believe.

It is a small place. You can walk its full perimeter in under an hour. But it contains an extraordinary number of distinct lives, an extraordinary number of small sounds, and a single overwhelming quality of being held high up and exposed and yet folded inward, cupped, kept. People have known about it for a very long time and have mostly chosen to leave it alone, visiting only to mark, briefly, that they were here, and then going back down to the warmer world below. The cirque does not seem to mind either way. It has its water and its marmots and its slow centuries of moss, and it goes on weeping quietly from every fracture in its stone.
