# The Reliquary at Sahn-Dor

You find it folded inside a thumb-sized cavity in the mountain — though "inside" is the wrong word, and "thumb-sized" is also wrong. The cavity opens, the way a problem opens when you stop trying to solve it, and you are standing on a balcony of black basalt, and the city is below you.

The first thing is the cold. Not biting, but persistent — the cold of altitude, of thin clean air that takes more breaths to satisfy than it should. Your exhalations fog and drift. There is moisture everywhere. The basalt under your hand is slick with it. Beads run down the railing, gather, fall, and somewhere far below make the small bell-noises of water meeting water.

The city sits in a glacial cirque that should not be possible at this elevation, and is in fact not possible — the mountain itself only finished rising into its present shape some short time ago, geologically speaking. The cliffs around the bowl are still sharp. They haven't been rounded yet. You can see fresh fracture-planes catching the daylight, faces that have never been weathered, edges that would cut. Scree-fans pour down from the rims in clean grey arcs, undisturbed by any path or root.

There are no plants. None. Not lichen on the rocks, not moss in the water-seeps, not algae in the standing pools. The bowl is mineral and metal and the long slow weeping of stone. A single pale crust on one north-facing wall might be a fungus or might be a mineral efflorescence; you cannot tell from here, and you suspect that the distinction, in this place, is not stable.

The city is the strange part.

It is built — densely, deliberately, with the layered logic of a real settlement that has grown over time, except that this settlement cannot have grown over time because the mountain itself is too young. Towers of a pale ceramic-looking substance rise in clusters of six and seven, linked by bridges so thin they read as drawn lines. Lower structures fan out from the tower-clusters in tessellated districts. There are streets. There are what must be plazas. There are aqueducts threading the whole thing, carrying the constant condensation off the cliffs down through the city in narrow open channels, and the channels glow faintly, not with light exactly but with a kind of attention, as if the water is being watched while it flows.

You watch the city for some time before you understand what is wrong with it, and what is wrong is that it is doing things. Not the way a city does things — no traffic, no crowds. The buildings themselves are working. A tower in the middle distance slowly rotates one of its faces toward the cliff and the rock there darkens and a thin runnel of new water begins. A bridge unspools itself from one anchor, drifts on some unseen current, and re-anchors thirty meters farther along. In the lowest district, a whole block is sinking — gracefully, without distress — into a square of mirror-bright fluid, and another block, identical, is rising out of a square beside it.

The technology here exceeds the descriptive vocabulary you brought with you. Matter is being treated as a slow weather. The city is processing something — the mountain, perhaps, or the cold, or the high thin water — and what it makes of that processing is itself.

For all this activity, it is nearly silent. The dominant sound is water: drip, run, the deep gulp of a cistern accepting an offering, the chime of a single droplet falling forty meters down a tower's flank to strike a resonant pan. Beneath that, when you hold still, a low hum at the threshold of hearing — the working of the place, the engines of its self-maintenance, pitched down into the bones of the mountain. No voices. No machines you would recognize as machines.

Fauna is rare but present. A long pale animal — segmented, the length of a forearm, with the gait of something between a centipede and a fish — drinks from one of the glowing channels and then vanishes back into a cleft. You see it once and never again. Higher up, something circles in the cold light, too distant to resolve, and it might be a bird or might be a small piece of the city that has come loose and is finding its way home.

The light is good. Daylight, almost ordinary, falling clean onto wet stone and pale towers. The sky overhead is the deep blue that altitude gives, and there are no clouds, but the air is so saturated that the cliffs themselves smoke gently where the sun warms them, and these vapors lift and drift and recondense as the mountain breathes.

The rules of the place, as best you can piece them together from the balcony:

The city builds itself out of the mountain's own moisture and minerals, drawing from the freshly exposed rock the elements it needs. It has no inhabitants in the sense you would mean. It is not a habitation but a *practice* — an ongoing act of fine-grained engineering whose purpose, if it has one, is not legible from where you stand. It may be tending the mountain. It may be tending itself. The two may not be separable.

It is small. That is the thing that keeps coming back to you. For all its towers and districts, the entire city would fit in the palm of a larger hand. You have entered a pocket. The whole cirque, the whole thin-aired sky above it, the whole working settlement — contained. You came through a cavity the size of your thumb, and what you found on the other side has its own weather, its own slow industry, its own scarce and careful animals.

You should leave soon. The balcony is cold and your breath is fogging and you are intruding on something that has been patient for a long time and will go on being patient after you are gone. But for another minute you stay, and watch a thread of water find a new path down a fresh black face of stone, and watch a tower turn, attentive, to receive it.
