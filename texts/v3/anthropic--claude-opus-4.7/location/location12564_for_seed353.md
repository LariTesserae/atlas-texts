# The Hothouse Combinatorium

There is no ground here. There hasn't been ground for a long time.

What you stand on — what extends in every direction across the plateau — is a packed, layered weave of root and stem and woody trunk-mass that has been pressed and grown and grown-over and pressed again until it has the density of cork, of black bread, of something between timber and coal. Walk on it and it gives, very slightly, and exhales heat through the soles of your boots. Cut into it and you'd find ten thousand years of vegetation compressed into a single readable column: a leaf here, a root-channel there, the ghost of a flower that opened once in the dark a millennium ago.

The plateau is high — not mountain-high but lifted, a broad table risen above whatever lies below, its edges long since worn down to gentle slopes. Once it had shape. Once there were ridges, valleys, peaks of growth. Erosion has done its slow work. Now it is nearly flat, an enormous calloused palm of plant-matter held up to a sky no one sees.

Because it is dark. Not lightless — there is a smoldering somewhere, a low red where the deep layers oxidize and cook — but dark in the way a furnace room is dark, where you see only what glows. And nearly everything here glows a little. The compressed substrate is hot, very hot, the kind of heat that dries your eyes and makes metal too painful to touch by midday-equivalent. There is no day. The heat comes from below, from inside, from the slow combustion of the world's own body.

There is almost no water. What moisture exists is held tight inside living tissue, recycled and re-recycled by the plants themselves through systems of vascular pumping that have nothing to do with rain. Rain does not fall here. The sky, if there is one above the canopy of older growth, is sealed off by layers of accumulated leaf-mass that have lithified into something like a roof of black mica.

And above the substrate — pressing into it, pushing out of it, *being* it — is the architecture.

This is the part that takes time to see.

The plants here have, over the long ancient stretch of this place, become builders. Not because anything told them to, but because in total absence of any other substrate they began building on each other, and the geometries that survived were the geometries that admitted heat-flow and structural load. So now there are pipes — living pipes — running everywhere through the substrate, thick as a person, thick as a house, conducting hot sap and gas between distant nodes. There are towers of fused trunk-wood, hollow, that vent steam from the deep layers. There are flying buttresses of woody fiber holding up arched halls whose ceilings are knitted from a million fine tendrils. Walkways. Bellows-organs that wheeze open and shut. Whole districts of chambered growth that function, recognizably, as factories.

This is the industrial part, and it is the strange part.

The plants run engines. Not metaphorically. Somewhere in the deep evolutionary past of this place, certain lineages began coupling their osmotic pressures to mechanical linkages — pistons of swollen tuber driving cam-shafts of hardened heartwood, the whole apparatus lubricated by the plants' own oils. The result, after enough time, is mechanized production. Vast slow factories of vegetal machinery, gear-trains of lignin meshing with gear-trains of lignin, the teeth replaced as they wear by the plant's own continuing growth. What do they produce? More of themselves. Specialized tissues, secreted compounds, structural members shipped along the pipework to wherever the colony-organism needs them. The whole plateau is a single distributed factory making itself, endlessly, in the dark.

It is mostly quiet. This surprises you. You'd expect the roar of industry, but the engines are slow — geological-slow in some places, only minute-slow in others — and the materials are soft. A great piston-tuber the size of a barn descends over the course of an afternoon with only the faintest creak, a long sigh of compressed air pushed through a fibrous valve. Sap gurgles in the great pipes the way blood gurgles in a sleeping body: continuous, low, easy to stop noticing. Occasionally something snaps — a tendon of overstressed fiber giving way somewhere — and the sound carries for kilometers across the plateau like a distant gunshot, and then silence resumes.

No birds. No insects. No fauna at all. The chemistry here is too hot, too dry, too saturated with the plants' own defensive alkaloids for animal life to have ever found purchase, or if it did, it lost. The pollination — if that's still the word — happens through the pipework, gametes pumped from one district to another along dedicated channels. Reproduction is plumbing.

But here is where it becomes turbulent, where the dynamic parameter asserts itself: the whole arrangement is unstable.

The plateau is always rebuilding itself. A district that was a factory yesterday is being cannibalized today; its members withdraw their tissue, redirect their flows, and within a week the chambers have collapsed and new growth is pushing up through the wreckage in some entirely different configuration. Pipes burst. Pressures shift. A great pump three valleys over fails, and the pressure-wave travels through the network and a tower near you suddenly inflates, splits, and reforms as something else — a sail of wet membrane, a hanging garden of black fruit, a new pipe heading off in a new direction toward some new need. You can stand still for an hour and watch the skyline rewrite itself.

There is no plan. There is no central anything. It is a bazaar of vegetable purpose, every cell trading with its neighbors, every district renegotiating its function with the districts around it, the whole plateau in a constant slow churn of self-revision. Storm-state, but in slow motion and in plant-time. A Solaris of chlorophyll-substitute and lignin.

And through it all, the heat. The low red glow from the deep layers. The exhalation of hot vegetal breath from every vent. Walk far enough across the plateau and you'll come to a place where a whole hectare has cooked itself down into char, a smoldering scar that the surrounding tissue is already encroaching upon, sending tentative new growth across the burn, beginning again.

Beginning, here, is what the place does instead of ending. It has been ancient for so long that ancient is its steady state. It will, presumably, go on like this — dark, hot, dry, packed, turning over inside itself — until something from outside intervenes, or until the slow combustion at its core finally outpaces the growth at its surface and the whole plateau collapses inward into its own furnace.

Neither has happened yet.

The pipes gurgle. A piston descends. Somewhere, a tower decides to become a different tower, and begins.
