# The Hollow Choir

There is a town inside a tree. Or there was a tree, and now there is a town, and the distinction has stopped meaning anything to either of them.

It sits low in a basin where the air pools warm and the ground stays damp without ever quite flooding — a held breath of a place, no more than three hundred paces across at its widest. The walls of the basin are not stone but bark, vast curving sheets of it rising into a canopy so dense it functions as a ceiling, and yet light comes through anyway, the normal honest light of an overcast afternoon, falling in shafts that have no visible source. The light arrives. That is all that can be said about it.

The town is grown, not built, though something built it. Houses are hollows in the wood, doors are scars that healed into hinges, streets are the long peeled grooves where some ancient bark split and the split became a thoroughfare. Stairs spiral up the inside of trunk-walls in even helices that no tree would make on its own. Whoever shaped this — and there is a *whoever*, you can feel it in the deliberateness — used no tools. They convinced the wood. They argued with it over centuries and the wood, eventually, agreed.

Everything is established. Weathered smooth. The galleries have the polish of a riverstone, the doorframes sag with the soft droop of long-settled timber. Moss has had time to choose its corners. Lichens have signed their names in three alphabets of color and then faded and signed again. The place is in its own state, has been in its own state, will continue.

And yet — *almost no one lives here.*

This is the first wrongness. For a town of this elaboration, of this many rooms and balconies and inner courtyards lined with fern and dripping orchid, there should be a population. There is not. You can walk from one end to the other and meet perhaps two presences — a figure seated very still in a doorway who may be a person or may be a knot in the wood that suggests one; a sound of breathing from a room three turns away that stops when you approach. The houses are mostly empty. They have been emptied, or were never filled, or are full of something that does not register as inhabitation. The density is wrong. The density is the wrongness made spatial.

But the *animals*. Oh, the animals.

The fauna is the population. Beetles the size of fists move in slow processions along the gutters. Tree-frogs in colors that don't exist outside this basin cling to every vertical surface, two and three deep. Snakes loop through the rafters like decorative cord. Something with too many legs and a face like a folded leaf watches from the eaves of every building. Birds — if they are birds — nest in the chimneys that were never chimneys, in the bread-ovens that never baked bread, in the wardrobes and the cupboards and the cradles. Mammals the shape of question marks drink from puddles that form on the floors when condensation runs down the walls in slow vertical rivers.

And every single one of them is screaming.

This is the second wrongness, and the larger one. The sound here is not loud — *loud* is insufficient. The sound is deafening in the literal sense: it takes your hearing away. The whole basin is a throat. Every creature is contributing a voice, and the voices are not animal voices, or not only. The beetles sing in soprano. The frogs deliver long oratorical passages in something that resembles Latin if Latin had been spoken underwater. The snakes hum a drone-note that vibrates in the marrow. The birds — the birds are reciting names. You can almost catch them. They are names you almost recognize.

And the wood itself is singing. The town-tree is a resonator, and every chamber within it amplifies and re-pitches the chorus, so that to walk from one room to another is to walk through different movements of the same impossible symphony. In the bark-cathedral at the basin's center the sound becomes so total that it stops being sound and becomes a kind of weather. You stand in it. It passes through you. You leave wetter than you came, though it has not rained.

It does rain, sometimes. Intermittently. The rain falls *inside* the canopy, from nowhere, in warm fat drops that taste faintly of sap. It collects in the bark-gutters and runs down the staircases and is drunk by the beetles, who briefly modulate their key in gratitude before resuming.

The rules of this place, as far as can be determined:

The town does not grow, but it changes. A room you slept in will not be in the same place tomorrow, though it will still be yours, and your belongings will be in it.

The animals do not eat each other. There is no predation, despite the abundance. The screaming is the ecology. The screaming *is* the food chain, somehow — energy passing through frequency, life maintained by chorus. To stop singing is to die, and nothing here stops singing, so nothing here dies, and the established weathered ancient balance holds and holds and holds.

If you sing too, the animals will make room for you. If you stay long enough to learn the names the birds are reciting, one of them will be yours, and after that you will not be able to leave, but you will not want to. The empty houses are for those who learned their names. They are not empty. You only think they are because you have not yet started screaming.

The light continues to arrive from nowhere. The warm damp continues to pool. Somewhere, very faintly, beneath the chorus and the chorus's echo and the echo's echo in the wood, there is a deeper sound — a slow, vast, woody creaking, as of something enormous turning over in a sleep so old it has forgotten it is sleep.

The tree dreams the town. The town dreams the animals. The animals sing the dream awake, over and over, and the dream, being sung, persists.
