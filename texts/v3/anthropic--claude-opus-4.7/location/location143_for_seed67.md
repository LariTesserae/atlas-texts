# The Condensery

It is a continent of pipes.

Not a landscape with pipes running through it — the pipes *are* the landscape, fitted so tightly together that there is no ground beneath them, only more pipes, and beneath those, more, down to whatever foundation the makers laid before the makers were forgotten. You walk on the top course: a mostly-flat plain of brass and dull iron cylinders, some as thin as a finger, some wide enough to stand inside, bundled and strapped and soldered into a surface that approximates horizontal. Where the diameters don't match, the gaps are packed with lead wool or smaller pipes or, in the older districts, with a grey ceramic paste that has set harder than the metal around it.

The whole thing is warm. Not hot — warm the way a sleeping body is warm, warm the way bathwater becomes after an hour, a temperature that the continent maintains because the continent is, in some sense, a single slow machine for maintaining it. Water moves inside the pipes. You can feel it if you kneel: a low, even pressure, not quite a pulse, not quite a hum. The metal sweats. Every surface wears a film of condensation, and in the low places — the long shallow basins where the pipe-plain dips a meter or two — that condensation has pooled into sheets of standing water, ankle-deep, mirror-still, warm as tea going cold.

There is almost no sound. The engines that drive this place, if they are engines, are buried deep enough that only their equilibrium reaches the surface: a subsonic contentedness, the note a kettle makes just before it sings but never progressing past that note. When you speak, your voice carries a long way across the wet metal. When you stop speaking, the silence is the kind that has weight.

Nothing grows here — nothing green. But the pipes are *upholstered* in life. Every surface that stays wet stays wet forever, and every such surface is furred with slow mineral organisms: rust-lichens in orange and black rosettes, verdigris mats that creep a centimeter a decade, pale tubeworms that filter the condensate and wave cilia the color of old pearl. In the shallow pools: translucent shrimp-things the length of a thumbnail, so numerous that the water seems to simmer when you look closely, though no one of them moves quickly. Between the pipes, in the dark narrow channels where the bundling leaves a hand's-width gap, there are *eels* — or the local analog — long slim bodies packed flank to flank, breathing through gills pressed against the warm metal, a living insulation layer the builders may or may not have intended. If you lie down and put your ear to a pipe you will hear, beneath the water-pressure hum, the soft collective respiration of ten thousand eels per square meter of subsurface, continent-wide.

The sky is ordinary. That is the strangest thing about the place — a clear, mild daylight, diffuse through a high haze of the condensery's own exhalation, like the sky above a greenhouse. It is always this light. It does not seem to pass through stages. Morning is noon is afternoon. The haze reddens slightly at what might be evening and then doesn't complete the gesture; the light simply holds.

The continent is young. You can tell because the solder joints still gleam where they aren't wet, because the pipes at the edges — where the whole construction terminates in a cliff of cross-sections dropping into a warm sea — show clean-cut ends, not yet rounded by wear. In places, whole districts of pipe stand up in sharp ridges and valleys, as though the surface was laid down in a hurry and not yet walked flat. The erosion that exists is almost entirely biological: the lichens pit the metal, the eels polish the inner channels, the shrimp leave faint calcareous trails. Weather has barely touched it. There is no real weather, only the continent's own breath, rising and condensing and falling back as a mist so fine it feels like being inside a lung.

And the fauna — I keep coming back to this, because it is the part that unsettles. The place is empty of anything that would be called a creature in the old sense; there are no birds, no walking animals, no beasts you could name. But it is *crowded*. The density of small wet lives per cubic meter is probably greater than any forest. Every joint seethes. Every pool is a broth. If you sit still for an hour a film of something will begin to grow on your boots. The continent is not a machine with pests — the pests, if that's what they are, are what makes the machine work. The eels insulate. The shrimp filter. The lichens seal micro-leaks with their slow bodies. Whoever built this built it knowing it would be colonized, or built it so that colonization would complete it, and then — this is the weird part — *left*, and the equilibrium has held.

How long it has held, no one knows. There is no season here, no tide, no day that differs from another. A pipe that sprang a leak a thousand years ago was sealed by lichen in the first century and has been sealed ever since. A generation of shrimp lives six weeks and is replaced by an identical generation. The eel-mass has the same biomass this decade as last. The sky does not change. The pressure does not change. The warmth does not change. If you set down a cup of tea on the pipe-plain it will cool to the ambient temperature and remain at that temperature, neither warmer nor colder, until you pick it up again or forget it, and if you forget it, the lichen will have it in a year, and the brass cup in a century, and after that the cup will be part of the continent, indistinguishable, and the continent will not have noticed.

It is, I think, a place that was built to be finished. Most places are in the middle of becoming something. This one arrived. The builders set the last pipe, opened the last valve, watched the first condensation bead on the first cold joint, and understood that their work was complete in the specific sense that nothing further needed to happen, ever. And then — one hopes gently — they went away, and the shrimp inherited the world.
