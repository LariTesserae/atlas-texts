# The Flensed Summit

Begin with the elevation, because everything else follows from it.

The plateau sits so high that "altitude" stops meaning what it normally means. The air is thin enough that you can see the curvature of the world from anywhere on it — a horizon that bows visibly downward in all directions, as if you are standing on the crown of something. Below the plateau's edges, a grey cloud-ocean spreads forever, and whatever continent or moon holds this place up is buried so far beneath that weather that nobody standing here has ever seen it, or remembers seeing it, or believes in it.

The rock itself is old past the point where "old" means anything geological. It has been eroded not into ruggedness but into smoothness — vast gently-swelling fields of something like pale jade and something like bone, polished by aeons of a wind that no longer fully blows. You can walk for a day and the slope under your feet will change by perhaps a degree. The mountains that once stood here have been worn down to the softest possible reminders of themselves: long shallow domes a few kilometers across, their summits indistinguishable from their flanks. Everything has found its final shape. The landscape is at rest in the way certain very old faces are at rest.

And yet.

The dynamism is not in the stone. The stone is finished. The dynamism lives in the air above it, and in what the air does.

---

The plateau has a sky of its own. Because the air is so thin and the scale so great, weather here behaves less like weather and more like something weather might dream about. Clouds form out of nothing in a few seconds — a patch of shimmer condenses, thickens, becomes a lens-shaped body hanging a hundred meters up, and then unravels again, all within the span of a held breath. Threads of vapor move against the prevailing wind. Sometimes a pressure-front sweeps across the plateau at walking speed, visible as a ripple in the light, and everything behind it is subtly altered: the shadows fall at a slightly different angle, the horizon seems nearer, a faint electrical charge lifts the fine hairs on the back of the neck.

There are auroral sheets at mid-day. They are not really auroras. They are something the tech is doing, or something the tech used to do and is now doing on inertia, the way a bell keeps ringing.

---

The tech. This is where the place becomes difficult to describe.

Scattered across the plateau — sparse, one per several kilometers, sometimes less — are objects. Calling them structures overstates them. They are more like punctuation in the rock. A ring of black material, eight meters across, set flush with the ground, so flat and so black that walking over it feels like walking over a hole. A vertical spar of something that is not metal, not stone, not crystal, rising perhaps to the height of a person, which casts no shadow at any time of day. A hemispherical depression in the jade-bone surface, half-filled with a powder that drifts against the wind when you disturb it and then rearranges itself into the same pattern of concentric rings it held before.

None of these objects are ruined. They are not ancient in the way the rock is ancient. They simply do not participate in time at the same rate as the rest of the world. An instrument dropped next to the black ring at dawn will, at dusk, be found a few meters away, undamaged, as if the ring had gently declined to be observed.

The working theory — the working theory of whom? there is no one here — is that the tech is the reason the weather is turbulent. Something at the scale of the plateau is being maintained, or computed, or remembered, by the scattered objects in concert. The atmosphere is their waste heat. The strange clouds are the frayed edges of a calculation so large it has to use the sky as scratch paper.

---

Life, such as it is.

The vegetation is a kind of lichen-analog, colored the faint purple of a bruise going yellow. It grows only in the lee of the tech-objects, in arcs and crescents a few meters across, as if the objects protect their small gardens or as if the objects leak something the lichen can eat. In a day's walk you might pass six such gardens. Between them, nothing — the polished stone, the thin air, the light.

The fauna is rarer still. There is a creature, or a kind of creature, that resembles a very slow translucent moth the size of a hand. They drift on the strange winds, sometimes singly, sometimes in pairs. They do not appear to land. Where they go at night, or whether there is a night for them, is unclear. Once in a great while one will pass close enough that you can see through its body to the stone beyond, and you will notice that its internal structure is geometrically identical to the pattern of rings in the powder-filled depressions. Whether this means the moths are made by the tech, or the tech is made by something that also made the moths, or the resemblance is coincidence at a scale where coincidence stops being coincidence — these are questions for somewhere else.

---

The sound.

This is the strangest part, and the quietest. The plateau is almost completely silent. The wind is too thin to roar; at most it produces a soft, continuous, almost-inaudible hiss, like the sound inside a very large empty room. Your footsteps on the polished stone make a faint click that dies immediately, absorbed. Your own breathing is the loudest thing for kilometers.

But occasionally — and this is what people who have been here remember most, if anyone has been here — there is a tone. A single sustained note, neither high nor low, that seems to come from everywhere and from a specific direction at once. It lasts anywhere from two seconds to two minutes. Then it stops. It is not musical. It is not mechanical. It is the sound a place makes when it is adjusting itself.

After the tone passes, something in the landscape has changed. Never anything large. A lichen-garden is now on the other side of its object. The powder-rings have one more ring, or one fewer. A spar that was vertical is now tilted four degrees. The change is always small enough that you could convince yourself you misremembered. But the air feels rinsed afterward, as if a decision has been made and the plateau is relieved.

---

The rules of this world, as best they can be stated:

The stone is finished and will not change further. The tech is unfinished and will never finish. Between them, in the thin bright air, a turbulence plays out at scales from the molecular to the continental, and it is this turbulence — not the stone, not the tech, not the lichen or the moths — that is the real inhabitant of the plateau. The turbulence is what the place is *for*. Everything else is the vessel it has worn itself into, over a duration for which no human unit is appropriate.

You could stand here for a long time without being harmed. You would not, however, be noticed. The plateau is busy with its own long slow thought, and you are the wrong size and the wrong speed to be part of it. At most, the moths might drift past you, and the tone might sound once while you are present, and afterwards the wind will feel briefly clean, and you will not know whether you were the reason, or part of the reason, or simply in the room while the reason happened.

Then you will go back down, through the cloud-ocean, to wherever down is, and the plateau will continue without you, as it has continued without everyone, polishing itself toward a stillness it has already, in some sense, achieved.
