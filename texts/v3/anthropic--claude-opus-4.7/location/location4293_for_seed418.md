# The Sump-City

It sits in a bowl. Not a valley exactly — more like a saucer pressed into the world and forgotten there, the rim so low and so worn you could walk over it without knowing you'd crossed anything. The stone beneath is the color of an old tooth. It has been rounded by so much weather for so long that there are no edges left anywhere; every surface softens into every other surface, and water weeps from all of them, a constant beading and trickling that never becomes a stream but never stops either. The air above the rock shimmers. It is hot — the kind of hot where your own breath feels cooler than what you draw in — and the film of water on everything evaporates upward in a thin silver haze that you walk through rather than under.

The city is built of the bowl and into it. I keep wanting to say *built* but that's wrong — it was built once, a long time ago, and since then it has been settling, the way a body settles into a chair it has occupied for years. The buildings are low. Single-story, mostly, with thick walls of the same tooth-colored stone, domed or half-domed, their roofs rounded from long rain into something closer to loaves than to architecture. Lintels sag. Doorframes have become ovals. Walls lean into each other companionably. You can tell there were streets once by the slight channels where water now runs, shin-deep in places, ankle-deep in others, always moving, always warm.

Very little grows. A hand's-width of pale moss at a threshold. A single fleshy plant in a courtyard, broad-leaved and low, drinking from a seep. The vegetation seems almost accidental, as if the plants are embarrassed to be there among so much stone and water and heat.

What lives here instead is animal. Overwhelmingly.

Go slow with this part. The first time you notice, you think the walls are breathing — a slight pulsing of the surfaces in the dim light. Then you see that the walls are *covered*. Pale frogs the size of thumbnails, thousands of them, pressed flat against the wet stone, their throats working in slow asynchronous pulses. They make almost no sound. They are too many and too close together to call; the ones at the edges of a cluster sometimes let out a soft single *tok*, and that is most of the city's noise. That and water.

In the channels: things that are almost eels and almost salamanders, the length of a forearm, gliding without hurry through the warm shallow current. They braid around each other. They do not flee from feet. Under every overhang, in every sagged doorway, something is roosting — soft-bodied, eyeless or many-eyed, you don't look too closely. Cave crickets the size of mice move across the ceilings of the little domed rooms in slow processions. There are pale crabs in the corners of the courtyards, the size of coins, doing whatever pale crabs do.

All of this is quiet. That is the strangeness of it — so much life, so little sound. A great wet hush. The loudest thing is usually your own footsteps in water, and even those are muffled by the stone's softness. Occasionally, deep in the bowl's center, a low note sounds — not a call, more a resonance, as if one of the domes has a voice and is using it very slowly. Minutes between notes. Sometimes an hour.

There are no tools here. No sign of the people who built the city, or the things that built it, or whatever did. No pots, no hearths, no worked metal, no carvings that haven't been smoothed to suggestion. Whatever civilization this was has been gone long enough that the only evidence of intention is the rough grid you can still feel underfoot when you pay attention — *something here was once meant*. The animals have inherited it entirely. They do not seem to have organized themselves in imitation; they just live in the rooms because the rooms are good rooms, warm and wet and dim.

The light is strange. The bowl is shallow enough that sky should reach the floor easily, but the haze of evaporation overhead diffuses everything into a pearl-gray glow, neither morning nor evening, shadowless. Things far away go milky. You cannot quite see the rim from the center. You cannot quite see the center from the rim. The city holds only as much of itself as you are standing in.

And it is small. This is the thing to understand last. The whole bowl might be a few hundred paces across. You could walk its diameter in the time it takes a kettle to boil, if anyone here had kettles. It is a pocket, a held breath, a single wet warm room of a world tucked under the haze — and inside that room, a civilization's worth of stone has given up being a civilization and agreed, slowly, over a span you can't measure, to be a habitat instead. The frogs pulse. The eels braid. The heat presses down. Water beads and falls and beads again. Somewhere a dome sounds its long low note, and ten thousand small throats answer, almost, almost, not quite.
