# The Brood-Caverns of Lower Anneth

You come down into it through a fissure in the dry upland, and at first you think you've found an ordinary cave — but then the walls begin to *move*, and you understand you've found something else entirely.

---

The cavern system opens beneath the surface like a lung that never quite finishes inflating. Not deep — never more than thirty or forty meters below the dry crust above — but wide. Wider than seems possible. You can stand in the entry chamber and see, by the strange even light, galleries receding into galleries, vaults stacked on vaults, the floor sloping away into a valley-sized hollow that the locals (when there were locals) called the Pan.

The light is the first wrong thing. It is full daylight down here, but there is no sun. The ceiling is studded with something — call them lume-nodes, fist-sized concretions of a pale mineral that emits a steady warm yellow glow, neither flickering nor fading. They are not arranged. They simply occur, in dense constellations near the high vaults, sparser in the low passages, almost absent where the rock turns black and glassy. The light they cast is shadowless and even, like the light inside a cloud, and it does not seem to come from any direction in particular.

The rock itself is *new*. This is the second wrong thing. Run your hand along the wall and you feel — not the slow patient curves of water-carved limestone, not the brittle facets of weathered stone — but something almost soft, almost warm, the grain of it still settling. The walls are forming. Mineral seeps from somewhere deeper, lays itself down in slow translucent sheets, and hardens into the rock you are touching only minutes after you have touched it. Whole pillars are growing. You can watch a stalactite extend itself the width of a fingernail in an hour. The geology has not yet decided what it wants to be. Erosion has barely begun its work; there are no smoothed surfaces, no rounded chambers, no debris fans. Everything is sharp and provisional.

The air is warm — not hot, but the warmth of a body, of a held breath. It is damp without being wet. Moisture beads on the lume-nodes and falls in slow heavy drops at unpredictable intervals: *plok*, somewhere off to the left, then silence, then *plok-plok* far above, then the trickle of water down a fluting in the wall, then silence again. There are no rivers here. There are pockets — basins the size of bathtubs, the size of small ponds — where the drip-water has collected into pools so still and clear you could lower a hand into them and not feel where the surface was. Around these pools the rock is darker, slicker, and the air just above them shimmers with condensation.

---

Now the third wrong thing, which is really the whole of the place:

It is *full of animals.*

Not a few. Not many. **Full.** The walls move because they are layered with living things — sheet-creatures the color of the rock, palm-sized and many-legged, that slide over each other in continuous slow currents. Above them, clinging to the upper walls and ceilings, are clusters of soft-bodied hangers like inverted teardrops, each one a hand's length, pulsing faintly in time with something. Between them: scuttlers, jointed and quick, the size of cats, running in sudden bursts along ledges; flyers no bigger than moths but in such numbers that the air itself seems to grain and ripple when they pass; long pale things in the pools, eel-shaped, that surface and submerge without ever quite breaking the water; and in the deeper galleries, larger presences — humped backs moving through the half-light, low calls answering each other across the Pan.

The sound is constant and layered. A wash of small clickings, like rain on dry leaves, from the wall-sheets. A rhythmic low pulse from the hangers, felt more than heard. Wing-hum. Water-drip. The skitter of claws. And underneath it all, at irregular intervals, a deep belling cry from somewhere in the Pan — not a roar, not a song, but a long sustained note like a struck bell, that travels through the rock as much as the air, and that the smaller animals momentarily quiet for, before resuming.

There is no vegetation. Not lichen, not moss, not fungal mat. The animals are not eating plants. What they are eating is each other and the rock — the wall-sheets graze the seeping mineral as it lays itself down, scraping the fresh stone with rasping mouths and leaving faint chevroned tracks behind them. The scuttlers eat the wall-sheets. The hangers drop on the scuttlers. The eels in the pools take whatever falls in. The large things in the Pan — you do not yet know what they eat, and you would prefer not to find out.

The density is something a surface-dweller cannot really prepare for. There is no square meter of floor or wall that is not occupied. To walk is to displace animals with every step; they part around your feet in waves and close behind you. They are not afraid. They are not curious. You are simply an obstacle the size of an obstacle, and they account for you and move on.

---

The fourth thing — and this one took me longest to see — is that **someone has been here.** Not recently. But long ago, and not all at once.

On a ledge above one of the larger pools, three flat stones have been stacked into a low cairn. On the cairn's top stone, a spiral has been pecked into the rock with a harder stone. The peck-marks are weathered, which is striking because almost nothing here is weathered; this means the cairn is *old*, older than the rock around it is finished. Whoever made it came down here when this chamber was a different shape.

Further in, in a side gallery, there is a shelter: four upright slabs leaned together to make a small cave-within-the-cave, the inside floor swept clean of animals (how?), a cold firepit at the center, and on the wall a row of tally marks in ochre. Forty-seven of them. Then nothing.

In another gallery: a tool. A long bone, worked at one end into a scraping edge, the haft wrapped in something that was once probably hide and is now a dark friable crust. It is leaning against the wall as if its owner set it down a moment ago and went to fetch water.

There are no bodies. There are no graves I can find. There is no writing beyond the tally marks and the spiral and a few other symbols I do not understand. Whoever they were, they were here in small numbers, lightly, with simple things. They knew how to live in this place — they must have, to live here at all — and then they were not here anymore.

---

The rules of the place, as best as I can determine them:

The rock grows. The light is constant. The water arrives without source and leaves without destination, recycling through some hidden cycle the animals depend on. The animals are far too many for any surface ecosystem to support; they must be eating the rock itself, drawing nourishment from whatever mineral process feeds the walls, and so the whole cavern is in a sense one slow flow — minerals up from below, into the walls, into the wall-grazers, up the food chain, and eventually (one assumes) back down to the depths in some form. The Pan is the throat of this. The belling cries from the deep galleries are the sound of something very large that lives at the bottom of the flow and may or may not be a single creature.

The dynamism is *extreme*. Nothing here is stable for an hour together. A passage you walked through in the morning may be half-closed by afternoon as new rock seeps into it. A pool may be dry tomorrow because the animals shifted and the drip-pattern moved. A gallery teeming with hangers may be empty in an hour because something passed through and ate them all. Maps would be useless. The cairn-makers, the tally-keepers, must have moved constantly, must have read the cavern the way a sailor reads weather — not as a place but as a set of unfolding conditions.

It is, in this sense, less a landscape than a *process* with a temperature and a light level. A warm wet stone-making animal-thick weather-system, regional in extent, that happens to occur underground.

---

I would not stay long. The cairn-makers did not, in the end.

But it is beautiful, in the wide vaulted way of cathedrals where the congregation is countless and small and entirely occupied with its own business, and the bells, when they ring, ring from below.
