# The Bellows Plateau

Start with the heat. Not the heat of a desert exactly — there is no sand, no sun-baked stone of the kind you'd recognize. The heat comes up through the soles, through the rock, a thermal pressure that has been here for so long that the word *here* has been shaped around it. The plateau breathes it out. Somewhere far below — kilometers, perhaps — there is a chemistry happening that has not stopped since whatever counts for the beginning of this place, and the rock above has learned to be a lid.

The plateau itself sits high. You feel it in the thinness of what passes for air: not a true atmosphere, more like a hot exhalation that thickens and thins on a slow rhythm, hours-long. The horizon, when you can see it, is too far and too curved. The light is ordinary — the light is the most ordinary thing about this place — clean, unfiltered, the kind of light that would suit an afternoon on a moor. It falls on something else entirely.

There is almost no water. There are veins of a glassy salt that runs in slow seams across the rock, and these seams sometimes sweat a bead of clear oil that hangs without falling, then withdraws. That is the closest thing to water. There are no plants. There are crusts — flat, palm-sized, the color of old bronze — that may once have been alive or may be the residue of something that was, scattered far apart, one every twenty paces or so. Pre-life, post-life, the difference does not seem to matter here. They do not grow. They have been the same crusts for a long time.

The rock is established. By that I mean: it has settled into its forms the way an old face settles into its expressions. Wind-shaped pillars, but the wind that shaped them does not blow now and may not have blown for ages. Mesas with their corners rubbed soft. Long shallow basins like the prints of enormous fingers. The erosion is finished, or near enough — the place has reached a kind of geological retirement, and the changes from here forward will be slow ones, hour-hands rather than second-hands.

And it is *empty*. Vast, vast empty. You can walk for half a day and pass nothing taller than your knee.

Now: the machines.

They are not built. That is what's hardest to convey. There are no rivets, no welds, no foundries. They are *of the rock,* extruded from it, the way a geode grows crystals — but what grows here grows as pistons, as flywheels, as great iron lungs the color of hematite, as gear-trains the size of a town square clicking once every several minutes. They stand alone at huge intervals across the plateau, one per horizon, each running its own slow industrial idea. No two are alike. One is a chimney that pulses a brown smoke that climbs ten meters and then is reabsorbed downward into a vent at its own base — a closed loop, a respiration. Another is an arrangement of cams and rods half-buried, that rises and falls with a sound like a foundry hammer striking once every forty seconds, and the sound carries across the plateau and is answered, distantly, by another hammer somewhere else, and another, in a polyphony so spread out you have to stand still for an hour to hear the pattern. A third is a stack of black drums turning against each other, screeching, throwing off a smell of hot oil though there is no oil anywhere you can see.

They were not put here. They emerged, the way the pillars and the mesas emerged. The plateau makes engines the way other places make canyons.

This is the reality breakdown. Not strangeness piled on strangeness — the strangeness of a single category error so old and so thoroughly absorbed into the world's grammar that the world has organized itself around it. *Industry without industrialists.* Mechanism as geology. The engines do not produce anything that anyone uses. They produce themselves, and their slow noise, and a kind of waste heat that contributes to the general warmth, and that is the whole of their economy.

The sound is loud. I should be clearer about this. Stand anywhere on the plateau and you are inside a sound-field: the near hammer, the far hammers, the screech of the drum-stack carried for kilometers on the thermal layers, the throat-hum of the chimney, the small ticking of gear-trains you can't see. It does not stop. It has not stopped. The plateau's silence, if it ever had one, has been replaced. You learn after a while to hear pattern in it — there *is* pattern, the slow polyrhythm of the hammers, the way the chimney exhales on a count that matches some other engine's intake — but the pattern is too large for a body to hold all at once.

And the fauna.

This is what overwhelms. Between the engines, in the empty spaces, the air is *full of animals.* They are small, mostly. Hand-sized. They are everywhere, in numbers that do not match the apparent emptiness of the landscape — because they are not standing on the ground. They are riding the thermals, the shudder-columns above the pistons, the warm exhalations of the chimneys. They look something like moths and something like crabs and something like neither. Some are translucent and the light goes through them in pinks and golds. They cling in clusters to the moving parts of the engines, thousands per flywheel, and they ride the strokes — up, down, up, down — and they feed on something there, perhaps the friction itself, perhaps the heat differential, perhaps a chemistry I cannot name. When a hammer falls, the cloud around it bursts outward and resettles before the next stroke. When the drum-stack screeches, a sheet of them peels off and reattaches further down.

There are larger ones. Slow ones, the size of dogs, that walk on too many legs across the open rock between engines, going from one to another on errands of their own. They are unhurried. They do not regard a person. They have been here longer than the concept of a person.

The ecology is the engines. The engines feed the fauna; the fauna, by riding the engines, keeps the bearings polished, the seals tight, the heat redistributed. They are not separate systems. Whatever the plateau is — and I no longer think it is properly a landscape; I think it is a single very slow organism with mineral organs and animal cilia — it has been in this configuration for a very long time and will be in this configuration for a very long time more.

The rules, as best I can state them:

Nothing here was made. Nothing here grows. Nothing here arrives or departs. The heat is constant on the scale of a life and varies on the scale of an age. The engines run because running is what this rock does at this temperature, the way other rock cracks or flows. The animals are part of the running. A visitor is not part of anything and will be unaccounted for — neither welcomed nor refused, simply not factored. You can stand at the foot of a hammer and watch it fall past you for an hour and the cloud of small bright creatures will reorganize around you as if you were a new pillar, and after a day they will have incorporated you into their flight paths, and after a week the plateau will have made you, in some small way, into one of its own slow features.

It is not hostile. That is perhaps the strangest thing to report. A place this loud, this hot, this dense with unfamiliar life, this committed to a mechanical purpose no one can name — you would expect hostility, or at least indifference sharpened into menace. But the plateau is *calm.* It has been doing this forever. It will keep doing this. Your presence is a minor weather, and it has weathered worse.

You leave, eventually, by walking off the edge — the plateau is high, and the descent is long, and the sound of the hammers follows you down for a long time, fainter and fainter, until at some altitude you realize you cannot hear them anymore and that you have been listening for them, without meaning to, for hours.
