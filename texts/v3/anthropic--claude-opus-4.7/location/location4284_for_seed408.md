# The Undergarden

It begins as a stairway — or what was once a stairway, now more a worn chute of packed clay and flagstones rubbed smooth by centuries of passage — descending from some upper world that, down here, no one mentions. The stairs arrive at a wooden door set in a wall of fitted stone. The door is ajar. Beyond it, the Undergarden.

The ceiling is perhaps four meters at its highest, vaulted in a patient brick arch the color of old terracotta. The vault was laid by hands, course by course, and you can still see the thumb-presses at the corners of certain bricks where the makers steadied the clay before firing. Between the bricks, fine roots have worked their way in — not destructively, more like long tenants who have come to an understanding with the masonry. The mortar is powdery where they touch it, but nothing falls.

There is no water here. None. That is the first strange thing, because the place feels like it should drip — the density of leaves, the warmth, the slight give of the earth underfoot all suggest a greenhouse or a jungle floor. But the ground is dry mineral loam, warm to the hand, and the plants do not drink. They feed on something else. Light, certainly — the light is the next strange thing — but also, it seems, on the warmth itself, and on a fine powdery substance that drifts down from small vents set into the vault at regular intervals. A pale gold dust. Where it lands on a leaf it is absorbed within a few hours, leaving the leaf a touch darker, a touch thicker.

The light comes from the walls. Not lanterns — the walls themselves. Set into the brickwork at shoulder height are plates of a yellow-white mineral, each about the size of a dinner platter, and these glow steadily with the flat, even brightness of a clouded noon. Someone placed them there. Someone quarried them, shaped them, mortared them in. The plates are warm to the touch but not hot. They do not flicker. They have presumably been glowing for a very long time, because the moss that grows on the brickwork just above each plate has formed into a shelf-shape, thick and green, the colony shaped by the decades of rising heat.

The vegetation fills the chamber almost entirely. There are perhaps only narrow winding paths between the growths, paths maintained — and this becomes clear quickly — by the fauna. Everywhere, leaves: broad waxy fronds the color of jade, ferny sprays that hang from cracks in the vault, bracket-like fungi stacked up the columns, and a kind of succulent that grows in rosettes along the floor, each rosette the size of a hand, each leaf bead-shaped and slightly translucent. The succulents are important. They are what hold the place's water-that-isn't-water — a clear thick sap, more like honey than like rain, which the animals lick from cracked leaves.

The animals are everywhere, but quiet.

This is the thing one notices next: the density. A small beetle with an iridescent carapace the color of gasoline is climbing the frond nearest you. Above it, on the underside of another leaf, a pale gecko-thing with too many toes is asleep. In the loam, small excavations — the burrows of something rabbit-sized, judging by the worn smoothness of the entryways. In the brickwork, swallow-shaped nests, though the birds that come and go from them are not swallows; they have long membranous tails and they do not sing, they click softly, like fingernails tapped on glass. A colony of bees — or the local equivalent, fat and slow and hairless, feeding on the gold dust rather than on flowers — drifts in a loose cloud near the ceiling, and their hum is the loudest sound in the whole room, and it is not loud.

The ecology has settled. You can see it in how the paths curve around the larger plants rather than through them, in how the droppings of the rabbit-things are deposited in specific middens that the beetles harvest, in how the gold dust never quite accumulates because something — many somethings — is always moving through it. Nothing is new. Nothing is collapsing. The place has been like this for a long time, finding its shape slowly, the way a river stone finds its shape, and now it simply is what it is.

There are signs of tending. Not recent — or not obviously recent — but unmistakable. Small clay pots, hand-thrown, sit at intervals along the paths, some holding the gold dust, some holding dried seed. A rake of bone and wood leans against a column, its handle polished black with use. Near one wall, a low bench, also of wood, and on the bench a folded cloth and a knife with a chipped obsidian blade. Whoever tends this garden has not tended it today. Perhaps not this week. But they will come back. The knife is oiled. The cloth is clean.

The room is not large. You could walk its length in thirty paces if the paths allowed, which they don't. It is a single chamber, a pocket, a held breath beneath whatever is above. There may be other chambers — there is a second door at the far end, smaller than the first, framed in darker wood — but this one is complete in itself. A world in a room. A warm, dry, green, crowded, quiet room, lit by stone, fed by dust, kept by someone, inhabited by many, very old and very much alive.

If you stand still for a while, the gecko-thing on the frond opens one eye and regards you, unconcerned, and closes it again. The bees drift. The glow plates hum, almost below hearing. Somewhere in the loam a small creature turns in its sleep and shifts a pebble, and the pebble clicks against another pebble, and that is the loudest thing that happens for a long time.
