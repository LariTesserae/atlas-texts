# The Upper Foundries of Hesh

At this altitude the air is thin enough that the light has nowhere to settle, so it keeps moving — sliding across the copper-sheeted rooftops, pooling briefly in the gutters, then lifting off again. The city is bright the way a lantern is bright: from within its own materials, not from any sun you could point to. There is a sun, technically. It hangs at roughly ten o'clock in a sky the color of wet slate. But it does not seem to be the source of the brightness. The brightness is older than the sun here, or unrelated to it, or — this is the thing you learn after a while — simply one of the city's outputs. The foundries make light the way foundries elsewhere make pig iron, and it is vented upward in slow pale columns that sheet against the underside of the clouds and diffuse.

Everything underfoot is built. There is no earth. Where you might expect a street to end in a patch of soil or a weedy lot, instead it ends in another manufactured thing: a grating, a riveted plate, a floor of hammered zinc that rings softly under your boots and then stops ringing, as if the sound is being collected somewhere. The city sits on terraces cut — no, *cast* — into the flank of a mountain that is itself probably not a mountain. The rock, where you can glimpse it between structures, has the glassy conchoidal fracture of something that cooled fast. Young geology. Or young architecture pretending to geology. It is hard to tell and the distinction gets thinner the longer you look.

The density is the first thing that presses on you. Buildings are not arranged along streets so much as *packed* — flue against flue, gantry threaded through gantry, a catwalk from one chimney passing through the open mouth of another's bell-housing. You can walk for an hour through Hesh without ever once touching a surface that was meant to be walked on; you simply pick your way across whatever is there. Pipes lean on pipes. A boiler the size of a barn shares a wall with a boiler the size of a house which shares a wall with a boiler the size of a kettle, and all three are warm, and all three are humming at frequencies too low to hear but low enough to feel in the sternum.

And yet it is *quiet*. This is what undoes visitors. A city this built, this industrial, should be a hammering din, but the foundries of Hesh run almost silently. You hear the hush of convection. You hear a distant tapping that might be a regulator or might be a bird. You hear your own breath, which is working harder than you expected because of the altitude. The machines have learned, over their long slow centuries, to do their work without advertising it. When something does make noise — a release valve, a tram-bell — the sound carries startlingly far across the terraces and people two districts away pause and look up.

Water is scarce and prized and everywhere in small amounts. It beads on the cold outer skins of the cooling towers and runs down in thin threads to be caught in copper gutters and routed inward. It condenses on the inside of the great glass flues when the light-columns cool, and drips, pock, pock, pock, into basins cut for exactly that purpose. There is one river, the Esh, which enters the city through an aqueduct at the high end and leaves through another at the low end, and in between it is divided and redivided into a thousand channels so narrow a child could step over them. You are never more than a few meters from water here, but you are rarely near enough to cup a hand in it.

The vegetation is sparse and specific. Lichens the color of old brass grow on the north faces of the chimneys. In the warm updraft of certain ventilation shafts, tufts of a grass with glassy black blades have taken hold; the locals call it *stovehair* and leave it alone. On certain terraces there are gardens, but the gardens are small and formal and grow things chosen for their tolerance of metal: a kind of rhubarb with leaves like hammered tin, a creeping vine whose flowers are, genuinely, small bells that ring when the wind is right. Nobody prunes these. They have been the size they are for a long time.

The fauna, by contrast, are everywhere. This is the part that takes adjusting to. Pigeons, of course — but the pigeons here are iridescent in a way that suggests they have been roosting in the light-flues, and when one takes off from a ledge it leaves a faint afterimage, a lag of a quarter-second during which the bird is in two places. Cats move between the hot pipes. There are the *clinkerhounds*, lean dogs bred or shaped by the city to scavenge slag; they are gentle and they cough. In the gutters there are small silver fish that should not be able to survive in water this cold and this mineral but do, evidently, because there they are. Above all there are the moths. At dusk, when the light-columns shift frequency and the whole city seems to lean half a degree, the moths come out by the thousand and fly in slow wide spirals around the chimneys, and their wings are patterned with what from a distance look like schematic diagrams.

This is where the weirdness, which has been lapping at the edges of the description, has to be admitted head-on.

Hesh does not quite obey. Distances are mostly stable but not always; a street you walked yesterday is sometimes a block longer today, and nobody remarks on it because remarking on it is considered unlucky and also because, frankly, everyone has experienced it and there is nothing to say. The light-columns, if you watch one for long enough, seem to be *remembering* things — you will see, drifting up inside the pale shaft, shapes that resolve briefly into a face, a gesture, a piece of furniture, something from someone's life, not necessarily yours. The foundries are processing something other than ore. The older workers have a phrase: *the slow reduction*. They will not explain it. There are doors in Hesh that open onto other doors in Hesh, and this is understood as infrastructure, not magic; the city has plumbing for meaning the way other cities have plumbing for water.

The weather participates. Rain, when it comes, comes lightly and briefly and often arrives in only one district at a time, as if the clouds were being metered. Snow is rare and when it falls it falls slightly upward for the first few meters before the air remembers what it is supposed to be doing. Shadows are sometimes the wrong length. None of this is alarming to the residents. It is the texture of the place. A child born in Hesh grows up understanding that the world has joints, and that those joints occasionally flex.

And across all of this — the density, the brightness, the soft hum, the brass lichen, the delayed pigeons, the remembering light — everything moves *slowly*. This is the final parameter and maybe the defining one. Hesh is not frantic. Change comes across decades. A chimney is taken down over the course of a generation. The river shifts its channel perhaps once in a lifetime, and when it does the city simply re-plumbs itself, patient as a coral. The machines have been running at the same rate for longer than anyone can remember and they will run at that rate for longer than anyone will remember. Whatever the foundries are reducing, they are in no hurry to finish.

Standing on one of the upper terraces, looking out across the packed copper and iron and glass of the city as it falls away down the mountain in bright layered tiers, with the columns of pale light rising and the moths beginning their evening spirals and the Esh whispering through its thousand channels, you understand — not in words, but in the chest, where the sub-audible humming lives — that Hesh is not a place that was built. Hesh is a place that is *being* built, continuously, slowly, by itself, out of itself, for reasons that are its own.

And the lichen grows. And the bells ring when the wind is right. And somewhere a valve opens, somewhere else a valve closes, and the light goes on being made.
