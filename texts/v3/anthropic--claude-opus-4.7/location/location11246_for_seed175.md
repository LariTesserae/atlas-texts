There is a shelf of mountain — not high enough to be cold, not low enough to be forgotten — where the rock has been weeping for so long it has forgotten how to be dry. Call it a terrace, though no one terraced it. The world did. A long patient pressing of water against limestone until the stone gave up its hardness in slow drips and grew, instead, a kind of soft openness: pocked, runneled, scalloped into basins that hold themselves like cupped palms.

The terrace is perhaps two hundred paces across. You could walk its boundary in the time it takes for a thought to complete itself. Beyond its edges the mountain falls away into mist that never quite clears and never quite condenses — a warm white breathing that suggests other terraces below, other shelves, but does not confirm them.

The basins hold water. Each basin is its own small decision the rock made, ages ago, about where to be hollow. The water in them is warm — not hot, not cool — body-warm, the temperature of something alive that isn't there. The largest basin is wide enough to lie down in. The smallest would barely hold a closed fist. There are perhaps thirty of them, scattered across the shelf with the kind of spacing that feels deliberate but isn't — the spacing of things that arrived at their positions through long argument with gravity and seep.

Between the basins, the vegetation. Here is where the place becomes itself.

Nothing here is quite a plant in the way plants are plants elsewhere. There are tall pale stalks, the color of the inside of a shell, that rise to about waist-height and end in a single flat leaf held horizontal like a small table. The leaves catch the condensing mist and channel it down hollow stems into the basins. They are doing the work of the rain, which never quite arrives but is always almost arriving. There are mossy cushions in the wetter places — these at least seem familiar — but they are warm to the touch, warmer than the air, as if they were running some slow internal fire. And there are the rope-things: vine-like growths that emerge from cracks in the limestone, drape themselves into basins, and seem to be drinking, though no one would say a plant drinks, exactly. They are the color of wet bone.

No animals. None. Not an insect, not a worm in the moss, not a shadow flickering in the water. The place has never known a thing that moved on its own behalf. This absence is the first thing the body notices and the last thing it accepts. The skin keeps expecting a fly. The ear keeps expecting a bird. Neither comes.

The sounds, then, are only the sounds water makes when left to itself: the smallest possible plink of a stalk-table tipping its accumulated mist into a basin. The slow gulp of a rope-thing shifting. The almost-inaudible hiss of warmth meeting cooler stone somewhere underneath. If you hold still, you can hear the terrace thinking in drips. The drips are not random. They have a rhythm, but it's a rhythm measured in the lengths of small lives — it takes a long held breath to know whether the next drip will fall before the breath ends.

The light is ordinary. This is perhaps the strangest thing. After everything — the warm water, the absent animals, the bone-vines — you expect the light to be wrong, to be greenish or to come from below or to flicker. It doesn't. It is the daylight of a slightly overcast afternoon, diffused through the mist, even and patient and entirely normal. It makes the strangeness of the rest feel deliberate, as if the place wanted you to see it clearly.

Now: the tools.

There are tools here. This is the contradiction in the description, and it is real. No structures, no walls, no shelter, no fire-ring, no path — but tools. Set down in careful arrangements on the flatter stones. Stone scrapers. A bone awl, though there is no animal here to have given the bone. A wooden haft with no head. A small clay bowl, sun-fired or fire-fired, no one to say. They are arranged as if someone had just stepped away.

But the moss has grown over the edges of the bowl. The haft has begun to soften where it touches the wet stone. The tools have been there long enough that the terrace is taking them back, slowly, into its grammar. They were not placed yesterday. They may not have been placed by anyone who would now recognize them as tools.

This is what the terrace is, in the end: a place that has been left, and a place that is being patient about it. The water keeps doing its work. The stalk-tables keep tipping. The basins keep their body-warmth, which comes from somewhere deep that the terrace does not need to explain. The tools sink, by fractions, into the moss.

There is a rule here, though no one wrote it: nothing hurries. The mist does not become rain. The water does not become steam. The vines do not become animals. The tools do not become artifacts of a known people. Everything is held just shy of its next state, and the holding is the place.

If you stayed, you would not be eaten — there is nothing to eat you. You would not starve immediately — the warm water and the rope-vines could probably be survived on, for a while. You would not be cold. You would not be loud. You would, eventually, set down whatever you carried, and find a flat stone, and arrange your things on it, and step away.

And the terrace would begin, with its long patience, to take them in.
