# The Cultivated Sky

It begins, if it begins anywhere, with the understanding that everything you can see was grown by hand, and nothing of it was grown recently.

---

The plateau sits high enough that clouds form *inside* it rather than above. You stand on what was once, perhaps, a mountain — but the mountain is no longer rock. The mountain is a single woven structure of living wood and pith and braided root-cable, raised over so many generations that the original stone has been entirely consumed, digested, replaced. The peak is hollow. The peak is a cathedral the size of a small country. And the cathedral is mostly empty air.

Walk its galleries and you walk on floors of plaited heartwood polished by centuries of bare feet, slick now with the constant condensation that drips from the green vaulting overhead. The wood is warm. It is always warm here — a damp, close warmth like the inside of a mouth — because the structure is alive and metabolizing slowly, and its slow metabolism is the climate.

Water beads on every surface. It runs in thin sheets down the inner walls, channeled by grooves that someone, long ago, carved into the living bark while it was still soft, knowing the tree would grow around the cut and keep the channel forever. The water tastes faintly of sap. It pools in basins that were grafted into being a thousand years before the current inhabitants were born.

---

There are inhabitants. Not many.

A figure passes you in one of the lower galleries — too far to call to, a half-kilometer down the curving floor, lit by the dim green that filters through the canopy-ceiling. They carry a hooked tool of bone and braided fiber. They are tending something. They will tend it for a week, and then walk for another week to tend the next thing. The plateau is vast enough that a person's life-work might be the maintenance of a single grove of pillar-trees, a single weeping junction, a single sluice in a single wall.

You could go a month here without seeing another human. The silence is not the silence of absence — it is the silence of a place too large to fill. Footsteps don't echo; the wooden vaults swallow sound. When someone sings, the song travels perhaps thirty meters and then is absorbed into the wet green, gone, as if the structure itself were listening and keeping it.

---

The fauna are everywhere and you mostly do not see them.

Pale slow things move in the upper canopy — long-limbed, patient, the color of lichen, with eyes that reflect no light because there is little light to reflect. They eat the fruiting bodies that emerge from the wall-wood in season. They are not hunted. Nothing here hunts them, because the entire ecosystem has settled into a kind of agreement so old it has become physics.

In the wet seams of the floor, small bright animals — frog-like, or perhaps something else that has decided to occupy the frog-shape — move between pools. They are loud only once a year, in a brief week, and then the silence resumes.

Birds, if they are birds, fly through the hollow interior in great looping migrations that take days to complete a single circuit. The cathedral is large enough that their flight has weather. They ride thermals generated by the metabolism of the walls.

---

Here is where it becomes difficult to describe.

The plateau is old enough, and woven tightly enough, and grown with enough intention, that **it has begun to think in the way that old places think** — which is to say, the rules have softened. A corridor you walked yesterday is longer today, because the structure dreamed in the night and the dream extended it. The pillars in the great hollow do not always agree on how many of them there are. Seven, when you enter. Nine, when you turn. Seven again, but one of them is now behind you, and you did not pass it.

The inhabitants accept this. Their tools are primitive — bone, fiber, fire-hardened wood, a little hammered copper — because complex tools would require a stable world to be useful in, and the world here is not stable in that sense. It is stable in a different sense: it is reliably itself, reliably strange, reliably warm and wet and slow.

There is a room, somewhere in the upper galleries, where water falls upward. The inhabitants do not consider this remarkable. They have a word for it that simply means *that room*. A child is taught: do not drink from the upward water, it forgets which direction it is going and so do you.

There is a gallery where your own footsteps arrive before you take them — a soft tapping a moment ahead, walking the path you are about to walk. You can follow yourself. You can choose not to.

There is, reportedly, a place near the heart of the structure where the wood remembers being stone, and if you lay your hand against it you feel a great cold weight for a moment, the memory of a mountain, before the wall remembers itself again and is warm.

---

Weather happens inside.

Mist rises from the floor pools in the morning — if morning means anything here, where the light is always dim and green and filtered. The mist climbs the galleries, condenses against the high vaulting, and falls back as a slow indoor rain that lasts for hours. The inhabitants do not shelter from it. They were born wet. Their hair, their skin, the woven fiber of their clothing, all carry a permanent damp that is not uncomfortable, only constant.

Sometimes, rarely, a wind moves through the hollow — a real wind, with weather behind it, generated by some pressure differential between one chamber and another a hundred kilometers away. When this wind passes, the whole structure creaks: a low, long, wooden groan that is the closest thing to a loud sound this place produces. It can take an hour for the groan to travel from one end of a gallery to the other. Children are told it is the cathedral turning over in its sleep.

---

Nothing here is new. Nothing here was ever not made. The distinction between architecture and forest collapsed so many centuries ago that the inhabitants have no separate words for them. The verb they use for *to build* is the same as the verb for *to wait*, and both are conjugated like the verb for *to grow*.

They are not building anything now. The building was finished long before any of them were born. They are only — and this is their entire civilization, their entire purpose, their entire quiet competent life — they are only keeping it alive. Tending. Pruning a stray runner. Clearing a clogged sluice. Singing the short songs that, they believe, the walls prefer to hear.

The walls may or may not prefer to hear them. The walls are very old and have begun to dream, and what an old dreaming place wants is not a thing a person can easily know.

But the songs continue. And the water beads. And the pale animals move in the canopy. And somewhere, a corridor decides, gently, to be a little longer tomorrow than it was today.
