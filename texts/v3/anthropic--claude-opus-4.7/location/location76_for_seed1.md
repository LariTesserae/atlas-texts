# The Rust-Marsh

You come to it across a flatness so old it has forgotten what it used to be. Once, maybe, this was mountains. Now it is a shallow pan the width of a country, tilted by some fraction of a degree, and everything that was ever lifted has been carried here by water that no longer remembers moving.

The first thing is the sound. Before you see anything properly you hear it: a vast, layered pounding — the slow four-count of enormous pistons somewhere below the waterline, and above that a higher clatter, irregular, metallic, like a thousand looms running out of phase. Threaded through both, a wet sucking, and the grinding of gear teeth the size of houses turning underwater. The air shakes in your chest. You feel the bass in your sternum before your ears find it.

There is no factory. There has never been a factory. No one built any of this.

---

The marsh is iron-colored and black and, in some lights, the deep violet of a bruise healing. Water stands everywhere, ankle-deep, knee-deep, skin-smooth in the wider basins, broken elsewhere by the backs of the engines. They surface in groups. A flywheel the size of a millstone breaking the meniscus, turning, submerging again. A bank of reciprocating rods pumping in a reed-bed that has no reeds — only the rods themselves, upright, greased with something that smells of wet copper and lightning. Boilers bubble, though nothing heats them; the water around them is cold enough to numb a hand in under a minute. Steam rises anyway, and the steam is where the light comes from. Everything here is lit by the exhaust of machines that are not burning anything, a dim pearl-grey luminescence that makes every surface look slightly damp, because every surface *is* slightly damp.

There is no sun. There may never have been a sun. The sky, if it is a sky, is a low ceiling of condensing cloud that drips continuously, a fine rain that is more like sweat, and the cloud is not above the machines so much as produced by them, their breath pooling against some invisible lid and falling back.

---

Nothing grows. The parameter for plants is almost zero and the landscape honors this — no moss, no algae, no scum on the standing water. But the density is wrong for a dead world. The place is *packed*. Every square meter holds something.

The fauna is the weirdness, and the weirdness is everything.

There are creatures here that have adapted to, or emerged from, or always-were the machinery. Low in the water, shoaling around the pistons, are things like flatfish made of hammered tin, their edges scalloped where the metal has been worked against itself for epochs. They feed — if that is the word — on the vibration. They press their bellies to the working rods and shiver in sympathy and grow a little. Their eyes are ball bearings. When one dies it sinks and is absorbed back into the rod it fed on; the rod gets imperceptibly thicker.

Higher up, clinging to the flywheels, herds of small six-legged things that look spun from steel wool and wet fur simultaneously — you cannot decide which, and looking harder makes the undecidability worse, not better. They graze on the grease. They are warm. They are the warmest thing here; you could cup one in your hands and stop shivering. They will not let you. They boil away into a cloud of needles when approached and reform further along the wheel.

And then the larger animals, which are where the reality breakdown gets honest about itself. A thing the size of a draft horse wades through the shallows on legs that are each a different machine: a piston leg, a clockwork leg, a leg that is a column of falling water held in the shape of a leg. Its body is a furnace-box with no fire in it. It has a face. The face is calm. It turns to watch you and you understand, with a certainty that does not belong to you, that it has been watching you for longer than you have been alive, and that the watching happened before the seeing. Causality is thin here. Things echo before they happen. You hear the splash of its next step two seconds before it takes the step, and then again as it takes it, and the two splashes do not quite agree on where its foot landed.

---

The erosion is the key, I think. Everything here is *worn*. The engines are smoothed to the point where their function is almost geological — you cannot tell anymore which surfaces were meant to meet which, which gears drive which. They have been running so long that they have sanded themselves into their neighbors. Housings have fused. Pipes have grown into each other like old tree roots, except there are no trees and never were. What's left is the motion itself, preserved in whatever substrate will carry it: in the iron, in the water, in the animals, in the rhythm your own heartbeat falls into within about ten minutes of arrival and cannot be shaken from for hours after you leave.

The place is in its final form. This is what I mean by ancient. Not ruined — ruins imply a falling-off from a peak. The Rust-Marsh has arrived. Whatever it was becoming, it has become. It could run like this, in this exact configuration of pound and drip and shoal and graze, for another hundred million years, and probably has, and probably will.

---

The rules, as best I can reconstruct them:

Water does not freeze here, though it is cold enough to. The machines forbid it, somehow; the vibration keeps the molecules from settling into lattice.

Sounds sometimes come before their causes, and sometimes long after. A hammer blow you hear at dusk may be struck at dawn. You learn to stop flinching.

Nothing rusts further. The rust that is here is the rust that will always be here. Corrosion has reached equilibrium with whatever slow counter-process the animals or the water perform, and the balance is exact.

If you stand still too long, a thin film of the pearl-grey light collects on your skin, and the six-legged things come to graze on it. This is not dangerous. It is, possibly, the closest thing to welcome the place offers.

No one made this. That is the part that will not stop bothering you, later, when you are somewhere warm and trying to describe it. Industry without industrialists. Production without product. An entire working world, saturated with labor, and no hand has ever touched a tool here, because the tools are the world, and the world has been running itself, alone, in the cold dim wet, for longer than your species has had a word for *machine*.
