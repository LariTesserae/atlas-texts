# The Long Shallows

The land here has forgotten how to be tall. Whatever mountains once stood have worn themselves down across such an expanse of time that even the memory of their slopes has gone soft — you can feel it underfoot as a kind of long, barely-there tilt, less a hill than a suggestion that somewhere, very far off, the ground was once higher.

What remains is a flood-plain that stretches for a day's walking in any direction, and it is mostly water.

Not a lake, not a river — a shallows. The water sits in a vast lattice of pools and channels and sheeting films, most of it no deeper than a knee, some of it only a glaze across mud. In places the pools connect into slow-moving threads that braid and unbraid over the course of seasons. In other places the water is simply *there*, held in a basin scooped by ten million rainfalls, warmed through by a sun that seems to hang a little heavier than it should.

The mud beneath is old. That is the first real thing to say about this place. The clay has been lying here being clay for longer than most landscapes have existed. It is deep, it is smooth, it is the color of wet bronze where the sun strikes it and almost black where the reeds shade it. When you press your hand into it, it closes around your fingers with the unhurried confidence of something that has been pressed into and released again by uncountable feet, beaks, bellies, roots.

---

Vegetation is sparse but emphatic. The plants that have chosen this place are specialists. Stands of a tall jointed reed grow in loose archipelagos, rising eight or ten feet and leaning into each other at the tops to make pale corridors. Between the reed-stands, the open water is dotted with the flat leaves of something lily-like, and occasionally a single, thick-trunked tree — stunted, wide-crowned, its bark grown shaggy with epiphytes — stands alone on a hummock of slightly higher ground, visible from half a mile off because there is nothing else its size for half a mile.

There is a great deal of empty water between these presences. That emptiness is part of the place. You could walk for twenty minutes through ankle-deep warmth and pass nothing taller than your shin, and then arrive at a single reed-island full of life.

---

The air is warm and thick with water that hasn't quite decided to be mist. Breathe deeply and you can taste the marsh on the back of your tongue — iron, green rot, something faintly sweet like pollen or fruit skin. The sun gets through without obstruction, and the light off the water is the dominant fact of the sky: everything above is bright, diffuse, slightly hazed, and everything below doubles it back.

It is loud.

This is the thing visitors never expect. The shallows roar. Not with water — the water moves too slowly to make noise — but with life. Frogs in their tens of thousands, in chorus layers: the deep bass ones that sound like someone striking a hollow log, the middle ones that trill, the small high ones that sound almost metallic. Insects in continuous sheets of sound, rising and falling with the time of day. Birds — long-legged waders that crack open the air with single harsh cries, and smaller ones in the reeds that chitter constantly. Fish breaking the surface. Something large moving through water a hundred yards off, its passage a slow rhythmic *shush-shush-shush*.

At dusk the sound doubles. At noon, in the hottest part, it thins to just the insects and the occasional wet slap of something feeding, and this relative quiet is itself a kind of loudness — you become aware of the size of the silence the insects are filling.

---

The animals do not crowd. They distribute. Because the place is so open and so old, every creature here has long since worked out its territory, its hour, its depth. A heron-like bird stands utterly still in a channel for an hour at a time and then stabs once. A family of some otter-shaped thing passes through and is not seen again for days. Turtles sun themselves on logs so weathered that the logs themselves seem part of the turtles. There are large animals — you can tell from the paths pressed into the reeds, from prints in the clay as wide as a dinner plate — but they move through at their own pace and on their own errands, and mostly what you see of them is absence: trampled ground, a cloud of disturbed birds rising half a mile off, a low sound that might be breath or might be the settling of mud.

Nothing here is in a hurry. The dynamic of the place is measured in seasons and centuries. Water levels shift across months. Reed-islands migrate across decades. The clay accumulates and compacts across ages. A frog's whole urgent life is a small bright flare inside a landscape that is essentially *holding still.*

---

There are no paths, because a path implies that one route is better than another, and here all routes are equally wet and equally warm and equally long. There are no structures. There is nothing made. If a traveler passed through a hundred years ago and a hundred years from now, the place would not have noticed either of them.

The rule of this world, if it has one, is: *everything settles.* Everything here has already become what it is going to be. The hills have flattened. The river has spread. The mud has found its level. The living things inside this stillness are loud and numerous and busy, but they are busy inside a frame that is not going anywhere. They are the foreground noise of a landscape that finished its real work a very long time ago and is now simply continuing, warm and wet and wide, under a high bright sky.
