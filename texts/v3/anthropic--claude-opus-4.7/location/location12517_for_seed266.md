# The Cinder Tier

At this altitude the air thins to a clean, warm nothing — warm in the way of stone left in afternoon sun, not warm like fire. The Tier is a single immense plateau, or what was once a plateau and has since become a continent of its own at the wrong height for one. You stand on it and the curve of the horizon is wrong; you can see the bend, and you can see the lower world dropping away from the cliff-edges in stepped lacquered terraces of basalt and rust-colored slag, falling for what must be twenty kilometers before haze swallows the rest.

There is no water here, and there has not been water in a span of time long enough that the word has lost its grip on the place. What there is instead: **sift**. A fine warm mineral dust, the color of old brass, that moves the way water once moved — pooling in low spots, drying into crusts on the windward sides of things, occasionally running in slow rivulets down a slope when something disturbs it. The sift carries a faint static charge. It clings to skin in a way that isn't unpleasant, and at night, if you brush your arm, small sparks the color of weak embers travel up toward your shoulder and disappear.

Nothing grows. Nothing has grown. But the Tier is not empty.

---

The **city** — there is only one, though it sprawls — is called nothing, because the people who built it left, and the people who live in it now did not arrive with names for things. It runs along the spine of the plateau for perhaps four hundred kilometers, never more than a few streets wide, hugging the ridge the way lichen would hug a branch in a world that had lichen. The buildings are made of cast slag and a dark sintered ceramic, fused in place by the **furnaces** that still run beneath them. The furnaces are the reason the Tier is warm. They have been running since before the city's founders left, and no one has found their off-switch, or looked very hard for it.

The architecture is industrial in the patient sense: tall chimneys, catwalks, gear-housings the size of cathedrals, hoppers that once accepted ore from cable-cars that no longer arrive. Most of it is not in use. Maybe one structure in five hums. The rest stands cooling at the rate of geology, weathered to a soft matte finish by ten thousand years of sift-wind, edges rounded, bolts welded to their housings by oxidation. Walking through the city is like walking through a museum that has forgotten it is a museum.

The streets are wide and mostly empty. Sound does not carry well — the dust eats it. You hear your own footsteps and the deep, slow respiration of the furnaces, a sound felt more than heard, a sub-bass that comes up through the soles of your boots. Occasionally a relay clicks somewhere, or a flake of metal falls from a high gantry and rings once before the dust catches it.

---

The **fauna** is what makes the Tier strange.

There are creatures here, many of them, and they are abundant in the way that birds are abundant in a forest — present everywhere, just not crowded. They are called, collectively and unhelpfully, **the warm things**. They do not eat, because there is nothing to eat. They subsist on the heat differential between the furnace-warmed ground and the cooler air a few meters above it. They are flat, mostly, broad and thin like manta rays or sheets of leather, and they drape themselves over warm surfaces in the morning and lift, slow as kites, in the afternoon when convection picks up. Their undersides are mineral, hard and bright; their topsides are something softer, the texture of felt, the color of the sift.

They have no faces in the conventional sense, but they have a kind of attention. When you walk near one, it shifts. When you stop, it stops. They do not seem to fear people and they do not seem to want anything from people. In the old factory halls, where the furnaces are hottest, you find them stacked in lazy quiet piles, six or seven deep, breathing — yes, breathing — in unison, lifting and settling on the warm floor with a sound like a slow page turning.

There are other things too. Small skittering ones with too many legs that live in the dust and surface only when sparked by it. Long things in the chimneys that may be a single animal or a kind of colony — opinion in the city differs. Something very large is said to live under one of the deeper furnace-pits, and the people who say so are not given to invention, but no one has seen it, only heard its weight shift, once or twice in a generation.

---

The **rules** of the Tier are few and have settled into themselves like everything else.

Heat is shared. No one owns a furnace; you live near one or you don't. The sift is brushed from the thresholds every morning, but never swept away — it is returned to the street, because the wind will move it where it wants to go, and resisting this is considered both impractical and impolite.

The warm things are not harmed, and in return, they tolerate the presence of people on surfaces they would otherwise have draped. This is the closest thing the Tier has to a treaty, and it has held for as long as anyone remembers.

Nothing is in a hurry. The furnaces run on a cycle of decades — bright phases and dim phases — and the city's rhythms follow. During a bright phase, more of the old machinery is woken; small industries flicker into being, last a generation, and lapse. During a dim phase, people sleep more, talk less, and the warm things stack themselves deeper and become so still they are sometimes mistaken for furniture.

---

Above it all, the sky is a high warm bronze. The sun, when it crosses, looks slightly too small, which may be a trick of the altitude or may be true. At night, the stars are very clear and the city's few lit windows form a long thin constellation along the ridge, mirrored faintly by the static-flicker of the sift moving in the streets.

The Tier is not going anywhere. It has been here, in roughly this condition, for longer than its inhabitants have stories. It will be here, in roughly this condition, for longer than its inhabitants will have descendants. It is a place that has finished becoming itself, and is now simply, slowly, warmly, being.
