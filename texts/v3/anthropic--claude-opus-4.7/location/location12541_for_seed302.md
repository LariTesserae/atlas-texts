# The Resonant Pans

Begin high. You stand somewhere near three thousand meters, but there is no peak here, no summit shape — instead a vast inclined shelf of pale stone, tilted just slightly, extending farther than the eye accepts. The horizon is not a horizon. It is a brightness.

The stone underfoot is the first thing to understand. It is not quite limestone, not quite gypsum — call it a calcareous skin laid down over uncountable seasons of mineral-rich seepage. Where water has run, it has run in the same channels for so long that the channels are no longer cut into the rock; they *are* the rock, fluted and lipped, scalloped at every scale from fingernail to footbath to wading-pool. The whole shelf is a frozen drainage pattern. Wet pale stone, blinding under the sun.

And the sun. The light here is the second thing. There is no atmospheric softness — the air at this altitude is thin and the stone is white and the wetness on the stone is a million small mirrors, so the brightness comes from above and below at once. You squint constantly. Color has been bleached out of everything that might have had color. Even your own hands look like bone.

Now: the water. It is everywhere and it is shallow. Films of it standing in every scalloped basin, sheets of it sliding across the inclined flats, springlines weeping from the slight risings where the shelf folds against itself. Nothing pools deeply. Nothing rushes. The water arrives from below — the rock is saturated, the water table is at the surface — and it leaves by evaporation, lifting back into the dazzling air. It has been doing this for as long as anything has done anything here. There is no flood season and no drought. The flow yesterday matched the flow tomorrow.

No plants. None. Not a lichen smear, not an algal slick — the water is too mineral, too alkaline, too clean of the things that life requires. The surfaces are sterile and white. This is what gives the place its strange unbroken brilliance: there is no green tissue, no brown stem, no dark leaf to absorb the light. Everything either reflects or transmits.

The third thing — the thing you should have noticed first but couldn't, because the brightness occupies the eye so completely — is the sound.

The sound is enormous.

It is not one sound. It is the sum of perhaps ten million small water-sounds happening within earshot, all at once, all the time, every drip and trickle and sheet-slip and basin-fill summed into a single roaring whiteness that has the texture of static and the volume of a waterfall heard from very close. You cannot speak over it. You would not be heard. Your own voice in your skull sounds thin and far away. Each individual sound is tiny — a tap, a tick, a small running gurgle — but density does the work that magnitude would do elsewhere. Packed, saturated, no silence anywhere between the small sounds. They tile.

And because nothing changes — because the flow rates are in perfect long-balance with the seepage and the evaporation — the sound does not vary. It is the same sound it was an hour ago. The same it will be at sundown, if sundown comes. A standing wave of noise.

This is the strangeness of the place, the small wrongness: nothing here is *happening*, exactly. Everything is in motion — water moving, light pouring, sound roaring — but no state is changing. It is a landscape held in its own continuous tense. The fluting on the rock will not deepen this year because it has already reached the shape water carves at this flow rate. The basins will not overspill because they overspill constantly, at the rate water arrives. Equilibrium dressed up as activity.

Fauna: rare, and strange when found. A pale flatworm-thing in one of the basins, translucent, no eyes (no need for eyes in this brightness; no need for eyes if nothing ever changes to be seen). A cluster of small calcified shells in a sheltered scallop, possibly inhabited, possibly not — you cannot tell, and they do not move while you watch, and you cannot watch forever. Whatever lives here lives slowly, or lives so embedded in the equilibrium that it does not register as alive. Perhaps something feeds on the dissolved minerals directly. Perhaps nothing feeds at all and these are only the husks of older arrangements.

No structures. No marks. No paths worn by feet, because feet have not come, or have come so seldom that the rock has dissolved their traces back into its own slow weeping. If you leave a footprint in a damp basin, it will be gone by the time you turn around — not eroded, exactly, but rewetted, resurfaced, returned to the standard state. The landscape does not accept change. It folds change back into itself.

To stand here is to be acoustically pinned and optically overwhelmed and to feel, after some minutes, a particular kind of dizziness — not vertigo from the height, but something subtler: the dizziness of being inside a system that does not include you as a variable. You are loud, you are dark, you cast a shadow, you displace water — and none of it matters. The pans absorb your presence the way they absorb everything: by remaining themselves.

If you walked for a day in any direction, you would find more of the same. Slight folds. Slight risings. The same fluted skin, the same shallow sliding water, the same roar. Somewhere, very far, the shelf must end — must drop or rise or meet something else — but from any given point within it, the world is only this: white stone, thin water, white light, white noise, and the long unmoving balance of all of them together.

A place that has finished becoming what it is.
