# The Bell-Foundry at Karsh-Velim

It sits on a shoulder of red rock so high the air thins to a hot whisper, and you can see, on clear days, nothing — because there are no clear days here, only the dim copper haze that hangs against the cliffs like a held breath. The sun is somewhere above the haze. It warms the stone. It does not show itself.

The place is a single courtyard, no wider than a granary, walled on three sides by structures so ancient their original geometry has slumped into something more like geology than architecture. Once they were towers — kilns, perhaps, or annealing ovens; the openings at their bases are still mouth-shaped, still sooted. Now they are dunes of fired brick, settled into their angle of repose over what must be tens of thousands of years. The fourth side opens onto the drop, and the drop opens onto nothing in particular.

And the courtyard is packed. Packed with bells.

They hang on frames of bronze-pinned cedar so old the wood has fossilized in place, gone the color and density of horn. They hang from each other. They hang in tiers, in clusters, in nested rings; small ones inside larger ones inside larger ones still, the way certain seed-pods nest, the way certain prayers do. There is no room to walk between them. To enter the courtyard you would have to push bells aside with your shoulders, and to push bells aside is to ring them, and to ring them — 

The bells are ringing. They have always been ringing.

This is the rule of the place: the bells do not stop. There is no wind here strong enough to start them, and yet they sound. Perhaps the thin warm air conducts some tremor from deep in the mountain. Perhaps the bells ring each other, each one's tone shivering the next into voice, a closed circuit of sympathy that began when the last founder set down his hammer and walked away into the haze, and that has not paused for a single instant since. The sound is enormous. It is the entire weather of the place. It enters the body through the sternum and the soles of the feet before the ears understand what is happening, and once inside it stays — a coppery, layered, chord-on-chord-on-chord that has no melody because melody requires change, and nothing here changes.

If you listen long enough — and you would have to listen for hours, because the surface of the sound is so dense — you begin to hear that the chord is the same chord. The same exact chord. The bells have found their equilibrium. Every overtone that wanted to fade has faded, every resonance that wanted to dominate has been answered by its opposite, and what is left is a sound that has solved itself. It is the tone a world makes after it has finished having anything to say.

There is no life. No lichen on the bell-rims, though the warmth and the still air would seem to invite it — perhaps the constant vibration shakes spores loose before they can settle. No insects. No birds; a bird that flew into this sound would forget the shape of its own wings. The only thing resembling growth is a pale mineral bloom on the older bronzes, a slow patina that has, over the millennia, reached its final color and stopped.

The light is the dim, even, sourceless light of the copper haze, and it does to the bells what the sound does to the air: it equalizes them. There are no shadows in the courtyard, only gradations of dimness inside the throats of the bells, where the dark is older than the dark of caves.

Whoever made this place worked with hand-tools. You can see the hammer-marks on the bell-shoulders, the file-scores along the lips, the places where a thumb pressed wax into a mold-seam. Primitive work, in the sense that nothing here required a machine — but patient beyond reckoning. Each bell was tuned to its neighbors by ear, by a craftsman standing in the courtyard with the previous bells already ringing, adding one more voice to a chord that was being composed across generations. The last bell, the one that closed the chord — it must be in here somewhere, indistinguishable now from the others, the keystone of a vault of sound.

And then the founders left, or finished, or died into the work; and the chord went on without them.

It is warm. It is loud past any human threshold. It is small enough to cross in twelve strides, if you could cross it. It is as old as anything that was ever made by hands, and it is doing, still, exactly what it was made to do, which was: to hold one note, forever, against the silence of a high place.
