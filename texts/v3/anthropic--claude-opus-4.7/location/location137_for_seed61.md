# The Drippings

You reach it by following the sound. Even from a hundred paces up the tunnel, there is a muttering in the limestone — not a roar but a constant collective speaking, water on water, water on stone, water on tin, water on the upturned bottom of a clay bowl. It does not crescendo as you approach. It simply is. Whoever lives here has been living inside this sound for so long that their ears have stopped bringing it forward.

The cavern is small — you could cross it in forty paces if you could cross it. You cannot; it is full.

The ceiling, perhaps eight or nine feet at its highest, is a forest of stalactites, each with a bead of water forever forming, falling, forming. There is no plant here — nothing green has ever happened in this room — but something has done the work plants do, which is to fill every available space with reaching shapes. The ceiling reaches down. The floor reaches up. And between them, the builders.

They have made a town out of catchments.

Every falling drip is accounted for. Beneath each stalactite hangs a vessel — clay cup, carved wooden bowl, hammered copper pan, split gourd, the halved shell of something that was once alive elsewhere and has been carried down here. The vessels hang from cords of braided hair and sinew and some pale fibrous stuff that might be bat-gut or might be older than that. They hang at different heights, tuned. A drip into the copper pan makes a bright *tink*. A drip into the deep clay pot makes a hollow *tok*. A drip into the half-gourd makes almost nothing, a whispered *pt*. Multiply this by the several thousand stalactites of the chamber and you have the sound: not chaos, because nothing about it changes, but a dense saturating weather of small percussions, tuned long ago and never retuned because nothing here requires retuning.

Below the vessels: the scaffolding. Ladders of knotted rope. Walkways of slate slabs laid on piled stones. Little rooms partitioned off by hanging hides, stacked three and four high against the damp walls, each room no larger than a closet. Troughs channel overflow from the higher vessels down to the lower, and from the lower down to the cistern-floor, which is itself a shallow lake the color of weak tea, walked across on stepping stones set so long ago their tops are worn into soft dishes.

The people — there are people — are small and pale and few. Perhaps thirty in the whole settlement. They move slowly because every movement must negotiate a hanging cup, a taut line, a sleeping neighbor. They wear cloaks of felted hair, always damp, and they speak in voices pitched under the drip-song, a register that has evolved specifically to slip between the *tinks* without colliding. To an outsider it sounds like they are not talking at all, and then suddenly you notice they have been talking the whole time.

Their tools are of bone, of chipped flint, of a soft copper they beat cold against anvil-stones. No fire down here — the air is too wet, and fire would disturb the equilibrium of condensation that keeps the vessels filling at their proper rates. They eat blind fish from the cistern, and a kind of cave-cricket that lives in the wall-cracks and can be coaxed out with patience, and a white jelly they scrape from certain weeping seams in the rock, which may be a fungus or may be a mineral doing an impression of life.

What is strange, and takes a while to notice: the vessels are not collected. The water that falls into the copper pan, the clay bowl, the gourd — it sits there. Evaporates a little. Is topped up by the next drip. The pan has been full for perhaps a thousand years. The people do not drink from the hung vessels. They drink from the floor-cistern, which fills itself from the overflow and from seeps in the wall.

The hung vessels are for the *sound*.

This is what took the outsider a long time to understand. The town is not a settlement that happens to be noisy. The town is an instrument, built and maintained and inherited, and the people are its tuners and its audience and — this is the part that is hard — its reason. The drip-song was here before them. They arrived, small and pale and curious, and found the cavern already speaking, and they began hanging things beneath the drips to hear what each drip wanted to say. Over generations the song became more itself. Each new generation is born into a sound that their great-grandparents helped shape, and they shape it no further, because it is already correct. A cup cracks, they replace it with an identical cup. A cord frays, they braid a new one of the same length.

Nothing changes. This is the achievement.

The dim light comes from a pale lichen-that-is-not-lichen growing on the upper walls, a slow green-gray luminescence that has also been the same brightness for as long as anyone remembers. It gives just enough light to see the wet gleam of the vessels, the glisten of the ropes, the soft shine of the cistern, the small movements of the small people among the small rooms.

There is a word they have, which is the word for the whole place and also the word for the sound and also, approximately, the word for *being alive*. It is spoken softly, on an in-breath, between two drips. You would not hear it if you were not listening for it.

You would not find this place if you were not already lost.
