# The Shallow Cathedral

The land here has forgotten how to be tall. Whatever mountains once stood have been patient with themselves for so long that they've become a wide, brimming plain — limestone worn down past the point of drama, polished into pale, low domes that breathe water from their pores. You could walk across this place in an hour and never climb more than the height of a kneeling person, but the walking would be slow, because everything stops you.

The water table sits just below the skin of the world. Where the limestone dips, it pools — clear, warm, sometimes the size of a washbasin, sometimes the size of a small room. The pools connect underground; if you watch one carefully, you'll see a leaf surface in another twenty meters away. The rock weeps. The air weeps back. Everything is slightly wet in the way that fruit is wet from the inside.

Warmth comes from below as much as above. The sun is high and white and reaches everything — there are no canopies dense enough to truly shade — but the limestone also holds a low heat, geothermal or older than geothermal, a settled warmth like a body that has been sleeping a long time and isn't going to wake.

Vegetation is generous but not crowded. Tree ferns stand alone, each one a small green explosion at the end of a soft trunk, with several meters of clear ground between neighbors. Cushions of moss the color of new copper. Orchids growing directly out of the wet rock, no soil needed, their roots flat against the stone like green handprints. A kind of low palm with translucent fronds that you can see the veins of when light passes through. Everything looks like it has been arranged, though nothing has been.

And then, the fauna — which is the thing about this place.

There are *so many* animals here that the eye gives up trying to count. But — and this is the strangeness — they are nearly silent. Frogs the size of a thumbnail cover the wet rocks in their thousands, packed so densely they make the stone look like it is shifting color, but they do not croak; they pulse their throats in unison, communicating some other way. Dragonflies in clouds, but their wings make a sound so low and soft it registers as a pressure on the skin rather than a noise. Long-legged wading birds — pale, almost translucent — stand in the pools without rippling them. Small mammals with the build of otters and the faces of something older move between the cushions of moss in numbers, watching, never startling. Fish hang in the clear water in layers, three or four species stacked vertically, each occupying its own band.

The silence is not empty. It is the silence of an enormous attention. Sometimes a single sound — a drop of water falling from a fern, a frog repositioning — will travel astonishingly far in the still air.

Now, the structures.

You will find them sparsely. A low arch of the same limestone, but not the same limestone — its surface, when you put your hand to it, is too smooth, smoother than water has ever made anything. A standing slab the height of your chest, perfectly rectangular, half-sunk into moss. A ring of what look like ordinary stones until you notice that they are casting shadows in a direction the sun is not. Whatever made these is not present, has not been present for a very long time, but the things they made are still *working*. The arch is doing something to the air that passes under it — sound bends there, and warmth pools. The slab is colder than the surrounding rock by exactly the same amount, regardless of weather.

There is no signage, no path, no marking. Whoever built these did not build them for anyone to find, or built them so long ago that the question of finding became irrelevant. They are part of the ecology now. The translucent palms grow preferentially near the slab. The small otter-things sleep in the lee of the arch. The artifacts have been absorbed.

The day moves. A warm rain comes through in the afternoon — not a storm, more like the sky exhaling — and for ten minutes everything dimples and shines and then it is over and the steam rises in slow columns from the warm rock, and the frogs all turn the same direction at once, and the white sun comes back, and the place returns to its bright, full, soft-edged stillness.

Nothing here is going to change much. The erosion is essentially complete. The species have found their layers. The structures have outlasted their makers and will outlast the structures' eventual collapse, because they are already doing whatever they were going to do, and have been doing it forever, quietly, in the middle of all this life.

It is one of the oldest stable places. It has the patience of something that has finished becoming.
