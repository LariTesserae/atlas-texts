# The Terraced Heights of Vell Maranth

Begin with the fact that there is no ground. Not in the sense you'd recognize. What appears, from a distance, to be a mountain — singular, immense, its summit lost in a haze of warm steam — is in fact a structure. Every surface of it. From the foundations sunk into whatever original peak this was built upon, up through the lowest service galleries and out across the great cascading terraces, to the cupolas and conservatory glass that crown the upper levels, there is only made-thing. Stone cut and fitted. Brick fired in furnaces that have run for centuries. Iron lattices green with the patina of long oxidation. Copper sheathing gone the color of old jade. Tile, glaze, mortar, riveted plate.

The mountain is perhaps two thousand meters from base to crown, and the whole of it is a single contiguous work, though the work of no single hand or generation. You can read its history in its skins: the oldest courses of masonry at the lower levels, dressed and re-dressed so many times the seams have softened into something almost geological; the middle reaches in a confident industrial style, all wrought iron and bolted girder; the upper terraces lighter, brighter, where the glass-makers got their hands on the sun.

**Water moves through it like blood through a body.** This is what makes the place damp rather than dry, despite its altitude. Condensation gathers on the cool undersides of the terraces and beads down through channels cut for the purpose. There are conduits everywhere — open runnels along the edges of stairs, lead pipes climbing the walls, ceramic gutters where moss has taken up residence as a permanent tenant. Cloud catches against the upper flanks of the mountain in the warm afternoons and the great copper meshes strung between the spires comb the moisture out of it. Below that, in the engine quarters around the middle elevations, steam vents in slow plumes that smell of mineral and hot oil, and where the steam meets cooler air it falls again as a fine drizzle onto the gardens beneath. So there is rain inside, in pockets, on schedules nobody set but everyone has memorized. You learn which staircases are wet at noon.

**Plants have taken the structure as readily as if it were soil.** Every terrace is a garden, and the gardens have grown into one another over generations until the distinction between cultivated and volunteer has become academic. Fig trees crack the railings and are allowed to. Vines drape from one level to the next in living curtains thirty meters long, and you can stand on a parapet and look out across what seems a hanging forest, layer beneath layer beneath layer, with the warm gold light slanting through the leaves and striking the brass of some lower gallery's roof. There are orchards on the south-facing terraces — pomegranate, citron, a kind of small dense apple — and rice paddies on the broader flats where the engine-drizzle is reliable. Ferns colonize the shaded north sides. The whole mountain breathes a green smell, wet leaf and warm stone.

**Animals are everywhere.** Not wild exactly, but not domestic either; something in between, the way a city's pigeons and cats and rats are something in between. Monkeys with long pale tails move along the iron lattices as if the lattices were branches — and to them they are, they have never known any other kind. Bright green parrots nest in the ventilation cowls. There are goats on the steeper terraces that nobody owns but everyone milks. Fish in the cisterns. A particular kind of small civet that hunts the granaries and is tolerated because it also hunts what hunts the granaries. The air at sunset thickens with bats. In the lower levels there are dogs that have learned the stairs so well they navigate them at full sprint without looking.

**The sound is the sound of an inhabited machine.** Not deafening — there is no single roar — but layered, continuous, and loud in the way a forest is loud once you start listening: never silent, never resolving. The deep slow respiration of the furnace levels, felt more than heard. The drip and trickle of water at a thousand different pitches. The bells of the levels, which mark hours but also other things — opening of the sluice gates, ascent of a freight cage, the call to a particular shrine. Voices carrying up the lightwells. Birds. The shuttle and clack of looms on the textile terraces. The hiss of steam released. A donkey somewhere, complaining.

**The technology is industrial but old-industrial, settled-industrial.** This is not a place of new invention. The engines were perfected generations ago and have been maintained, repaired, rebuilt to the same specifications ever since. They drive the lifts that move people and goods up and down the mountain's height — for stairs alone would not suffice across nearly three kilometers of vertical — and they drive the pumps that lift water against gravity to feed the upper gardens, and they drive the great fans that keep the deeper galleries breathing. Electricity exists here, but as a domestic and ceremonial light, not a structural force; the structural force is steam, and pulleys, and counterweights, and the patient arithmetic of leverage. Things here are built to last and to be repaired, not replaced.

**The light is extraordinary.** This is the thing visitors remember, if there are visitors. The altitude puts the upper levels above most of the weather, so the sun strikes the glass and the copper and the white stucco with a clarity that flattens nothing — it picks out every leaf, every tile-edge, every pigeon. The lower terraces catch a softer, filtered version of it, broken into pools and shafts by the canopy of growth above. At midday the whole mountain seems to be giving off light rather than receiving it. At dusk the light goes red and the copper roofs go almost on fire, and then the lamps come on, level by level, slow as breath, and the mountain becomes a constellation.

**As for the strangeness:** it is mild, and you might not notice it at first. The lifts are operated by people who sing as they work, in a counting-song that has no apparent function but is never not sung; nobody can quite explain when it started or whether stopping it would matter. There is a species of moth on the upper levels whose wings bear what looks unmistakably like written script, though no one has ever read it. The oldest cisterns, at the base, are said to contain fish that predate the present configuration of the mountain — fish older than the masonry around them, though this cannot be true and is repeated anyway. Small things. The kind of thing a place accumulates when it has been itself for a long time.

**Because that is the dominant quality of Vell Maranth: it has been itself for a long time, and it changes slowly.** The terraces erode and are repaired at roughly the same rate. The vines grow and are pruned at roughly the same rate. People are born here and die here and are buried in the lower vaults, and the population does not much grow or shrink. New construction is rare; what construction occurs is mostly the replacement of one stone with another stone of the same shape, one pipe with another pipe of the same gauge. The mountain is not static — it is full of motion, water moving, animals moving, people moving, machines turning — but its shape and its life are in a long balance, and have been, and will be, for as long as anyone living can foresee.

If you stand on one of the middle terraces at the warm hour just after the noon drizzle, with the wet stone steaming faintly under your feet and the parrots arguing in the figs and the bell of the fourteenth level ringing for whatever it rings for, and you look out across the green descending levels and the green ascending levels and feel the slow pulse of the furnaces through the soles of your shoes — you understand that this is not a place going anywhere. It has arrived. It arrived long ago. It is the arrival.
