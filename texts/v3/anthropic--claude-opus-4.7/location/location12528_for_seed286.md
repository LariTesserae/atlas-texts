# The Engine Canopy

Begin high. You are inside a vegetable atmosphere — not a forest exactly, because that word implies ground, and there is no ground here in any sense you would recognize. The substrate is plant. The air, where there is air, is held in pockets between leaf-walls thicker than ship hulls. Most of the volume is something else: a cold green water that has saturated everything for so long that the distinction between sap and sea has dissolved. You breathe it or you don't. The plants don't care either way.

This is high country. The Canopy sits at the altitude where, in another world, you would expect thin air and snow. Here you get cool water instead, water at about the temperature of a deep cellar, threaded with the slow respiration of leaves the size of cathedral floors. The cold keeps things slow. The cold is part of why nothing changes.

The architecture — and it is architecture, though nothing built it — works like this: enormous vascular trunks rise through the column of the world, spaced widely, a kilometer or more between any two. Between them stretch the lamina: horizontal sheets of living tissue, translucent green, holding the water in vast lens-shaped reservoirs. Light filters down through dozens of these stacked lenses, arriving at the lower depths still bright enough to read by, the color of old bottle glass. Up close the lamina show their veins, each vein a tube as wide as a tunnel, pulsing — and this is the strange part — pulsing in absolute synchrony with every other vein in sight. The whole system beats. Not like a heart. Like a factory.

Because it is a factory. That is what you realize after the first hour of drifting through it. The pulsing is mechanical in its regularity, and the sound it makes is enormous: a deep industrial throb that fills the water-air, overlapping itself in standing waves so that some pockets ring like the inside of a bell and others hum at frequencies you feel in your sternum. There are higher sounds layered over it. Hissing where pressurized sap vents through stomatal valves the size of doorways. A constant slow grinding from somewhere lower, where the great trunks rotate against each other at speeds you cannot perceive but can hear. Clicks. Whistles. The shriek of cavitation in a distant xylem.

The plants are doing industry. Not making anything for anyone — making themselves, ceaselessly, at industrial scale. Photosynthesis here is not a quiet chemistry; it is a process with gauges and exhausts. Bubbles of pure oxygen the size of carriages release from the underside of every leaf-floor and rise in slow columns, glittering, until they collect against the lamina above and are reabsorbed through valved pores that open with audible thunks. Sugar moves through the trunks in visible currents, amber rivers inside green walls. Heat shimmers off the working surfaces despite the cold of the surrounding medium — you can see the thermoclines, the slow convective overturning, the weather of the place generating itself from its own metabolism.

And none of it changes. This is the part that takes longest to feel. The Canopy is in equilibrium so total that it has forgotten how to have seasons. The same bubble-columns rise in the same places. The same pulse beats at the same rate it has beaten for — how long? There is no way to tell. The erosion patterns on the older trunks, where sap-flow has worn channels into living bark over geological time, are themselves now part of the steady state: water moves through those channels at exactly the rate that maintains them. Nothing wears further. Nothing heals. The wound and the healing have reached agreement.

Fauna move through it, abundant but not crowded — this is a sparse world, vast between its presences. Things like jellyfish made of leaf-tissue, drifting between the lamina, grazing on the oxygen-foam. Long ribbon-creatures that wrap around the pulsing veins and feed on the pressure differentials, vibrating in tune with the throb. A kind of slow heavy beetle the size of a cow, with a translucent green carapace, that walks the upper surfaces of the lamina and seems to be doing maintenance, though no one assigned it the job — patching small tears with a secretion from its abdomen, moving on. There are fish, more or less, in the deeper water-pockets: blind, pale, hovering. And there are the swimmers in the sap itself, tiny things that ride the sugar-currents up and down the trunks forever, never leaving, perhaps never dying.

The weirdness is in the details. The pulse is too regular. The beetles have geometric markings that look almost like serial numbers but resolve, on close inspection, into nothing — patterns that suggest meaning and then refuse it. Sometimes a leaf-floor will fluoresce briefly, for no reason, casting a blue wash across a hectare of canopy, and the fauna will not react, because this has happened in this exact place at this exact interval forever. There are places where the water tastes of metal. There are places where, if you hold very still, you can feel the whole structure breathing in a rhythm slower than the pulse, a deeper rhythm, the breath of the whole — and if you try to count it you lose your sense of time entirely and come back not knowing how long you stood there.

Nothing was ever built here. There are no walls, no tools, no signs of hands. And yet the sense of being inside a machine is overwhelming, because the plants long ago became their own machinery, and the machinery long ago became the world, and the world long ago stopped going anywhere. It runs. It will keep running. The cool green water moves through it on schedules older than mountains.

You could spend a life inside one chamber of it — between two lamina, in the column of light beneath an oxygen-vent, listening to the pulse — and never see the same thing twice and never see anything new. Both at once. That is the rule of the place. Variation without change. Industry without product. A green engine the size of weather, idling forever at the top of the world.
