# The Mooring

There is a thing the size of a small continent suspended in the upper atmosphere of a young world, and I have been trying to decide what to call it.

It is not a cloud. Clouds are passive — they assemble where the air tells them to assemble. This is sovereign. It hangs at roughly eleven kilometers, give or take a kilometer in either direction depending on the hour, and it weighs in the order of trillions of tonnes, and it does not fall. Whatever holds it up is not lift in any sense an aerodynamicist would recognize. The air around its underside ripples in standing patterns, faint hexagonal interference, as if pressure itself is being asked to behave geometrically. You can feel the holding when you pass through it — a downward push that suddenly hesitates, like a wave deciding not to break.

Begin at altitude, then. From below, looking up, the Mooring shows a flat dark underside scored with bright meltwater channels. It rains constantly from this underside — not the rain of a storm but the rain of a melting thing, threadlike, near-vertical, ten million slow waterfalls falling into the troposphere and never reaching the ground because the world below is also weather. Half the rain evaporates back into the Mooring's lower haze; half escapes downward and joins the planet's permanent stratus. The exchange has been going on, by the look of the runoff scars, for some thousands of years. Maybe longer. The geology is too new to date.

Climb onto it and the scale opens up.

The upper surface is a plateau, but "plateau" is the wrong word — plateaus are stable, finished. This is raw. It is mostly water, in three states simultaneously, organized into provinces. There are basins of liquid water held in shallow depressions that should not be shallow, given the mass involved; the basins are kilometers across and only meters deep, and the water in them has the wrong meniscus, climbing the rim a hand's breadth higher than gravity allows. There are slabs of clear ice with bubbles in them arranged in lattices too regular to be accident — diamond cubic, sometimes, sometimes stranger geometries. And there is fog, but the fog has structure: it moves in coherent volumes, drifts shaped like long lozenges or stacked discs, sliding past each other on currents that aren't wind.

The temperature is cool, not cold. Twelve degrees, perhaps. You can stand here in a sweater. This is impossible at eleven kilometers and is one of the first things you stop noticing.

The noise. I keep forgetting to describe the noise, because it is everywhere and continuous and the mind files it under silence after a while. The Mooring sings. Not metaphorically — there is a low pressure tone, somewhere around an A-flat below the bass staff, that comes from the whole mass, and over it a constant chorus of higher sounds: the chime of ice forming and unforming, the hiss of provinces of fog dragging past one another, the deep arrhythmic crack of new geometry being asserted somewhere deep inside the substrate. When two of the fog-lozenges collide they ring like struck bells, low and long. When a basin overspills its rim the falling water sings on the way down. The whole place is loud the way a cathedral organ at full draw is loud — you feel it in the sternum first.

There is almost nothing alive here. A few patches of something that might be lichen and might be a mineral crust the color of dried tea, clinging to the leeward sides of the ice-slabs, in tufts no bigger than a hand. Nothing moves of its own accord. No insects. No birds, though birds couldn't reach this altitude anyway. The vegetation is a footnote, an asterisk: *life is permitted here but has not yet been invited.* The Mooring does not seem hostile to it. It just hasn't gotten around to it.

What it has gotten around to is making itself.

This is the part that's hard to describe without sounding mystical, and I want to be precise. The Mooring is *under construction*. Not by anyone — there are no builders, no machines, no scaffolds. The construction is happening at a level I would call material if I had a better word. Watch a single point on an ice-slab for ten minutes and you will see the lattice in it shift: bubbles migrate, planes of cleavage rotate, the whole crystalline organization revising itself toward some configuration it has not yet reached. Watch a basin and the water in it will, over the course of an hour, change its salinity, its viscosity, the angle at which its surface meets the rim. Watch the underside rain and the channels feeding it will rearrange like the dendrites of a slow lightning.

Erosion implies a finished form being worn down. Here there is no finished form. The Mooring is doing the opposite of eroding. It is *accreting toward something*, and whatever the something is, it is being decided in real time, by the place itself, in negotiation with the air around it and the pressure beneath it and possibly with considerations I have no access to.

It is turbulent. I should have said that earlier. The provinces shift. A basin that was here yesterday is now an ice-slab; the ice-slab that was here is now a province of fog; the fog that was here has condensed into a new basin two kilometers east. The transitions are not violent — there is no shattering, no boiling — but they are not slow either. You can watch them happen. You can stand on an ice-slab and feel it begin to give, not melting but *deciding* to be water again, and you step off it onto something firmer and look back and the slab is gone, a pond in its place, perfectly calm, already wrong in its meniscus.

The hexagonal interference patterns on the underside, I think now, are not a side effect. I think they are the place thinking. I think the standing waves in the holding-pressure are the substrate of whatever process is making the decisions about which province becomes which. If that's true, then the Mooring is not a landscape with weather inside it. It is a single very slow computation taking up a small continent of sky, using the phases of water as its working memory, and the rain falling off its underside is the heat exhaust of a calculation whose purpose I cannot guess.

This is the technology of the place. There are no artifacts. There is nothing built. The technology is the geology, or the geology is the technology — the distinction has stopped being useful. Someone made this, or something made this, or it made itself, and the engineering is happening at the scale of how water decides to be water. Current physics does not forbid this exactly. Current physics simply has not been asked to describe it yet.

From the highest point of the Mooring — a rounded swell of blue ice maybe two hundred meters above the rest — you can see the whole upper surface curving very slightly with the curvature of the thing itself, which has its own minor horizon at perhaps forty kilometers. Beyond that the air shimmers and the eye loses purchase. Above, the sky is the clean hard blue of high altitude, with a sun that looks ordinary and behaves ordinarily. Below, when the underside-rain thins enough to see through, you can sometimes glimpse the planet — green and gray and very far, an ordinary world, not knowing or perhaps not caring that this great cool thinking thing hangs over its weather like a held breath.

It is empty. That's the last thing. For all its turbulence, its sound, its constant revision, the Mooring is *empty* in a way few places are. The volume between any two presences here — a fog-lozenge and an ice-slab, a basin and the next basin — is vast and untenanted. You can walk for an hour and see nothing closer than the horizon. The emptiness has the quality of a held room: not abandoned, not lonely, just *not yet occupied*. As if the place is being prepared. As if the long calculation in the pressure-lattice is, among other things, an invitation being slowly composed, and one day, when the geometry has finished deciding itself, the Mooring will be ready to mean whatever it has been working toward meaning, and something — or someone, or no one in any sense I would recognize — will arrive to read it.

Until then it hangs there, singing its low note, raining its slow rain, rearranging its provinces of water, eleven kilometers up, cool, loud, almost empty, and very busy with becoming.
