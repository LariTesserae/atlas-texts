# The Cistern-Town of Low Hour

You come down into it by way of stairs that were not cut but laid — each step a different stone, fitted by hand, the mortar between them dark with old damp. The stairs descend longer than seems reasonable. By the fortieth step the sky is gone, replaced by a ceiling of overlapping slate and tarred timber, and by the eightieth you understand that the ceiling *is* the sky here, and that whatever weather happens, happens beneath it.

The town sits in a shallow basin — not a valley exactly, more like the inside of a wide, low bowl that someone has roofed over and then filled, patiently, century by century, with everything they could think to build. Houses lean on houses. Cisterns sit on the roofs of cisterns. Ladders climb between levels that don't quite agree on what a floor is. Nothing here grew. Nothing here was found in place. Every surface — the cobbles, the beams, the great curving gutters that carry condensate down from the underside of the roof-sky — every surface is *made*, shaped by a hand, fitted by a hand, and most of it bears the marks of the chisel or the adze or the wet thumb of the potter who pressed it into being.

There is no soil. Where you'd expect dirt, there is fired brick, or packed ash bound with lime, or planking laid over planking laid over the bones of older planking. If you dig — and people do dig, sometimes, looking for lost rooms — you find more town. It goes down. No one knows how far.

---

**The water.**

It is not raining, but water is everywhere anyway. The roof-sky sweats. All day and all night, droplets gather on the undersides of beams and the lips of slates and fall — irregular, uncountable, a slow loud rain that never quite becomes weather. The sound is the dominant sound of the place: *tik. tik-tik. tok.* Ten thousand individual drips landing in ten thousand different vessels, because every household has put out bowls and tubs and great copper basins to catch what comes. Each vessel has its own note. Walking through a neighborhood is like walking through a tuned instrument whose tuning shifts by the hour.

There are streams, too — narrow channels cut into the streets, carrying the overflow from the upper cisterns down toward the lower ones. They run cold. You can dip a cup. The water tastes faintly of the wood it has passed through, the lead of old solder, the lime of a hundred patchings. It is drinkable. It is, in fact, the only thing to drink.

In the lowest part of the basin there is a pool — the Sink, they call it — where all the runoff finally collects. It is black, and large, and no one has measured its depth. Things have been dropped into it that did not return.

---

**The cool.**

The air sits at the temperature of a cellar in autumn. Stone-cool. You feel it first in the hands. Breath shows, faintly, near the lamps. The cold is not aggressive — it does not bite — but it is patient, and it gets into the seams of your clothes, and after an hour you understand why everyone here wears layered wool under oiled canvas, why the smiths work bare-armed and steaming and the children sleep three to a bed.

The cool keeps the wood from rotting too fast. It keeps the water from going sour in the cisterns. It is, in some sense, what makes the town possible. Higher up — past the stairs, in whatever world is up there — it must be colder still, because the roof-sky is always weeping its condensate down.

---

**The light.**

Dark. Not pitch — there are lamps, hundreds of them, oil-burning and tallow-burning and the occasional pale phosphor-jar that the alchemists sell at the market. But the lamps are local. Each illuminates its own small district of stone and timber, and between them the streets are deep blue, deep brown, the color of the inside of a closed eye. You navigate by the rhythm of pools of light, by the sound of the drips (each cistern has a voice, and the locals know them by ear), by the smell of the bakers' chimneys and the tanners' vats and the cold mineral breath rising off the Sink.

When you look up, the roof-sky shows pinpricks — not stars, but the small holes where chimneys vent, each one ringed in soot, each one leaking a thin column of smoke up into wherever-it-goes. From below they look like stars that have been put there on purpose, which they have.

---

**The makers.**

The technology is hand-work. There is iron — nails, hinges, the blades of the cistern-keepers' hooks — but no machines beyond the simple ones: lever, wedge, pulley, wheel. Bellows. Looms. The lathe in the cooper's shop is turned by a boy with a foot-pedal. The great pumps that move water from the lower cisterns to the upper ones are turned by people walking inside wooden drums, slowly, conversationally, for shifts of two hours at a time. Everyone takes turns. The drums *creak*, and the creaking is part of the music of the place — a slow groaning bass beneath the constant tik-tok of the drips.

What they make, mostly, is more town. The town is always being made. There is no point at which it was finished. The carpenters and the masons and the tile-setters work continuously, repairing what the damp has rotted, extending a roof here, shoring up a beam there, fitting a new cistern into the gap above an old one. The town is in a state of *emerging* — it has been emerging for as long as anyone remembers, and there is no plan for it to stop.

This is, perhaps, the strangest thing about the place. There is no original. There is no first stone. Everything is replacement, repair, accretion. The town has the geology of a coral reef, slow and constructed and alive only in the sense that its making does not cease.

---

**The fauna.**

Rare. There are no dogs here, no horses; nothing large. But in the rafters live the pale moths that the lamp-keepers tolerate, because the moths eat the mildew that would otherwise eat the beams. There are fish in some of the deeper cisterns — small, eyeless, descended from stock brought down generations ago. The cistern-keepers feed them crumbs and call them by name, though the names are inherited and the fish are not.

And in the Sink — well. The Sink has its own fauna, and the people who live nearest it speak of it carefully and not often. Something moves under the black surface, sometimes. The drips that fall into the Sink make a different sound than the drips that fall anywhere else: deeper, slower, as if the water were thicker there. The Sink-keepers leave offerings of bread and copper at its edge. Whether the offerings are eaten or merely sink is, they say, not the point.

---

**The strangeness.**

A few things, in passing:

The shadows in this town do not always agree with their sources. A man passing under a lamp may cast two shadows, one of which lingers a moment after he is gone. No one finds this remarkable; it is simply how light works here, in the cool and the damp.

There are doors in the lower streets that no one will open. They are marked with a particular knot of rope, tied in a particular way, and the children learn the knot before they learn their letters. Behind the doors: older town. Town that has been closed off. Town that the town has decided is no longer town.

The drips, occasionally, fall *upward*. This is rare enough to be worth pointing at when it happens — a single bead of water rising from a basin, hesitating at the rafters, and vanishing into the roof-sky as if returning whence it came. The cistern-keepers say this is the water going home. The alchemists say it is a question of pressure. The children say it is a wish escaping, and try to make one when they see it.

---

**The hour.**

There is no day and no night here, only the hour the bells decide. The bells are bronze and hand-cast and hang at the corners of the cisterns, and a bell-walker comes by every so often to strike them in the agreed sequence. When the bells say morning, the bakers light their ovens and the smell of bread floods the lower streets. When the bells say evening, the lamps are trimmed and the drum-walkers shift, and a quieter set of drips takes over — the slow ones, the ones the locals call the *sleeping rain.*

You arrived, let us say, near the middle of an hour that has no clear name. The bells have not rung for some time. The town goes about its low cool wet making, and somewhere, far below, in the Sink, something has just turned over in the dark.
