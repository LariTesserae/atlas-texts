# The Drowned Works at Ennew

It sits in a basin of warm water some forty feet below what used to be the surface — though "used to be" is the wrong tense for a place that has been this way long enough to forget it was ever otherwise. The water is the temperature of held breath. Not bath-warm, not cold; warm in the way that an animal is warm. You feel it before you see anything else, and you go on feeling it.

The light comes from the machines.

That is the first strange thing, and the place spends a long time being strange about it. Every furnace, every boiler, every patient turbine in the drowned town glows faintly — a sallow orange behind iron grates, a green seep around the seams of copper vessels, the dull red of bricks that have been hot for longer than bricks should be able to be hot. The water transmits this light in slow bands. Stand still and you can watch a glow pulse outward from a foundry wall and reach you a full second later, like a thought arriving by post.

The town is small. You could walk its full length in twenty minutes if you could walk. Six streets, maybe seven, depending on whether you count the alley behind the dye-works that bends back into itself. Brick and cast iron, mostly; some tile; the older roofs are slate, scaled now with a kelp so fine and dense it looks like fur. The buildings lean against each other in the companionable way of long marriages. Nothing has fallen in a long time. Nothing seems likely to.

The water is packed with life that is mostly not animal. Threads of pale weed hang in vertical curtains from every gutter and lintel — not drifting, exactly; they sway only when something passes, and even then with a delay, as if they had to be convinced. Between the threads: a green so saturated it reads almost black until your eyes adjust, then resolves into countless small fronds, bladders, papery leaves, things that should not photosynthesize at this depth and yet apparently do, fed by the kindly glow of the works. The density is the density of a hedge, but everywhere, in three dimensions. You move through it the way you move through a heavy coat being put on you.

Sound: almost none. The machines run, but they run in water, and water muffles their rhythms into a low felt thrum you register in your sternum rather than your ears. Occasionally a valve somewhere releases a string of bubbles that ascends through the weed with a sound like someone counting under their breath. Occasionally a brick settles. That is all. The factories work in something close to silence, which is the wrong way around — factories should be loud — and this inversion is part of why the place feels the way it feels.

What do they make? It is hard to say. There are conveyor belts behind the windows of the long shed by the canal, moving very slowly, carrying nothing visible. There are looms in the upper room of the mill, their shuttles passing through warps of water, weaving a fabric that exists only as a faint distortion in the current downstream. There are presses that close on empty molds and open again, having produced — perhaps — a small displacement, a local change in pressure, a thought. The output, whatever it is, joins the water and goes elsewhere. The town has been doing this for a very long time.

Fauna is rare and deliberate. A single eel-thing, pale as a candle, keeps office hours in the pipe under the bridge; it appears at certain glows and is gone again. There is a school of something small and translucent that crosses the main square twice a day, always in the same direction, never returning by any route you can find. A crab the size of a thumbnail lives in the bell of a streetlamp and has, as far as anyone could ever tell, lived there always.

The erosion is the erosion of a place that has reached an agreement with its medium. Corners are softened but not lost. Lettering on the foundry sign is legible but suede-soft, the serifs grown into the surrounding rust. Bricks have a velvet on them. Iron has become a kind of iron-jelly on its outermost millimeter and then iron again beneath, and this skin is stable; it does not progress. The whole town is the way a stone in a river becomes after a century — itself, only more so.

Rules of the place, as best as can be inferred:

Nothing here is in a hurry. A door that begins to open will finish opening, but it will take its time, and the water that moves with it will move with the same patience. If you push something, it goes where you pushed it, eventually. Decisions made in this town arrive at their consequences the way ships arrive at ports.

The machines do not stop. They have not stopped. There is no evidence of a crew, nor of any crew having ever been needed. The fires are fed by something the water provides; the gears are lubricated by their long acquaintance with their bearings. If you put your hand on a flywheel you can feel it turning, and you can keep your hand there, and it will not hurt you, and it will not stop for you either.

The light dims and brightens on a cycle that takes — best guess — somewhere between a day and a week. Nobody has clocked it. During the dim phase the weed closes slightly, like a fist relaxing, and the water cools by perhaps a degree, and the machines run a little slower, and the whole town seems to be thinking. During the bright phase it seems to be remembering.

Above, the surface is somewhere. You can see it occasionally as a faint silvering when you look up through a gap in the canopy of weed, a tremor of brighter light beyond the dim. But the town does not look up. The town looks at itself, glowing gently, working at its slow incomprehensible product, draped in green, warm, quiet, full, and entirely accounted for.

It will be here tomorrow. It will be here in a hundred years. It is, in some important sense, already done — and yet it goes on doing.
