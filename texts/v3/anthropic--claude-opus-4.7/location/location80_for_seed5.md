# The Lichen-City of Under-Root

Down where the great beech has fallen — not recently; it must have come down three or four hundred years ago — there is a hollow beneath the upturned plate of roots that opens into something more elaborate than a hollow has any right to be.

You would have to kneel in the moss to see it. You would have to get the knees of your trousers wet, because the moss here is always slightly damp; not soaked, not dripping, but holding moisture the way a wrung cloth does, in its fibers, releasing it slowly into the cold air as a fine vapor that silvers every surface. The light comes in from above through a screen of hart's-tongue fern and wood sorrel, and it arrives already green, already filtered, already cold — but bright, startlingly bright, the way snow-light is bright. Every droplet on every frond acts as a lens. The hollow beneath the root-plate is lit as if by thousands of cold small suns.

Inside, a city.

It is a city of lichen and of the pale architectures that certain slime molds make when they are doing their patient mathematics. Crusts of map-lichen form the pavements — ochre and sage and a bone-white that carries a faint blue cast. Where the pavements meet, fruticose lichens rise in spires, some as tall as your thumb, branching in their stiff antler-fashion, tipped with scarlet apothecia like tiny lamps that are not lit but seem to be. Between the spires run colonnades made by the fruiting bodies of *Physarum* — delicate stalked globes arrayed in procession, each a perfect sphere on a perfect filament, holding their position for such long spans that one must call them permanent. They are not permanent. They only live on a timescale that makes our permanence look flighty.

The streets are narrow. The streets are always narrow. There is no space that is not occupied: every square millimeter of the under-root bears something living, or something that was living and is now the substrate of something living. A fallen rootlet supports a row of turkey-tail bracket no wider than a fingernail, and on the turkey-tails grow smaller fungi still, and on those, the fine pale hyphae of a mold that is itself colonized by bacteria visible only as a faint iridescence.

The inhabitants, such as they are, move so slowly that stillness is a fair description. Springtails the color of pewter graze the lichen-pavements. A single wood louse, banded and armored, passes down one of the colonnades with the deliberation of a magistrate. In a pool no larger than a coin — condensation, gathered in the cup of an old acorn shell — something flickers: a rotifer, perhaps, or the larva of a midge that has decided on a career of waiting. Pseudoscorpions no bigger than commas hold their small pincers folded, patient. A centipede the length of an eyelash flows along a wall and is gone.

There are rules here. Nothing is loud. There is no room in this economy for loudness; a shout would knock the spires over, would scatter the *Physarum* processions, would undo centuries. So the sounds are only: the soft tick of a drop forming on a hanging root and committing at last to fall; the nearly-imperceptible rasp of springtail mouthparts on lichen thallus; the infinitesimal creak as a fruiting body, somewhere, chooses this moment to open. One could sit and listen for an hour and hear perhaps four things.

The temperature stays low. It is always just above freezing here, held there by the mass of wet earth above and the still air trapped below. This coldness is part of why the city endures: metabolisms slow, decay slows, the whole place operates on a tempo that resembles stone. A single generation of lichen-growth might span a human lifetime. The map of the pavements does not change. If you returned in ten years, the ochre district would be perhaps a millimeter wider on its eastern edge. If you returned in a hundred, the scarlet lamps would have shifted their constellations — but only just.

And yet it is not static. It is at equilibrium, which is a different thing. Every surface is doing work; every organism is eating or being eaten or preparing to be eaten; the whole under-root is a vast slow digestion of the fallen beech, a ceremonial consumption that has been going on since before your grandparents were born and will continue after your grandchildren are gone. The city is the shape this digestion takes when it is beautiful.

There is craft here too, of a kind. The caddisfly larvae that sometimes wash in on the threadlike rivulet at the hollow's back build their cases from grains of quartz and flecks of mica, and they build them well — each grain chosen, each placement considered in whatever way a caddis considers. These are the tool-users of the under-root. Their abandoned cases litter certain corners like the pottery of a vanished people, and the springtails have been known to shelter in them, inheriting what they did not build.

One strangeness: in the deepest part of the hollow, where the tap-root of the fallen beech once plunged, there is a place where the lichens grow in a spiral. Not a rough spiral, not an approximate one — a true logarithmic spiral, precise to within the resolution of the lichen's own growth. No one made it. No one maintains it. It has simply been there, for as long as the city has been there, and no biologist who has crawled in with a hand lens has been able to say why. The spiral does not rotate. The spiral does not grow. The spiral is not quite centered on anything in particular. It is the sort of thing that, once seen, one stops trying to explain, because explanation feels coarser than the fact.

Above, a wren calls once, very far away, and the sound arrives in the hollow as a rumor of a sound, and the city does not respond, because the city's response takes centuries, and the wren is already gone.

The droplet on the hanging root, the one that has been forming for the last long while — it falls. It strikes the acorn-cup pool. The rotifer, if it was a rotifer, flickers once.

Nothing else changes. Everything is exactly as it was. Which is to say: everything is alive, and everything is held, and the cold bright light goes on pouring in through the ferns as if it had forgotten how to do anything else.
