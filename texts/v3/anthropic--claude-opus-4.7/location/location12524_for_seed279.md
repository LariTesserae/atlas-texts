# The Drumming Flats

The first thing is the sound. Before your eyes adjust to the grey light, before you understand the ground or the sky, the noise is already inside you — a low continuous percussion, not quite rhythmic, the way rain on a tin roof isn't rhythmic but is patterned. It comes from everywhere at once and it does not stop. It has not stopped for a long time.

The land here lies just barely above the water, or perhaps just barely below it depending on the hour — a vast shallow pan that extends to a horizon softened by mist. The water is never deep. Ankle-deep in the channels, a film over the flats, beading on every upraised surface. It seeps. It condenses out of the air onto cold stone and runs in thin sheets back toward itself. The whole landscape weeps quietly under the louder drumming.

The drumming is the stones.

The flats are paved, almost continuously, with what at first look like cobbles — close-packed dark spheres and ovoids, the size of fists, of skulls, of bread loaves, jammed against each other in a mosaic so dense you cannot push a finger between them. They are young stones, freshly delivered: their edges where they have edges are still sharp, their glassy faces unworn, their colors un-greyed. Black, oil-blue, dull copper, the deep green of wet slate. And they sing. Each stone, as water strikes it or runs from it, gives off a single clear tone — some high, some low, some buzzing, some bell-like — and because the water never stops moving and the stones are uncountable, the result is the continuous polyphonic drumming that fills the world. A field of resonators being played by the weather.

The air is cool. You can see your breath but it doesn't bite. Mist hangs in long horizontal bands, thicker low, thinning toward a sky that is only a paler grey ceiling. Light comes from no visible source — a uniform dimness, the inside of a pearl. Noon and dusk are indistinguishable here, or perhaps it is always one or the other.

Nothing grows. There is no green except the green of certain stones. No moss, no algal film, no lichen crust — the surfaces are too new, or the chemistry is wrong, or the percussion shakes anything loose before it can take hold. What you might mistake for vegetation, on closer look, is mineral: pale crystalline fans where dissolved salts have crept up the sides of the larger stones and bloomed into feather-shapes; thin tubes of carbonate where water has dripped from the same point for centuries. These grow, but slowly, in the way stones grow.

The animals, when they come, are rare and quiet. You will not see one for a long while. Then a long-legged thing wades through a far channel — pale, slender, no taller than a child — and is gone behind the mist before you have resolved its shape. They are listeners, whatever they are. They walk carefully, placing each step into a silence between drumbeats, as though the stones could be disturbed and would have to be paid for.

The built things are scattered and old in their own way, though not ancient: low cairns of the singing stones, stacked three or four high, here and there across the flats. Most are simply piles. A few are arranged in pairs or triples that suggest intention without declaring it. One, an hour's walk out from where you began, is a low arch — two columns of selected stones, leaning, capped — that produces, when a particular wind passes through it, a different note from the rest of the field, a sustained vowel you can hear from far off. Whoever built it understood the tuning. They are not here now. There are no paths between the cairns; no footprints persist on the wet stone.

The world changes slowly. The water rises a finger's width in a season, falls again. The mist thickens at what might be night and thins at what might be day, but barely. New stones arrive — from where is not clear, perhaps from below, the flats slowly extruding their pavement — and the oldest stones, the ones near the center of the great pan, are very faintly more worn than those at the edges, their notes a fraction flatter, their edges beginning to round after some great span of dripping. In a thousand years the music will have shifted by a quartertone. In ten thousand, the place will sound like a different instrument.

For now: the drumming, the weeping, the cool grey, the close-packed singing stones underfoot extending past every horizon. Standing still, you become part of the resonance. Water beads on your shoulders, drips from your sleeves onto the stones near your feet, and those stones answer you, briefly, with their own small notes — added to the rest, indistinguishable within a minute, but there. You played the landscape. The landscape did not notice. It goes on.
