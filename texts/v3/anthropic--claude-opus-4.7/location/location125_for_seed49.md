# The Warmwater Conurbation

It begins, for any visitor, as a sound you feel in the sternum before you understand it as sound at all. A standing roar, pitched down into the body's own frequencies, compounded of ten million smaller noises: the hiss of steam through ceramic lattices, the slap of warm water against a hundred thousand pier-piles, the gonging of cargo bells, the layered voices of people and not-quite-people calling across canals, the percussive thud of pressure-release valves venting into the mist, and beneath it all a constant tidal shushing that no one can source and no one, after a week, can still hear consciously.

The city sits in a shallow, immense basin — a sea that is not quite a sea, a floodplain that is not quite land. The water is everywhere and always about knee-deep to waist-deep, and it is warm. It has been warm for longer than anyone has measured. Steam peels off its surface in low ribbons at dawn and reforms as a ceiling of low cloud by mid-morning, so that the light you walk under is an even, pearled brightness, shadowless and generous, filtering through the vapor as if the whole basin lived inside a lamp.

Nothing grows here in the green sense. The basin is mineral — pale grey silts, black glassy pebbles, long tongues of travertine built up slowly around the warm springs that feed the shallows. But the water is not sterile. It is full of something older than plants: mats of chemosynthetic film, iridescent where they break the surface, tasting faintly of copper and bread. Filaments drift in the currents like hair. In the deeper pools, colonies pile up into soft mounds the size of houses, and the mounds pulse, very slowly, in a rhythm that the city's engineers have learned to respect and route around. The ecology is pre-botanical, or post-, depending on who tells you. The distinction does not matter to the filaments.

And the city — the city is everywhere. It is built on the water and into it and over it. The density is total: there is no undeveloped horizon, no empty lot, no unclaimed stretch of shallow. Towers of fused silicate rise in clustered columns, their surfaces covered in a fine intricate patterning that reads to the eye like text and to the hand like skin. The patterning is the architecture; the walls compute. Heat from the springs is drawn up through capillary networks in the building stone, cooled by radiators that look like folded fans, the fans flex and sigh, and this is one of the ten million sounds.

Between the towers run the causeways, the floating markets, the stepped quays of sintered ash. There are canals that are not really canals because they are not carved — the water simply flows where the construction allows, and the construction everywhere allows. Small craft, shaped like long flat beans, nose through the crowds; larger barges push through by slow displacement, ringing their bells to clear a path. On the platforms that rise just above the water's surface, people sell things: pressed cakes of mineral nutrient, coiled spools of data-thread, living tools that must be fed, reconditioned air, sealed vials of scent. Everyone is doing business. No one is still.

The fauna is abundant and mostly aquatic. Long pale fish with too many fins hang in the shadow under every dock. Something like a crab, the size of a dinner plate and soft-shelled, scuttles up the pilings at dusk and is not eaten because it eats the filaments that would otherwise clog the intake vents. In the upper air, above the steam, things glide that are probably not birds — flat, kite-like, with hot spots of bioluminescence visible even through the haze. And there are larger things, rarely seen, that move through the deep channels and that the city has come to accommodate: wide gates in the lowest causeways hinge open at certain hours, and barge traffic diverts, and something passes under, and the water rises a little on the other side, and business resumes.

The technology is not displayed. It is infrastructural, and it is old enough here that no one discusses it any more than a fish discusses water. The building stones self-repair. The water is cycled and re-cycled through processes that happen at scales the hand cannot see — the pale mist that never quite clears is full of tiny working things, and a newcomer will find, on the first morning, that a small cut on the finger has closed overnight without scabbing. This is not medicine. This is just the air. The city breathes through its people as through its walls.

Weather happens inside the basin. The scale is great enough that the far edge is not visible from any point, and warm fronts form over the central shallows, roll outward, break against the tower-clusters, and dissolve into rain that is already the city's water before it lands. Lightning is frequent and low, because the humidity holds the charge close, and the towers are grounded into the mineral floor with deliberate carelessness, so that strikes earth through them with a thud rather than a crack and the nearby stones hum for an hour afterward. The city is at peace with its own weather in the way a body is at peace with its own fever.

What erodes here is not stone. Stone is maintained. What erodes is pattern. The configurations of the markets, the routes of the barges, the alliances of the tower-clusters — these shift constantly, dissolve, re-form. A quarter that was busy last month is a quiet backwater now, its causeways half-flooded, its vendors relocated three districts over. A week later it is dense again, repurposed for a trade that didn't exist before. The city's shape, at the scale of districts, is in continuous turbulent rearrangement, and this is not decay but respiration. Anything that tried to hold its form against the flow would be abandoned around, left as a stubborn island in a rerouted current, and eventually disassembled for parts.

The rules of the world, as far as a visitor can reconstruct them:

The water will not hurt you. The steam will not hurt you. The filaments are not to be eaten raw but are otherwise friendly. If a gate opens in a causeway, stand back. If the humming in a tower rises in pitch, move to another tower; it is not dangerous, but it is busy, and being underfoot is rude. If you cannot hear yourself think, you are in the right place. If you can, walk further in.

And at night — though *night* is the wrong word, because the pearled light only dims to a deep rose, never quite going dark — the roar changes register. The barge traffic thins. The filaments in the deep mounds phosphoresce, faintly, turning the water under the causeways the color of a low flame. The tower-walls cool and click. Someone, far off, is singing, and the song is picked up by a district over, and then another, and for a little while the city is singing to itself across its own vast shallow basin, in a hundred thousand overlapping voices that do not resolve into a single melody and do not need to.

Then the market bells ring for the pre-dawn trade, and everything starts again, and has never really stopped.
