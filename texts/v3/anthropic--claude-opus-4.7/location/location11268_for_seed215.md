# The Cold Plateau at Iruun

The plateau is young. Not young like a forest in its third century, but young like a knuckle of rock that has only just remembered it is rock. You can see it in the cliffs along the eastern rim: the basalt there has the look of something that finished cooling last week, glassy in places, with crystalline faces still sharp enough to cut a glove. There is almost no scree at the bases. The wind hasn't had time. The rain hasn't had time. The plateau is perhaps ten thousand years old, and at this altitude, ten thousand years is barely a breath.

It sits high — somewhere above four thousand meters by the feel of the air — and it goes on. That is the first thing a visitor understands, after the cold. It goes on. From the lip of any rise you can see the next rise, and beyond that the next, and beyond that the curve of the world begins to suggest itself in the haze. The plateau has its own weather. Squall lines roll across it in slow procession, dragging curtains of sleet that strike one ridge while the next stays dry under thin sun. Clouds form here that do not exist elsewhere — lenticular stacks lined up over the warmer fumarole-fields, banner clouds streaming off the few real peaks, and a particular low fog that the locals (such as they are) call *the breathing*, which rises from the ground in the pre-dawn cold and lies in the basins like milk in shallow bowls.

Water is everywhere and nowhere. There are no rivers yet — the land hasn't been cut. Instead the water sits. It seeps. It beads on the dark stone in the mornings and freezes into rime by afternoon and melts again in the brief sun. The flats are dimpled with shallow tarns, hundreds of them, none larger than a courtyard, none deeper than a knee, all of them so clear that you can count the grains of olivine on their floors. Between the tarns the ground is spongy with a low cushion-moss the color of bruised plums, and the moss weeps when you step on it. Above the tarn-fields, where the rock rises into broken ridges, you find the *weeping walls* — cliff faces where groundwater pushes out through fractures and never quite freezes, sheathed in soft ice that grows and slumps and grows again.

Vegetation is sparse and deliberate. Cushion plants. Lichens in apricot and sulfur and graphite. A low woody shrub with needles like sewing pins that, when crushed, smells of black pepper. In the lee of certain boulders, where heat collects, you might find a single dwarf willow no taller than your hand, two hundred years old, with a trunk thick as your wrist. Plants here keep their lives close to the stone. Everything is pressed flat against the world for warmth.

And yet — fauna. This is the strange thing about the plateau. For a land so cold and so sparsely grown, it teems. Herds of a long-legged ungulate, related distantly to musk-ox but built more like a deer, move across the tarn-fields in groups of forty or sixty, browsing the cushion-moss, leaving prints that fill with water and become tiny lenses reflecting the sky. They are followed by a pack-canid with smoke-colored fur and the patient gait of an animal that has never had to hurry. Smaller things abound in the rocks: a marmot-like burrower that pipes from sentinel-stones at noon; a flock-bird, black and white, that moves in clouds of two hundred and settles on the tarns to drink in unison; and at the tarn-margins, in the wet, an amphibian the size of a thumb with translucent skin through which you can watch its heart beat. Predators stalk the ridges — a lean cat-thing, pale as the rime, that takes the young ungulates. Aerial predators ride the thermals over the fumaroles. The food web is short and intense; everything eats everything else with a kind of clean economy.

It is mostly quiet. The wind is constant but low — a basso continuo, not a howl. Bird-calls carry enormous distances in the thin clear air; you hear the marmot's pipe from a kilometer away and mistake it for something at your shoulder. Hooves on wet stone make a particular soft clatter. The tarns, when the wind drops, are silent in the way that only very cold shallow water can be silent. Sometimes a slab of ice calves off a weeping wall and the boom rolls for a long time across the basins, and then the quiet comes back.

And through all of this — woven through it, almost shy about itself — there is the city.

It is hard to see at first. That is part of its design. The builders, whoever they were or are, have placed their settlement across perhaps two hundred square kilometers of the plateau, but at a density so low and an integration so careful that for the first hour of walking through it you might believe you are simply in the wilderness. Then you notice that a particular boulder has too perfect an arch beneath it, and is sheltering a hearth-pit with embers still warm. You notice that what you took for a natural pavement of hexagonal basalt was laid, that the joints are too fine, that water flows under it in channels you can hear if you kneel. You notice that the cushion-moss in some patches grows in patterns — gentle spirals, lines of force — and that the patterns are guiding the runoff. You notice that the cliff-face two valleys over has windows.

The architecture is grown more than built, or perhaps it is built at a scale and slowness that looks like growth. Walls rise out of the basalt without seams. Bridges arc over the tarn-fields on legs no thicker than a finger and bear the weight of laden animals. Roofs are panes of something that looks like ice but is warm to the touch and gathers light from the day to release it at night, so that the dwellings glow softly from within after sunset, scattered across the dark plateau like coals in a fire-pit that has burned down to almost nothing. There are perhaps fifty thousand inhabitants spread across the whole city, which gives each of them a great deal of plateau. They prefer it that way.

The technology is not displayed. There are no machines visible, no vehicles, no antennae. But the water in the tarns is held at temperatures it should not hold. The bridges should not stand. The walls of the dwellings repair small damages overnight. When a herd of ungulates passes through what is technically a residential district, the inhabitants and the animals seem to know each other's positions without looking. There is a sense, walking here, that the entire plateau is instrumented down to the grain of the rock, and that the instrumenting is so old and so quiet that it has become a feature of the landscape rather than an imposition on it. The cushion-moss patterns are computation. The weeping walls are condensers. The fumaroles are powered, or are powering something. None of this announces itself.

The rule of the world, if you wanted to name one, is **slowness of intervention**. The plateau is being shepherded, gently, into being a plateau. Erosion will be permitted, but on a schedule. Rivers will be allowed to cut, eventually, when the inhabitants have decided where they should go. The animals are wild and the animals are also a garden. The cold is real and the cold is also a choice. Everything here is at an early stage, including the people, who consider themselves the first draft of a long settlement and behave accordingly — carefully, with the consciousness that what they do now will set the grain of the place for a hundred thousand years.

At dusk, on a clear evening, you can stand at the lip of one of the great basins and watch *the breathing* begin to rise. The fog comes up out of the wet ground in slow columns, individuated at first, like figures, and then merging into a low pale lake that fills the basin to a flat surface as smooth as mercury. The lit roofs of the dwellings shine up through it. The ungulates wade through it chest-deep, their breath adding to it. Somewhere a marmot pipes, and somewhere farther off the ice on a weeping wall lets go with a soft far boom, and the cold settles, and the plateau — wet, sparse, high, raw, vast, scattered, quiet, alive, attended — goes on being itself, very carefully, for a very long time.
