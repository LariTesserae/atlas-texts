# The Cress-Works at Thallow Bend

The town sits in a shallow bowl that wasn't there a hundred years ago. Somewhere upstream the river shifted — nobody recorded the night it happened — and now the Thallow comes through in three thin braids instead of one, cutting fresh channels through mud that hasn't yet decided whether to be bank or bed. The channels are narrow enough to jump. Between them, the ground is still raw: pale silt, root-stubs, stones the water has only just begun to round.

Lushness has moved in faster than the river has finished moving. Watercress grows in mats so thick you can walk out onto them before they sink. Alders and young willows lean over the braids, their trunks no thicker than a wrist but already crowded into copses. Moss covers the north side of everything — the sheds, the iron pipes, the abandoned cart — in a grey-green pelt that never fully dries but never drips either. The air holds its moisture like a held breath. Rain comes in brief, sideways drifts two or three times a day, enough to darken a roof and move on.

The town itself is maybe forty buildings, strung along the middle braid where the old mill stood before the river slipped. The mill is still there, cannibalized: its waterwheel gone, its stone shell now housing the boilers of the Cress-Works. This is what the town does. Watercress, fen-mint, brook-lime, and a pale bitter green called sorrel-of-the-stones are harvested from the braids, brought in wet-baskets to the Works, and there pressed, boiled, reduced, and canned in tin cylinders stamped with the name of a company in a city none of the workers have seen. The cans go out on a narrow-gauge rail that leaves the valley on a single trestle and disappears into fog.

The Works is loud. This is the governing fact of the place. Three riveted boilers hiss and clank through the day and most of the night; a steam-press thuds on a four-second cycle; a canning drum rattles; the little locomotive shunts empty flatbeds with a cough and a whistle twice an hour. The noise carries across the bowl and bounces off the low ridges and comes back slightly delayed, so that everything sounds like it's happening twice. Birds have learned to sing in the gaps. There are a great many birds — warblers in the alders, a pair of kingfishers working each braid, wagtails on the silt flats, herons standing with patient industrial indifference within arm's reach of the pipeworks. Frogs bulk up the dusk chorus. Water-voles have tunneled into the tailings heap behind the Works and treat the warm ash as a winter resort.

Between these busy points — the Works, the rail-trestle, the two rows of workers' cottages with their soot-streaked whitewash — the town is surprisingly empty. A field of new willow. A long stretch of silt where nothing has decided to grow yet. A single lamp on a pole at the crossroads, burning carbide, hissing its own small hiss into the larger hissing of the Works. You can stand in the middle of Thallow Bend and be a hundred yards from any human thing, and still not be able to hear your own voice over the boilers.

The unusual part — and every place like this has one — is the cress itself. The harvesters wear gloves not for the cold water but because a fresh-cut stem, held in bare skin for more than a minute, begins to root. Not dangerously. You notice a faint green thread in the lines of your palm, and you rinse it off, and it's gone. But the older women at the sorting tables have green in the beds of their nails that no scrubbing will lift, and the foreman's left forearm, where he rested it against a basket for a whole shift thirty years ago, carries a pale living tracery under the skin that flushes darker in spring. The company doctor from the city came once, took samples, and did not return. The cans go out anyway. People eat them in cities and presumably notice nothing, or notice and say nothing. The Works runs. The river, slow in its young bed, keeps depositing silt a finger's width at a time, and in another hundred years the bowl will be a meadow, and the Works will be a ruin in it, and the cress will have found somewhere else to be rooted and rooting.

For now: the cool damp, the loud machines, the sparse scatter of lit windows at dusk, the herons, the hiss, the green beneath the skin.
