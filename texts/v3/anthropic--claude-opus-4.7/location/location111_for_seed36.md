# The Greenhouse Cavern

Below the surface — not deep, perhaps the depth of a few tall trees stacked — there is a hollow the size of a small valley. It is lit. That is the first strange thing.

The light comes from the ceiling, which is not stone but a translucent lattice of pale green tissue, veined and membranous, glowing with a steady, shadowless brightness. It might be sunlight filtered through some impossibly thin sheet of leaf; it might be the tissue itself, phosphorescing with stored sun. The light does not change. It does not dim at one end or brighten at another. It simply is, at the intensity of a bright overcast noon, and it never stops being that.

Beneath this ceiling, plants have taken everything.

The floor is not earth — or rather, the earth is so far down beneath the packed root-mass that no one could reach it. The ground is roots: great braided cables the thickness of a thigh, woven over and under themselves, with smaller roots filling the interstices, and smaller ones filling those, down to a felt-fine mesh that accepts a footstep and springs back. Walking across it is like walking on a mattress stuffed with warm ropes. It gives off heat. The whole cavern gives off heat — the air sits at something like the temperature of a summer afternoon in a southern country, heavy and still.

The walls are plants too, rising in tiered banks: broad fleshy leaves the size of doors, layered like roof tiles; cascades of vine pouring down from ledges of bracket-fungus-that-is-not-fungus; pale trunks, smooth as candle wax, going up into the ceiling-canopy and disappearing into it as if the trunks and the ceiling were the same organism seen at different scales. Perhaps they are. There is no clear boundary between what grows from the floor and what hangs from the roof. Everything meets in the middle in a slow green press.

Moisture is intermittent. Condensation collects on the undersides of the largest leaves and falls, every so often, as fat warm drops — *plok* onto a lower leaf, *plok* into the root-felt — but there is no rain and no stream. In a few places the roots have cupped small pools, each the size of a washbasin, their surfaces skinned with something like duckweed. The pools do not seem to drain or fill; they are simply there, at a level that has been their level for a long time. A hand dipped into one comes out warm.

Sound is sparse. The plok of a drop. A slow creaking when some great leaf settles minutely under its own weight. The occasional soft hiss of a stoma venting. No wind — the air does not move, except where heat from the root-floor lifts it in slow columns you can feel against your face but not hear. No birdsong. No insect hum. Once in a long while something small moves in the understory — a pale, blind, segmented thing the length of a finger, or a slow shelled grazer the size of a fist working at a leaf-edge — but you might walk for an hour and see nothing at all living besides the plants. The fauna are rare here, and quiet, and seem not to have learned fear because there is nothing to fear.

Here and there, built things. They are small and old and not quite explicable. A low wall of stacked woody nodes, waist-high, running for twenty paces and stopping. A cairn of the smooth, seed-like stones the big vines drop. A single archway made of two bent saplings lashed with root-fiber, standing in the open, leading nowhere. No metal. No fired clay. No sign of tools beyond what teeth and hands could do. The builders, whoever they were, used only what the cavern offered and took nothing away. The structures have been absorbed a little — leaves creep over them, tendrils have found the knots in the lashings — but not much. Nothing here hurries.

And that is the last and deepest quality of the place: nothing hurries, nothing changes. The cavern is in equilibrium. The plants grow, yes — you can see new pale shoots — but for every shoot that emerges, an older leaf yellows and is drawn back in and digested, and the total mass, the total arrangement, remains. The light does not cycle. The temperature does not vary between day and night because there is no day and no night. The moisture condenses and falls and evaporates and condenses again at exactly the rate it did a thousand years ago. This is not a place being built, and it is not a place falling apart. The erosion that shapes ordinary landscapes — the carving of water, the cracking of frost, the slow grinding of wind — has never begun here and will never begin. The cavern is raw, in that sense: geologically unformed, pre-weather, a pocket the world forgot to finish working on. But in another sense it is utterly complete, a steady state that arrived early and stayed.

It extends. Walk in any direction and the ceiling of tissue continues, the root-floor continues, the tiered green walls recede into a soft green distance. There is no horizon, exactly — the light is too even for that — but there is a sense of openness, of a space that goes on for a mile or two before, presumably, meeting some boundary you have not seen. Somewhere in the middle distance, through the haze of warm still air, you can make out the suggestion of a larger trunk, a column-tree wide as a house, holding up its section of ceiling like a pillar in a hall.

You could live here. Something did, once, long enough to stack a wall and raise an arch. Then it left, or it became something else, or it is still here and has grown so slow and so quiet you would have to sit motionless for a year to notice it breathing.

The drops fall. The leaves hold. The light does not change.
