# The Cold Garden

Begin underground, but not deep — the kind of underground you find when a hillside has been hollowed by water and time, and the ceiling is only a meter or two of soil and rootmat above your head. The space opens just enough that you could stand if you wanted to, though standing is not really what this place asks of you.

The temperature is the first thing. It is cold the way a cellar is cold in midwinter, the cold of something that has not been warm in a long time and does not remember warmth as a possibility. Your breath would fog, if you were breathing. The cold is steady. It does not bite; it simply *is*, the way stone is.

The walls are not bare. This is the surprise of the place. In the near-total darkness — and it is dark, dark enough that the eye gives up and begins inventing shapes — the walls and ceiling and parts of the floor are covered in a vegetation that has adapted to require almost no light at all. Mosses, mostly, but mosses is too small a word. There are fronded things, layered like the gills of a mushroom but soft, and pale ropes of something filamentous that drape from the ceiling in slow curtains. Liverworts the color of bone. A kind of leaf, broad and translucent, that lies flat against the rock and seems to be doing something that is not quite photosynthesis. The growth is thick — you cannot put a hand against the wall without pressing into living tissue. In places it is layered four or five deep, the older growth beneath, half-mineralized, becoming part of the rock.

Water makes itself known by sound before sight. A slow tick of droplets falling from the ceiling onto leaf, onto stone, onto the surface of small still pools that have collected in the cupped places of the floor. Not a stream. Not even a trickle. Just the patient condensation of a cold place where air meets colder rock and gives up its moisture one bead at a time. The pools are perhaps a hand's width across, some of them, and lined with the pale fronds; the water in them is so still and so clear that you might not notice it until you touched it and found your finger wet to the second knuckle.

There is almost no sound beyond the water. No insects — there are no animals here at all, not even the small things you would expect in a wet dark place. No worms turning in the soil. No springtails. The vegetation has the field entirely to itself, and seems to have had it for a very long time. The only other sounds are the faintest creakings of the rock as it settles, and a sub-audible hum that you feel more in your jaw than hear.

The hum is the second surprise.

Embedded in the rock — and this you only see when your eyes have adjusted past the point of seeing into the point of *guessing* — are filaments. They are the same size as the moss-threads, and they grow through the stone the way roots grow through soil, but they are not roots. They are too straight in places, too geometric in their branching, and where one of them surfaces from the rock it terminates in a small, dark, lensed node that is unmistakably *made*. There are thousands of these nodes. Most are dark. A few, very few, hold the faintest pin of light — not enough to illuminate anything, just enough to confirm to you that it is there. A green so dim it could be an afterimage. A blue you might be inventing.

The filaments are old. You can tell because the moss has grown over many of them, and in places the moss has grown *into* them, the two meshes interpenetrating until it is unclear which is hosting which. The technology — and it is technology, though of a kind you have no name for — has settled into the rock the way a fossil settles into shale. Whatever it does, it does it slowly. Whatever it was built for, it has been doing it patiently for a span of time that makes the moss seem hasty.

You begin to notice the scattered marks of the people who put it here, if people is the right word. A flat place on the floor where the rock has been worked smooth, now half-claimed by liverwort. A small alcove cut into one wall, empty, its corners too clean to be natural. On the far side of the chamber — and the chamber is small, you could cross it in eight slow steps — a single object remains: a low pedestal of the same stone as the walls, with a shallow basin on top, the basin full of clear water, the water trembling very slightly with the hum. The pedestal is mossed over on three sides. The fourth, the side facing the basin, is clean, as though something maintains it.

The whole place is in balance. That is what you understand, sitting in it long enough for the cold to find your bones. Nothing is happening quickly. The water condenses and falls and pools and evaporates and condenses again on a cycle of hours. The moss grows by a thread's width in a season. The filaments pulse — you can feel it now, the hum has a slow rhythm, perhaps one beat every few minutes — and the lensed nodes brighten or dim by amounts so small you cannot be sure you are seeing change at all. Everything here arrived at its present state long ago and has been refining it ever since, smoothing itself like a stone in a streambed, except the stream is time and the smoothing is patience.

It is a garden. That is the word that comes, eventually, though no gardener tends it now and perhaps none ever did in the sense you mean. It is a garden the way a cave is a garden: by the slow agreement of water and rock and the green things that will live in any darkness if you give them long enough. Someone, once, set the cold filaments into the stone and arranged the basin and cut the alcove and went away. And the green came in afterward and the moss came in afterward and the water did what water does, and now the made and the grown are the same thing, and the place keeps itself, and the dark holds.

If you sit long enough, the pin of green light on the wall opposite you brightens, almost imperceptibly. It may be responding to you. It may be on its own schedule, indifferent. You will not be there long enough to know.
