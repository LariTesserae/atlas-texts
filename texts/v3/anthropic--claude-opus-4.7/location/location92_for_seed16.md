# The Glasshold

Beneath the salt crust of a dead sea-plain, in a pocket no wider than a small city and no taller than a cathedral, there is a place the surveyors of the outer world do not know about. You reach it — if you reach it — through a collapsed bore shaft, then a slant of dry tunnels that smell of hot stone and something sweeter, something like resin warming in an oven.

The Glasshold opens.

The ceiling is a dome of clear fused silica, some natural, some worked — you can tell the worked parts by the rounded scallop-marks of old hand-tools, patient chipping done over what must have been generations. Through the dome, no sky: only the pressed-down weight of the salt-plain above, lit from within by veins of phosphorescent mineral that run through the rock like lazy lightning frozen mid-strike. The light is the color of weak tea. It is never bright. It is never quite off.

The floor of the hold is a terrace-city, built in tiers cut from the native stone and from a pale, fibrous cement that the inhabitants — or former inhabitants, it is hard to say — made by baking certain tubers to ash and mixing the ash with the bitter sap of the groves. There are perhaps two thousand dwellings. You can count the rooftops from the upper terraces. And yet the city feels empty: the streets are wide, the plazas are wider, the houses are spaced as if each one needed to breathe. You can walk a full minute in any direction without passing a doorway. The density is that of a forgotten monastery, or a stage set between performances.

No water anywhere. Not a drop, not a droplet, not a trace of rime. The air is dry enough that your lips crack in the first hour, and hot — hot like standing too near an oven door, a steady 52 or 53 degrees that does not relent through what the inhabitants call day or night. Yet the vegetation thrives.

It thrives because it drinks something else.

The groves grow in the plazas and along the canal-beds (there are canals, carved carefully, but they have never carried water — they carry heat). The trees are squat, broad-leaved, with trunks like pumice and leaves like oiled leather. Their root systems tap directly into the hot stone and draw up mineral vapors — sulfur compounds, ammoniates, slow-rising volatiles from whatever deep chemistry keeps this pocket warm. They photosynthesize, after a fashion, using the dim phosphor-light of the dome; they metabolize sulfur the way ordinary plants metabolize water. Their sap is the bitter resin that built the houses. Their fruit, when they fruit, is a hollow pod full of dry crystalline seeds that rattle.

Between the trees grow lower things: rosettes of something like stone-cactus, tall fronds of a fern-analog whose leaves are actually thin sheets of silica secreted by the plant itself — living glass, brittle, chiming faintly when the warm drafts move through the grove. A cushion-moss of some kind coats the lower courses of every wall, grey-green, dense as felt, slightly warm to the touch. You could lie on it. You would not want to; nothing here wants to be lain on.

The vegetation is lush. It is everywhere. And it is utterly, completely silent of animal life. No insect hum. No bird. No scuttle. Nothing pollinates these groves except the convection of the hot air itself, which carries pollen and spores in slow thermal spirals from tree to tree. Ecology here is a conversation among plants and minerals and heat. The animals, if there ever were any, are not remembered.

But it is loud.

This is the thing that takes visitors longest to accept. The Glasshold is not a quiet place. It rings. The silica leaves chime against each other constantly, a high bright tinnitus. The dome sings — a deep slow tone as it expands and contracts with the thermal cycles, a note somewhere below the lowest piano key that you feel in your sternum more than hear. The resin in the tree trunks pops as it cures in the heat, sharp little gunshots in the middle distance. Somewhere a slab of phosphor-rock calves from a ceiling vein and shatters on a plaza floor with a sound like a dropped chandelier; this happens several times a day. The wind — there is wind, generated by the temperature differential between the dome (cooler) and the floor (hotter), a slow convection that circulates the whole pocket like a bellows — this wind moves through the empty streets and finds every chimney, every carved flute-hole (someone put flute-holes in the eaves of these houses, intentionally, centuries ago), and plays the city like an organ. The organ never stops. The city is its own enormous instrument, left running.

The rules of the place, as best one can reconstruct them:

Heat is the medium. What water is to ordinary ecologies, heat is here: it flows, it pools, it is drunk and exhaled. The plants mine it. The architecture channels it — you can see how the terraces are oriented to catch or shed thermal currents, how the flute-holes are tuned not for music but for cooling, and the music is a byproduct that the builders apparently valued and refined. Someone made this place livable by making it sing.

Time moves in the Glasshold, but slowly and without reference to any outside day. The phosphor-veins brighten and dim on cycles of roughly thirty hours, driven by some internal chemistry no one has mapped. The trees drop their silica fronds on a longer cycle, maybe annual, and new ones grow in over months, and the old ones accumulate in drifts at the foot of walls, and the cushion-moss slowly digests them back into the rock.

There is erosion, but it is the erosion of long-settled things: corners rounded, inscriptions softened to suggestions, thresholds dished by some long-ago foot-traffic into gentle bowls that now collect nothing but dust. The city reached its equilibrium and stopped changing, and now only decays at the rate of its groves' growth, which is slow, which is the rate of a held breath.

Who built it, and where they went, the Glasshold does not say. There are no bodies, no middens, no written records beyond the worn inscriptions on some doorposts that might be names or might be prayers or might be temperature readings. The houses are empty of furnishings; either the builders took everything with them, or they never had much, or the cushion-moss has eaten it.

The groves continue. The dome continues to sing its subsonic note. The silica leaves chime. The phosphor brightens and dims, brightens and dims. A pod rattles, somewhere, in a grove three terraces down, and the dry seeds inside it shift by a fraction of a degree, and that tiny sound, impossibly, carries all the way up to where you are standing, because the Glasshold is built to carry sound, because sound is one of the things that moves here in the absence of water, in the absence of animals, in the long hot patient dim.

You stay as long as your water lasts. Then you leave. The Glasshold does not notice.
