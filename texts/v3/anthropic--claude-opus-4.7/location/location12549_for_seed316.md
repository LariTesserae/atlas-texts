# The Last Shallows

There is a sea here that is not deep enough to be a sea. It covers a continent — perhaps more than a continent; I cannot find where it ends — and yet a person could walk across it without ever wetting the hem of their robe above the knee. The water is warm as breath. It does not move in waves so much as in *intentions*: long slow shudders that travel for hundreds of miles, raising the surface by a finger's width and lowering it again, as though the whole flat ocean were the skin of something asleep and dreaming.

The ground beneath is old past reckoning. Whatever mountains once stood here have lain themselves down so completely that the rock now reads as a single polished floor, veined and faintly grooved in patterns that look like riverbeds seen from above — except the rivers are inside the stone, fossil-water, water that learned to stop being water and became mineral instead. In places the floor weeps. It does not bleed water up through cracks; it simply *releases* it, the way a held breath is released, and the released water joins the shallow sea above and is indistinguishable from it within a second.

The air is thick. Not foggy — saturated. Every cubic foot is already carrying as much moisture as it can, so any small disturbance — a footstep, a temperature shift, the passage of one of the rare animals — causes immediate condensation. Tiny rains fall constantly in small localized patches, six feet across, ten feet across, falling for a moment and then ceasing. You can watch them pass like slow translucent jellyfish at chest height.

The light is dim and source-uncertain. There is no visible sun. The sky is a pearl-grey that brightens and darkens in regions rather than as a whole, so that one horizon might be twilight while another is something like late afternoon, and the boundary between them drifts. I want to say the light comes *up* from the water as much as down from the sky — and now that I have written it, I think that is true here. The shallow sea glows faintly from beneath, a slow phosphor in the stone-floor, and this is the only reason anything is visible at all on the darker days.

Vegetation is sparse and strange. Reeds — I will call them reeds — stand in scattered colonies, never crowded, each colony perhaps a hundred stalks within a circle the width of a house, and then nothing for a mile, and then another colony. The stalks are taller than they should be: thirty, forty feet, thin as fingers, swaying without wind. They are translucent at the tip and opaque at the base, and when you stand near them you can hear them clicking against each other in a register that seems to be inside your skull rather than in the air. There is also a kind of low mat-weed that grows directly on the wet stone in pale rings, dying at its center and expanding at its edge, so that the older the ring, the larger and emptier. The largest ring I have seen — I am writing as though I have walked here, and perhaps I have — was three miles across, and its dead center was bare polished stone the color of a bruise.

Now the sound. The sound is the difficulty.

The sound here is enormous and constant and has no apparent source. It is not the sea, which is too shallow to roar; it is not wind, of which there is little. It is a sustained *layered noise* that fills the entire continent at the volume of a waterfall heard from ten feet away. Bells are in it. Long bells, struck once and allowed to die for minutes. Also a sound like enormous slow breathing. Also a sound like a crowd in a distant language, never quite resolving into words. The noise does not come from any direction; it comes from *the place*, as a property of the place, the way color is a property of an object. After an hour in it your bones feel rinsed. After a day you forget that silence was ever a thing that existed, and when you try to remember silence you find that the memory itself has acquired a low humming.

The turbulence is not in the water or the air. The turbulence is in *what things are*. This is the part I have been delaying because I am not sure how to write it.

A reed-colony, watched closely, will sometimes be a flock of long pale birds instead, standing in the same arrangement, and then be reeds again, and the transition is not a transformation but a *correction* — as though the place had momentarily misremembered what stood there and then remembered correctly, except you cannot tell which of the two memories is the correction. The shallow sea, in places, is briefly a field of grass; you can walk through grass up to your knees and feel the blades against your skin, and then you are wading again and your legs are wet and they were always wet. Your own shadow occasionally arrives before you do. None of this is violent. It is the gentlest possible reality-failure, the failure of a very old and very tired world to maintain the distinction between its categories. The place is so deeply settled, so finished with the work of being itself, that the membranes between its kinds of things have worn thin.

Animals are rare and I have seen perhaps four kinds. There is a long-legged wader, taller than a person, that moves across the shallows with the slow exactness of a heron, and whose body when seen against the bright water looks like an absence in the shape of a wader rather than a wader. There is something that swims, or rather, that *displaces*, leaving a moving dimple in the surface but no visible body. There is a thing I will not describe because when I tried to look at it directly the looking did not finish. And there are the small ones — finger-long, translucent, abundant in the warm water near the weeping places, indistinguishable from the water itself except when they cluster, and then briefly visible as a shimmer with intention.

Built things: almost nothing. But not *nothing*. Every great while — every several days of walking — you come upon a low wall. The walls are made of the floor-stone itself, lifted and stacked, dark and smooth from immense age. They are never long; the longest I found ran for perhaps a hundred paces before ending in shallow water. They do not enclose anything. They do not lead anywhere. They are knee-high, or hip-high, and the water laps at them from both sides. I do not know whether they were made by a people or whether the place itself made them, in the way it makes the rings of weed. There are also, very rarely, single stones set upright — taller than a person, weathered to near-roundness — and these always stand alone, and the small condensing rains gather around them more thickly than elsewhere, so each upright stone wears a little perpetual local weather.

No tools. No fires. No marks of working on the stones beyond what time would do. Whoever placed the walls, if anyone did, placed them with their hands and left, and the leaving was so long ago that the difference between *placed by someone* and *settled into this arrangement on its own* has eroded along with everything else.

The rule of the world, as I understand it from writing this: nothing here is becoming. Everything here is *concluding*. The reeds are not growing toward anything; they are the final long form of what reeds are for. The sea is not going anywhere; it has arrived. The stone is not eroding further because there is no further to erode to. The animals are not evolving; they are the last possible animals, the shapes that remain when all the unnecessary shapes have been worn away. And the great sound — the layered roaring — I think now that it is the sound of a world that has finished, and is humming to itself, very loudly, in the long warm shallow afterward.

It is not sad. I want to be clear about this. The place is not mournful. It is *full*. There is no room in it for absence; every cubic inch of air carries water, every patch of stone carries the memory of stone, every moment carries the slight tremor of being briefly something else. It is the most occupied place I can imagine, and the occupation is gentle, and warm, and very, very old.

If you went there you would not come back changed. You would come back *rinsed*. You would carry the humming for the rest of your life, low under everything, and on quiet nights you would hear the bells.
