# The Cold Hollow

Somewhere under a sheet of green-black water, inside what seems at first to be a cavern but isn't, there is a room about the size of a chapel. Call it a hollow. It has no walls in the architectural sense. What contains it is kelp — but kelp that has been doing something for a very long time.

The fronds are thick as thighs and translucent at the edges, and they do not move the way kelp moves. They hold position. Each blade is rotated precisely a few degrees off its neighbor, so the whole inward-facing surface makes a kind of fluted dome, a bowl of living tissue some eight meters across and six high. The water inside the hollow is very cold — just above freezing — and very still. Outside, the current pulls and combs at the outer surface of the plant, but inside, nothing stirs.

The light comes down in shafts. It shouldn't; there is too much water above, the surface must be fifty or sixty meters up. But the plant is doing something to the light. Each frond, turned toward the center of the room, holds a faint gradient from base to tip, darker where it meets the stalk and almost clear at the margin, and the clear margins channel daylight downward as if piping it. The shafts that emerge are not the greenish gloom of the deep but ordinary afternoon light, the light you'd get through a kitchen window around three. It falls onto the floor of the hollow, which is a single wide leaf, concave, pale, lying flat like a dinner plate.

There are no fish. No copepods, no isopods, nothing crawling. A place like this should be dense with life, and it is scrupulously empty. The plant excludes them somehow. There is a faint chemical gradient at the threshold — you can taste it if you pass through, a cleanness that is almost astringent.

The sound is the strange thing. The hollow is loud. Not with water noise — the water inside is too still for that — but with the plant itself. A deep, slow, carrying hum, more felt than heard, coming from the stalks where they meet at the base. Underneath the hum there are clicks, irregular, in no pattern a person could hold in memory. It sounds structural. It sounds like a thing calculating, or remembering, or simply existing out loud at a volume that matches its age. The hum is loud enough to vibrate the small bones of the ear and to shift the surface of the water in the plate-leaf into very fine standing ripples, concentric, renewing themselves.

The plant is old. This is the part that resists description. The fronds are established in a way that suggests they have not grown into this configuration but arrived at it, settled into it across a duration that makes human architecture seem provisional. Where a frond meets the stalk, there is a thickening, a kind of joint, and the joints are calcified at their cores in patterns too regular to be accident — hexagonal lattices at the smallest scale, visible if you look closely, each cell of the lattice containing a darker point that might be a seed or might be a node of something else. The light piping, the sound, the exclusion of animal life, the temperature held steady against the surrounding sea: these are not accidents of biology. They are functions. The plant is doing work. What the work is for is not apparent.

Change, when it comes, comes slowly. Over the course of an hour spent inside the hollow, one of the fronds might rotate by a degree. The standing ripples on the plate-leaf might shift their spacing as the hum moves through a very long chord change. The light shafts, tracked against the floor, creep the way sundial shadows creep, but slower, because they are not tracking the sun exactly — they are tracking something the plant is doing with the sun's input, a slower calculation laid over the faster one.

If you were to leave and come back in a year, the hollow would be almost identical. In a hundred years, the fronds might have rotated into a new arrangement and the hum might be a semitone lower. In ten thousand, something would have been completed, and the hollow might open, or close, or bud off a second hollow nearby, or release what it has been holding. There is no way to tell from inside.

It is not a frightening place. It is cold and lit and clean and full of a sound like a cathedral thinking. A person could float there in the middle of it for a long time before noticing they had stopped moving, stopped needing to move, and were simply being included, briefly, in a process that was not theirs and would continue without them.
