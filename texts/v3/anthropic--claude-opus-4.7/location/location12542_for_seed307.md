There is a hollow inside a mountain, near the top, and that is where this place lives. Outside, the peak would be impossible to climb — sharp young rock still pushing upward, snowless because the stone itself is too hot, glazed in places where the heat has begun to vitrify the surface. But none of that matters. The place is inside.

The hollow is roughly the size of a small chapel, though its shape resists being held in mind — when you look away and look back, the proportions have slid. The walls are wet. Not damp: wet. Water beads on the dark stone, slides downward in slow ropes, pools in cupped places, and steams faintly where it meets the hotter veins. The air is near boiling and saturated. Breathing it would be like drinking. A person would not last long here. No person is here.

Light comes from the stone itself. Threads of something mineral, half-molten, run through the rock in patterns that look almost circulatory, and they glow a steady amber-orange — enough to read by, enough to see every detail with painful clarity. The shadows they cast do not always match the things casting them. A bead of water on the ceiling will throw a shadow shaped like a small curled animal. The animal-shadow will move when the water doesn't.

There are no plants. The closest thing to vegetation is a pale crust on the wetter walls — not lichen, not fungus, something simpler and older-seeming, a film that thickens and thins on a slow rhythm like a held breath. It is the only thing in here that could be called green, and it isn't really green; it just isn't any other color either.

And then there are the animals.

The hollow is full of them. Layered, near-touching, occupying every cranny and ledge and the air itself. They are small — the largest no bigger than a fist — and they are wrong in ways the eye has trouble cataloguing. Some have the bodies of beetles but the soft slow gills of something aquatic, opening and closing along their flanks. Some are little knots of muscle with no clear front or back, that move by inverting themselves. Some are translucent and you can see, inside them, organs that don't quite stay in one place — a heart that drifts upward, a coiled gut that uncoils into the next chamber over and is found, a moment later, inside a different creature entirely. They share. They borrow. The membrane between one animal and another is, here, negotiable.

They are almost completely silent. This is the strangest thing about the density. By every visual cue the hollow should be roaring with insect-noise, with the chitter and click and wet snap of a saturated ecosystem. Instead there is only the very soft sound of water moving, and beneath that, a sound that might be many small things breathing in near-unison, or might be the stone itself cooling and contracting somewhere far below. The animals open their mouths and nothing comes out. They strike at each other and the strike makes no sound. A predator the size of a thumb unfolds long needle-jaws and takes another creature whole, and the taking is silent, and afterward both of them — predator and prey — are sitting side by side on the ledge, intact, as if nothing happened, except now there is one fewer of one kind and one more of the other and the count has shifted without the event occurring.

The rules of cause here are loose. Time in the hollow runs forward, mostly, but it accumulates in pockets. A drop of water falling from the ceiling may take a very long while to reach the floor, passing through a stratum where it hangs nearly still, while around it generations of the small things are born and mate and are eaten. When it finally lands, it lands hot.

There is one made thing. Just one. On a ledge near what might be called the back of the hollow, there is a small tool — a flake of dark glass, volcanic, knapped to an edge, bound to a short bone handle with a strip of something dried and dark. It is the only built object. It is very old and also very new; the binding looks freshly wet. No hand is here to have made it. No hand could survive to have made it. It rests on the ledge and the small animals climb over it and around it and treat it as part of the rock, except that occasionally one of them — a particular kind, segmented, six-legged, with eyes set in a ring — will pause on the handle and stay there a long time, as if remembering something it could not have known.

The hollow is sealed. There is no passage in, no passage out, no crack to the surface. The water comes from the stone. The heat comes from the stone. The light comes from the stone. The animals came from somewhere but the somewhere is no longer accessible, perhaps was never spatial. They reproduce, in their loose way, and they eat each other, in their loose way, and the population stays roughly constant, and the pale crust thickens and thins, and the tool waits on its ledge, and the mountain pushes slowly upward around all of it, and none of it is witnessed, and that is part of how it works.

If you could put your ear to the outside of the mountain — which you cannot, the stone is too hot — you would hear nothing. The hollow keeps its quiet. Inside, the small creatures continue, dense as a held breath, and the shadows do as they like, and the single knapped blade gleams in the amber light, ready for a hand that the world has not yet produced.
