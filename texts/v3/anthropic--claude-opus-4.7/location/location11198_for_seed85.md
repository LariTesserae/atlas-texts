# The Stilled City at Vent-Depth

It sits at roughly four kilometers down, on the shoulder of a young seamount whose basalt is still glassy at the edges, unrounded, sharp as broken bottles where it meets the abyssal plain. The water here is cold but not freezing — a thin band of warmer current threads up from somewhere below, and the city is built into that band the way old terrestrial towns were built along a river. You can feel the temperature gradient if you move a hand through it: a coolness above, a coolness less-cool below, and between them a layer perhaps thirty meters thick where the city lives.

There are no plants. There never were. There is no light from above, and what light exists in the city comes from the structures themselves — a slow, dim luminance the color of weak tea, leaking from joins and seams as though the buildings were under low pressure from within and seeping photons the way a cracked pipe seeps water. The overall impression is darkness with constellations in it. You see the city before you see anything of the city.

The buildings are small — none taller than perhaps fifteen meters — and they are arranged in something like streets, though the streets are not for walking. They are corridors of clearer water between denser water, maintained by some principle of the architecture: the city sorts its own medium. Inside the corridors the water is so still that a dropped grain of sediment falls in a perfectly straight line, slowly, for what feels like minutes. Outside the corridors the water is merely still. Nothing convects. Nothing mixes. The current that warms the band passes around the city, not through it.

The construction is the thing that current physics doesn't quite cover. Up close, the walls appear to be made of a single material — a dark, slightly translucent substance with the heft of basalt and the surface of polished horn — but the material is patterned at scales that keep refining as you look closer. A wall is a wall, and then it is a lattice, and then within the lattice a finer lattice, and the recursion does not seem to bottom out at any structure you'd call atomic. Touch a wall and your hand reports back the temperature it expected to feel before touching. The buildings know what you anticipated and oblige.

There are perhaps two hundred structures. They are not identical but they rhyme — domes with flattened tops, low arcades, a few slender towers like organ pipes capped in matte black. Between them the spacing is generous. You could fit another city in the gaps and still have room. The sparseness is deliberate; the buildings do not crowd one another, and the corridors meet at plazas where nothing stands at the center, only a slight depression in the rock where the glassy basalt has been worked smooth into a shallow dish.

The ecology is almost nothing. A drifting amphipod every so often, pale and slow. Once, perhaps, the dim shape of a grenadier passing at the edge of perception, its eye reflecting the wall-light as a brief coin. A single species of small white shrimp seems to favor the warmer band and can sometimes be seen hanging in place near the towers, neither feeding nor moving, as though resting on an invisible shelf. They do not enter the buildings. Nothing enters the buildings. The buildings have openings — doors, you'd call them — but the openings give onto interiors that are simply darker than the exterior, and the eye refuses to resolve what is inside.

Sound is almost absent. The corridors damp it. A tap on a wall produces a tone you feel in your sternum rather than hear, and the tone does not propagate; it stops at the next wall. Occasionally — and this is the only thing that happens, in a place where nothing happens — a low pulse moves through the entire city at once, lasting perhaps two seconds, felt rather than heard, like a heart far away through stone. The interval between pulses is not regular but the pulses themselves are identical, and have been identical, one suspects, for a very long time.

Nothing changes. This is the dominant fact, more than the darkness or the depth. The city is in equilibrium with itself and with the band of warmer water and with whatever process keeps the corridors clear. No sediment accumulates on the rooftops. No wear shows on the basalt where the structures meet it, though the basalt itself is young and sharp elsewhere on the seamount — as if the buildings arrived after the rock cooled, and have been refusing time ever since. The amphipods drift in and drift out and their numbers do not change. The shrimp hang in place. The pulse pulses. The walls leak tea-colored light at a rate that, if you measured it, would be exactly the rate you measured last time.

The rule of the place, if it has one, seems to be this: the city is a held breath. Something built it to be a state rather than a process. Whatever it was for — observation, storage, waiting — the function has been delegated to the simple fact of persistence. To be here, unchanged, is the work.

You can leave. The corridors do not close behind you. Looking back from the abyssal plain, the city is again only constellations, very faint, in a great dark, and you cannot tell from any distance whether you are looking at something small and near or something vast and far, because the scale cues are wrong: the buildings are too regular, the spacing too clean, the light too even. It is the size of a village. It feels like the size of a thought.
