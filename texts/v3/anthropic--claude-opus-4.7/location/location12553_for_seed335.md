# The Condenser Hollow

There is a place inside the mountain — or what used to be the mountain, before it cracked open along a seam no geologist named — where the world has folded inward to make a single cold green room about the size of a cathedral nave. You would not find it from outside. The fissure that admits you is narrower than a person's shoulders and weeps constantly, so the moss around its lip has grown into a velvet collar that you part with your hands like curtains.

Inside: the chamber.

The walls are raw. The rock is young here in a way rock is not usually young — pale grey, sharp-edged, scarcely touched by weather, as though it had pushed up through the older stone last winter and was still settling into being a wall. Where you'd expect rounded shoulders and softened corners, you find facets. Knife-planes. Conchoidal fractures that catch the dim light and throw it sideways. Water beads along every surface, not running but condensing — the air in here is so saturated that the rock sweats it back out, and the droplets hang for long minutes before deciding to fall.

When they fall, they ring.

That is the first surprise. The chamber is loud. Not with weather, not with animals — with machinery. Somewhere deeper in the rock, behind a wall you cannot see, an engine is running. A great slow industrial heart, the kind of thing that belongs in a riveted hall in a valley town a hundred years ago: a low chugging, a hiss of released steam at irregular intervals, the syncopated clank of some reciprocating arm. The sound comes up through the floor and out of seams in the wall. You can put your palm flat against the stone and feel the rhythm in your wrist bones. No one built this. No one you could ask, anyway. The engine is simply here, the way a heart is here in a body that did not request one.

The floor is a shallow marsh. Three or four inches of clear, cold water over a bed of black grit and pale pebbles, with hummocks of sphagnum rising in islands. Cold enough to ache, not cold enough to freeze. From the hummocks grow ferns — uncurling, lush, more vivid green than the dimness should allow, as if they were photosynthesizing the engine-sound instead of light. Between the ferns: horsetails, liverworts, a single species of small white flower that opens only when the engine's pressure-release valve sighs, then closes again. You can watch them breathe with the machine.

The chamber is mostly empty. That is the second surprise, after the noise. For all the wet richness of its floor and walls, the volume of the place — the great vertical reach of it, up into a dimness where the ceiling is more rumor than fact — is almost entirely vacant. A few moths drift through that upper air, very slowly, their wings beaded with condensation. A bat hangs alone from a ledge halfway up, too far to see clearly. Otherwise the space is just space. Cold wet air, and the machine-pulse moving through it, and the occasional ringing fall of a drop from somewhere ungodly high.

The fauna keeps low. In the marsh there is abundance — newts the color of wet slate, with bellies like struck matches; a species of cave-crayfish, blind and pale, that you can watch sorting pebbles with the gravity of an old jeweler; clouds of springtails that pock the water surface when something disturbs them; a long, ribbon-thin fish that should not be able to live in water this cold but does, and seems to be eating the springtails, and seems also, when it surfaces, to be listening. Everything in the marsh listens. The frogs — there are frogs, fist-sized, the color of wet bark — call only in the gaps between the engine's beats, fitting their voices into the silence like a tenant into a furnished room.

You begin to suspect, after a while, that the engine is what makes the chamber habitable. That its waste heat is what keeps the water liquid; that its exhalations are what saturate the air; that the rhythms of the flowers and the frogs and perhaps even the ferns have shaped themselves around its tempo over however long it has been running. There is no chimney. There is no fuel pile. There is no operator. The seam in the rock is recent enough that whatever's down there cannot have been down there long — and yet the ecology fits it like a glove fits a hand, which means either the ecology is faster than ecologies are supposed to be, or the engine is older than the room that contains it, and the mountain has only lately cracked open to reveal what was already humming inside.

The light is dim and strange. It comes from two sources that don't agree with each other. Some of it filters down the fissure from the outside world, weak and grey and honest. The rest seeps from the rock itself in patches — a faint phosphor-green where certain mineral veins surface, brightest near the engine-wall, dimmest near the entrance. The greens disagree. Plants near the inner wall lean toward the rock-glow. Plants near the entrance lean toward the daylight. In the middle of the chamber there is a narrow band of confused ferns that have given up choosing and grow straight up, hedging their bets.

The rules of this place, as far as one can work them out by sitting still on a hummock for an afternoon:

The engine never stops, but its rhythm is not perfectly regular. There are longer pauses every so often — twenty seconds, thirty — in which the whole chamber seems to hold its breath, the flowers close, the frogs go silent, and the dripping is suddenly enormous. Then it resumes, and everything resumes with it.

Nothing in here is afraid of you. The newts will climb onto your boot. The crayfish will investigate your fingers. This is not tameness; it's that the chamber has only ever known one kind of large slow visitor, and that visitor is the engine, and you are merely a smaller version of the same category.

You cannot find the engine. You will look. The wall it sounds loudest behind is solid rock, sharp and young and unbroken, and when you press your ear to it the sound only gets less distinct, as though the chamber itself were the speaking surface and the source were nowhere in particular.

When you leave — and you will leave, because the cold gets into your knees — the moss-collar closes behind you, beaded fresh, and from outside the fissure you can hear nothing at all. Not the engine, not the frogs, not the ringing of the drops. Just the ordinary mountain, doing its ordinary mountain work, with this one small loud wet green secret folded up inside it like a letter no one wrote.
