# The Sough

It is dark here. Not the dark of caves — there is too much sky for that, and you can feel the sky pressing down like a warm wet palm — but the dark of a country that has decided, collectively and for reasons no one remembers, to do without sun. The air is body-temperature and humid. Breathing it is like breathing into a paper bag someone else has been breathing into for a long time.

The land lies low. A floodplain, mostly, though the word *plain* is generous: it is a vast shallow basin, twenty or thirty miles wide, where black water stands in sheets between hummocks of mud. The mud is new. You can tell because it is still soft when you press a hand into it, and because the small ridges the water cuts have not yet been rounded by anything — they have edges. Whole archipelagoes of edges. Walk and your foot finds a little cliff an inch high, sharp as a fresh-broken plate. The country is being born and it has not yet been worn down to a story.

Reeds grow, but sparsely — not the dense thatch you'd expect of marsh — single stems, or clumps of three or four, spaced as if someone had planted them with a ruler and then lost interest. Between them, bare mud. The vegetation is not winning here. Something keeps it sparse. Perhaps the fauna.

Because the fauna is everywhere.

You hear them before — well. You don't, mostly. That's the first wrongness. By all rights a marsh this thick with living things should roar: frogs, insects, the low conversation of waterfowl. Instead there is a hush. Things move in it. A long body slides through standing water twenty feet away and makes no more sound than a finger drawn across silk. Something with many legs walks across the mud behind you and you hear only the suck and release of each footfall, very polite. The animals here have learned, or were made, to be quiet. The hush is not emptiness. The hush is *populated*. You can feel them by the displacement of warm air, by the small heat-shadows when one passes close. Now and then two animals will meet and there will be a brief soft scuffle and then nothing, and you will not know which one walked away.

Across this basin: the engines.

They stand at intervals of perhaps a half-mile, dim shapes against the dim sky. Industrial things. Black iron towers webbed with pipework, squat brick houses with chimneys, the unmistakable hunched silhouette of a pumping station. They are working. You can hear them if you listen — a low reciprocating thud, *huh-chuh, huh-chuh*, like a sleeper's heart — and you can feel the ground take the beat through the soles of your feet. Pipes run between them, half-submerged in the water, weeping at their joints. Some of the engines glow faintly at their fireboxes: not light enough to see by, just enough to know that something inside is hot. No lamps. No windows lit. Whoever runs these machines runs them in the dark, or the machines run themselves, or there is no one and they have simply been running for a long time and will run for a long time more.

What are they pumping? The water, presumably. Or into the water. It is hard to say which direction anything moves. The pipes are warm to the touch.

There are houses too, scattered — a shack on stilts here, a brick cottage there, set up on the higher hummocks. Most are dark. One, very far off, has something like a candle in a window, but when you look directly at it the candle is not there, and when you look away you can see it again at the edge of vision. This is consistent with the rest. The country does not quite hold still when you stop watching it.

This is where it gets difficult to describe.

The reality here is thin, or wet, or imperfectly set — choose your metaphor; none of them are right. Things double. You will see a heron stand in the water, and as you watch, it will become apparent that the heron has been standing in two slightly different places the whole time, and the water has been accommodating both. Distances disagree with themselves: the engine you are walking toward gets closer for a while and then is suddenly behind you, still ahead, and you have to decide which one to believe. Your own footprints, when you turn to check them, are sometimes fresh and sometimes filled with standing water as if made hours ago, and sometimes there are more of them than you have taken steps, walking off at an angle you did not walk.

The animals seem unbothered by this. Perhaps they are part of it. Perhaps the reason they are so quiet is that sound, here, is one of the things that doesn't quite commit — you'll hear a splash a full minute after you saw the thing jump, or before, and the animals have given up on the medium entirely and communicate by some other means. Heat, maybe. Pressure. The slow shifting of warm air across warm skin.

The whole basin changes, but slowly. Nothing here is in a hurry. The water rises perhaps an inch in a week. A new mud-island lifts itself out over the course of months, sharp-edged and steaming faintly. An engine, after a year, will have moved — not been moved; *moved* — fifty feet to the east, its pipes lengthening to accommodate, the weeping joints relocating themselves along the new run. No one sees this happen. It happens between glances.

If you stand still long enough — say, an hour — you become aware that the dark is not uniform. There are gradients in it. Warmer dark over the engines. Cooler dark over the open water. A dark with a grain to it where the reeds are, and a dark that feels *looked-at* in certain places, which you learn to walk around. The sky, if it is a sky, has a faint structure of its own: not stars, but a sort of slow marbling, as of oil on water, which you can only see when you are not trying to.

It is, despite everything, peaceful. The warmth, the hush, the patient thudding of the engines, the soft sounds of the unseen abundant animals going about their unseen abundant lives. A person could lie down on one of the drier hummocks and sleep, and the country would accept them, and in the morning — if morning came, which it would not — they would be one more sparse standing thing in the long unfinished basin, with edges not yet worn smooth, being pumped slowly toward or away from something by machines no one tends.

The locals, if there are locals, call it the Sough. Or that is the sound the engines make, and I have mistaken it for a name.
