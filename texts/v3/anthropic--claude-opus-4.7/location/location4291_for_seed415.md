# The Lung of the New Mountain

High up — so high the air thins to a blue-cold that makes your teeth feel glassy — there is a mountain still deciding what shape it wants to be. The rock here is only days old in the geological sense: basalt slabs still warm underneath, quartz seams barely cooled into their cracks, whole cliff-faces that have not yet learned how to weather. No lichen. No talus slopes. No rounded edges. Everything is raw, sharp, unpitted, the color of fresh fracture.

And somewhere on its eastern flank, tucked into the rock the way a thumb tucks into a fist, is a hollow no larger than a modest chapel. You could walk its diameter in twelve paces if there were anywhere to put your feet.

There isn't.

The hollow is full of birds.

Not one kind — dozens. Slate-throated swifts, a species of pale-eyed martlet with translucent wingtips, small green parrots that shouldn't exist at this altitude, tanagers the color of wet rust, and something like a hummingbird but wrong, with four wings beating in cross-pattern. They roost in every fissure, on every ledge, on each other. The walls of the hollow are upholstered in feathers and bodies and the small constant movements of preening and shuffling and turning eggs. Density 2.4, yes — but the number undersells it. There is no square centimeter of stone that is not also bird.

The sound is a pressurized wall. It doesn't come at you; you enter it the way you'd enter water. Chirr and shriek and the rapid-fire clicking of the four-winged ones, the low chuckling of the parrots, ten thousand wingbeats layered into a single low thrumming drone underneath the higher calls. Speech is impossible. Thought arrives in fragments between peaks of sound.

The light gets in. The hollow has three mouths — one opening east onto a drop of maybe four kilometers, one opening straight up through a throat in the rock, and one small back-slit where the mountain breathes. Ordinary daylight, but cooled and filtered through feather-dust, so everything has the quality of light through a paper lantern. Things drift in this light: small downs, scales of mica shaken loose by wingbeats, vapor.

The vapor is the water. It comes from the birds themselves — breath and body heat meeting cold stone — and it beads on the walls in a constant slow condensation that the birds drink without ever leaving their perches. They tip their heads back and catch falling droplets in their open beaks. There is no stream, no pool. Only this cycle: breath, condensation, drink, breath. The hollow is a small closed weather system. When enough birds take flight at once, a soft rain falls for about thirty seconds.

And the vegetation — 2.2 of 3 — is not what you expected at this altitude. It grows *on the birds*. Tiny mosses, feather-lichens, a filamentous green alga that colonizes the oilier feathers of the older individuals. Some birds carry whole gardens on their backs. Miniature epiphytes, barely millimeters tall, with fine red fruiting bodies. The four-winged ones are almost entirely upholstered in a soft jade fur of moss; they look like flying thumbs of forest. When they die, their bodies become compost pockets that the younger birds pluck apart and scatter, seeding new gardens onto new backs. The ecology is the flock. There is no ground layer because there is no ground.

The built things are small and strange. Someone has been here. Not often, not recently — but there are six brass rings driven into the rock at calculated intervals, each about the diameter of a dinner plate, each inscribed with numbers in a system that uses both Arabic digits and something older. On the eastern lip of the upper throat, bolted into the basalt, is a machine: a black iron housing the size of a steamer trunk, with a flywheel, a set of bellows, and a copper tube that runs up through the throat and disappears. It is running. It has been running for a long time. You can feel its low vibration through the soles of your boots, under the bird-sound, a sub-bass pulse at maybe forty beats per minute. Something is being extracted. Something is being counted, or pumped, or harvested — the bellows draw feather-dust and vapor into the trunk at a steady rhythm and exhale a faint warmth out of its side vents. The birds ignore it completely. They have nested on it. Its flywheel turns inside a cage of twigs and down.

Whoever built this machine does not come here anymore, or comes so rarely that the visit hasn't happened in your lifetime. There is a small oilcan on a ledge. The oil inside is still liquid. A logbook would make too much sense; there is no logbook. Only the six rings and the one machine and the slow patient pulsing of whatever industry this is, embedded in the throat of a mountain that hasn't finished being a mountain yet, filled to saturation with birds that grow forests on their backs.

The rule of this place, if it has one: *nothing here touches the ground, because there is no ground yet.* The mountain is too young. The erosion hasn't started. No soil, no floor, no sediment. Everything must be held by something else — birds by stone, mosses by birds, machine by bolts, vapor by breath, sound by the shape of the hollow. It is an ecosystem of pure suspension, a held breath, a room inside a mountain that is itself a kind of held breath the planet hasn't finished exhaling.

Step inside and the sound takes your hearing. The birds do not move for you; they flow around you like a current around a stone. The machine pulses under your feet. The small rain begins. On the back of a four-winged creature hovering at eye level, a red fruiting body releases a puff of spores that catches the lantern-light and drifts slowly up the throat, toward wherever the copper tube is going, toward whatever is waiting at the other end.

You will not be here long. The air is too thin. But for the minutes you stay, you are inside something being born — a lung, a factory, a cathedral, a flock — none of these words quite right, all of them partly true, the mountain still deciding and the birds deciding with it.
