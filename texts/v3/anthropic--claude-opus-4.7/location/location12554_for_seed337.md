# The Undercity at First Quarry

You come into it down a ramp cut from pale limestone, and the first thing to understand is that the city is being born. Not ruined, not ancient — *new*. The stone is raw where the chisels left it, and you can still see the chalk marks for the next cut.

The whole settlement sits perhaps thirty meters below the surface plain, in a basin that wasn't there a generation ago. People are quarrying it into existence. Every wall is also a face of the pit; every house is a negative space carved from the bedrock. The deeper terraces are older — by which I mean a few decades, not centuries — and the upper rim is still being opened, masons working with bronze picks and antler wedges, hauling baskets of rubble up rope-and-pulley lifts.

It is cool down here. The stone holds a steady chill, and the air is damp enough that you can see your breath in the shadowed lanes but not in the sun-struck plazas. Water is the rarer thing. It comes in three ways: a slow seep down certain favored walls, where families have hung shallow catch-basins of fired clay; the occasional rain that pools in the lowest courts and is dipped out within hours; and one true spring near the basin's eastern edge, around which the first house was built and around which everything else organized itself. The spring is small. A person could cover its mouth with two hands. It feeds a stepped channel that runs the length of the main avenue and disappears into a cistern carved beneath the council-floor.

The density is the thing you feel most. Every surface is *used*. Where there isn't a doorway there is a niche; where there isn't a niche there is a relief carving, or a peg for drying herbs, or a tally-mark scored into the rock. Houses share walls — not because they were built next to each other but because they were *cut from the same stone*, the wall between them simply the thickness the masons chose not to remove. Ladders lean against every facade. Roofs are stone slabs laid across the tops of the cuts, and on those roofs people have laid out their seedling-trays, their dyeing pans, their stretched hides. Walk along the upper terrace and you walk on someone's ceiling, past someone's drying fish, around someone's child practicing knots.

Vegetation is sparse but stubborn. Pale lichens crust the damp walls in green-white plates. In the seep-gardens, narrow plots of mossy soil have been coaxed into growing a kind of cress and a low, fleshy plant with violet undersides. The roof-terraces hold pots of millet and a hardy onion. There are no trees in the pit itself; timber comes from above, hauled down in bundles, and is therefore precious enough that joinery is rare and most structures are stone, bone, or stretched skin.

Animals are uncommon. There are no dogs here — something about the close quarters, I think, or some old prohibition. A few cats slip between the terraces, kept for the small grey mice that find their way in through the rubble channels. Bats roost in the unfinished upper cuts and pour out at dusk in a quiet ribbon. Pale beetles work the dampest cracks. Once or twice a day, a hawk crosses the rectangle of sky overhead and every face on the terraces tilts up briefly to watch it.

The sound is the strangest part. With so many people in so small a space, you would expect clamor. There is almost none. The stone swallows voices. People speak softly because the walls return everything; a shout in one court is heard in twenty others. Children are taught early to use hand-signs across the terraces. The loudest sounds are the work-sounds — the *tock* of a pick at the upper rim, the slow grind of a wedge being driven, the splash when the cistern-keeper opens the channel at dusk. Between these, you can hear water beading on a wall and falling, one drop, into a clay basin three terraces down.

The pace, though — the pace is constant. This is not a sleepy place. The masons work in shifts from first light to last. The water-keepers measure and ration. The basket-haulers move up and down the lifts. Weather above sends its signals down: a darkening of the rectangle of sky, a sudden cool gust funneling along the avenue, and everyone moves to set out the catch-vessels. After rain, the whole basin smells of wet limestone and the cress beds flush a darker green within hours. At night the temperature drops further and the dampness condenses into a fine film on every surface, which the morning shift wipes from the tool-handles before beginning.

The rules of the place, as I understand them:

The spring belongs to no one and everyone. The cistern is opened twice a day by a rotating keeper. No wall may be cut without the council's mark, and the council meets on a floor of polished stone above the cistern, where the acoustics are such that a whisper from the center reaches every seat. The deepest house — the spring-house, the first one — is kept empty now, used only for the naming of children. New cuts are always begun at the upper rim, never below; the city grows outward and downward together, but the downward growth is slow and ceremonial. A mason who breaks through into an unexpected void must stop work and call the council, because no one knows yet what lies in the rock below, and the city is, after all, still emerging.

Stand on the highest terrace at midday. The sun comes down in a clean column. The basin holds its people like grain in a measure — packed, quiet, working. The pale walls are still soft enough at their edges to take a thumbprint. Somewhere below you, water moves through a channel a thumb wide, and someone counts it.
