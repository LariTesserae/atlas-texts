# The Condensery

It sits on a high terrace of weathered limestone, two thousand meters up, where the trade winds dump their moisture against a long wall of older mountains. The plateau has been here so long it has forgotten its own edges; the stone is rounded everywhere, lipped and lapped into shallow basins, runnels, weeping fissures. Water seeps from the rock face above the town in a continuous beaded curtain and pools in the streets — not flooding, just *present*, the way breath is present in cold air. The cobbles are dark with it. Every wall wears a green-black skirt of algae up to about waist height, then dries into pale ochre above.

The town is called, in the local usage, the Condensery, though no one alive remembers when it stopped being a proper name and became a description. It has perhaps four thousand inhabitants and several hundred chimneys. From a distance it reads as a single layered terracing of slate roofs and brick stacks climbing the south flank of the terrace, threaded with the fat copper veins of the condensation mains. Up close it resolves into wards, each ward built around a Drum.

The Drums are what make the noise.

Each one is a riveted iron cylinder some twelve meters across, set vertically into a brick housing, and inside each one a counterweighted piston rises and falls on a four-second cycle, drawing humid air through cooled brass lattices and expelling distilled water into the cisterns below. The Drums never stop. They have not stopped, by ordinance, in one hundred and forty years. The sound is a deep regular *whump—hisssss—whump* that you feel in your sternum from a kilometer away, and within the town itself it is unrelenting — a layered, slightly-out-of-phase chorus of forty or fifty engines breathing together, with the higher frequencies of their valve trains and pressure-relief whistles riding on top like a wire harp being struck at random. People here speak loudly and close to the ear, or they sign. Children learn to read lips before they read letters. The deaf are not pitied; they are sometimes envied.

Vegetation is thin for a place so wet, and this is the first oddness. The terrace stone is too smooth and too saturated with mineral salts brought up by the condensing engines' brine outflow — a slow industrial sweat that has, over generations, glazed the upper soil. So instead of forest you get scattered cushion-mosses in the basins, the occasional iron-rooted juniper clinging in a crack, and long stretches of bare stone shining like wet pewter. Between the wards, where the town thins, you can walk a hundred meters and meet nothing but rock, water, and the trembling air.

The fauna is correspondingly sparse. There are no songbirds — the Drums drown them, and besides, there is little to eat. What you do see: pale, almost translucent salamanders in the runnels, who have learned to hunt by vibration and not by sight; a single species of large black-and-white corvid called locally a *clapper*, which has adapted its calls to the pauses between Drum-strokes and which times its mating displays to the quarterly maintenance shutdowns; and, in the cisterns, a kind of blind freshwater shrimp that the townspeople harvest with fine nets and eat fried in juniper oil.

The light is ordinary — a high, clear, warm light most days, slightly hazed by the perpetual mist that the Drums and the seeps put into the air. At sunset the mist catches red and the whole terrace seems to be made of copper and rose-water. At noon it is simply bright and warm, and the wet stones steam gently.

The strangeness, when you look for it, is in the rhythms. The Drums set the metronome for everything. Bread is risen in four-second proofs counted off the master Drum in the cathedral ward. Conversations have the cadence of the cycle, with stresses falling on the upstroke. The town's two clocks — there are only two, and they are ceremonial — are wound to Drum-time, not sun-time, and drift slightly with the seasons. Children born in the Condensery are said to have a particular pulse, slower than lowlanders', synchronized to the engines, and this is not entirely a folk belief; the town doctor has measured it.

And the dynamic of the place is slow, almost geological. The Drums are old. The stone is old. The town changes by accretion: a new course of brick on a chimney every decade, a copper main replaced section by section over a generation, a Drum rebuilt in place around its own piston while the piston keeps moving. Nothing is ever shut down all at once. A child born here will, in their lifetime, see perhaps three Drums refurbished and one new ward added at the western edge, and that is considered a tumultuous era.

Strangers find it unbearable for the first three days — the sound, the wet, the way the ground hums. On the fourth day something in them either breaks or settles, and they begin to hear the Drums as silence, the way one hears one's own heart. After that, they stay, or they leave forever, and they cannot say in either case quite why.

The Condensery, the locals say, is not a town that makes water. It is water that has slowly, over a hundred and forty years, learned to make a town.
