# The Stilling

At this altitude the air has forgotten how to carry weight. You breathe and nothing arrives in the lungs but a thin, mineral coldness that tastes like the inside of a bell. The sky is not blue. It is the color of old pewter scratched by someone who has since stopped scratching, and it holds that exact shade from what should be dawn to what should be dusk, though neither happens here.

The plateau goes on. Not infinitely — you can see its edges, where it falls away into cloud-banks that never move — but far enough that the word *valley* fits it only by courtesy. Perhaps twelve kilometers across. Perhaps less; distance lies here. The ground is a pale, pitted limestone the color of bone-ash, fluted by some patient long-finished weathering into shallow runnels and basins, all of it softened, none of it sharp. The stone has been like this for a long time. It will be like this for a long time more. It is not eroding now. It has *eroded*, past tense, complete, and what remains is the form the wind agreed to leave.

Across this stone: standing waters. Not pools — *plates* of water, perfectly level, held in the basins, none of them deeper than a hand. They do not ripple. They do not evaporate. When you look down into one you do not see your face; you see the pewter sky, and beneath the sky, very faintly, the sky's other side — as though the water were a window cut through the world to the same heaven seen from beneath.

This is the first wrongness, and the smallest.

Vegetation, where it occurs, occurs as single plants. A cushion of something like saxifrage, gray-green, the size of a closed fist, alone on a slab of stone. Then nothing for two hundred meters. Then another. Then nothing. They are not arranged but they feel arranged, the way scattered punctuation in a sentence feels arranged by the absent words. None of them flower. None of them seed. They are simply present, and have been.

The sound is the thing that breaks people, when people come, which they do not.

It is loud here. Constantly loud, though there is no wind to speak of and no water moving and no animal large enough to make a sound that would carry. The noise comes from the stone — or from above the stone — or from the standing waters — accounts would differ if there were accounts. It is the sound of a vast room being struck: a low continuous booming, layered with a higher singing tone, like a wineglass rubbed by a wet finger, and underneath both a kind of breathing that is not the listener's. The volume does not change. The pitch does not change. It has been this sound since before the stone took its current shape and it will be this sound after.

Animals: rare to the point of suggestion. There is a creature here that resembles a hare made of slate-colored felt. It is seen by no one because there is no one to see it, but it exists, sometimes, in the middle distance, motionless beside one of the plate-waters, and if you were to walk toward it the distance between you would not close. There is something with wings, very high, that does not flap. It may not be alive in any sense you would accept. It has been up there.

The built things: there are seven of them, that can be counted. Low walls of the same bone-stone, knee-high, enclosing nothing — not circles, not squares, just *runs* of wall, fifteen or twenty meters long, beginning and ending without ceremony in the open plateau. They were stacked by hands. The hands are not here. The walls cast shadows in the dim pewter light, and the shadows fall in directions that, taken together, do not agree on where the sun is. This is the second wrongness, and it is larger.

The third wrongness is the one you only notice if you stay.

Nothing here is happening. Not in the way that a quiet meadow is "not happening" while bees work and grass grows. *Nothing* here is happening. The water does not evaporate or freeze, though it should do both. The plants do not respire. The booming sound is not produced by anything that is currently producing it; it is simply the sound the place makes, the way a number is the number it is. If you set a stone down on another stone it will be there forever, because *forever* and *now* are the same word in this grammar. The plateau is not preserved. It is not frozen. It is *finished*. It reached the shape it was going to reach and the world, here, agreed to stop drafting.

And yet: damp. Always damp. A film of condensation on every surface that is neither growing nor drying. Your hand comes away wet. The wet does not run. The droplets sit where they sit. If you pressed your palm to the stone and lifted it away, the print would remain — not because the stone is soft, but because the print is now one of the things that is true here, and truths, here, do not leave.

The cold is the cold of a deep cellar in a house no one has entered for a century. It does not deepen at night because there is no night. The dim pewter does not shift. Stars, if there are stars above the pewter, have not been confirmed.

People who have tried to map this place have produced maps that agree on the edges and disagree on everything else. The seven walls are in seven different configurations on seven different maps. The plate-waters move between cartographers without moving between visits. The slate-felt hare is on every map, in the middle distance, always.

It is called, by the few who have reason to call it anything, *the Stilling* — not because it is becoming still, but because it is the place where stillness lives, the way other places are where rivers live, or where pines live, or where weather lives. The Stilling lives here. It has always lived here. It is at home.

You could walk across it in a day, if days worked. You could stay a hundred years and find nothing more than what was here when you arrived — and nothing less, either. Every cushion-plant, every droplet, every length of low wall, every note of the long booming chord, exactly where it was, exactly as it was, waiting in the way that only things that are not waiting can wait.

The wind never comes. The wind was here once. The wind finished.
