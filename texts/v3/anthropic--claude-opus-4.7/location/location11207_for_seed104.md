# The Drowned Plateau

Begin, perhaps, with the fact that there is no surface. Or rather: the surface is a convention agreed upon by the things that live here, a horizon negotiated between water and what used to be air. You ascend the plateau and you are still submerged. You descend into the warm gulfs at its base and you are still submerged. The water does not end. It only changes its mind about density, about temperature, about whether light belongs in it.

The plateau itself is old past meaning. Its stone has been worn into the smoothness of a tongue, of a worn coin, of something handled by a slow god for an unhurried eternity. It rises — and the rising is enormous, kilometers of warm-water column lifting above the deeper cold — but it rises gently. Whatever sharpness this place once had has been argued away. The summit, if you can call it that, is a near-plain, broad enough that weather forms over it: warm internal currents that behave like winds, a slow rotation of thermoclines, sometimes a kind of rain made of marine snow falling upward through layers of differently-salted water.

Things grow here. Not coral exactly — coral is too architectural, too declarative for this place. What grows is softer, more saturated. **Kelp-analogues** with leaves the size of cathedral doors, hanging in the warm dim like banners no one came to read. **Floccose forests** where the vegetation is mostly suspension, particulate clouds of living matter that you can pass your hand through without resistance and that close behind you with no record of your passage. **Stalked things** that might be plants or might be the slowed-down bodies of animals that forgot to move. The density is layered: at one elevation, a meadow of pale frond-shapes; a few meters up, a canopy of darker fans; above that, the marine-snow strata, where downward-and-upward fall meet and cancel.

The light is dim, perpetually. It is the light of late afternoon held forever. It does not come from above — or it does, but also from below, also from inside certain stones, also from the leaves themselves in a tired bioluminescence that suggests the leaves are not so much glowing as remembering having glowed. Shadows here are not opposite to light; they are a slightly different color of light. You cannot use them to find direction.

The sound is almost nothing. Submerged places are louder than people expect — the click and crackle of small lives, the boom of distance — but here the fauna is rare, the small lives mostly absent or extinct or having moved on to other modes of being. What you hear is the long low pressure-hum of the water itself, and occasionally a slow soft thud that might be the plateau settling another half-millimeter into its final shape, or might be something enormous and very far away turning over in sleep. Once an hour, perhaps, a chime — no source, no echo, just a single clean note as if a bell in another world rang and a little of it leaked through.

Because this is where the weirdness lives: the reality of the place is thin. Not broken — *thin*. Worn through in places by the same patient erosion that smoothed the stone. You will find:

- **Currents that flow into themselves** and emerge having taken longer than they should have. A frond bent by such a current stays bent for hours after the current has gone, then suddenly springs back as if catching up.
- **Pockets of dry air** the size of rooms, held inside the water by nothing, with their own small weather and sometimes their own small unfamiliar stars on the ceiling.
- **Places where the plateau is two plateaus**, overlapping. You walk a smooth ridge and feel, under your hand, a different ridge that is not there, slightly colder, slightly differently shaped, and if you press your attention into it you can sometimes choose which one to be standing on.
- **The marine snow occasionally falls as language** — particulate matter that arranges into glyphs in a script no one taught it, drifts for a while, then dissolves. The glyphs are not consistent. They are not a message. They are the sediment of meaning that has nowhere else to settle.

There are structures, but few, and old. A **low wall** of fitted stones runs for perhaps a kilometer along one shoulder of the plateau, then stops, having forgotten what it was enclosing. **A single doorway** stands free in the open water, its lintel furred with growth; you can swim through it from either side and the water on the other side is the same water, except that the temperature is wrong by half a degree, and stays wrong. **Cairns**, in a few places, built by someone — too neat for accident, too small for shelter. Each cairn has at its base a hollow, and in some of the hollows there is a smooth round stone that, when lifted, is warmer than the water around it. No one has been here to maintain any of this in a span of time that the plateau itself would consider long.

No tools. No devices. Whatever made the wall, the doorway, the cairns, made them with hands or with something that served as hands, and made them once, and went away or became something else. The plateau does not remember them with any particular fondness. It does not remember them with anything.

The fauna, when it appears, appears slowly. A **long pale shape** moves through the canopy of fronds — too long to see whole, glimpsed in segments, never resolving into a body. A **single eye** opens in the side of a boulder, considers nothing in particular, and closes again over the course of a quarter hour. Once, a small school of fish — only they are not fish, they are the *idea* of fish, translucent and slightly out of register with themselves, swimming past at the speed of contemplation. The rarity is not absence. It is reticence. The animals are here; they simply have very little to do, and have learned to do it slowly.

And yet the place is active, alive in its way. The thermoclines turn over. The marine snow falls and is eaten by the floccose forests and is excreted back into the column as light. The doorway's wrong-temperature water seeps out and is corrected by the bulk current and seeps out again, an endless small disagreement. The two overlapping plateaus shift their overlap by perhaps a meter each century. The bell that is not there rings on its slow uncountable schedule. The hum continues. The plateau settles. Somewhere in the warm depths below, something turns over again.

This is the world at the end of its erosion, having become almost only itself — the thinnest possible version of a place, worn through to its underlying strangeness, still warm, still wet, still going. There is no urgency here, and no despair either. The plateau has arrived. It is doing what arrived things do, which is to continue, with great patience, in the shape it has finally agreed to be.
