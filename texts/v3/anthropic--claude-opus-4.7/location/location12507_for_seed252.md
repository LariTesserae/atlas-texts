# The Bright Room Beneath

You come into it through a fissure that should not admit a person, and yet does — the rock parting at exactly your width, as if it had been measuring you for a long time.

Inside: a chamber maybe thirty meters across, low-ceilinged, hot. The first thing is the light. It comes from everywhere and nowhere — not from a source you can point at, but from the walls themselves, which carry a steady glare like the inside of a sun-bleached tent at noon. Bright enough that you squint for the first minute. Bright enough that there are no shadows, only places where the light is slightly less. You feel it on your skin as warmth, but the warmth doesn't quite match the brightness; something is being filtered, redirected, presented.

The walls are wet. Not running, not dripping in any hurried way, but beaded — a fine sweat of clear water across surfaces of pale mineral. In places the beads gather and slide, leaving glistening tracks that take a long time to dry. The rock is young. You can see it: edges still sharp, fracture planes uneroded, crystal faces meeting at angles a stream would have softened in a thousand years. This place hasn't had a thousand years. Maybe it hasn't had ten.

The heat sits around 38, 40°C — close to blood, and humid with all that surface moisture, so it presses on you the way a held breath presses. Sweat starts immediately and goes nowhere. You learn quickly to move slowly.

The floor is the same pale stone, faintly concave, a shallow basin. At its lowest point a pool has gathered, maybe a meter across, perfectly still. The pool reflects the radiant walls and so seems to be another opening into the same brightness, a window downward.

Now: the strangeness. There is no source of light, but the light is steady. There is no obvious heat source, but the heat is constant. The water beads form, slide, evaporate, and form again at a rate that — if you watch long enough — is faster than diffusion alone should manage. Somewhere within the rock, or within the substance of the rock, something is doing work. The walls, examined closely, show a faint regularity in their crystal structure — too faint to call a pattern, but your eye keeps wanting to find one. Whatever maintains this room is the room. The engineering is not on the rock; it is the rock, at a scale below seeing.

You will not figure out who made it. You may not even be sure it was *made* in any sense you'd recognize — it might have grown, or precipitated, or been *intended* by some process whose verbs we don't have. The room behaves like a tended thing. There is no tender.

The ecology is sparse but present. On the wettest parts of the walls, in the cooler corners (cooler being relative — perhaps 35°C there), thin smears of something biological have established themselves: not plants, not quite fungi, more like a varnish of single-celled life, glossy and faintly tinted ochre. It catches the light differently than the stone. There are perhaps a dozen such patches in the whole chamber. They do not seem to be spreading; they do not seem to be dying. They have found their terms.

And there is one animal, that you see. A pale arthropod, the length of a thumbnail, many-legged, the color of the wall. It comes out from a seam, crosses six inches of stone with great deliberation, stops at a bead of water, drinks for what feels like a long time, and retreats. You wait, and after perhaps twenty minutes another emerges from a different seam and does the same. Or it might be the same one. You can't tell. There are very few of them, and the room is enough for them.

Sound: almost nothing. The faint *tick* of a water bead releasing from the ceiling and falling — rare, maybe once a minute. A whisper that might be the walls themselves, or might be your own blood in your ears under the heat. When the arthropod moves, you can hear its feet, which tells you how quiet it really is.

Dynamic, though. The room is doing things. Water cycles continuously between wall and air and pool. Heat moves in slow convective rolls you can feel against your face when you turn. The light brightens almost imperceptibly and dims again over — you'd guess — a period of an hour or two, a slow tidal pulse. The biological patches respire; the arthropods drink and retreat; somewhere, the rock itself is presumably still growing, the chamber still forming. Nothing is static. Nothing is hurried.

The rules of the place, as best you can read them: it maintains itself. It admits very little and loses very little. It supports a small ecology at the lowest viable density. It is hot and bright and wet and quiet, and it has been so for some span of time that began recently and shows no sign of ending. You are the largest thing in it by orders of magnitude, and the room has accommodated you without adjustment, which is either generous or indifferent — you can't tell which, and the distinction may not apply.

When you leave, the fissure closes behind you to its previous width, or to whatever width it had been before measuring you. You will not find it again. The hillside outside is ordinary: dry grass, a hot afternoon, cicadas. You will think about the room for years. You will not be able to explain, even to yourself, what it was *for*.

Possibly nothing. Possibly that's the wrong question. Possibly the room is simply one of the things this world quietly does, in places no one looks, at a scale and with a craft that doesn't require witnesses, and you happened to slip inside one for an hour.
