# The Worn Plateau

Begin with the altitude. You are very high — high enough that the air thins in the lungs of anyone who climbs to meet this place, high enough that the sky loses its blue at the zenith and tends toward the dark violet of something close to vacuum. But the plateau itself is not jagged. That is the first strangeness: a place this elevated ought to be sharp, new, geologically young. This one is old past reckoning. It has been at this altitude so long that the mountains it must once have been have worn themselves down into broad, soft undulations, like an animal that lay down and dreamed for so many ages that its bones became hills.

The stone is the color of wet slate where it shows, but it mostly does not show. Most of it is wrapped in a skin of moss — not the bright emerald of low wet places but a deep grey-green, close-lying, the kind of moss that grows one millimeter per century and has therefore covered everything. Lichen rosettes the size of dinner plates, then the size of tables, then the size of courtyards, map outward across the flats in concentric rings. Each ring is another thousand years of patient radial growth. Walk across them and you are walking across clocks.

The temperature is always just above freezing. This is important to the water. There is no snow cover — the plateau is too dry for that — but there is condensation everywhere, beading on the moss, beading on the stone, running down in thin films into the hairline fissures. In the low spots, reflecting pools gather. They are perfectly still because there is almost no wind at the ground — the wind is higher, inaudible, streaming in the violet sky above. The pools are black from depth and from the dark stone beneath. You can kneel by one and see your face, and behind your face the sky, and behind the sky something else that you decide not to look at too long.

The vegetation beyond the mosses is sparse but emphatic. Every few hundred meters, a tree. They are not a species you can name. They have the bearing of bristlecone pines — twisted, half-dead, the living bark a narrow ribbon spiraling up an otherwise silvered trunk — but their needles are a pale translucent blue-green and hang in long curtains, and water beads along them, and drips, and each tree stands in a faint halo of fog of its own making. Between the trees: groundcover of tight cushion plants, star-shaped succulents filled with antifreeze sap, and in the wet hollows, ferns whose fronds uncurl at a pace you can see if you watch — not fast like time-lapse, but perceptibly, a slow gesture, the way a hand opens.

There is no animal life. No birds overhead, no insects in the moss, no fish in the pools. This is not a dead place — the plants are clearly alive, the water moves, weather happens — but the branch of the tree of life that walks and flies and crawls never arrived here, or arrived and left, or was subtracted. The quiet that results is distinctive. You can hear water, you can hear the occasional tock of a stone contracting in a cold current of air, you can hear your own breath loud in your ears. You cannot hear the small constant chitter that, elsewhere, is the background noise of a living world. Its absence is a presence. It rings.

Now the built things. The plateau has a city on it — or the plateau has cities on it, in the way that an old forest has mushrooms. They are scattered. You can walk for a day between them. Each one occupies a few square kilometers of the gentler slopes, and each one is made of the local stone, drystacked, the walls chest-high to shoulder-high, laid without mortar by hands that understood the grain of the rock. Towers go up three, four, five stories, tapering, with ladder-holes instead of stairs. Roofs are slabs. Windows are simply square absences, sometimes with a stretched hide across them, sometimes open to the weather. Everything is covered in the same slow moss, so that the cities look grown rather than built, the way coral reefs look grown.

No one lives in them right now. But they are not ruins. They are kept. Somebody sweeps the plazas — you can tell by the lines in the lichen. Somebody repairs the walls when a stone falls; you can find fresh stones set in with new-cut faces. Somebody leaves offerings in shallow stone bowls at thresholds: small heaps of the blue-green needles, a cupped palmful of rainwater, a single dark pebble. The tech of these builders is pre-metal: the tool marks on the dressed stones are the marks of harder stones and of bone, and the wooden elements (rare — wood is rare here) are bound with twisted sinew-looking cord that turns out, on inspection, to be the ropy runners of a plant.

And here is where the place starts to become itself.

The cities are arranged, across the plateau, in a pattern. Not a regular grid — something more like the arrangement of cells in a honeycomb that has been gently pulled in one direction, or the arrangement of rings around a central stone dropped in water a long time ago. Stand on a high point and look: they are spaced by some rule. And at the center of the pattern, if you walk toward it, is nothing. A wide flat. A place where no city has been built and no tree grows and the moss itself thins to bare stone. It is the size of a small sea.

In that flat, reality is tired.

This is the best way I can put it. The weirdness of this world is not monsters and not magic. It is attenuation. At the edges of the bare flat, the moss grows in patterns that are too regular — fractally too regular, the same motif at every scale — as if something were copying itself from a template and the template were showing. Walk further in and your shadow starts to lag. Not by much. A quarter-second behind you. Then half. Your shadow is thinking about catching up. Further in, and the horizon, which should curve away, begins to curve very slightly upward, so that you feel you are standing in a shallow dish whose rim is the rest of the plateau. Further in, and sounds arrive before the events that make them: you hear your foot strike, then feel it strike. Further in, and you do not go further in, because something in you understands without being told.

The people who built the cities built them around this. Not to contain it — nothing contains it — but at the distance where life is still possible and reality is still approximately itself. The offerings at the thresholds face inward, toward the flat. The shallow bowls are filled with rainwater not for drinking but because the flat takes water from them, slowly, even when it does not rain, and the builders long ago learned that if the bowls are kept filled the flat stays the size it is, and if the bowls empty for too long the flat grows.

The weather of the plateau is its own. At this scale — and the plateau is continental, it has no edge you can reach in a lifetime of walking, it has latitudes of its own — clouds form locally, in discrete cells, and drift. You can see one coming from a long way off: a single cloud, dark-bellied, trailing a grey skirt of rain, crossing an otherwise clear sky. It will rain on a strip of country a kilometer wide and then move on. The trees lean very slightly toward the direction these clouds come from, as sunflowers lean, because over enough millennia the prevailing rain is a kind of sun. In the far distance, you sometimes see weather systems organized around the bare flats — spiral arms of cloud, slow hurricanes a hundred kilometers across, rotating once a season. The flats are the eyes of these storms. Nothing falls on the flats. The flats will not accept it.

The dynamic of the place is slow but unmistakable. The mosses creep. The ferns unfurl. The pools fill and drain on a rhythm no one has yet measured. The cities are swept and repaired by hands you do not see, because whoever keeps them keeps themselves out of sight, or lives in a time that passes beside yours at a different rate, or is very few, very widely scattered, very patient. Once in a great while, at the edge of vision, you see a figure cross a plaza in a distant city, gather a stone, and be gone.

And overhead, all of this, the violet near-vacuum sky where no bird ever flew, where the wind streams silent at altitudes no breath could take, where sometimes at night — and the nights are long and very cold, and very clear — you can see the curvature of the world you are on, and understand that it is not the curvature of a planet. It is some other curvature. The plateau is a surface, and that surface is vast, and what it is a surface of is a question the old builders stopped asking so long ago that the answer has been worn down by their not-asking into the same smooth ancient shape as the hills.

That is the place. It has been this way for a very long time. It will be this way for a very long time more. The bowls are kept full. The moss grows its millimeter. The flats do not widen this year.
