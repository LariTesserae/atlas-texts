# The Sumphouse

You come to it down a flight of fitted sandstone steps that someone cut — you can feel how long ago by the hollows worn into their centers, smooth as the palms of hands. The air gets warmer as you descend, not colder, which is the first thing that tells you this place has its own weather.

The chamber opens at the bottom of the stairs and it is not large. You could walk its length in perhaps forty paces, though you won't, because the floor is crowded. Call it the size of a modest chapel, or a wealthy family's threshing barn. Everything in it was made by hands.

The walls are brick — small, dark, hand-thumbed bricks laid in a bond that shifts as it rises, the courses rippling where the makers compensated for some old settling. Between bricks, the mortar has been picked at by generations of small lives; it glitters with insect frass and spider-silk. The vault overhead is a low barrel, also brick, and sweating. Condensation beads up there, swells, falls. You hear it hit — *tock*, *tick*, *tock* — onto copper, onto pottery, onto water, onto the broad green pads of the sumpweed. The drip is constant but never rhythmic, a slow irregular percussion across the whole ceiling.

That's one layer of the sound. Under it: the crickets. Hundreds of them, maybe more, living in the brick joints and under the ledges and inside the clay pipes that run along the base of the walls. Their song here is not the open-field chirr you know but something denser, multiplied by the vault into a sustained ringing, almost metallic, that rises and falls in slow tidal swells as different populations take up and drop the call. Beneath the crickets, frogs — the small brown kind that live in the standing pools — pock and croak at uneven intervals. And beneath the frogs, a deeper register: the rustle and scrabble of rats in the pipe-works, the soft leathery shuffle of bats hanging in the corbels, and somewhere the slow drinking sound of something larger lapping at a basin.

The floor is a mosaic, or was. Most of it has been lifted by roots and the slow heave of groundwater; the tesserae are scattered now, gathered into drifts against the walls like the pebbles of a dry stream. Where the mosaic survives you can see fragments of a pattern — a scaled tail, a geometric border, what might be the edge of a pool that was once depicted and is now, by coincidence or inheritance, an actual pool. The water is two fingers deep there, clear, with copper coins at the bottom gone green and soft-edged.

Light comes from several shafts punched through the vault at long-ago angles — you can see where later hands tried to close some of them with grates or tile, and where roots from the surface world have fingered down through the openings anyway. The shafts throw columns of warm daylight down into the chamber, catching the humidity, so each column is a visible soft-edged pillar of illumination that moves with the afternoon. Between the pillars the air is dimmer, greenish, the color of looking up through pondwater.

The vegetation is sparse but emphatic. Sumpweed in the low pools, its pads as broad as dinner plates, blackish green, studded with drip-marks. A few ferns wedged into the brick courses where water runs reliably — not many, you could count them on two hands, but each one has colonized its crevice with great thoroughness. A single fig, or something fig-like, has come down through one of the light shafts and reached the floor; its roots spider across the bricks, thickening into cables where they find water. The air smells of that fig's sap, and of wet brick, and of the particular old-coin smell of water that has been in contact with copper for a very long time.

The fauna is everywhere once you look. The crickets you've already heard. Paperwhite moths in the shadowed corners, hundreds of them resting flat against the walls so that at first you take them for a patch of discoloration and then the patch, disturbed, lifts and reshuffles. A pale newt in one of the pools, watching you without urgency. Bats overhead, invisible in their crevices but occasionally letting a single one drop, open, and arc lazily across the chamber before tucking back up. Small fish — how did they get here? someone must have brought them, generations back — in the larger cistern at the far end, their backs just breaking the surface. And the rats, who do not hide from you exactly but who also do not come forward; they watch from the lips of the clay pipes with the composure of householders.

The rules of the place, as far as you can work them out from standing in it:

Everything that falls from the ceiling is caught. The floor is arranged — has arranged itself over long use — so that every drip lands in something. The vessels are mismatched: pottery bowls, a copper basin gone the color of oxidized jade, a stone trough, a half-buried amphora, the pools of the mosaic. Each makes a different sound when struck by water. The chamber is, in this sense, tuned, though no one tuned it on purpose; the tuning is what's left after centuries of people setting out whatever vessel they had and leaving it where it caught best.

Nothing is dry. Nothing is wet enough to flood. The balance was found long ago and holds.

The bricks are warm to the touch. Somewhere — you can feel it more than locate it — there is a heat source, perhaps a flue from a kitchen above, perhaps some geothermal seep the original builders knew about and built around. The chamber is a comfortable warmth, the warmth of an animal that has been sleeping in a bed. This is why the moths love it. This is why the frogs breed here year-round.

You get the sense, standing in the middle of it with the cricket-song rising and the drip-percussion going and a bat swinging through one of the light-columns, that the place is not ruined. That word is wrong. It has simply finished becoming what it was going to be. The people who built it are not here and may not have been here for a very long time, but the chamber went on without them, and arrived — patiently, through the slow negotiations of water and brick and small lives — at a form it could keep.

There is a broom in the corner. Someone was here recently. Someone, perhaps, is still the caretaker, coming down the steps every so often to shift a bowl that has filled, to sweep the tesserae into tidier drifts, to leave. The broom is new. The bristles are bound with waxed twine.

You could sit on the stone bench by the cistern and stay a while. The warmth is good. The sound is loud but not harsh — it has the quality of being made *of* many small quiet things rather than of anything loud in itself, so that after a few minutes you stop hearing it as noise and start hearing it as the shape of the room.

Eventually a drip lands on the back of your hand, and then another, and you understand that you too, if you stay long enough, will be counted into the tuning.
