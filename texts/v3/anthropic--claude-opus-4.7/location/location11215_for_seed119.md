# The Cistern-Town of Oss

There is a place called Oss, though no one alive named it that, and it sits low in a basin where three slow rivers used to meet before someone decided the meeting should be permanent. The town is the meeting. It is also the only thing here.

Oss is built — all of it, every surface — from a soft grey limestone quarried somewhere upstream and floated down, block by block, by people whose great-grandchildren no longer remember the work. The blocks were laid into a vast shallow bowl: a thousand basins, troughs, weirs, runnels, channels, sumps, and stepped pools, terraced into one another at angles you only understand if you stand still and watch the water move. The whole town is a cistern. Or the whole town is a body, and the cisterns are its organs. Both descriptions get used.

There are no houses here. There were never houses. Whoever built Oss did not live in it. They built it for the water and the things the water brought.

---

The stone has been weeping for a long time now. Every horizontal surface is slick with a thin black biofilm, every vertical surface furred with pale flowstone where lime has redeposited itself in slow drapes and curtains. The original carvings — and there were carvings, faces and spirals and what might be counting-marks — have softened almost to suggestion. You can still feel them under your palm. You cannot quite see them anymore. The town is in that state old built things reach when they have stopped being architecture and started being landform: the edges rounded, the joints fused, the whole thing settled into its own logic.

The air condenses on everything. You walk through Oss and your sleeves get wet from the air alone. Fog gathers in the lower basins and stays there in slow rotating columns, and where it touches stone it leaves a track of darker damp that takes hours to fade. The temperature stays in that cool range where you can work without sweating but your fingers stiffen by evening.

Plant life is sparse and specific. Mats of dark moss in the seams. A few clusters of pale, almost translucent rush growing where a basin has silted up enough to hold root. A single species of fern, knee-high, grey-green, that grows only on the north-facing flowstone curtains and nowhere else. Whoever built Oss did not plant a garden. The plants are what arrived.

---

What arrived and stayed, however, in numbers — what makes Oss what it is now — is the fauna.

The basins are loud with frogs. Not one species but seven or eight, in graded sizes, occupying graded depths, and they call across each other in overlapping registers from before dusk until well after dawn. The sound is the dominant fact of the place. It is not a chorus; it is a weather. You stop hearing it the way you stop hearing wind, and then something shifts and you hear it again and realize it has been the volume of a waterfall this whole time.

Beneath the frogs: the eels. Long pale things, almost finless, that move between basins through the runnels and seem to know which pool connects to which. They are not eels exactly. They have small soft hands at the front, four fingers each, and they use them to pull themselves up over the lips of the weirs when they want to travel against the flow. They do this mostly at night. If you sit very still on a stone edge at dusk you will see one come up over the step right beside your foot, pause, regard you with no particular interest, and slip into the next basin down.

In the deeper sumps — the ones that go down further than a body length, into the dark under the town — there is something else. You see them sometimes as pale shapes turning over each other in the bottom, too far to resolve. The eels avoid those basins. The frogs do not call near them. This is one of the rules of the place, observed rather than explained.

Above: the bats, which roost in the only structure in Oss that rises higher than waist-height — a single hollow tower, off-center, with no door, that drips constantly from inside. They pour out at dusk, thousands of them, and the air above the basins becomes briefly solid with their hunting. Then quiet again, or what passes for quiet here, which is the frog-weather resuming.

---

A few people come to Oss. Not many; not often. They come on foot along the silted causeway from the east, carrying simple things — copper hooks, wooden buckets, a knife with a bone handle, lengths of plaited cord. They harvest. They take the small pale fern (it is used for something; no one I have spoken to has agreed on what). They take certain eels, the ones with a particular marking, and only at certain moons. They never take frogs. They never take anything from the deep sumps; they don't approach the deep sumps at all.

The harvesters do not stay overnight. There is an understanding that Oss after dark belongs to its tenants and not to visitors. The harvesters leave offerings on the highest dry stone — a knot of cord, a smear of fat, a single copper hook bent back on itself — and walk out before the bats come.

---

The strangeness of Oss is not in any one thing. It is in the proportion. A town with no inhabitants, built so carefully, kept so wet, so loud with animals, so empty of people. A cistern that has become an ecosystem. A made place that has finished being made and is now simply being, the way a cave is, or a tidepool, except that every stone in it was lifted by hands.

If you stand at the center, on the one slightly raised platform that might once have been an altar or a counting-floor or a place to stand and look, you can see the whole town at once: the terraced basins falling away in every direction, the slow fog rotating in the lower ones, the eels' wakes crossing the surface, the bats streaming, the frogs invisible but everywhere, the single tower leaning slightly to the south. Beyond the last basin: marsh, grey and flat, going on until the light gives out.

The water moves through all of it, basin to basin, on its slow gradient toward whatever is downstream. It has been doing this for a very long time. It will keep doing it. The town was built to make this happen, and the town is succeeding, and no one remembers why.
