# The Undercasting

Seventy meters down, in a vault the size of a small parish church, the foundries are still warm.

You reach the place by a stair cut into basalt so recently exposed that the tool-marks haven't weathered — the edges of each step still bite. The rock here is young in the geological sense and young in another sense too: this chamber was opened only a generation ago, or opened itself, and the walls remember being deeper. There are seams where fresh minerals have wept out and crystallized in pale rosettes, and cracks where the stone is still settling, ticking faintly as it cools.

The heat hits you before the light does. It is close to what a body can stand — dry on the tongue, wet on the back of the neck. The air carries the mineral smell of hot iron and the faint sweetness of something like creosote. Somewhere above, a vent system is supposed to be working, and sometimes you hear it sigh, but mostly the heat just sits.

The chamber is a town. That is the word for it, though no one lives here now in the full sense. Brick kilns have been built against brick kilns; chimney flues rise and bend and enter the flues of their neighbors; cast-iron walkways lattice the upper air at three or four levels, bolted into the rock and into each other. Pipework the color of dried blood threads everything — some pipes as thick as a man, some no wider than a finger — and the pipes breathe. Every so often one will shudder and issue a short puff of steam that condenses almost instantly on the cooler iron of its neighbor and runs down in dark beads. This is the water of the place: not rain, not river, but the slow sweat of a system that was designed to run hot and has not quite stopped running.

The light comes from pilot flames. Hundreds of them, maybe more, set into niches and burner-heads along the walkways, each no brighter than a candle, each blue at the base and yellow at the tip. They were lit for a reason that nobody recorded, and the gas that feeds them seeps up from below through channels no one mapped. The overall illumination is the color of a bruise going gold. You can read by it if you hold the page close.

Plants have come in, but not many, and not the plants one expects. In the condensation-damp crooks where pipes meet walls, pale ferns have taken hold — true ferns, but etiolated, almost translucent, fed on mineral-laden drip. A kind of moss grows on the cooler chimneys, black and crisp, and when you brush it, it flakes like burnt paper. In one corner a single fig tree, impossibly, has wedged itself into a cracked kiln and sent roots down through the firebrick. Its leaves are small and leathery and dark. It fruits, apparently, though the fruit is the size of a pea and nobody has tried eating one.

No animals. Not a rat, not a roach, not a spider. Whatever drove them out — the heat, the fumes, some older eviction — is still in force. The absence is what makes the quiet so peculiar. You expect, in a place this built, the rustle and scurry of the things that live on human leavings, and it isn't there. The silence has a shape. Into it fall only the pipe-sighs, the tick of cooling stone, the small pop of a pilot flame eating a richer pocket of gas, and — rarely — the dull gong of something settling far off, a wall or a cistern adjusting itself.

And yet the place is busy. That is the thing that takes longest to understand. Once you've stood there for an hour you start to see it. A valve on the third tier opens; a gauge on the first tier twitches; ten minutes later a kiln on the far side exhales a breath of warmer air and a drop of condensate falls from a specific pipe into a specific copper basin, where it joins perhaps a thimble's worth of water already collected. The basin, when full, will tip — you can see the counterweight — and send its water down a runnel to somewhere else. Everything is doing something. Everything is slow. The cycle-time of this ecosystem is not measured in heartbeats but in hours, in days. It is a metabolism.

The weirdness is small and consistent. The pilot flames all lean, very slightly, in the same direction, regardless of any draft you can feel. The fig tree's shadow sometimes does not match its posture. And if you stand still long enough in the central yard — a little hexagonal clearing where six walkways meet above a sealed well — you will hear, under everything else, a sound that is almost a voice and almost a bellows, at the edge of the audible, keeping a rhythm slower than breathing.

The rule of the place, as far as anyone has worked it out, is this: it was built to make something, and it is still making it, and the making is what keeps the chamber open against the pressure of the rock. If the foundries ever fully cool, the walls will close. So the pilots stay lit. So the pipes sweat. So the ferns drink. So the fig, against all likelihood, fruits in the dark.
