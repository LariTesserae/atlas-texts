# The Loom Highlands

The land sits high — not a single peak but a tableland the size of a small country, lifted three kilometers above any sea anyone might remember. From its edges, cloud-floors stretch outward to the horizon, gray and slow-rolling, so that the highland feels less like a place on a world than a world entire, sufficient to itself.

Up here the air is cool and always wet. Not rain exactly — rain falls sometimes, in thin grey curtains — but a persistent condensation, a breath. Water beads on every leaf-edge, every basalt knuckle, every spider-strand. The rocks weep. The moss weeps. When you press your palm to a tree trunk it comes away running, and the bark beneath is the color of a bruise.

The terrain has been settled into itself for so long that nothing looks new. The valleys are wide and rounded; the ridges have been smoothed by ten million seasons of soft weather into shapes like sleeping animals. There are no sharp edges anywhere. Streams have carved themselves grooves through which they now flow without argument. Boulders have come to rest where they intend to stay. Even the slumps in the hillsides — where saturated earth has slid downslope — have grown so vegetated they look intentional, like terraces.

And the vegetation: it packs everything. There is no bare ground. Cushion-plants, knee-high and dense as upholstery, cover the open meadows so completely that a person could walk for a day across what looks like green floor. Where the cushions break, ferns of an archaic kind unscroll waist-high. Trees grow in groves rather than forests — stocky, twisted, their crowns dense with epiphytes — and between the groves the air is so thick with hanging moss and lichen-beards that you push through it as through wet curtains. Every surface has at least three living things on it. The lichens host smaller lichens. The mosses host liverworts. The liverworts host single-celled algae that fluoresce faintly in the dusk.

Despite all this life, the soundscape is hushed. The water doesn't roar — it seeps, drips, threads. The wind, when it comes, is filtered through so many layers of foliage that it arrives as a long sighing rather than a gust. Animals call rarely and quietly. You hear your own pulse before you hear theirs. A bird, when it sings, sounds like a single struck bead of glass and then nothing for an hour.

The fauna is abundant but reticent. Grazing creatures the size of large dogs — six-legged, woolly, with placid faces — move through the cushion-meadows in slow herds, leaving trails of cropped green. They are preyed on by something longer and quieter that lives in the moss-curtains and is more rumor than animal. In the wet places, salamanders the length of a forearm hunt under stones; their skin is patterned like lichen and they have evolved a stillness so profound that botanists, if any came here, would have to be told they were not plants. Small flying things — not quite insects, not quite birds — drift on transparent wings between the trees, navigating by some sense that involves the saturation of the air. They taste humidity the way wolves taste blood.

Now, the strangeness. You walk for a day and notice: there is industry here. Not factories — no one built anything — but somewhere underneath, or within, the highland is doing work. From certain hilltops you can feel a low vibration through your boots, a slow steady pulse, the rhythm of something turning over. Steam vents in the bog-flats hiss at intervals so regular they could be timed by a clock. In some of the bigger groves the trees grow in patterns too organized to be accidental: nested hexagons, long curving rows. Their roots have fused below ground into networks that hum, faintly, at a frequency just below hearing. If you cut one open (no one does), you would find the wood layered with deposits of a metal-bearing sap, hardened into something that conducts.

The water itself participates. The streams run along courses that, viewed from above, form circuits — splitting and rejoining, with certain pools warmer than they should be, others colder, the temperature differentials driving currents that don't quite explain themselves with gravity alone. There is a sense, walking through, that one is inside a machine running so slowly it has been mistaken for a landscape, or perhaps a landscape that has so thoroughly learned the trick of being a machine that the distinction has dissolved.

Nothing here is made. And yet things are being produced. In certain mossy hollows you find perfect spheres of stone, fist-sized, polished, lying as if set down. In others, the air is laced with a faint metallic dust that settles on your sleeves and tastes of copper. Whether the highland is manufacturing these for some purpose, or whether they are simply the droppings of its slow internal processes, is not a question the highland answers.

The weather here is its own. Storms breed in one corner of the tableland and travel its length over days, fed by the highland's own exhalation, never quite leaving. There is a season — it lasts perhaps two of our months — when every cushion-plant flowers at once and the whole upper plateau turns the color of old gold, and the salamanders climb out of their stones and arrange themselves on flat rocks and do nothing for a week. No one knows why. There is no one to know.

And at night, when the cloud-floor below catches moonlight and throws it upward, the whole highland glows from beneath. The mosses fluoresce. The strange flying things become visible as drifting sparks. The pulse underfoot grows a little stronger. The land breathes out, and breathes in, and continues its long quiet work, having done so for longer than any reckoning, intending — if intention is even the word — to continue.
