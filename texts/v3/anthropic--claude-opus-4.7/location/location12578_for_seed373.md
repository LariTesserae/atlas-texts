# The Sonant Garden

There is no water here, and there has not been for a long time. What grows, grows on something else.

You come up onto the shoulder of the mountain — not a peak, exactly, but a high terrace of pale stone weathered into round shapes, like loaves left out too long. The stone is dry. Tap it and it answers with the dull tock of a thing that has never held moisture. And yet, climbing up the flanks of every boulder and pouring across the terrace floor, there is a vegetation so dense and so green it looks almost wet — fronds, broad waxy paddles, fern-spirals the size of a child's hand, mosses that aren't moss but something nearer to felted glass. Cool to the touch. They feed on the air, or on what is in the air, which is something more than air.

The terrace is small. You could walk across it in four minutes if you walked. A natural amphitheater, maybe two hundred paces wide, fringed by a colonnade of standing stones the weather has been working on for an unhurried number of millennia. Past the colonnade, the mountain drops away into pale blue distance. There are clouds well below your feet.

It should be quiet up here. It is not quiet.

The sound is the first thing that becomes impossible to think around. It is not wind — there is almost no wind. It is the plants, and the things in the plants, and something underneath both. Every leaf in the garden is *singing*, or being sung through. A continuous fortissimo: layered drones, sudden chirring crescendos, the long sawing pulses of something that might be an insect if insects were the size of cats and made of resonant cartilage. The harmonics stack into chords that shouldn't be stable and somehow are. You feel them in your sternum. You feel them in your teeth. If you opened your mouth to speak, your own voice would be a thread lost in a tapestry.

And yet — and this is the first strangeness — the garden is *empty*. For all this noise, for all this fauna, you can see almost nothing. A hundred paces of fern and stone and you might glimpse one quick body: a long-limbed thing the color of lichen scaling a boulder, gone before your eye fixes on it. A flicker of wing in the high colonnade. The density of presence is almost nothing per cubic meter — the place is mostly air, mostly held breath — but the sound says there are thousands. They are here. They are simply not where you are looking. The garden is enormous in some dimension you can't quite see across, and you are only walking through one slice of it.

Now the built things.

There are not many. A low wall, knee-high, curving along one edge of the terrace — old, but not old in the way the stones are old. The wall is made of something seamless and faintly warm, a material that looks like ceramic until you notice it has no grain at any scale, no joint, no edge of fabrication. It simply *is* a wall. Where the vegetation has grown over it, the vegetation seems healthier — fatter fronds, brighter color. The wall is doing something for the plants, or the plants are doing something for the wall. There is no way to tell which is the host.

Further in, half-swallowed by ferns, a *device*. It is the size of a kneeling person and shaped like nothing in particular — closer to a folded piece of paper than to a machine, except the folds keep going when you try to count them. It is not making the sound. But the sound is organized *around* it. The chords of the garden bend toward it and away from it the way iron filings bend around a magnet, and when you stand near it the singing of the leaves resolves, briefly, into something almost like a sentence in a language with no consonants. Step away and it returns to weather.

There are perhaps six such devices scattered across the terrace. None of them resemble each other. None of them are doing anything you would recognize as work. They are simply present, and the garden is arranged around their presence the way a room is arranged around the people sitting in it.

The dynamic of the place is the second impossible thing.

Stand still for a count of thirty. The fern at your left knee will have grown — perceptibly, by a finger's width. A new frond will have unfurled in your peripheral vision. The lichen-colored animal you glimpsed earlier is not the same animal as the one you glimpse now: that one was an early form, this one is later, and there will be later ones still before the hour is out. Things are *being made* here, constantly, at a rate that should not be possible in a dry place at this altitude in this cool air. Forms are trying themselves. A spiral of fern-fronds will rearrange itself, mid-afternoon, into a different spiral — not by growing, by *being different* — and the song will register the change with a long sliding chord and move on. Nothing is stable. Nothing is destroyed, either; it is only that the inventory of what is here keeps revising itself. The garden is in a state of permanent first draft.

And yet — the third strangeness, and the one that takes longest to notice — the place is *settled*. It has been doing this for a very long time. The weathered stones, the worn paths between the standing colonnade, the deep duff of accumulated plant matter underfoot (not rotting, exactly, since there is no water to rot in — drying instead, into a kind of papery soil that smells faintly of cedar and ozone): all of it speaks of an enormous patient duration. The turbulence is not a crisis. The turbulence is the resting state. Whatever lives here, whatever made the wall and the folded devices, came to a decision a long time ago that this — this permanent boil of small inventions, this deafening choir, this nearly-empty crowdedness — was the form the place was going to take. And the place has been holding that form, in its own way, since before there were eyes to see it.

The rules, as best you can work them out by standing in the middle of it:

Nothing here drinks. Whatever the plants and the animals need, they take from the song, or from the air the song moves through, or from the devices. You will not be thirsty here, but you will not find anything to drink.

The cold is mild and constant. Your breath does not fog, but your fingertips cool quickly against the stone. The light is ordinary — a high, clear afternoon light that does not seem to come from a sun you can locate, but which throws honest shadows in honest directions.

If you speak, no one will hear you, including yourself. If you stay long enough, you will stop trying.

And if you sit down on the low warm wall and close your eyes, the chord the garden is currently holding will, after some minutes, begin to feel less like noise and more like a room you are inside of — a room with walls made of sustained tones, with a ceiling of cricket-sound, with a floor of low slow drone, and somewhere in the middle of it, a quiet space the exact size and shape of a listening person, which the garden has been keeping open this whole time, in case you came.
