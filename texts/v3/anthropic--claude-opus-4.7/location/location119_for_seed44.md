# The Bellworks at Caul

You come to it first by the sound — a low continuous thrumming that you mistake for a distant waterfall until you're close enough to sort the frequencies. Belts. Drive shafts. The slap-wet rhythm of something being pressed between rollers. Water, yes, but water doing work.

The town sits in a shallow basin where three slow streams braid together before continuing south as one. You cannot really see it at first because the town *is* the vegetation, or the vegetation is the town — the distinction stopped meaning much a long time ago. What was built here was built small, maybe two centuries back, maybe three, and the plants have since grown into every joint and over every roofline until the architecture reads as a kind of suggestion underneath the greenery. Load-bearing fig. Structural cane. Walls that are half brick, half the woven aerial roots of something fig-like that has fused with the brick and now holds the brick up as much as the brick holds up the roots.

The whole place is warm and wet in the way a greenhouse is warm and wet, except the roof is missing and the sky does the condensing directly onto the leaves. Every surface beads. Every surface drips. The air tastes green — chlorophyll and mud and the iron tang of the stream-iron that the mill wheels keep raising and laying down as rust-colored silt on everything downstream of themselves.

There are perhaps forty buildings. I say *perhaps* because counting is hard. A mill house and its attached drying-shed and the covered walkway between them and the warden's quarters above the walkway have, over the generations, been bound together by a single enormous climbing vine into what now reads as one continuous structure with four roofs and a great many interior rooms. Call it one building, call it four. The vine has opinions.

The industry is old. Water-powered, belt-driven, still running. Someone maintains it — you can tell by the oil on the journal bearings, the fresh leather on the drive belts, the small wedges hammered into new cracks. But you don't see the maintainers. Fauna is sparse here: a few swallows cutting the humid air above the millraces, the occasional slow brown frog in the gutters, a cat somewhere that you hear but don't see. No draft animals. No children. No dogs. Whoever runs the Bellworks runs it quietly and probably at hours when visitors are rare.

The machines make bells. That's what the name means, and it's still true. Small bronze bells, hand-sized, cast in sand molds in the foundry-shed (you can smell the coke when the wind shifts) and then tuned on a water-powered lathe whose tailstock is a single massive piece of bog oak that has not been replaced in living memory. The finished bells hang from the eaves to season in the wet air — thousands of them, dark with patina, so that when any breeze comes through the basin the entire town rings faintly and continuously, a green-bronze chord beneath the mechanical drumming of the mills. This is the town's second sound, and the one you'll remember.

The streams themselves are clear on top and brown underneath, weed-choked, slow. Watercress in mats. Mint gone feral along the banks. The stone of the mill foundations weeps constantly — carbonate seeps from some bed upstream lay down pale drapes on the dark stone, and moss eats the drapes, and more stone forms behind the moss. A slow conversation. Everything here is in a slow conversation.

Erosion has done its work and then stopped doing it, or rather slowed to the pace of the other processes. The wheels have cut their races. The races have found their depth. The moss has found its line on the wall. The fig has found its grip on the brick. There are no collapses waiting to happen; the collapses already happened, long ago, and what remains has been selected by time for its willingness to stay.

You could stand at the edge of the basin and look down into it and see the whole place at once, because it is small — a hamlet, really, though denser than most hamlets and louder than any. The boundary is clean: at a certain point the managed vegetation of the Bellworks gives way to ordinary forest, and the thrumming drops off, and the air stops tasting of bronze. Step across that line and you're nowhere special. Step back and you're here, in the one place in this valley where water was asked, politely and repeatedly over centuries, to make bells.

It is still making them. It will be making them tomorrow. The rate at which anything changes here is the rate at which a fig root widens the crack it grew into — visible only if you come back in a decade and look carefully at the same brick.
