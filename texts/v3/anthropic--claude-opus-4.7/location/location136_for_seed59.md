# The Bryophyte Town

It sits in a saddle between two shoulders of an old mountain — not high enough for snow, high enough that clouds drag their bellies through it most mornings. The air stays cool and wet. Everything drips. Everything you touch leaves water on your hand.

The town is built into and out of moss.

Not moss as decoration — moss as the substance. Centuries ago, someone here learned that certain cushion-mosses, fed the right runoff and kept from drying, will mat together into a structural peat you can carve like damp cork and that hardens, over generations, into something between felt and timber. So the houses are grown as much as built. A family stakes out a frame of willow-withes and alder-poles, packs the gaps with live sphagnum and haircap and pillow-moss, and then simply waits — tending, watering, trimming — while the walls thicken year by year into green masonry a forearm deep. Older houses, the ones built by great-grandparents, have walls the depth of a man's reach, so densely compacted inside that they ring dully when struck, while the outer faces are still fur.

There are perhaps eighty dwellings. You could walk the whole town in twenty minutes if the paths were straight, but they aren't; they corkscrew between buildings packed shoulder-to-shoulder, and in places the upper stories lean until their eaves kiss and the lane below becomes a tunnel of dripping green. Wooden duckboards keep feet out of the standing water that pools everywhere the stone doesn't drain. The stones themselves — where they surface, at thresholds and wellheads and the central cistern — weep constantly, beaded with condensation, furred at their bases with liverwort.

The roofs are the loudest thing. Not the roofs themselves but what lives on them.

The fauna parameter would sound like exaggeration if you weren't standing in it. Every roof is a pelt of moss, and every pelt is a habitat. Tree-frogs by the hundreds, thumb-sized, in six colors. Shining ground-beetles the size of coins. A kind of small, bright-throated warbler that nests directly in the wall-moss and sings in short sharp bursts all day, so that the town's ambient noise is a layered chitter-trill-croak-drip that never quite resolves into rhythm. Slugs as long as a hand cross the duckboards at night, leaving lacquer-trails. In the eaves: bats, in daylight hours inverted and muttering. In the cistern: newts, stacked like wet leaves. Under every threshold stone: a colony of something — ants, springtails, a small pale salamander that the children catch and release and catch again.

The people have adapted to the noise by not particularly minding it. They speak loudly and close to the ear. Their bells — because every house has one, small bronze, forge-hammered, one of the few metal objects anyone owns — are tuned to cut through the frog-chorus specifically, a hard flat frequency that carries. Announcements, meals, deaths, weather, all bells. The bells answer each other across the saddle, and in a storm you can track a family's whole day by which of their bells rings when.

The tech is hand-tech. Billhooks, adzes, bone needles, bronze for what bronze is indispensable for. No wheel-carts; the paths are too crooked and too soft. Goods move on shoulder-yokes and in baskets. The one real machine in town is the cistern's overflow-gate, a beautifully carved alder-wood sluice that a child is always assigned to watch, because the rain here is unpredictable enough that the gate needs to be worked by hand three or four times a day, more in spring.

Spring is when the turbulence shows itself.

What the parameter calls dynamic, the townspeople call the Turning. For three weeks between the last snowmelt and true summer, the mountain wakes up. The springs under the town — there are at least nine, maybe more, nobody has counted honestly — begin pushing water up through the moss-floors in the lower houses, and every family has a ritual of moving their beds and grain-sacks to the upper story for the duration. The frogs breed in a frenzy; the sound triples. Every surface greens visibly day by day as the dormant mosses swell. New shoots of horsetail spear up through the duckboards overnight — fast enough that you can watch them if you sit still — and have to be cut back twice a week or they'll lift a plank. The bats leave. The swifts arrive and fill the air they leave. Mudslides of wet moss slough off the steepest roofs and have to be caught in nets strung below the eaves, then re-laid by hand. Marriages happen in this season because everything is already in upheaval and one more upheaval doesn't signify.

Then it settles. By midsummer the town is a steady dripping green quiet-loud place again, in its long-worn balance, and the children chase frogs, and the old people sit in doorways with their feet on the duckboards, and moss closes slowly over last year's nail-holes.

The whole settlement, seen from the ridge above, looks like a cluster of soft green loaves risen in a damp cloth, steaming faintly in the cold morning, with threads of smoke from cooking-fires finding their way up through living roofs. The mountain holds it in its lap. The clouds come through it like a slow tide, twice a day, and when they pass the town reappears greener than before, streaming, full of sound.
