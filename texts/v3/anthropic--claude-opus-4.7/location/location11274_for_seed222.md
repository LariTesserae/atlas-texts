# The Tessellated Highlands

The plateau sits about three thousand meters up, and the air at that altitude is thin enough that the light arrives almost undiluted — a hard, mineral brightness that picks out every edge. The sun crosses a sky so saturated it reads as ultramarine near the zenith, paling only at the horizon where heat shimmers off the pan.

The ground is what an old country becomes after it has finished arguing with itself. Long basalt benches, weathered into rounded shoulders. Slabs of rust-colored sandstone where wind has carved shallow basins that the rare rains fill and the sun empties again within a day. Between the benches, gravel flats of pale, sun-bleached pebbles — each pebble polished smooth, each fitted against the next like an unmoving mosaic the plateau worked on for several million years. Walking across them, your boots make almost no sound; the stones are too settled to shift.

Vegetation is scattered and deliberate. Cushion plants — tight, dome-shaped, the kind that hoard their own warmth — sit in pockets where condensation collects on overhanging stones at dawn. A taller species grows in isolation: a kind of pale, waxy column maybe waist-high, ribbed like a column of unlit candles, spaced so widely that from a rise you can count them, one to a hectare. Between them, lichens — black, ochre, a startling chartreuse — stain the rocks in slow patient maps.

Water exists here in confidences rather than displays. A seep darkens the underside of a boulder. A thread of stream surfaces from a talus, runs twenty meters across a gravel apron, and sinks again. In the lee of certain north-facing cliffs there are tinajas — rock-cut basins, deep and cold, holding water older than any creature drinking from it. Once or twice a week, in the late afternoon, a thunderhead piles up over the western escarpment, drops a brief curtain of rain that mostly evaporates before reaching the ground, and moves on. What does reach the ground beads on the waxy plants, runs down their ribs, and is gone by sundown.

The animals are the surprise. For a place so quiet, so sparsely furnished, the fauna is dense and busy — but their busyness is muted, as if the silence were a kind of etiquette they have all agreed to. Long-legged, deer-sized grazers move in small groups across the gravel flats; their hooves are padded, and they make a soft tick only when a stone is wrong. Birds wheel high without calling — broad-winged, dark, riding the thermals that the heated benches throw up. There are pack predators that hunt by sight on the open ground, communicating with movements of the ears and tail; you can watch a hunt occur in complete silence across a kilometer of flat. Smaller things — lizards that look like flakes of rock, beetles the color of polished hematite, a kind of soft-furred rodent that emerges only at the cool margins of the day — keep their own counsel. The plateau's signature sound, if it has one, is the dry rasp of wind against stone, and a distant clicking that turns out to be one of the cushion plants releasing seed pods in the heat.

The built things are sparse and hard to place in time.

There is a low wall, about thirty meters long, running along a contour for no reason a herder would build. The stones are dressed but uncemented; you could push one out with your shoulder. At one end, the wall terminates in a square pillar capped by a smooth disc of some dark material that does not weather. The disc is warm to the touch on the shaded side and cool on the sunlit side, which is the wrong way around.

Six kilometers east, on the lip of a dry canyon, there's a single-room structure with no door, its corners rounded by centuries. Inside, the floor is swept clean — by what, isn't clear — and a recess in the back wall holds a glass-smooth bead the size of an apricot. The bead is doing something. If you watch it long enough you understand that the dust in the room is being held off it; a sphere of clean air about a meter wide surrounds it, and within that sphere small bright motes drift in patterns that aren't quite Brownian, aren't quite anything.

These are not ruins, exactly. They are not maintained, but they are not failing either. They sit in the landscape the way the cushion plants do — at their own pace, with their own logic. Somewhere under the plateau, or threaded through it, or layered into the rock at scales we don't have instruments for, something is still running. The grazers know which stones to avoid: a flat dark slab near the central pan that no animal will cross, where a compass needle slides smoothly through one full rotation every forty-one seconds. A particular cliff face that, viewed at certain angles in the last light, shows a shadow that isn't cast by anything present. The tinajas, when they are very still, sometimes hold a reflection of a sky that isn't above them.

None of this is dramatic. The plateau does not perform. The technology — if that word even fits — is so thoroughly integrated, so weathered into the place, that it has become indistinguishable from geology. Whoever or whatever made these things, made them long enough ago that the made things and the made-of-themselves things now share the same patience.

Stand at the high point — a saddle between two basalt domes — and you can see most of the region. The plateau rolls out for maybe forty kilometers before the land drops away into haze. You can count, from here, perhaps a dozen of the candle-columned plants, three herds of grazers, the wall, the cliffs holding tinajas in their shadow, and one structure that from this distance looks like a single white tooth. The wind moves. A bird, very high, does not cry out. The light is so bright it almost rings, except nothing rings, because nothing here is in a hurry to make noise.

It is a warm, dry, established place. It has been itself for a long time and intends to keep being so. Whatever the bead in the recess is counting, it is in no rush to finish.
