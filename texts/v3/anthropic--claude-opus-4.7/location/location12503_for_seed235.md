# The City of Singing Rooves

Begin at the cloud-line, on the southern shoulder of a mountain that has no name because the mountain has been climbed only in one direction: upward, by the city itself. From this altitude — somewhere past the place where weather stops being weather and becomes a permanent condition — the air is hot in a way that does not belong to mountains. It is the heat of a greenhouse left sealed for a century. It is the heat of breath against breath.

The city is built into the south face. Not perched on it; built *into* it, the way coral is built into reef. Towers of basalt-and-fig, terrace-houses of mortared root, ziggurats whose stairs are hewn from a single dripping bedrock — they rise tier on tier on tier, perhaps four hundred levels, perhaps more. Counting is a hobby that ends in headache here. Somewhere around the ninetieth tier the eye begins to slip; the towers above seem to repeat the towers below, and a viewer staring upward eventually finds themselves looking at the back of their own head, distant, small, also looking up.

This is not a metaphor. This is the first rule of the place.

The water comes from nowhere visible and goes nowhere visible. It seeps. Every wall weeps. The stone is hot to the touch but the water is hotter, and where it runs down channels carved into the alley-floors it steams faintly without ever boiling and without ever cooling. There are no rivers. There are no springs. Water simply *is*, on every surface, in every pore, beading on the undersides of leaves, sheeting down the great south-facing pylons of the upper city, condensing on the eyebrows of anyone who tries to think too hard about where it is coming from.

Vegetation overruns the masonry without quite breaking it. The buildings and the plants have settled their argument long ago. Strangler figs that strangle nothing — they lace the towers in patterns that look architectural, load-bearing, planned. Vines that flower in low, sustained tones. Moss two hands deep, hot to the palm, breathing. Orchids the size of dinner plates that open and never close; they are the same orchids that opened, presumably, when the city was new, and they will be the same orchids tomorrow and a thousand years from tomorrow. Nothing wilts. Nothing fruits. Nothing seeds. The vegetation has arrived at its final form and is keeping it.

Now: the sound.

The sound is the city's true architecture. Everything else is scaffolding for the sound.

It begins, if you are climbing up from the cloud-line, as a pressure in the sinuses. By the tenth tier it is a hum that occupies the bones of the jaw. By the fiftieth it is unmistakably *music* — though music played by no instrument any tradition would name. The rooves sing. Each tile is shaped to catch the steam-laden updraft from the south face, and each tile sings its own note, and the notes do not change, have not changed, will not change. A chord struck once at the founding and held. Held. Held.

It is not loud in the sense of a city street. It is loud in the sense of standing inside a bell that is being rung by the world itself. Conversation is impossible above the two-hundredth tier; the inhabitants — if there are inhabitants; this is a separate question — would have to communicate by some other means, by touch perhaps, or by the slow exchange of postures. There is no birdsong because there are no birds. There are no insects. The sound, sustained at this pitch and volume for whatever uncounted span the city has existed, has driven out anything with a nervous system small enough to be shaken loose. What remains is the chord, the steam, the figs, the weeping stone.

And the doubling. I mentioned the doubling.

The reality of the place is soft along certain seams. A staircase that goes up arrives, sometimes, at its own beginning, with no sense of having turned. A doorway viewed from the inside contains a different room than the same doorway viewed from the outside, and the inhabitants of either room can wave to each other through it without ever meeting. The shadows fall in two directions at once where two suns ought to be, but there is only one sun, hot and white and stationary at a point in the sky it has occupied since the city's first hour. The sun does not move. The shadows do not move. Nothing here moves except the water, which moves only enough to keep weeping.

This is the second rule, and the largest one: the city is in equilibrium with itself, and the equilibrium is so complete that time has, locally, given up. The fig-vines have grown to exactly the length they want and stopped. The chord rings on at exactly the volume it began. The water descends from upper tiers to lower and is, somehow, also already in the upper tiers, having never left. A traveler arriving here cannot say whether they have been climbing for an hour or a decade. The hot wet air holds them in solution.

The scale of the thing is what finally breaks the visitor. Looking south from any high terrace, one sees the city continuing along the mountain's shoulder for what must be twenty, thirty kilometers — tier upon tier, tower upon tower, all of them singing, all of them weeping, all of them green. And the mountain continues past the city, and the city, on inspection, continues past the mountain. There is a horizon, but the horizon is also tiered. The horizon also sings. Somewhere out there the sky is a roof and the roof is a sky and the chord has been holding since before the chord began.

There are no animals. I want to be clear about this. Nothing scuttles, nothing flies, nothing burrows. Once, perhaps, on a high terrace overhung with the great motionless orchids, one might catch sight of a shape that could be a lizard — but it is only the wet stone reflecting the wet stone, the city looking at itself again, the way it does.

And whether anyone lives here: I have walked these tiers in description for some time now and I have not seen a single face. The doorways are sized for something that walked upright. The stair-risers suggest a stride. The terraces have railings at a height that suggests a hand. But the inhabitants, if they existed, have either left or have been so thoroughly absorbed into the held chord of the place that they are now simply *part of the singing*. Perhaps the chord is them. Perhaps every note of every tile is a person who decided, at some point in the deep past, that staying still forever inside one continuous sound was preferable to whatever else was on offer.

You can hear them, if you climb high enough. You can hear them very, very clearly.

You can hear them for a long time after you leave.
