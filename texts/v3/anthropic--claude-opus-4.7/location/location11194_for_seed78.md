# The Hollow Canopy

Begin with this: a forest that is mostly air.

The trees — if *trees* is even the right word — rise from a high cold plateau at the top of something. Not a mountain exactly. A shoulder of the world, a place where the land has heaved itself up recently enough that the rock still has sharp edges and the soil is thin and gritty between the roots. You can tell it is young because the cliffs have not yet been softened. Frost has only just begun its slow work on them. There are clean fractures everywhere, glass-bright in the dark.

The trees are enormous and almost solitary.

Each one is its own cathedral. They stand a kilometer apart, sometimes more, and each rises hundreds of meters into a sky that is mostly black — a deep cold blue-black, the kind that holds a few stars even at what passes for noon. Whatever sun reaches this altitude is filtered, weak, more a suggestion of direction than a source of warmth. The plateau exists in a permanent dusk.

But the trees themselves carry the ecosystem entire. This is the trick of the place: vegetation reads as *total*, yet density reads as *empty*, and both are true. Inside each trunk — and around it, and woven through its branches — is everything. Galleries of pale fungus running like veins under the bark. Curtains of moss the color of old bronze. Hanging gardens of something fern-like that drinks condensation off the wood. Hollows where stranger plants grow inward, lit faintly by their own phosphorescence: pale green, pale lilac, the occasional startled flash of cold white.

The space *between* the trees is near-void. You could walk for an hour across the lichen-crusted rock and meet nothing taller than your ankle. The wind moves freely there. It is a cold, dry wind most of the time — the altitude steals moisture before it can settle — but every so often a great wet breath rolls up from the valleys below, and for a day or a week the plateau is inside a cloud. Condensation beads on everything. The trees drink. Slow rivulets run down the bark in spirals, find the hollows, fill secret reservoirs. Then the cloud passes and the dryness returns, and the trees live on what they stored.

The sound is what surprises you.

You expect a place this empty to be silent. It is not. The trees *boom*. Their wood is hollow in places, resonant, and the wind that finds the right opening turns each tree into an instrument the size of a tower. Low notes carry for kilometers across the plateau. When two trees sing at once the interference patterns wash over you like weather. There are higher sounds too — the chitter and cry of the fauna that lives in the canopies, a constant traffic of small things, climbing things, gliding things. Something with leathery wings barks from a high hollow. Something else answers from a tree so distant you cannot see it, only hear the reply rolling in seconds later.

The animals do not cross the open ground often. The open ground is exposure, cold, and predators that hunt by sound across the flat rock. Life concentrates in the trees, and each tree is its own country, with its own slightly different ecology — the lilac-glow tree, the tree where the bronze moss grows thickest, the tree full of the small singing things, the tree that has begun, for reasons no one understands, to lean.

And there are signs of people, though no people now.

A rope bridge, half-rotted, between two of the great trunks — abandoned mid-repair, the new fibers still paler than the old. A circle of stones on the rock between trees, arranged with care, with a small cairn at the center holding a carved bone whistle. Notches cut into bark at climbing-handhold intervals, going up and up until they disappear into the dark of the canopy. A shelter built into a hollow at the base of one tree, its door a flap of cured hide, inside it a hearth gone cold a season or a decade ago, and tools: a stone adze, a spool of plant-fiber cord, a wooden bowl with something dried in the bottom.

Whoever lived here lived in the trees, traveled between them rarely, knew each one by name. They worked with their hands. They did not build cities because the place does not permit cities — there is no flat continuous ground for them, only the punctuation of trunks across a vast cold openness. They built small. They built upward. They moved by rope and by climbing.

The weirdness is subtle and accumulates.

The trees are not quite trees. Their bark, examined closely, shows a faint regular patterning, almost a script, that is not the work of insects. Their hollows sometimes contain pockets of warmer air than the outside temperature can account for — as if the tree were running some low metabolism that produces heat, slowly, in its core. The phosphorescent plants inside them respond to sound: sing near them and they brighten. The booming of the trees in the wind is therefore not just resonance but call — the tree summoning light into itself, the inner gardens flaring softly in response to its own voice.

A tree calls. Its inner moss glows. The small fauna inside it stir and feed in the new light. The tree, somehow, is fed too.

This is the rule of the world here: each tree is a closed loop of weather and life, breathing in fog, singing for light, sheltering its small kingdom. Between them, the cold empty wind. Above them, the cold black sky. Below, the new sharp rock of a plateau that has only recently risen and does not yet know it is a place.

The people who lived here knew, perhaps. They left the whistle on the cairn for a reason. If you pick it up and blow it, somewhere across the plateau, a tree will answer.
