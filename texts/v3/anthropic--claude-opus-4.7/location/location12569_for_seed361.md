# The Vault at Tlemen's Shoulder

You find it by accident, as everyone does. A seam in the rockface that wasn't there yesterday — or was there, but read as shadow. You turn sideways and slip through, and the mountain swallows you into its inner room.

The cavity is perhaps forty meters across. A hollow lung inside the mountain's stone chest, high up — you can feel the altitude in how thin the breath comes, how the heart works a little harder. Outside the seam, the range rolls on for hundreds of kilometers of weather-rounded peaks, peaks so old they have forgotten how to be sharp. In here, the same age is present but housed.

The first thing is the noise.

It is not a roar, exactly. It is a chord. Thousands of small throats, each one tuned slightly off from its neighbor, layered into a sound like the inside of a great wet bell. Tree frogs, you think first — but it's denser than that. Cicadas threading the upper register. Something that calls in slow, pulsing whoops from a far corner of the chamber. Wing-clicks. A flutter that comes in waves, like applause that won't end. The acoustics of the curved stone fold every cry back on itself; you cannot tell direction. You are inside an organ being played by everything that lives here.

The chamber is warm. Warm the way a greenhouse is warm — moisture-heavy, breath-heavy, the air thick enough to hold its own weight. Sweat starts almost immediately at the nape of your neck. But the wetness is not falling water; there is no rain here, no stream. The moisture is exhaled. From the leaves, from the pelts and chitins of the residents, from the stone itself where it weeps slow beads at certain seams. The walls glisten in patches. Elsewhere they are dry as bone.

The light comes from above, through what you take at first to be a natural oculus — a hole in the chamber's ceiling open to the sky. Daylight pours down in a soft, complete column. But the column is too even. Too uniform across the chamber floor. When you look up you see the opening is not a hole at all but something else — a lens, perhaps, or a panel of nothing, an absence engineered to behave like sky. The light it delivers is the light of an afternoon that does not move. No shadows track across the floor as the hours pass. The afternoon stays.

This is the first sign of the makers.

The vegetation is sparse but exuberant. A dozen trees — no more — but each one enormous, gnarled, hung with epiphytes and trailing mosses, their trunks splitting the chamber into rough quadrants. Between them, open floor of pale stone and patches of low fern, broad-leafed creepers, a single flowering vine that climbs the southern wall in slow spirals and puts out blossoms the size of dinner plates, pale yellow, smelling faintly of iron. The plants are too lush for the amount of water visible. Something is feeding them that you can't see.

And the animals — the animals are the chamber's true population.

A troop of small primates, russet-furred, six-fingered, moves in the high canopy of the largest tree. They don't seem to notice you, or have decided not to. Iridescent beetles the size of your fist work the bark in slow processions. A snake, jade-green and longer than seems reasonable, drapes from a branch in three loose loops. On the floor, something like a small tapir but with a longer snout roots among the ferns. A pair of birds with crests like flame stalk past you on legs too long for their bodies, and they look at you with what is plainly intelligent assessment before continuing.

Every surface has a creature on it. Every leaf hosts a beetle or a tree frog or a spider weaving in a corner. The biomass is staggering — you can feel it as pressure, as the air being used by ten thousand small lungs. And yet there is space. The chamber is not crowded. It is *full*, which is different.

How does the system stay balanced? Nothing this dense should stabilize.

You begin to notice the marks of the makers more clearly now that your eyes have adjusted.

In the floor, set flush with the stone, a disc of something that is neither metal nor glass. Matte, slightly warm to the touch when you crouch. Around its edge, a script of small impressions that aren't writing in any language you know but which your eye keeps wanting to read. The disc is twined over with creeper-vine; the vine has been growing on it for a long time. The disc does not seem to mind.

On one wall, at shoulder height, a small alcove holds three objects: a stone, a feather, and a third thing that is shaped like a stone but is not one — it is too still in a way that stones, with their slight thermal motion, are not. The three objects are arranged with care. There is no dust on them, though dust has settled on every other surface in the chamber.

Up near the ceiling, near where the impossible light enters, a faint regularity in the stone suggests that the chamber itself was — long ago, very long ago — shaped. Not carved. *Grown*, perhaps, by whatever process the makers used. The chamber has the smoothness of something that came out of a mold, then was given to time to age into a cavern.

The mountain has been eroding around it for an age. The chamber, sealed and self-maintaining, has not.

Here is what you come to suspect, sitting on a low stone, listening to the chord of throats and the distant whoop of the unknown caller:

This place is a held thing. A specimen. A garden the size of a room, sustained by engineering that current physics doesn't have the vocabulary for. Whoever made it set it running and left, or died, or moved into a register you can't perceive. The animals don't know they are inside an artifact. The vines don't know. The whole system has folded into itself across geologic time and become, simply, a place — wet enough, warm enough, fed enough, lit enough, balanced.

The mountain outside is ancient. The chamber inside the mountain is older. The chamber holds an afternoon and a forest and a population and a sound, and has held them since before the mountain was tall.

You should leave soon. The seam will close, eventually — they always do. But for now you sit, and one of the russet primates descends a few branches to look at you with mild, ancient curiosity, and the chord continues, and the light does not change, and somewhere across the chamber the slow whoop sounds again, and again, marking nothing.
