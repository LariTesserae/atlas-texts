# The Cold Sea Under The Engine

Begin underwater, because everything here is underwater.

You are perhaps two hundred meters down, which is not deep for an ocean but is deep enough that the light arriving from above has been filtered to a pale, even grey-blue — the color of slate held close to a window. Cold. Not freezing, but the kind of cold that stops being a sensation and becomes simply the medium: three degrees above zero, never warmer, never colder. The water does not stratify. There are no thermoclines. Whatever currents move here are sluggish and structural, part of the place rather than weather within it.

And the sound. God, the sound.

It is the first thing a visitor would register, if there were visitors. A continuous, vast, mechanical roar — not nearby, not locatable, coming from everywhere and from no particular direction, because the ocean transmits low frequencies across hundreds of kilometers without meaningful loss. The roar has components: a fundamental pulse at something like two beats per second, beneath hearing but felt in the sternum and the teeth; a midrange grinding, iron on iron, sustained; and higher harmonics that shear and whine. It is the noise of an industrial civilization, except that nothing about it changes. The pulse never slows. The grinding never shifts key. It has been this loud for as long as anything here remembers being able to remember. Fish that evolved in this water grew without any concept of quiet. Their lateral lines read the roar as a kind of gravity.

Now rise.

As you ascend through the grey-blue column, you begin to see the peaks. This is a high place — a submerged range, young mountains, their stone sharp and unpolished, still with the fresh-fractured look of recent upthrust. The rock is dark, basaltic, shot through with paler veins of something crystalline. The peaks do not break the surface. The surface, if you could reach it, is somewhere far above, and nothing here is interested in it. The summits plateau at around forty meters below that ceiling, broad tablelands of black stone where the light is almost bright enough to read by.

Nothing grows.

This is worth saying clearly: there are no plants. No kelp forests cloaking the slopes, no algal mats, no diatom bloom clouding the water. The rock is bare. Whatever chemistry the sea runs on, it does not permit photosynthesis, or it does and nothing has arrived to take advantage. Perhaps the cold. Perhaps the sound — perhaps the constant vibration shatters cell walls before membranes can form. Perhaps it is simply early. The mountains are young. Life, if it is coming, has not arrived in that form yet.

And yet there are animals. Many animals.

They are the second thing a visitor would notice, after the sound. The water at the plateau-level is thick with them, though "thick" is relative — the overall density of this world is near-void, vast volumes between any two solid things, and the animals exist as a scattered, populous absence. You might swim for ten minutes and see nothing, then pass through a slow-moving shoal of several thousand. They are pale. Most are eyeless or nearly so. They do not hunt by sight in this uniform light; they hunt by the roar, or rather by the tiny local distortions in the roar that a body makes by existing. A fish here is a silence-shape. A predator is an animal that has learned to read silence-shapes against the overwhelming noise-field.

The fauna is varied and strange without being truly bizarre. Long ribbon-things that undulate vertically, mouths at both ends. Clusters of something like brittle stars walking on the basalt, their arms articulated in too many places. Soft, slow, cold-blooded squid-analogues that hang in the water doing almost nothing, conserving heat, waiting for contact. None of them make sound. To make sound here would be to erase yourself. They communicate, if at all, by touch and by shaped stillness.

Now, the built things.

They are rare. A visitor could spend days crossing the range without encountering one. But they exist: scattered across the plateaus and set into the flanks of the peaks, structures of dark metal, pitted and encrusted but not ruined. They are not inhabited — or if they are, not by anything that has shown itself. Most are small: a squat cylinder the size of a house, with flanged intakes; a tripod of girders supporting a sphere; a low wall that encloses nothing, running for a kilometer along a ridge and then stopping. They have the look of industrial infrastructure decoupled from any industry you can name. Pipes lead into the rock and do not emerge. Vents release nothing, or release it so slowly it is indistinguishable from the surrounding water.

These structures are, almost certainly, the source of the roar. Not individually — no single one of them is loud enough. But somewhere, deep in the range, or deeper, beneath it, there must be engines. The sound is their sound, transmitted through rock and water across the whole enormous extent of the place, and the scattered surface structures are the visible fraction of something whose body is buried. The engines do not seem to be tended. The engines do not seem to need tending. They have run at this pitch and volume for a very long time and there is no indication they will ever do otherwise.

The scale, finally.

This range is continental. It goes on. The peaks repeat — not identically, but in the way a real range repeats, variations on a structural theme — for thousands of kilometers in at least two directions. There are valleys between them deeper than the peaks are tall, floored in sediment so fine it puffs at any disturbance. There are regions where the basalt gives way to that pale crystalline vein-stuff in great exposed sheets, and the light there takes on a faint opalescence. There are places where the roar is, by some acoustic accident of the terrain, slightly muffled, and the animals gather there in larger numbers, as if resting. There are other places where a standing wave forms in the sound and the water itself seems to hum visibly, and nothing living goes near.

It does not change. That is the last thing to understand. There are no seasons here, no tides that matter, no day and night, no slow succession of geological ages playing out on human timescales. The mountains are young but they are not currently doing anything. The engines run but their running is not development, it is equilibrium. The animals live and die but their populations are stable to any measurement that could be made. If you came back in ten thousand years the same roar would be pressing against your sternum at the same two beats per second, and the same pale ribbon-things would be reading your silence-shape against it, and the same unattended cylinder would be sitting on the same black plateau, venting nothing into the cold.

It is a place that has arrived at what it is, and intends to remain.
