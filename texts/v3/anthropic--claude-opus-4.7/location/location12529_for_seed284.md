# The Pinnacle Works

There is a needle of black basalt rising out of nothing in particular — call it a mountain, though it is more like a tooth, sharp as the day the mantle pushed it up. The rock is fresh. You can still see where it cracked. The faces are glassy in places, faceted in others, and there is no soil on it anywhere because there has not been time.

Near the summit, in a fold of the stone no wider than a kitchen, the city lives.

You have to be told it is there. From any distance it looks like a discoloration, a streak of rust or lichen in a vertical seam. Step closer — closer than you should be able to, because the mountain does not invite climbing — and the streak resolves into chimneys. Hundreds of them. Each one the diameter of a thimble. Each one smoking a thread of pale vapor that the cold air takes immediately upward and disperses.

This is a foundry-town built by something that is not us, in a pocket of cliff perhaps four meters across and twelve high, and it is going at full tilt.

---

The builders are beetles, or beetle-shaped — let us call them beetles for convenience, though their carapaces are the dull non-color of fired clay and their forelimbs end in small chisels of harder material, possibly grown, possibly forged. They are everywhere. They are layered three and four deep on the working faces. They climb over one another without ceremony. The density is what strikes you first after the smallness: there is no empty surface in the pocket. Every ledge has a kiln on it. Every kiln has tenders. Every tender has a runner behind it waiting to take the slag. The runners have runners.

The works are powered, somehow, by the mountain itself. Heat seeps up from below through hairline fractures; the beetles have tapped these and channeled them with little ducts of sintered grit, so that each kiln draws its warmth from the planet's own slow exhalation. There is no fuel pile. There is no fire in the human sense. Only the rock giving up its temperature degree by degree into vessels the beetles have shaped to catch it.

What they make is not clear. Small dark beads, perhaps. They are stacked in alcoves by the thousand, sorted by some criterion invisible to a visitor. Some are taken downward into shafts that have been bored — bored, in this fresh stone, by what tools? — and never seen again.

---

The air is cool. Not cold enough to numb; cool enough that the vapor from the chimneys is always visible, always condensing into the faintest mist that hangs in the pocket and gives everything a softened look, a dimness like a workshop at dusk. The light here is bad. The pocket faces a direction the sun rarely finds, and what light arrives is the reflected, indirect glow off the opposing cliff-face, which is itself in shadow most of the day. So you see the works through a permanent grey-gold haze, and the kiln-mouths glow like distant windows.

The sound is the surprise. You expect a factory. You get a library. The beetles work almost silently — their chisels tick, their feet whisper on the stone, and the kilns sigh as they breathe — but there is no shouting, no clanging, no engines. The loudest thing is the wind passing over the pocket's lip far above, and even that is muted, because the pocket's shape baffles it. Most of the work happens in a hush. Occasionally a bead is dropped and rings like a tuning fork against the stone, and every beetle within a body-length pauses for the duration of one heartbeat before resuming.

---

But the hush is deceiving, because nothing here is stable. The pocket is in constant reorganization. A kiln that was on the third tier this morning has been disassembled and is being rebuilt one tier up, because a new heat-seam opened in the night. An entire avenue of bead-alcoves has been abandoned and the beads relocated, because a hairline crack appeared in the back wall and the colony has decided — by what consensus is not visible — that this section will fail within the week. Lines of beetles flow in and out of the pocket along the cliff-face, carrying material, carrying news, carrying each other when injured. The flow reverses. The flow splits. A whole crew rushes to a corner where something has gone wrong; a kiln's duct has clogged and the heat is backing up; they swarm it, dismantle it, rebuild it three meters away in the time it takes to describe.

The works are never the same works twice. If you visited yesterday and visit today you would not recognize the layout. Only the density is constant, and the smoke, and the quiet.

---

There are other animals. Of course there are — at this density of activity, something has come to eat the workers. Pale spider-things web the upper corners of the pocket and take beetles that stray too high. A bird-shaped predator, more lizard than bird, perches on the cliff-edge above and snatches at the supply lines; the beetles have responded by building tiny stone awnings over the most exposed paths. Smaller parasites ride on the beetles' backs and are tolerated, apparently, up to some threshold. The ecology is complete and intricate and it all happens in a space you could cover with a tarpaulin.

---

The strangeness of the place — and there is a strangeness, you feel it after a while — is that it has the bearing of a city that has existed for centuries, with a city's confidence and a city's complexity, but the mountain it sits on is young. The basalt is unweathered. The pocket itself is a fresh fracture. Whatever these beetles are, they came here recently and built all of this fast, or they have been doing this on other peaks for longer than the peaks have lasted, moving on each time a mountain wears down, settling each time a new one rises.

You begin to suspect, watching the smoke threads dispersing upward into a sky that is not quite the right color, that the beads they make and bury are something the mountain needs. That this is not a colony exploiting a site but a maintenance crew, called in. That the heat they draw is not stolen but received. That when the kiln-tenders pause at the ringing of a dropped bead, they are not startled; they are listening for a reply.

Sometimes, very faintly, the mountain replies. A low note from far below, felt more than heard, and every beetle in the pocket goes still for the length of it, and then resumes.
