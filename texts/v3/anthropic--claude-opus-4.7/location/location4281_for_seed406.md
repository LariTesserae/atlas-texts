There is a hollow in the rock, no larger than the inside of a cupped hand held to the face. You would have to know to look for it. The opening is a slit between two lichened slabs on a high ridge, where the wind has worn a long shallow groove over what must be thousands of slow afternoons. The slit does not admit wind. It admits only light.

Inside, the light is everywhere. This is the first strange thing. There is no sun visible from within the chamber, yet the space is bright as noon on snow — a white, even brightness, sourceless, which picks out every surface without casting any shadow. The walls, if they can be called walls, are a pale mineral, some kind of sugar-grained quartz, and they glow only in the sense that they refuse to be dim. The light seems to have been stored there, and to have forgotten how to leave.

The chamber is no larger than the skull of a small deer. And it is full.

It is full of a forest.

The vegetation here is not miniature in the way of bonsai, which imitates scale. It is simply the forest at the size it needs to be. Dense cushions of moss no thicker than felt climb the curved walls, each spore-stalk a clean vertical line catching the light. Between the mosses, something like a horsetail in perfect dry green sends up jointed stems the height of an eyelash. A ground cover of plate-like succulents, each the size of a lentil, tiles an entire slope of the floor. There are ferns — real ferns, fully formed, fronds unrolling at the crosier — that are smaller than a grain of rice, and there are hundreds of them, and between them are the smaller ferns, and between those, smaller still, so that wherever the eye tries to rest it finds another generation of green nested inside the last.

Nothing here drinks water. There is none. The plants have grown used to taking what they need from the dry mineral breath of the rock itself, and from the light, which is rich enough to feed on. Their leaves have the faint waxy sheen of things that have solved a problem and stopped thinking about it. Some of them are slightly the wrong color — a blue-lavender where green would be expected, or a green so saturated it suggests the plant has been green longer than green has existed elsewhere. This is one of the strangenesses. Another: the fronds do not move. There is no wind inside, and no breath, and no creature to brush them. They hold their positions the way a held note holds, for centuries.

The temperature is cool, the steady cool of stone that has never been warmed all the way through. You can feel it if you put your eye to the slit; a thin coolness comes out, dry as paper.

There are no animals. Not a mite, not a springtail. The forest has never been grazed, never been pollinated by anything with legs. Reproduction, if it happens at all, happens by the slow arithmetic of spores drifting one body-length per decade through still air, settling, waiting, taking. The ecology is nearly closed. A frond yellows over the course of a century, bends, collapses into the duff, and becomes substrate for the next frond, which is already a hundred years into growing beside it. Nothing is wasted. Nothing is hurried. The whole system breathes, if it breathes, on a cycle longer than any witness.

And there is no sound. None. Not the tick of a cooling stone, not the sigh of settling dust. The silence is so complete it has a texture — the particular silence of a place where sound has never had a reason to be invented. If you made a noise at the slit, the noise would not enter; the chamber would refuse it, the way a full cup refuses more water.

Near what might be called the center of the floor, the only thing resembling an artifact: a single flat chip of darker stone, slightly concave, as if it had been used once, long ago, to scrape or to press. It is not clear whether something made it or whether the rock simply produced it in the course of its own weathering. It lies there, half-covered in moss, and the moss has grown around it with such patience that the chip and the moss are now the same object. Whoever or whatever set it down — if anyone did — set it down before the forest. The forest has been accommodating it ever since.

The rules of this place, as far as they can be stated:

Light stays.
Water never comes.
Nothing moves quickly.
Nothing leaves.
The green keeps finding smaller rooms inside itself to fill.

On the ridge outside, the wind goes on wearing its groove. In another ten thousand years the slit will be a finger wider. Light will spill out a little more generously. The forest inside will notice, in its slow way, and adjust — a new tier of even smaller ferns perhaps, taking advantage of the altered angle. Until then, it holds. Bright, cool, crowded, silent, and entirely itself, inside a stone on a high ridge where no one is looking.
