There is a shallow basin in the warm hours of an overcast afternoon. The light comes from everywhere and nowhere — a high, even gray that throws no shadows. It is the kind of light that makes you stop checking the time.

The basin is perhaps two hundred paces across, ringed by a low rim of pale stone — sandstone, by the look of it, the color of old bone or weak tea. The stone has been worked on for a long time by something patient: wind, probably, and the occasional rains. It is rounded everywhere it might have been sharp. The rim is waist-high in places, knee-high in others, dipping to nothing where shallow watercourses once entered and left. Those courses are dry now, but their floors are darker than the surrounding ground, and where you kneel and press a palm to the silt, the silt is cool and faintly damp. Water has been here recently, in the sense that geology means recently — within a season, within a year. It will be here again.

At the center of the basin, slightly off-center actually, stands a single juniper. It is not large. It has the twisted, deliberate shape of a tree that has decided, long ago, what it is going to be. Its bark is shredded into long vertical strips. A few sprigs of gray-green needle catch the diffuse light. Around its base, the ground is a little darker — a ring of held moisture where condensation runs down the trunk at dawn and soaks in before the warmth lifts it back up.

Scattered around the basin floor are perhaps a dozen other plants: tufts of a wiry bunchgrass, two or three low cushion-shrubs with tiny leathery leaves, a single clump of something with a pale flower already gone to seed. They are spaced as if by agreement. There is more empty ground than plant — bare pale silt, small stones, a few flat slabs of the same sandstone that makes the rim, lying where they fell from it a long time ago and have since been polished by weather into smooth tablets.

No insects hum. No birds call. If anything lives here that moves, it is too small or too still to register, or it is elsewhere today. The quiet is not the quiet of absence so much as the quiet of a room where nothing needs to be said. Occasionally a breath of warm air moves across the basin and the juniper's needles make a sound almost too soft to name — not a rustle, more a settling.

On the northern rim, half-tumbled, are the remains of a low wall. Four or five courses of dry-stacked stone, running maybe ten paces before the stones have slumped back into being just stones again. Whoever stacked them did it carefully — the joints are tight where they still hold — but it was long enough ago that lichen has done its slow gray-orange work across every face. There is no roof, no hearth-stone, no clear plan. It might have been a windbreak. It might have been the wall of a small enclosure for something that needed enclosing. It is not asking to be understood.

A little way off from the wall, embedded in the silt so that only its upper curve shows, is a piece of fired clay — the lip of a vessel, perhaps, broken cleanly along an old line. It is the same color as the stone. You could walk past it a hundred times.

The basin holds its temperature evenly. The air is warm but not hot; the stone, when you touch it, is the same temperature as the air, as if everything here has agreed on a number and stayed there. Even the dampness in the old watercourse silt is neither cold nor warm — it is simply present.

If you sat on the rim and watched, you would notice, over an hour, almost nothing change. The light would not shift; the cloud above is uniform and slow. The juniper would not move except for that rare soft settling. No shadow would lengthen because there are no shadows. The seed-head of the spent flower might tremble once. The damp patch under the juniper might be a fingernail's width smaller, or larger, depending on whether the air is taking water or giving it back.

This is a place that has finished becoming itself. It is not dying and it is not growing. The rain will come and the watercourses will run for a few hours and the silt will darken and then it will be like this again. The juniper has been this size, more or less, for longer than seems reasonable. The wall has been falling for so long that it has forgotten how to finish falling.

You can stay as long as you like. The basin will neither welcome you nor mind you. When you leave, your footprints in the damp silt of the old watercourse will be the most recent event for some distance, and they will last until the next rain, which is not a hurry.
