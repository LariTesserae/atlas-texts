# The Stridulum

It sits inside a mountain, or what was a mountain once — a pocket hollowed high in the basalt, maybe three hundred paces across at its widest, closed-roofed, airless to the sky. You reach it by a fissure that narrows and then opens, and the first thing is the heat. Not the heat of fire. The heat of bodies. Something close to blood-warm but dryer, a fever held steady by the stone.

The floor is dust, and the dust is not dust. It is the shed carapace-powder of a hundred million small lives, ground fine by the weight of their descendants walking over it. Ankle-deep in places. It smells of pepper and old keratin and something faintly sweet, like the inside of a beehive without the honey.

There is no water here. There has not been water for a very long time. The creatures that saturate this chamber have solved the problem by another route — they metabolize the mineral itself, scraping trace salts and hydrates from the walls with mouthparts fine as needles, and what moisture they need they make from the air they breathe out of each other. A closed loop. A self-drinking room.

It is dark. Not the dark of a closed eye but the dark of a place that has never been lit. And yet the dark is not still. The dark moves. The dark is the fauna.

They are roughly the size of a thumbnail and roughly the size of a hand and roughly the size of a cat, arranged in no clean tiers but folded through each other — the small ones living in the joint-plates of the medium ones, the medium ones nested under the shelving backs of the large ones, the large ones carrying whole neighborhoods on their shells. They are chitinous. They are, most of them, blind. They navigate by touch and by the minute rasp of their own legs against each other, which is why the place is called, in the mind of whoever names such places, the Stridulum — though there is no one here to name it, and the word is only a sound that fits.

It is mostly quiet. That is the strange thing. With this many bodies in this small a space, you would expect a roar. Instead there is a dry whisper, a sort of continuous soft applause that never crests, because the creatures have learned over a long, long settling-in that loud is dangerous and quiet is long life. When one of them startles, the startle travels as a wave of stillness, not noise — a ripple of held-breath moving outward through the packed dark, and then resuming.

They move over each other constantly. There is no floor that is not also ceiling to something, no wall that is not also road. The density is such that a dropped pebble would not reach the ground; it would be passed, hand over hand, leg over leg, deeper into the mass and perhaps eventually out through one of the cracks in the outer wall, where the smallest carry-creatures tip waste into the long fall outside the mountain.

There are a few built things. I did not expect them, writing this. But there they are: small cairns, knee-high, made of the larger shed plates stacked with a care that is not random. Whether the creatures built them or whether something else passed through once and left them, a long time ago, is not a question the chamber answers. The cairns are old. The dust has climbed halfway up their sides. The creatures walk over them the way they walk over everything, and maintain them without seeming to — a plate that slips is replaced within a day by a plate of similar size, though no individual creature appears to be doing the replacing. It is the sort of order that emerges when enough small intentions overlap.

There is no plant. There has never been a plant. But on the north wall — if north means anything inside a sealed stone — there is a spread of something pale and slow that is not quite fungus and not quite mineral, a crust that grows by accretion from the exhalations of the swarm. It is the closest thing the chamber has to a garden. The oldest creatures, the ones whose shells have gone soft with age, crawl to it and press themselves against it and are slowly incorporated, which is how this place disposes of its dead: not by decay, because nothing decays in this dry heat, but by being built into the wall itself. The wall is thickening, century by century. The chamber is, very slowly, getting smaller.

This is the balance it has found. The heat is the heat of its own metabolism. The dark is the dark it requires. The density is the density at which the loop closes — fewer, and the air would not stay breathable; more, and the walls would not feed them. It has been this way, in this same saturated hush, for longer than anything outside would guess from the quiet plain face of the mountain.

If you stood in it, you would feel them immediately on your boots, on your legs, palm-warm and investigating, not hostile, not welcoming, only continuous. You would be, for the duration of your visit, another surface. The cairns would remain unexplained. The crust on the north wall would continue its patient accumulation. And when you left, pushing back out through the fissure, the wave of stillness your leaving caused would travel inward for a long time before the whispering resumed, and the chamber would go on being exactly what it is, which is full.
