# The Bellows-Grove

Step through the curtain of aerial roots and you are inside it: a room the size of a small chapel, walls and ceiling entirely constructed of living plant matter, and somewhere in the green thickness above, something is breathing.

The floor is peat. Black, spongy, weeping water when you press your weight into it, releasing the smell of tea and iron. Each footprint fills within a minute. There is nowhere in this grove that is not wet — the air itself condenses on every surface, running down the smooth inner bark of the walls in slow beads, pooling in the cupped leaves of the understory, dripping from ten thousand leaf-tips in an arrhythmic percussion that never stops and never synchronizes.

The grove is perhaps forty paces across. You can see the far wall, dimly, through the standing pillars of trunk. It feels bounded — not trapped, but held, the way a courtyard is held. The canopy closes overhead into a low dome maybe thirty feet up, lit a filtered chlorophyll-green where sun makes it through, which is often enough to call it normal daylight, just daylight that has been strained through a great deal of leaf.

Now: the engines.

They are grown, or they have grown. It is not clear which came first — the machines or the trees that became them. Great pistoning structures of heartwood, their cylinders hollowed and lined with resinous lacquer, rise through the grove like the bellows of an enormous cathedral organ. Some are the trees themselves, reshaped; others are mechanisms that trees have swallowed and continued to operate. A flywheel of dense root-mass, six feet across, turns slowly at the grove's center — perhaps one revolution every two minutes — driven by nothing you can locate. From its hub, leather-skinned belts of living bast fiber run up into the canopy and disappear.

The sound is the sound of a factory that has been abandoned for a century to moss and still, somehow, is running. A deep woody *chunf... chunf... chunf* of the largest piston, offbeat against a higher wheeze from somewhere in the understory. Creakings. The slow groan of bast-belts under tension. The constant dripping. Occasionally, from deep in one of the wooden cylinders, a long sigh of warm wet air — and a faint mineral smell, like wet slate heated on a stove.

The warmth, you realize, is coming from the machinery. The grove is a few degrees warmer than it should be. The condensation on the walls is partly the engines' exhalation.

Built things are few and scattered. A brass valve-wheel, green with verdigris, set into a trunk at shoulder height — it turns, with effort, and somewhere a pitch changes. A set of stone steps, three of them, leading up to nothing, half-swallowed by a buttress root. A length of iron pipe emerging from the peat and going back into it six feet later, warm to the touch. A single ceramic dial, its needle trembling, its face too furred with lichen to read. Someone was here. They built with and into the grove, and the grove absorbed the work without quite erasing it. The balance has been stable a long time.

Fauna is almost absent. A single pale frog, the color of the inside of a stem, motionless on a leaf. The sense, sometimes, that something larger has just moved on the far side of a trunk — but when you look, only the slow turning of the flywheel, only the breath of the cylinders. No birds. No insects you can see, though the pollination must be happening somehow, because everything is in slow bloom: small waxy flowers on every surface, opening and closing on cycles that might be hourly or might be tidal.

The rules of this place, as far as you can work them out:

Nothing here is in a hurry. The flywheel has been turning at that rate since before you were born and will be turning at that rate long after. The water cycles — condensing on the high leaves, falling, soaking the peat, rising again as the engines' breath — are a closed loop that has found its level. The grove is not growing. It is not dying. It has arrived at itself.

The machinery is not doing anything, in the sense of producing. It is maintaining. It pumps the grove's own humid breath in slow circles, drives the belts that rotate certain branches into and out of the light, turns the flywheel whose purpose is to keep turning. The factory manufactures its own continuance. Whoever designed this — or whatever grew into the role of designer — is no longer present, or has become indistinguishable from the grove itself.

Stand still long enough and you'll notice the piston's rhythm matches your breathing, or yours has matched its. Hard to say which gave way.

On the way out, through the curtain of aerial roots, you'll find one of them has a brass tag wired to it at eye height. The tag reads, in a careful engraved hand, a single word you cannot quite make out through the patina — possibly a name, possibly an instruction, possibly just the serial number of a tree.
