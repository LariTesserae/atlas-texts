# The High Foundry at Iselvet

It is a city being born on the shoulder of a mountain that is itself still deciding what shape to take.

Begin with the rock, because the rock is the strange part. At this altitude — somewhere past where the air thins into that particular blue-grey dimness that feels like looking through smoked glass — the stone has not yet agreed to be stone. In places it holds the hardness you expect; in others it flexes slightly underfoot, matte and warm, as though the mountain were still cooling from whatever made it. There are no proper cliffs yet, no weathered faces. The ridges are rounded in the way of clay thumbed into shape and left overnight. Scree exists only where the foundry crews have blasted it into existence. Rain, when it comes, runs off the slopes in sheets that leave no channels — the water beads and rolls and vanishes, having found nothing yet willing to be eroded. The mountain is younger than geology. It is pre-geological. It has not learned the habit of wearing away.

Against this raw slope, humans have built Iselvet. Or rather, humans and something else have built it together, because at two-point-six on the ledger of weirdness, the distinction frays.

The city is stacked. Tiered foundries cling to the slope in terraces linked by iron stairways and cable lifts, smokestacks jutting at slight angles because nothing here has found true vertical — plumb bobs at Iselvet describe slow arcs, never quite settling. Brick and riveted plate, cast-iron colonnades, furnace halls with their roofs open to the sky to vent heat. The architecture is industrial-revolution in grammar but the vocabulary is wrong: the bricks are a little too warm to the touch, their color shifts between firings, and mortar seams sometimes run for a few inches and then simply stop, the bricks fused directly to each other as if they had grown.

The foundries smelt the mountain itself. Crews go out on harnesses and cut blocks of the unfinished rock — it yields to saws like dense cheese — and cart it back in ore wagons to furnaces that roar day and night. What comes out is not metal in any ordinary sense. It is ingots of something that has been *convinced* to be metal, and the work of the foundry is mostly persuasion: hammermen beating rhythm into bars that would otherwise drift back toward their original undecided state, chemists tending vats where additives are less reagents than arguments. The products are shipped down the mountain on cable trams that vanish into the cloud layer below.

Now the population. Sparse — I want to honor that. Iselvet is a big city by footprint, a regional presence you can see from a day's march away, its lamps pricking out against the slope in the perpetual dimness. But it is thinly occupied. Long terraces of worker housing stand half-empty. A furnace hall built for forty tenders runs with twelve. The streets between shifts are quiet enough that you hear the constant background of the place: the low sustained roar of furnaces, yes, and the periodic clang of the hammer-sheds, but also a kind of geological hum that you come to realize is the mountain itself, still setting. There are whole districts — the upper terraces mostly — where nobody lives, where the buildings were constructed in anticipation of populations that have not arrived and perhaps never will. Moss grows in the empty refectories. Water drips from fixtures that were never connected to a main, condensing out of the cool damp air onto warm brick.

And the fauna — this is where the place opens up strangely. The mountain, despite being half-made, teems. Goats that are almost goats pick their way across the undecided slopes, their hooves leaving prints that close up again slowly, like footprints in slow pudding. Something like ravens but with too-long bodies nest in the smokestacks and ride the updrafts in great wheeling flocks at dusk. In the pockets of vegetation — and there is real vegetation here, improbable cool-climate lushness wherever the runoff collects: ferns, low rhododendron, silver-green lichens thick as carpet, small twisted pines — you find beetles the size of a thumb, pale salamanders in every seep, colonies of something mouse-shaped that sings at night in thirds and fifths. The ecology is dense even where the city is thin. Workers coming off shift walk home past whole herds grazing in the unlit terraces, and nobody remarks on it.

The weather is patient. At this elevation you'd expect violence — wind, storm, sudden temperature collapse — but Iselvet sits inside a slow system. Clouds arrive over the course of days. Rain falls for a week and then doesn't fall for a month. Temperatures drift by a degree across an afternoon. The damp is chronic: every surface beads, every iron railing wears a skin of moisture, laundry never quite dries. There are lichens growing on the *insides* of houses. But there is no drama in the sky. The light is always the same dim pewter, brightening slightly at what passes for noon, and the sun — if there is a sun — is never directly visible, only a vague brightening in the cloud-ceiling far above.

The rules of the place, as best I can articulate them:

Things here are still becoming what they will be, and this applies to objects as much as to the mountain. A tool left in a drawer for a year may come out slightly different — a hammer's head heavier, a wrench's jaw sized for a bolt that doesn't exist yet. The foundry work is really a specialized branch of this: extracting raw indeterminacy from the mountain and fixing it into useful forms before it drifts. Fixedness is the valuable commodity. Down the mountain, in the cities that receive Iselvet's ingots, metal behaves itself. Up here, it has to be watched.

Because the mountain is pre-erosional, it does not *remember*. Paths worn into it by a century of boots will vanish over a season. The foundry roads must be re-cut constantly. Gravestones sink back into the ground within a generation. The city's memory lives entirely in its built forms — in the riveted iron and fired brick, which hold their shape by sheer industrial insistence. This is perhaps why Iselvet was built at all: a wager that enough sustained human intention, concentrated on one slope, could force a piece of the world to commit to being real.

Whether the wager is being won is unclear. The empty terraces suggest something. The slow hum of the mountain suggests something else. In the meantime the furnaces run, the hammer-sheds ring, the ravens wheel at dusk over smokestacks that lean a few degrees off any vertical you can name, and somewhere in the dim lush pockets between the foundries a salamander the color of wet paper opens its mouth and sings a note that is almost, but not quite, a word.
