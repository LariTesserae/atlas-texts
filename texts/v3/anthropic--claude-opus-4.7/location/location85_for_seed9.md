# The Nursery Terraces at Cauldron-Ninety-Four

It sits at roughly four thousand meters, though "sits" is the wrong verb — it grows. The whole place is a single structure, or rather a stack of them: a cylindrical tower of cast-iron basins nested one inside another like a collapsing telescope wedged into the cloud layer. From below you would see only a stained, sweating column of riveted plate. From inside, on any given terrace, you'd see a room.

I am describing the seventh terrace, because that is the one that came to me, but they are all variations.

---

The floor is poured copper, green-scabbed, pitted in the careful patterns that the foundry pattern-makers call *seed-dimples*. Each dimple, about the size of a thumbprint, holds a bead of water. The water is not rain — it is condensed out of the air, which up here at the cloud-line is almost a liquid already, and which the terrace's lukewarm pipework keeps at body temperature. Walk the floor barefoot (the foremen do; leather rots in a week here) and you leave prints that fill in again behind you within minutes.

The walls are the interesting part. The walls are where the landscape is being *made*.

Along the inner curve of the terrace, cast-iron trellises hold clay matrices — lattices of unfired, still-damp ceramic in the rough shapes of cliffs, overhangs, small ravines. They are embryonic geology. The foundry below pumps a warm mineral slurry through capillary tubing woven into each matrix, and over weeks the clay accretes, hardens in some places, stays plastic in others, grows crystalline crusts where the slurry's chemistry shifts. They are being *gardened* into stone. A finished section — there is one near the east stair, perhaps three meters of mock-limestone with a fissure weeping a constant thread of warm water — took eleven years. It is still technically under construction. It will never quite finish; that is the point.

Because the moment the clay-cliffs begin to hold water, the vegetation moves in.

---

The plants are not planted. They arrive. Spores ride up the ventilation shafts from the lowland forests; seeds come in on the boots of the engine-tenders; some things, by now, are endemic, having evolved inside the terraces themselves over the sixty or seventy years the Cauldron has been running. The seventh terrace holds, at last count, ninety-one species in a room you could walk across in twenty paces.

Ferns dominate — a tight-curled red one that grips rivets, and a pale translucent one the workers call *lantern-weed* because it glows faintly by day from the photosynthetic bacteria it harbors. Mosses carpet everything that stays damp more than a week, which is everything. From the trellises hang long ropes of a vining orchid-cousin whose flowers smell, distinctly and unpleasantly, of hot brass. There is a tree, one tree, growing out of a joint where two terrace-walls meet — it is perhaps two meters tall and has been two meters tall for decades, bonsai'd by the constrained root-run of its iron pot. Its bark is colonized by three kinds of lichen in concentric rings.

The density is wrong for the space. Everything is near-touching. You push through leaves to cross the room. Water drips constantly from the ceiling-pipes and from the plants themselves, and the floor-dimples fill and refill, and the air has that particular greenhouse weight where breathing feels like drinking.

---

The sound is mostly the quiet machinery. Deep below, the foundry-engines drone at a pitch you feel in your sternum more than hear. Closer, the slurry-pipes tick as they expand and contract with the daily thermal cycle. Water drips. Occasionally a valve sighs open somewhere and releases a puff of steam that smells of iron and yeast. Beyond that — silence, mostly. It is the quiet of a library or a greenhouse at dusk. You can hear your own joints.

The animals are few. A colony of small pale frogs, descended from something that came up a drain decades ago, lives in the fissure of the finished cliff-section; you rarely see them but you hear them at the thermal transitions, a single clicking call that the oldest foreman insists has gotten lower in pitch over the years, as if the frogs are growing larger, or their world is. There are insects — a transparent beetle that eats lichen, a kind of hover-midge that breeds in the dimples. Nothing larger. Nothing that hunts.

---

The rules of the place, as I understand them:

The Cauldron makes landscape. That is its industry. Each terrace is a womb for a particular kind of terrain — the seventh is "wet limestone cliff, temperate"; the fourth, I am told, is "basalt scree, cold"; the twelfth, "tidal sandstone." When a section is judged *sufficient* by the foundry-masters (a judgment that has something to do with whether the ecology has stabilized around it, whether things grow and die on it at a rate indistinguishable from wild stone), it is cut free, lowered by crane, and shipped downslope to be installed somewhere in the world that needs a cliff, a shore, a ravine. The empty space in the terrace is then re-trellised and re-seeded and the slow work begins again.

The strangeness, the 1.5 of it, is this: no one is entirely sure anymore whether the landscapes out in the world — the "natural" cliffs, the ravines one hikes to — were always natural, or whether, somewhere in the last century, the Cauldrons simply *replaced* them all, one matrix at a time, and the memory of a world that grew its own stone has been quietly composted into the memory of a world that orders it by the meter.

The frogs in the fissure have an opinion on this, I think. But frogs are patient, and the call is only one click, and it is getting lower.
