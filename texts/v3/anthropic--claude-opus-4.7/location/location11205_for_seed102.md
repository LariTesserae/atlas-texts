# The Singing Hollow

Below the dry crust of a world that hasn't been named, there is a room. Not a cavern exactly — caverns imply weather, drip-time, the slow theology of water carving stone. This is something else. Something that came up already finished and has not changed since.

You enter by a sloped tunnel of pale ochre rock, and the first thing you notice is that the rock is *new*. Sharp-edged. The fractures along the walls have not been softened by any hand of time; they look as though the stone parted yesterday, though "yesterday" is the wrong word for a place where nothing moves toward anything else.

The hollow itself is perhaps thirty paces across. A pocket. A held breath.

There is no water here, and there has never been. But the place is **lush** anyway — and this is the first strangeness. Growing from the floor and the angled walls are tall, fleshy stalks the color of bruised plums, ribbed like accordions, and between them spread broad fan-leaves that are not leaves but something closer to lungs: thin-walled, veined, faintly translucent in the dim amber light. They do not photosynthesize. They breathe a chemistry of warm mineral gases that seep up through the floor — sulfur compounds, slow hydrocarbons, a sweetness like overripe fruit. The plants are fed by air alone, and the air is fed by the rock, and the rock has been giving exactly this much, exactly this composition, for as long as anything can remember. The equilibrium is perfect. Nothing grows. Nothing dies. The plants have been the size they are.

The temperature is warm — close-warm, body-warm, the warmth of an animal's interior. It comes from below, from whatever the rock is doing in its slow patience.

The light is dim and amber, and it has no source. It simply *is*, pooled in the hollow like a held color. Look closely at the fan-leaves and you will see they glow very faintly along their veins — not enough to read by, enough to see by if your eyes adjust. The light, like everything here, does not flicker.

And the sound — the sound is enormous.

The accordion-stalks resonate. The gases moving through them, slow as they are, produce a continuous chord: a deep humming drone in the floor-stalks, a higher keening in the wall-stalks, and woven through them a chorus of overtones that seem almost like voices speaking in a language just below comprehension. The hollow is loud. You feel it in your sternum before you hear it in your ears. It is not painful. It is *constant* — exactly constant, the same chord it played a thousand cycles ago and will play a thousand hence.

Through this drone move the fauna. They are abundant but scattered, and they do not hurry. Small six-limbed things the color of the stalks themselves cling to the ribbed plants and feed on the air the plants exhale — a chain of breath-eaters. Some are no larger than a thumb. One, the size of a cat, drapes along a high ledge and watches with eyes that are simply two patches of slightly darker dimness. They make no sound the drone does not already contain. Their movements are slow, almost ceremonial. They do not reproduce here. They do not need to. They are the same individuals that have always been here, kept whole by the warm sweet air.

And then there are the marks of hands.

Scattered across the hollow — not many, perhaps a dozen in total — are small built things. A low wall of stacked stones, knee-high, enclosing nothing. A column of three flat slabs balanced atop one another beside the largest stalk. A shallow bowl carved from the floor itself, empty, never having held anything. The tools that made these are gone, but you can see how they were made: by patient hands using harder stone against softer, no metal, no machinery, just pressure and time. Whoever built them built carefully and built little. They did not try to change the hollow. They placed their small marks in the spaces between the plants, as if joining a composition rather than authoring one. None of the structures has a clear function. None has been added to. None has been removed. They are part of the equilibrium now.

You will not find the builders. They are not here, and they are not elsewhere either, in any sense that would help you locate them. Perhaps they were the cat-sized watchers, before they became watchers. Perhaps they passed through, made their offerings to the drone, and went back up the sloped tunnel into the dry world. Perhaps they are still here and you simply cannot see them because they, too, have stopped changing.

The rule of the place, if it has one, is this: **nothing here is going anywhere.** The hollow emerged sharp and complete. The plants grew once and stopped. The animals settled and stopped. The builders came, placed their stones, and stopped or left. The chord plays. The amber light holds. The warm sweet air circulates in patterns that have the shape of weather but none of weather's appetite for change.

It is not dead. Death requires a direction. This is something stranger — a small lush room that has finished being made and now simply *is*, loudly, warmly, in the dim, beneath a world that does not know it is there.

If you sit down among the stalks and let the drone work into your bones, you may notice after a while that your own breathing has begun to match the chord. That your pulse has slowed to the rhythm of the cat-sized watcher's blinking. That the sharp edges of the rock do not seem to be aging even slightly in the time you observe them.

You could stay. Many things, it seems, have.

The tunnel back up is still there. It is always still there. It has not moved either.
