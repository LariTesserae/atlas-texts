# The Drowned Belfry

Begin with the sound, because everything else is secondary to it.

It is the sound of a bell struck underwater, except the bell is the size of a cathedral and the water is the size of a sea, and the strike never stops — it renews itself every few seconds from some source that cannot be located, because the source keeps moving, or keeps being in several places, or keeps being the listener. The note is low enough to push against the sternum. Higher harmonics ride on top: a constant shimmering overtone like a wet finger circling crystal, and under that, further down, a rhythm of deeper thuds that might be the pressure of the water itself remembering how to be solid.

There is no silence here. There has not been silence here for a long time. Whatever lived here before the sound either left or became part of the sound.

---

The place is a submerged summit. A mountain peak, young — the rock is still sharp at its edges, fractured in clean conchoidal breaks, the kind of surfaces that haven't yet been rounded by the long patience of weather. It rises to within maybe forty meters of some surface, though "surface" is the wrong word, because above the peak the water thickens rather than ending. It goes from water to something denser — a ceiling of liquid that holds itself up by refusing, the way a dream refuses to resolve into waking.

The light comes down through this ceiling in slow pulses. Dim, green-gold, the color of old brass left in a stream. It beats in time with the great bell-note, or the bell-note beats in time with it, or they are the same phenomenon experienced through different organs.

---

The summit is small. You could walk its ridge in fifteen minutes if walking were possible, which it is and isn't. The water here has the wrong viscosity in patches. In some places it moves through you with the usual cool insistence; in other places it has forgotten it is water and behaves like warm air, or like a thought, or like the inside of a mouth. Step from one patch to another and your weight changes, your buoyancy, your sense of which direction is down. Down is often a suggestion rather than a law.

The temperature is warm — bath-warm, bloodwarm, the warmth of something just recently alive. This is part of why the place feels wrong. Deep water should be cold. High places should be cold. This place is neither. It holds its heat the way a held hand holds heat: from contact with something.

---

On the ridges, sparse life. A kind of kelp, or what would be kelp if kelp grew in thin pale spires and held still against the current. They do not sway. The current moves; they do not. They are rooted in the fractured rock by filaments that have grown into the fractures, widening them, and the filaments continue down into the mountain for depths that no one has measured because no one has been here to measure.

Between the spires: the occasional drifting thing. A translucent disc the size of a dinner plate, pulsing slowly, no visible organs. A cluster of small dark shapes that might be fish but turn out, when you look closely, to be the shadows of fish whose bodies are elsewhere. These are rare. You can go long intervals seeing nothing alive, only the rock and the spires and the beating light.

---

And the structures.

There are four of them on the summit, arranged in no particular pattern, unless the pattern is the point and I am not seeing it. Each is small — the size of a shed, a shrine, a wellhead. Each is built from the same dark stone as the mountain but fitted, cut, placed. Someone did this. Someone came here, in water this deep, and built these things. Or the mountain grew them up out of itself in imitation of building. The joints are too clean for erosion and too strange for hands.

One structure is a low arch with nothing under it. One is a cube with a single circular opening, through which nothing can be seen — the inside is the wrong color, a color I don't have a word for, something like the color of the word "hollow." One is a stack of three flat stones, and between each stone is a layer of water that does not mix with the water around it; you can see the boundary, faintly iridescent. One is a spiral, a low spiral wall maybe knee-high, coiled inward, and at the center is simply more rock. No altar. No vessel. Just the terminus of the spiral and a slightly darker patch of stone.

There are no inscriptions. There is no tool-work fine enough to suggest a chisel. The stones were placed, and that is all that can be said.

---

The world here has rules but they are local rules. Near the arch, sound has direction — you can tell where the bell is coming from, and it is coming from a specific place in the water, some hundreds of meters off, a location that stays consistent as long as you stay near the arch. Step away and the bell is everywhere again.

Near the cube, time pools. A dropped pebble falls, but the falling takes longer than falling should take, and when it arrives at the rock it arrives with the sound it would have made earlier, as though the sound had been waiting.

Near the stacked stones, the water between the layers is not the water of this sea. It tastes of somewhere else. It tastes, if you were foolish enough to draw some up and taste it, like rainwater from a season you do not remember having lived through.

Near the spiral, you can hear yourself thinking. That is to say — the bell, for a radius of perhaps ten meters around the spiral, becomes quieter, and into that quiet your own thoughts arrive with an unusual clarity, and also with a sense that something else is listening to them. The spiral is the only place on the summit where the sound is not deafening. This is why nothing living goes near it. The spires lean away.

---

The turbulence is not in the water — the water is almost still, a great warm held breath. The turbulence is in the rules. Every few minutes something reorganizes. The light shifts its rhythm. The bell changes its interval. A spire that was white becomes, over the course of a breath, the color of dried blood, and then white again. The arch, briefly, is not there; then it is there again, in a slightly different place, and the rock beneath where it used to stand is unmarked. A school of shadow-fish passes through the cube's opening and does not come out the other side, and the opening, for a moment, is full of the color hollow, and then empty again.

The place is remaking itself constantly out of itself. Nothing new is added. Nothing is ever removed. But the arrangement is restless, and the arrangement is the place.

---

I think — and this is the part I am least sure of, the part that arrives late and might be wrong — I think this summit is not underwater in the usual sense. I think something has happened to this mountain. I think it was a peak in air once, and something came and covered it, and the covering is still here, and the mountain is still here, and the covering and the mountain have not yet finished negotiating which of them is the world. The four structures are from before. The bell is from after. The spires grew in the transition and do not belong to either era.

The warmth is the mountain's. It is still warm from having been in the sun.

It has been warm for a very long time.
