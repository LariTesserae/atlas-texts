# The Cooling Room

High on the flank of a mountain that doesn't appear on any survey — not because it is hidden, but because it wasn't there six years ago — a fissure opens in the basalt at about four thousand meters. From below, it looks like nothing: a darker seam in darker rock. You have to climb up through the scree, past the place where the lichens give up, to find it.

Inside, the cave is not a cave. It is a room.

---

The chamber is maybe fifteen meters across and vaulted like the inside of a kiln. The walls are hot — not painfully so, but enough that if you press your palm against them you pull it back after three or four seconds. They are smooth in the way that volcanic glass is smooth, except the smoothness continues down into scales too small to see, and then past those scales into something the eye stops being able to parse. If you look at the wall sideways in the dim light, you notice that it is *patterned*, and the pattern is doing slow work: hexagons within hexagons within hexagons, rotating through each other at the rate of a minute hand. Something is being computed, or cooled, or both.

The heat comes from below. There is a pit in the center of the floor, rimmed in a lip of fused stone, and the pit glows — not red, but a pale lilac, the color you sometimes see at the bottom of a welder's arc. Warm air rises off it in columns that you can see because of dust. The dust is not ordinary dust. It drifts upward in spirals that are a little too organized, pauses at certain heights as if reading the air, and disperses. Some of it settles on the ceiling in faint rings.

---

There is sound. The room is loud — not with echoes, but with a sustained low chord, like an organ pedal held underneath everything. Beneath that, a ticking: irregular, sharp, the sound of small things crystallizing or small welds completing. Every few minutes the chord shifts by a fraction of a semitone and the ticking redistributes itself across the room, moving from the north wall to the east, the east to the floor, the floor to the ceiling. It is impossible not to feel that the room is *paying attention* to which part of itself is doing the work.

When the chord shifts, the temperature shifts with it. There are weather systems in here. A cool draft will run along the floor for a minute, condensing a narrow line of moisture against the base of the far wall — enough water to bead, to run, to collect in a shallow groove that carries it toward the pit, where it vanishes in a faint hiss before reaching the lilac light. Then the draft stops and the wall dries, and a patch of the ceiling, somewhere else, begins to weep its own small rain.

---

Plants have found this place. Not many. At the lip of the pit, where the rim is cooler, a cluster of what looks like rosemary grows out of the seam between stone and engineered surface — the leaves are darker, harder, more waxed than any rosemary has a right to be, and if you snap one it smells of rosemary and also ozone. Higher up, in a niche near the ceiling where the condensation pools, there's a mat of something mosslike that has chosen to be the color of old copper. A single vine, pale and blind, crosses the north wall in a straight diagonal line as if following an instruction.

No insects. No birds. Nothing moves on its own legs in here. But the plants are not lonely — the room is doing too much, and they seem to be listening.

---

The built elements are few and hard to tell from the rock. Someone, or something, has set three small markers into the floor — cylinders of a dull metal the size of a thumb, arranged in a triangle around the pit. They are warm but not hot, and if you hold your hand over one the low chord gets fractionally quieter, as if the room has turned to face you. On the wall near the entrance, at shoulder height, a palm-sized patch has been worn smooth — by what, over how long, is unclear. There are no tool marks anywhere. Whatever made this chamber did not carve it; it arrived already made.

There is also, tucked into a dry fold of the east wall, a small ceramic cup. Glazed. Ordinary. Empty. Someone has been here.

---

The rock outside the chamber is new. That is the strangest thing, and the one you only understand after you leave. The mountain is young in the geological sense — the basalt is sharp-edged, the scree hasn't rounded, the ridges above still have the faceted freshness of lava that cooled last decade rather than last million years. Nothing has had time to erode. And the chamber sits inside this newness like a pearl in a shell that grew around it, except the shell grew in months instead of millennia, and the pearl was placed there first.

The best guess — and it is only a guess, made by standing at the lip of the pit and feeling the chord travel up through your sternum — is that the chamber is a radiator. Something, somewhere, is running hot. It needs to shed heat into rock, into air, into the slow patient cold of a high mountain. So it made a mountain. The mountain is the heat sink. The chamber is the interface. The lilac light is the thinnest edge of whatever is being cooled, reaching up through the floor to touch the world for a moment before sinking back.

The plants and the ceramic cup suggest that whoever or whatever runs it does not mind company. Only that the company be quiet, and willing to sit in the heat, and listen to the chord shift, and watch the dust read the air.
