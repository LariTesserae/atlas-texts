There's a hollow in the crown of a mountain — not a caldera, nothing so violent, just a long slow surrender of stone to its own weight. The peak has been weathering for a span of time that doesn't bear thinking about, and what was once a sharp summit is now a rounded bowl, perhaps two hundred meters across, its rim worn to the smoothness of old bone. From the slopes below, you would never know the hollow was here. The mountain looks merely tired, merely old. You would have to climb to the very rim to find it, and almost no one does.

Inside, there is no standing water. There has never been standing water, not in any age the rocks remember. But the bowl catches mist. Every morning, every evening, and through most of the cool blue hours of night, cloud-banks drag themselves across the high air and snag on the rim, and what they leave behind is enough. It beads on leaves. It collects in the cupped petals of certain flowers. It runs as a thin film down the bark of the trees and into a spongy understory of moss and lichen that has been thickening for so long it is no longer clear where the soil ends and the moss begins. The mountain drinks fog. Everything in the bowl drinks the mountain.

The vegetation is layered and close. Trees here are short — none taller than four or five meters, because the wind that scours the rim shears anything that tries to rise higher — but they grow with the density of something much grander, branches interlocking, leaves overlapping in three or four canopies stacked within that modest height. The wood is dark and twisted. The leaves are small, waxy, dark green with a faint silver underside that flashes when the air moves. Between the trees: ferns waist-high, cushions of moss the size of sleeping animals, vines that loop from trunk to trunk in unhurried arcs. There are flowers — pale, tubular, mostly — but they are not the main event. The main event is the green itself, dim and warm and saturated, packed in tight.

It is warm in the bowl. Warmer than it has any right to be at this elevation. The stone holds heat through the night, the canopy traps it, and the constant slow respiration of so much packed life adds its own faint furnace. You sweat lightly just by standing still.

The light is dim because of the canopy and because the mist filters everything that comes down from the sky. Even at noon the bowl is the green-grey of a held breath. At dusk it goes almost black, but not silent — never silent.

The sound is the thing you notice first, and then can never stop noticing. The bowl is loud. Not with weather, not with water, but with bodies. Insects in numbers that defeat estimation: a low constant drone from somewhere in the middle canopy, a higher chirring from the understory, the click and tick of hard wing-cases against bark. Birds — many kinds, all small, because nothing large could maneuver in this density — calling and answering in dozens of voices that braid together into a continuous fabric of sound. Tree-frogs, despite the absence of pools, breeding in the cupped reservoirs of bromeliad-like plants whose leaves hold a few teaspoons of mist-water each. Something larger moves through the moss at ground level, often; you hear it but rarely see it. There are small furred climbers in the branches, quick and curious, with long fingers. There are pale moths the size of a hand. There are columns of ants moving along the trunks in lines so dense they look like flowing rope.

The fauna is overwhelming. The animals do not fear you, because nothing in their long history has taught them to. They land on you. They investigate. A beetle the color of a wet plum walks across the back of your hand and you let it, because brushing it off feels rude in a place this full of living things. The understanding settles in slowly: you are outnumbered by a factor it would be foolish to calculate. You are a guest in a room that has been occupied for ten thousand thousand years.

And there is a settlement.

This is the strangest part, and the part that explains why anyone is here at all. Built into and among the trees, woven through the canopy and propped against the inner curve of the bowl's stone wall, there is a small town. It is hard to see at first because it does not contrast with the forest — the walls are bark and packed mud and woven cane, the roofs are living moss laid over frames of bent wood, the walkways are vines lashed into bridges. But once your eye adjusts you realize the density is not only botanical. There are dwellings stacked three and four high against the rim-stone, connected by ladder and rope-bridge and the occasional staircase carved directly into the rock. There are perhaps three hundred structures. Perhaps four hundred. They press against one another, share walls, share roofs, share the trees that hold them up. It is an urban density at a hidden scale — a city the size of a village green, a city that fits inside a hat.

The people who live here — and there are people, though I will not describe their faces because that is not mine to invent — work with simple tools. Bone, wood, stone, a little hammered copper for the things that absolutely must hold an edge. They weave. They carve. They cultivate certain of the moss-cushions for fiber and certain of the flowers for their nectar, which they collect drop by drop in tiny cane vessels. They do not farm in any large sense; they cannot, there is no room. They live embedded in the ecology rather than imposed upon it. Their houses house insects and frogs alongside families, and this is not considered a problem or even a fact worth noting.

Their rules, as far as a brief look can determine: do not cut the old trees. Do not kill the climbers in the branches. Take only what the mist gives. Stay below the rim — above the rim is the wind, and the wind is not a friend. When something dies, return it to the moss, which will accept it as it accepts everything, slowly.

The whole place is in motion. Not fast motion — the trees are not going anywhere, the stone is not going anywhere, the people have lived in this exact configuration for longer than they can count generations — but the small constant motion of a system at full saturation. Insects emerging, dying, being eaten, decomposing into the moss. Birds nesting, fledging, falling, feeding the climbers. Mist gathering, beading, dripping, evaporating. A leaf falls every few seconds somewhere in the bowl, and somewhere else a new shoot is unfurling, and the net amount of green is exactly what it was an age ago.

Nothing here is new. Nothing here is ending. The mountain has worn down to the shape it wants to be, and the life inside it has settled into the configuration that the shape allows, and the people have folded themselves into the configuration the life allows, and now everything is simply continuing, loudly and warmly and in the dim green light, packed close, hidden, going on.
