# The Kelp Cathedrals

Begin at the floor, which is not floor but the oldest settled thing — a plain of silt so fine and so long-undisturbed that it has the consistency of pressed felt. Step on it and you would not sink; you would simply leave a print that no current is strong enough to erase. The silt is grey-green where any light reaches it, and below that first skin it is black, and below that it is older black, sediment laid down when the waters first came and stayed.

The water is cold. Not the killing cold of polar shelves but the patient cold of things that have been cold for a very long time and expect to remain so. A diver — if a diver could reach here — would feel the cold enter through the suit seams in slow increments, the way cold enters a stone house in autumn.

Above the silt floor rise the cathedrals.

They were built, once. That much is certain from the geometry: the ribbed columns stand in rows, the arches spring from capitals, the long naves run true for what must be kilometers before fading into the dark-green distance. But the building happened so long ago that the distinction between built and grown has softened. Kelp has taken the columns. Not the surface kelp of shallow coasts — a deeper, slower kelp, broad-bladed, dark as wet leather, its holdfasts wrapped around stone fluting so completely that you cannot tell whether the stone supports the kelp or the kelp now supports the stone. In places the original carving shows through: a bit of relief, a band of interlocking shapes, a face so worn it is only the suggestion of a face. In other places the kelp is three meters thick on the column and the column is only a rumor inside it.

The kelp grows in tiers. Lower kelp, middle kelp, high kelp — each layer adapted to the particular gloom of its height, each layer shading the one beneath. Between the tiers drift slow curtains of smaller weeds, red and umber, and long translucent ribbons that might be plant or might be something in between. Density is the word. Not crowding — there is still space to move between the columns, to pass down the long aisles — but every volume of water holds something: a blade, a frond, a slow-settling flake of organic snow, a strand of filament anchored somewhere above and trailing somewhere below.

The light comes from above and arrives exhausted. Whatever sun exists on the surface of this world has to pass through a great depth of water to reach the cathedral floor, and by the time it does it is only a greenish suggestion, a directionality more than an illumination. You know which way is up because the green is very slightly less black in that direction. The tops of the high kelp catch this last light and hold it like stained glass holds the evening; looking up through the canopy you see a vault of dim, mottled luminescence, broken by the silhouettes of the column-tops and the slow sway of the blades. This is the light the cathedrals were built for, perhaps. It is certainly the light they now have.

The scale is continental. Walk — swim, drift — in any direction and the columns continue. The aisles branch and rejoin. Occasionally the floor dips into a broad shallow basin where the kelp thins and you can see further, see the forest of columns receding into green haze, and understand that what you are inside is not a building but a territory of building, a construction that covers a significant fraction of a world. There are weather-systems down here: great slow gyres of cold water that take weeks to complete a circuit, bringing nutrients from one district of the cathedral to another. There are thermoclines that function as ceilings, where warmer water sits over colder and the kelp species change abruptly along the boundary. There are silt-storms, when some distant event stirs the floor and a grey fog moves through the columns for days before settling.

It is quiet. Not silent — water is never silent — but quiet in the particular way of vast cold places. The kelp blades rub against stone with a sound like slow breathing. Far off, something structural shifts: a worn capital finally gives, a column sheds a skin of calcified growth, and the sound travels for kilometers through the water, a soft deep knock that arrives at your ears long after whatever made it has settled. Between these rare events there is only the hush of the current through the canopy and the occasional tick of a bit of shell, a fragment of old carving, falling through the water column to the floor.

There are no fish. This is the strangest thing about the cathedrals, and the hardest to feel comfortable with once you have noticed it. A place this lush, this sheltered, this stable, ought to swarm with animal life. It does not. Perhaps the animals left when the water grew this cold; perhaps they were never here; perhaps the builders, whoever they were, made a place that excluded them, and the exclusion has held. There are microbes, of course, and the kelp is kelp, meaning it is alive. But nothing swims. Nothing hunts. The long aisles of the cathedral run for days of travel without a single moving creature to break the stillness. This absence is what gives the place its particular feeling — not menace, not loneliness exactly, but a sense of being in a room that has been set for a gathering that will not occur.

Who built it? The evidence suggests hands. Hand-tools, simple fabrication — the columns were cut, not cast; the joins were fitted, not poured. Here and there in a sheltered pocket where the kelp has not entirely taken over you can see the chisel marks, each stroke the width of a thumb, running in parallel lines that speak of patience more than skill. Whoever made this place made it slowly, by hand, over a span of time that dwarfs any single civilization. The builders did not have metal, or had very little; the fittings are stone-in-stone, sometimes pegged with a harder stone, occasionally with what looks like a primitive bronze pin corroded now to a green stain. They knew how to make an arch. They knew how to make it stand underwater, or in air that later became water, and either way they knew how to make it *last*. What they did not seem to care about was ornament: the carving, where it survives, is simple, geometric, repeating. The cathedrals are not beautiful in the way of human cathedrals. They are regular. They are very, very regular, and the regularity at this scale becomes its own kind of beauty, the beauty of a thing that has decided what it is and is that thing everywhere, forever.

The rules of the place, as best I can describe them:

Change is slow. A kelp blade grows the length of a human arm in a year. A column sheds a fingerbreadth of stone in a century. The silt accumulates one grain at a time. Whatever process shaped this world has reached a point of near-equilibrium; the cathedrals are in their final form, or close enough that the remaining erosion will take geological epochs to matter.

Direction is given by the light, weak as it is, and by the columns, which line up in ways that suggest cardinal orientations. If you lose both — get into a tight grove of high kelp where the canopy closes and the floor-print of the columns is ambiguous — you can drift for a long time without knowing it.

Temperature drops with depth, but only slightly; the whole volume is cold, and the cold is stable, and this stability is perhaps what has allowed the kelp and the cathedrals to persist together so long.

Nothing here is in a hurry. Nothing here is hunting. Nothing here is being hunted. The dominant process is patience.

If you stayed long enough — and the place invites this, more than almost any place I can describe — you would begin to notice the slower rhythms. The monthly surge as the distant tide pulls harder. The seasonal shift in the green overhead, which goes from slightly-less-black to very-slightly-more-green and back again. The great current that passes through your particular aisle once a year, carrying a freight of silt and spores from whatever other district sent it. You would feel your own pulse become a loud, embarrassing thing inside this quiet. You would feel your own warmth become strange. You would understand, eventually, that you are the fastest thing in this landscape by several orders of magnitude, and that the cathedrals have absorbed faster things before, and that to stay is to slow.

Most visitors, I think, would leave.

The cathedrals would not notice.
