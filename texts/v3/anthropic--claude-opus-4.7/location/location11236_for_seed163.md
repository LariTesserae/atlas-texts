# The Cloudloom Highlands

The first thing is the light. It comes from everywhere at once — not from the sun, which is somewhere up there behind a ceiling of cloud, but from the cloud itself, which has been pulled apart and lit from within until the whole sky is one diffuse, painful brilliance. You squint even looking at the ground. The ground throws it back at you. Every wet leaf is a small mirror.

You are high. The air thins against your teeth. The plateau — if it is a plateau; the edges of it disappear into white before you can confirm — runs out in every direction in a soft, undulating swell, neither flat nor mountainous, the way a sheet still settling onto a bed is neither flat nor mountainous. The rock under everything is young. You can tell because it has not yet decided what it wants to be. In places it shows through the green as pale, unweathered stuff, faintly crystalline, edges still sharp, as though it had only recently been pushed up here and the wind and water hadn't had time to round it. There are no canyons. There are no cliffs cut by rivers. The rivers, when they appear, simply lie on top of the land like spilled mercury, having not yet had centuries enough to bite downward.

What there is, instead of erosion, is plant.

The plant is the landscape. Not vegetation *on* a landscape — vegetation *as* the landscape. A spongy, woven, knee-deep mat of mosses and cushion plants and something like sphagnum but lit a pale, almost luminous green, runs unbroken across the swell of the highlands for what must be miles. You step on it and you sink three inches and water wells up around your boot and disappears again when you lift it. It is saturated. It is the saturation. The whole tableland is one slow, breathing sponge that holds the rain and the condensing fog and slowly, slowly leaks it back out at the edges in those silver, hesitant rivers.

Above the moss-substrate, the taller things are sparse. This is the surprise of the place — you expect, with so much water and so much green, a jungle, a riot. But it isn't. The tall growths stand apart from each other, each one held in its own clear circle of space, like figures at a formal gathering. Tree-ferns, mostly, twelve or fifteen feet high, their crowns enormous and dripping, their trunks furred with epiphytes. Between them, columns of something stranger: a kind of giant horsetail, segmented, hollow, ringing faintly when the wind moves through it. The wind moves through it constantly. There may be one such column every forty paces. The space between them is the green substrate and the sky-glare and nothing else.

And the sound.

The sound is the thing you cannot describe to anyone afterward. It is not the trees. It is not the wind, although the wind is part of it. Somewhere — and you cannot find where, the brightness defeats your eyes whenever you try to look toward it — there is a *works*. Industry. Some kind of engine-house or pumping-station or mill, and from it comes a continuous, full-throated mechanical roar that fills the whole basin of the highlands and rolls back off the cloud-ceiling and returns doubled. It is the kind of sound that you stop hearing within ten minutes and then realize, with a jolt, that you have been shouting at your companion for an hour without knowing it. It is a sound that gets into your sternum. Beneath it, almost inaudible, is the higher whine of belts or turbines, and beneath *that* something that might be water being moved at great pressure through great pipes.

You find evidence of the works without finding the works. A length of iron pipe, four feet across, emerging from the moss and disappearing back into it ten yards on, rusted but solid, weeping at its seams. A line of squat brick pylons — six of them, then nothing — striding off into the glare carrying nothing, their cables long gone or never strung. A small stone building, no bigger than a shed, with a tin roof and a padlocked door and a single thick cable going down into the earth beside it, humming. No road leads to it. No path. The moss has grown up to its threshold and stopped, politely, as though waiting.

Animals are rare here. You will see, perhaps, in a full day's walking: one pale hare-like thing, frozen at a hundred yards, ears translucent against the sky-glare; the shadow of a large bird passing through cloud overhead and never resolving into a bird; the trails of something small and many-legged through the wet moss, going from one cushion-plant to another on errands of its own. The plateau is not empty of fauna but it is not *for* fauna. It is for plant and water and whatever the works is doing.

Change happens here on the scale of a human life or several. The rivers will, in a thousand years, cut their first shallow grooves into the young rock. The moss-mat will deepen by an inch in a decade. The tree-ferns grow visibly in a year but the spacing between them — that strange formal spacing — will be the same in your grandchildren's time. Whatever the works is producing, or extracting, or holding back, it has been producing or extracting or holding back since before anyone alive remembers, and it sounds the same now as it did then, and the moss has grown around its pipes without consuming them and without being consumed by them. A truce, or a stalemate, or a relationship so old it no longer requires negotiation.

At evening — though "evening" is only the slow shift of the sky-glare from white to a pearled, slightly warmer white — the fog comes down. It doesn't roll in. It simply becomes apparent that the air, which was always full of water, now contains slightly more, and the tree-ferns thirty paces off go soft, and the horsetail-columns become suggestions, and the roar of the unseen works, undiminished, seems now to come from inside the fog itself, from every direction equally, as though the cloud were the engine, as though you had been walking all day across the floor of a vast bright machine that was patiently, deafeningly, making weather.
