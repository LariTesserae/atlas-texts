# The Sun-Well

There is a chamber under the dry country, and it is full of light.

You would not expect this. The opening is small — a fissure perhaps two arm-spans across at the surface, choked with pale grass — but the rock beneath has been hollowed by some patient water across an age that is no longer fully present. What remains is a rounded room, perhaps thirty paces from wall to wall, with a floor of polished travertine that dips toward its center like the inside of a shallow bowl. The ceiling rises into a soft dome, and the dome is pierced. Not by one shaft but by many: dozens of narrow flues bored through the limestone overburden, each one no wider than a wrist, each one angled differently. They were drilled, it seems, by hand — there are chisel-marks in the upper reaches, the slow work of someone with a copper pick and a great deal of time — but no one is here now, and no one has been for a long while. The drillers left only their geometry.

The flues catch the sun. From mid-morning until late afternoon, the chamber is shot through with rods of light, each one slow-moving, each striking the floor at its own angle. Where they cross, the air glows. Dust drifts in the beams — there is always a little dust, very fine, lifted by the warm air that rises out of the deeper passages and exits up through the flues, a soft constant breath. You can feel it on your face if you stand at the center: warm air going up, cool air going nowhere in particular.

The walls are damp. Not wet — damp. A film of moisture beads on the lower stone where the rock is cooler, and along certain fissures the water gathers enough to fall, drop by drop, into small basins it has carved itself. These basins are the only standing water: six or seven of them, none larger than a washing bowl, each rimmed with a pale lip of redeposited mineral. The drip rate is slow. You could count the seconds between drops and lose the count to your own breathing.

Vegetation is sparse and specific. A few ferns hang from the flues where seeds have fallen through — small, dark, hardy things, their fronds always wet from the condensation that runs back down the stone. A pale moss grows in a ring around each basin, exactly to the height the water once reached and no higher. Nothing grows on the floor. The floor is too bright.

The strangeness is in the fauna, and you do not see them at first because the light is too strong. They are at the edges. Pale, soft-bodied creatures — somewhere between a salamander and something you have no name for — cling to the walls in the shadowed seams. They are abundant. Once your eyes adjust you realize the walls are *furred* with them, hundreds, each the length of a finger, each translucent enough that the rods of light pass through their bodies and emerge faintly tinted. They do not move much. When they do move, it is to reposition slightly so that a beam of light passes through them at a new angle. They appear to be photosynthesizing, or doing something analogous — there are green threads inside them, structures that pulse very slowly. They eat the light. They drink the wall.

They make almost no sound. Occasionally one will release a small clicking note, like a pebble tapping stone, and another will answer from across the chamber, and then there will be silence again for a long time. The dripping continues underneath. Above all of it, the faint hiss of air moving up through the flues, a sound so constant you stop hearing it and then hear it again when you remember to listen.

The rules of the place seem to be these: the light comes in but cannot easily leave. It enters as beams and is broken, scattered, absorbed by the salamander-things, reflected from the wet stone, until the whole chamber holds a soft general radiance even in the corners. The water enters as vapor and condensation and leaves as the slow breath upward through the flues, carrying with it whatever the chamber has warmed. The animals neither arrive nor depart, so far as anyone could tell; they were here when the drillers came, and they were here when the drillers left, and they will be here.

Nothing happens quickly. A drop falls. A salamander shifts. A beam of light moves the width of a hand across the floor over the course of an hour. The chamber has been like this, more or less exactly like this, for a span of time that makes the word *long* feel inadequate — the basins have grown their rims at the rate of a hair's breadth per generation, and the rims are tall.

If you sat down at the bowl's center and stayed, the place would not change for you. It would continue. The light would track. The wall would breathe. And eventually you would notice that you, too, had begun to keep its time: your breath slowing to match the drift of air through the flues, your attention loosening into the slow pulse of the green threads in the walls. This is, perhaps, what the drillers came for. It is not clear they ever left. It is only clear that they are not, now, here in any form you would recognize, and that the chamber is full of light, and quiet, and warm, and waiting in no particular direction for nothing in particular to occur.
