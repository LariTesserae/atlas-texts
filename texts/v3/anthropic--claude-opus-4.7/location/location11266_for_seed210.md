There is a hollow high in the mountains, no bigger than a small chapel's footprint, walled on three sides by sheer young stone — stone so recently uplifted that its faces are still glossy where they broke, the quartz veins inside them not yet dulled by weather. The fourth side opens to nothing: a clean drop into cloud. You cannot see the valley. There may not be a valley.

Water is everywhere without ever falling as rain. The rock weeps. Every surface beads, and the beads gather into rills that run down the cliff-faces in slow steady ribbons, never swelling, never drying. The moss drinks what it needs and the rest goes — somewhere. The pool at the center of the hollow is perhaps four paces across and perfectly still. Things drip into it constantly and it does not ripple. This is the first sign that something is wrong with the rules here, but it is a quiet sign, and easy at first to mistake for stillness of the ordinary kind.

The temperature is the cool of a root cellar in spring. Breath fogs faintly. The mosses are the deep saturated greens of places that have never been hot — cushion moss, thread moss, a liverwort like wet green lace climbing the lower rock. Ferns unfurl from crevices, and one small twisted rowan grows out of the cliff at an impossible angle, its berries the color of arterial blood, far more numerous than the tree should be able to bear. The vegetation is lush but spaced: a clump here, a curtain there, and between them bare wet stone. Nothing crowds. Everything has its place and keeps it.

And the sound. The sound is the strange thing, or the second strange thing.

The hollow is loud. Not with water — the water is silent, the weeping makes no noise at all — but with the steady industrial roar of unseen machinery. Deep reciprocating thuds, the sustained whine of turbines, the clatter of belt-driven looms, the hiss of released steam. It comes from inside the rock. It comes from beneath the pool. It comes, faintly, from inside the rowan tree if you put your ear to the trunk. There are no machines. There has never been anyone here to build machines. The stone is too young to contain ruins; it has been above the cloud-line for perhaps a few thousand years, geologically newborn. And yet the sound of a great works in full production pours out of every surface, unwavering, never louder, never softer, never pausing for shift-change or breakdown. A factory that is not there, running on nothing, making nothing, forever.

The animals do not mind. The animals are the third strange thing, and the most numerous.

There are pikas in the scree, more than the scree should support — small round bodies stacked on every ledge, watching. There are dippers at the pool, six or seven of them, who walk into the still water and out again without disturbing it. A pine marten drapes itself along a branch of the rowan and does not move for hours, but its eyes track. Salamanders the color of wet slate lie in every crevice. A pair of choughs ride the updraft at the cliff's open edge, never landing, never tiring, the same two birds in the same two arcs. Beetles in the moss. A single hare, white-pelted though it is not winter, sitting upright by the pool as if it has business there.

None of them eat each other. None of them eat at all, that you can see. The pikas do not crop the moss. The marten does not stalk the pikas. The dippers do not take the larvae that must surely live in the pool. They are abundant and they are at peace and they are, all of them, listening to the factory that isn't there, with the attentiveness of workers at a shift they understand.

Nothing changes. The light is the dim even grey of an overcast hour just before dusk, and it has been that hour since you arrived and it will be that hour when you leave. The water weeps at the same rate. The choughs trace the same arcs. The rowan berries do not fall. The machinery does not pause. If you sit on the wet stone for what feels like a long time and then stand, the wet patch beneath you is already gone, the moss already sprung back, as though you had not been there — or as though the hollow has a single correct configuration and reverts to it the moment you stop pressing against it.

The rules of the place, as best as they can be stated:

Water moves but does not accumulate or diminish. Sound exists without source. Life is present without metabolism. Time passes without consequence. The stone is new and will stay new. Nothing here is becoming anything else.

It is possible the hollow is a held note — a single sustained chord struck somewhere at the world's making and still ringing here because nothing has yet come to damp it. It is possible the machinery-sound is the noise that holding makes, the cost in audible labor of keeping one small place exactly itself against the running-down of everything around it. It is possible the animals are the workers, and that their stillness is the work.

You can leave by the way you came. The way you came is always there. The hollow does not mind whether you stay or go; it is not for you. It was here before the question of you, and the turbines under the pool will go on whining their patient whine long after.
