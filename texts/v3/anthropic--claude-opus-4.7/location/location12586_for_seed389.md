# The Kiln-Cities of the Rising Cordillera

There is a mountain range being born. Not in the human sense — not a slow grinding of plates over millennia — but in something more like the way bread rises, or a tooth pushes through gum. The peaks here are still soft at their cores. You can see it in the way certain cliff faces, in the late afternoon, will *bulge* almost imperceptibly outward, then settle. Geologists, if there were any, would call this pre-orogenic. The people who live here just call it the Lifting.

The altitude is brutal. We are well above any line where lungs should function — and yet the cities cling to the ridges anyway, terraced into the new stone, a hundred thousand kiln-fired ceramic dwellings stacked roof to floor along the cordillera's spine. Smoke rises from them not in great plumes but in thin, continuous threads, because everything here burns slow. The air is too thin for vigorous fire. A coal that would flash in lowland atmosphere here glows for days, patient as a held breath.

The heat does not come from the sun, mostly. It comes from below. The mountains are warm to the touch, even at this elevation — warmer the higher you climb, which is the first wrongness a traveler notices. Snow does not form here. Ice does not form here. Instead, at certain altitudes, the rock simply *sweats*. Water beads on the basalt in the cool of pre-dawn, runs in thin rivulets down the carved gutters the city-builders have cut into every surface, fills cisterns and basins and the long ceramic aqueducts that connect district to district like capillaries. By midday the sweat has stopped. The stone is dry and hot enough to cook on, and indeed in many quarters this is how cooking is done: a flatbread laid directly on a south-facing wall.

There are no plants. None. Not moss, not lichen, not algae in the cisterns. The world is too young for them, or too hot, or some other reason no one has settled on. What grows instead — and only in the deepest, dampest cellar-shafts where the sweat-water pools — is a kind of slow mineral fur, pale greenish, that the residents harvest with stone scrapers. It is not alive in any sense a biologist would recognize. It accretes. It can be ground into a paste that has nutritive properties, or seems to. People eat it. People have eaten it for generations. No one has died of starvation here, so something is working.

The animals are few and strange. There is a creature the size of a cat, with a body like fired clay and a soft luminous spot where eyes might be, that walks the rooftops at twilight and is considered lucky. There are things in the deep shafts that no one looks at directly. There are, occasionally, flights of something high overhead — too high to resolve — that cast moving shadows across the terraces and are spoken of only in the past tense, as if to acknowledge them in the present would be impolite.

The cities are *quiet*. This is the thing that strikes a visitor most, after the heat and the altitude. There are foundries here, forges, great wheel-houses where geothermal pressure drives the bellows and the hammers — industry on a scale that should fill the valleys with thunder. But the thin air swallows sound. A hammer strike at fifty paces is a soft tap. A foundry in full operation sounds, from across the canyon, like someone gently shuffling cards. The workers speak in low voices because loud voices simply do not carry; they have learned to read lips, to read the angle of a shoulder, to communicate in the small gestures available to those who share narrow ledges for a lifetime. The cities hum rather than roar. A constant low almost-music of working metal, working stone, working steam, all muted to the threshold of attention.

And everywhere, the slowness. Things change here on geological time even when they are not geological. A wall takes a generation to build, not because the masons are unskilled but because the stone they cut is still soft enough that it must be allowed to cure in place, year by year, before the next course can be laid. A child apprenticed to the bellows-houses at six will not be trusted to oversee a single furnace until forty. The festivals come not annually but at intervals of seven, eleven, nineteen years, marking events in the long rhythm of the Lifting — a particular peak passing a particular altitude, a particular fumarole opening or closing. The people are not in a hurry. The mountain is not in a hurry. The fire underneath is not in a hurry.

At night, when the sweat-water begins to return and the foundries bank their coals, you can stand on the highest terrace of the highest city and look out across the cordillera — ridge after ridge of soft-cored stone, each crowned with its constellation of dim orange windows, each trailing its thin thread of smoke straight up into the windless dark — and feel the whole range pushing, gently, slowly, against the sky. Rising at the speed of patience. Carrying its cities with it. Going somewhere none of them will live to see.
