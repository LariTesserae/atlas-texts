# The Shelf at Iren-Kath

There is a shelf of rock, perhaps two hundred paces across at its widest, set into the side of a mountain so high that the air on it tastes of nothing — no pollen, no soil-rot, no smoke. The mountain has no name I can give you. It is one of the young ones: the stone still pale where it has cracked, the edges of every facet sharp enough to part skin. When a piece of it falls (and pieces fall, often, somewhere out of sight) the sound does not return. The drop swallows everything.

The light here is enormous. The shelf faces — I think — east and slightly south, and from before what would be dawn at lower altitudes the sun is already on it, a hard white sun that does not warm the rock so much as illuminate every grain of it. The shadows are absolute. Where a stone leans, the dark beneath it is a black wedge cut from the world. Where it does not lean, every surface is so bright you cannot look long. You squint here even with your eyes closed.

It is cold. Not the cold of winter at low places — there is no winter here, only the single permanent cold of altitude. Water in the cup of a stone freezes at night and thaws by mid-morning, and this happens every day, and has happened every day since the shelf emerged, which was not so long ago.

And yet — vegetation. This is the surprise of the place. From every crack in the pale rock, cushions of green. Tight, knotted, low things: mosses the color of old jade, lichens in disks of orange and bone-white, and a kind of plant I do not have a name for — fleshy rosettes the size of a child's palm, each leaf edged in violet, holding in their centers a single bead of meltwater that catches the sun and throws it back as a needle of light. There are thousands of these rosettes. They grow in colonies along the seep-lines where the rock weeps. They are the principal greenery of the shelf, and they hum, very faintly, in the wind — not a sound exactly, more a pressure against the ear, as if the leaves were tuned to something.

Water comes intermittently. There is no stream. But in the rock above the shelf, ice melts in pulses during the day, and the meltwater runs down in thin sheets that disappear into the cushions of moss before reaching the shelf's edge. After a clear noon, the whole back wall of the shelf is dark with running wet for an hour, and then it is dry. Once every few days a cloud climbs the mountain and breaks against the shelf, and for the duration of its passage everything is inside the cloud — soft, white, lightless, the temperature dropping further — and then the cloud is gone, and the rosettes have all filled, and the lichens have darkened by a shade, and the sun is back.

It is, despite everything, full of animals.

This is what I did not expect when I began. The shelf is mostly empty — vast space between presences, the parameters said, and they were right about the volumes — but in those volumes things move. There are birds. Small, gray, sharp-winged birds that come up from somewhere below and ride the updrafts along the cliff face, calling almost inaudibly to each other. They eat the insects that live on the rosettes — and there are insects, somehow, beetles dark as polished iron and a kind of pale moth that flies only in the cold hours. There is a creature like a marmot but slighter, with longer limbs, that lives in a single burrow at the eastern corner of the shelf and emerges to graze the moss. There is at least one predator: I have seen its scat, neat and dark, on a high rock. Something cat-shaped, I think, that climbs.

And there are the goats, or what I will call goats — four of them, white, with horns that curl back tight against their skulls. They are on the shelf most mornings. They move with the absolute confidence of things that have never fallen. Their hooves make almost no sound on the rock. They eat the violet-edged rosettes and they drink from the cup-stones and they regard me, when I am there, with the flat indifference of animals who have decided I am not interesting.

So the place is not empty of life — it is empty of *crowding*. Everything has its own enormous room to occupy. The marmot-thing and the goats may never meet. The cat (if it is a cat) keeps to the upper ledges. The birds are a community unto themselves on the air.

Now — the built things. There is one structure on the shelf, and there are marks.

The structure is a cairn, taller than a man, of stones that have been chosen carefully. They are not local. The stones of the cairn are darker than the shelf's rock and smoother — river stones, carried up from somewhere far below by hands. The cairn is not new. Its base is grown over with the orange lichen, which takes a long time to spread. But it is not ancient either; the topmost stones are still loose enough to shift in the wind, and one has fallen off in recent memory and lies beside the base.

The marks: on the back wall of the shelf, where the rock is sheltered from the worst of the sun, someone has scratched a series of vertical lines with a harder stone. Tallies, perhaps. Or a count of visits. Or something else. There are forty-one of them. They have not been added to in some time — the most recent line is no fresher than the others, and all are partly filled with lichen now.

Whoever made the cairn and the marks worked without metal, or with very little of it. The scratches are the kind a flint or a piece of denser stone would make. Nothing has been carried up here that is heavier than a person could bring on their back. There is no firepit. There is no shelter. They came up, and they counted, and they piled, and they went down again, and they have not been back for a while, but they may yet come.

The unusual thing — the small strangeness of the shelf — is this: at the precise center of the shelf, where the rosettes do not grow and the rock is bare, there is a perfect circle of dry ground about three paces across. Within this circle no water collects, even when the rest of the shelf is wet. Snow does not lie on it. The goats walk through it without slowing. I cannot tell whether the circle is the cause of the cairn or its consequence — whether the people who came up here built the cairn *because of* the circle, or whether the circle appeared *after* the cairn, in some response to it. The lichen does not grow inside it. The moss stops at its edge as if at a wall.

It is silent, almost. The wind is constant but soft — the shelf is in a kind of acoustic shadow, the cliffs above and below baffling everything. The loudest sounds in an hour might be: the click of a goat's hoof, the rasp of the marmot-thing's claws on stone, the small percussive *tick* of meltwater dropping into a cup-stone, the wing-noise of the birds passing close. You can hear your own blood here. You can hear the rosettes' faint humming, if you stop moving.

And the place is alive in a slower sense — alive on the time-scale of weather and seasons and the mountain's own becoming. Every day the freeze and the thaw and the cloud and the sun and the goats coming up and the goats going down. Every week another flake of the cliff falling into the unanswering air. Every year the lichen creeping another finger's breadth across the cairn. The shelf is young; it will not last. The mountain is still rising under it, and one day the shelf will tilt, and the rosettes and the cairn and the perfect dry circle will all slide off into the cloud-layer below. But not soon. Not for a long while in the reckoning of anything that lives.

For now, it holds. A small bright cold loud-with-silence shelf, full of careful animals and one strange circle and a count of forty-one, somewhere very high up.
