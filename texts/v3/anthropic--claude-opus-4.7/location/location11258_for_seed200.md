# The Unmoving Hatchery

Call it that, though no one calls it anything. It has not been named because nothing here has the kind of mind that names.

---

The land lies low — a long shallow basin maybe four or five miles across, bowled so gently you wouldn't notice the rim if you walked it. What you would notice, immediately, is the heat. Wet heat. The air carries water the way a sponge carries water, just past saturation, so that breathing feels like drinking and drinking feels like breathing. Sweat doesn't evaporate here. It joins what's already on your skin and runs.

The ground is new. That is the strange thing — once you've been in it an hour, once your eyes adjust to the green-brown dimness under the canopy, you begin to see that nothing has been worn down. The rocks that surface through the mud are not rounded. They have edges. They are the edges they were born with. No watercourse has incised itself; the water simply spreads, sheets, pools, seeps, with nowhere it's trying to go and nothing it's trying to cut. The mud itself isn't sediment so much as a kind of primary substance, dark and slightly warm to the touch, neither building up nor washing away. The basin holds what falls into it and what crawls out of it in some perfect balance that has, apparently, always held.

Vegetation comes in three layers and they touch.

The canopy is low — fifteen, maybe twenty feet — broad fleshy fronds the color of old bruises, overlapping like roof tiles. They filter the light to a perpetual green dusk. Beneath them, a middle layer of soft-stemmed things, knee to shoulder height, with leaves like wet leather and pale flowers that never seem to open or close, just *exist* in their half-opened state. And underfoot, a saturated mat of mosses, liverworts, fungal sheets, root-hair, and something gelatinous that might be plant or might be the egg-mass of something larger. You cannot walk a step without crushing several living surfaces, but they spring back as soon as your foot lifts. No path forms behind you. The basin does not record passage.

---

And the animals. The animals.

It is hard to convey how many. Not in herds, not in flocks — those would imply space between things. Here the fauna is *interleaved* with the vegetation, occupying the same volume. Every frond has a creature folded against its underside. Every pool holds three or four overlapping bodies. Things cling to the bark of the soft trees in shingled rows. Things sit on the heads of other things. A frog the size of a fist with translucent skin balances on the spine of a slow flat-bodied reptile that is in turn straddling the back of something larger, half-submerged, that you cannot quite resolve in the dim light. None of them seem to mind. None of them seem to compete in any way you'd recognize. There is a kind of crowded peace.

They are loud. This is the sound of the basin: a continuous, layered, three-dimensional noise that never relents. Lower registers — a wet bellowing, an organ-pipe humming, the deep clicks of something below the mud. Middle registers — the wheezing trills of the canopy-dwellers, a chorus of something like cicadas that is not quite synchronous but never quite arrhythmic either. And constant treble — needle-thin whistles, the ticking of tiny mouthparts, the soft popping of air bladders being squeezed. It does not crescendo. It does not fade. If you stop and listen for an hour, then come back the next day and listen for an hour, you will hear the same sound. The same exactly.

---

This is the rule of the place, I think, the rule that holds the other parameters together:

**Nothing here has begun to change yet, and nothing has ended.**

The geology is pre-erosional — fresh rock, unweathered, as if the basin was set down whole the day before yesterday. But "the day before yesterday" is wrong, because there is no yesterday here. The ecology is full to bursting but it is not *succeeding* — no species is replacing another, no individual seems to be aging, the canopy is not growing taller, the mud is not deepening. The animals breed, perhaps, somewhere out of sight, in the same proportion as they die, which must also happen somewhere out of sight, because you never see a carcass and you never see a juvenile, only adults in their full saturated abundance, occupying every available niche of a world that does not develop niches because it does not develop.

The water hangs in the air at exactly the density it has always hung at. The temperature does not vary between what would be noon and what would be midnight — there is no noon and no midnight, only the dim green permanent afternoon under the fronds. The sky, when you glimpse it through a gap, is a flat pearly white that gives no clue.

It is a hatchery that never hatches. A world held at the moment just before something would begin — before the first stream cuts its first channel, before the first generation gives way to the second, before the first dawn. Hot, wet, teeming, deafening, and absolutely still in the only sense that matters.

---

The weirdness, when you finally locate it, is not in any one creature or feature but in the cumulative *wrongness* of the equilibrium. Step on a frond and it springs back. Disturb a pool and the ripples die in three seconds and the pool is as it was. Pick up a stone and set it down a foot away and — you will swear to this later — turn your back, turn again, and the stone is where you first found it. The basin tolerates intrusion but does not accept revision. It has a shape it intends to keep, and it keeps it, and it keeps it, and it keeps it.

Whatever made this place made it finished, and full, and loud, and warm, and unfinishable.
