# The Lung-Room

Begin underground, but not in the way you're thinking. There is no ceiling of stone. There is a ceiling of leaves — pressed flat against itself layer over layer over layer, a stratified canopy compacted into something more like sedimentary rock than vegetation, except it breathes. The whole upper surface of the chamber rises and falls on a slow cycle, maybe forty seconds in, forty seconds out, and with each exhalation a fine warm mist falls from the underside of the leaf-strata like sweat from a sleeping animal's belly.

The room is perhaps eight meters across. That is all. A single hollow embedded somewhere in a mass of living tissue too large to picture — you can feel the largeness pressing in from every direction but you cannot see it, you only see this room, this damp warm pocket of nearly-nothing.

The floor is moss, but the moss is at attention. Each frond stands upright like a tiny green soldier, and when you step (if you step — there is no clear way you arrived) they bend away from your foot a half-second before contact, so you never quite touch them. The ground reads your intention. The ground is polite.

Light comes from inside the walls. The walls are vegetable — vast cellular structures the size of fists, packed in a honeycomb that recedes into the leaf-substance, and each cell glows with a normal daylight color, the color of a clear afternoon at the latitude you grew up at, wherever that was. The cells know. They have calibrated themselves to you.

There is almost nothing in the room. This is important. Despite the total saturation of plant-matter pressing in on every side, the *interior* is hollow, near-empty, a held breath. Three things occupy it:

**One.** A single fern, waist-high, growing from the exact center of the floor. It is young — you can tell because the fiddleheads are still curling open, and they curl open *visibly*, in real time, the way a clock's second hand moves. By the time you have looked at it for a minute it is measurably larger. It is racing through its life. Whether it will die at the end of your visit or whether it has already died many times in the seconds since you noticed it is unclear.

**Two.** A flint hand-axe, knapped, resting on the moss. Nobody made it. There is nobody here and there has never been anybody here. The axe is simply a fact of the room, the way the fern is a fact, the way the breathing ceiling is a fact. It is sharp. It is warm to the touch, the temperature of a held hand.

**Three.** Something passes through. Not often. Maybe once in a long while a creature ambles across the room — you'll see it: a beetle the size of a thumbnail, or a translucent salamander, or once, a thing with too many legs that you choose not to describe to yourself. They cross from one wall to the other and the wall opens to admit them and closes behind them and the room is empty again.

Now the sound.

The sound is enormous. This is the contradiction at the heart of the place — a tiny near-empty room that *roars*. The breathing of the ceiling is a low cathedral wind. The walls hum at the pitch of a struck bowl, each cell humming its own note, the chord shifting as cells brighten and dim. From beyond the walls comes the muffled report of the larger organism: distant tidal surges of sap, the crack and groan of growth at industrial scale, occasional deep bell-tones that might be the closing of valves the size of cities. You cannot hear yourself think. The room is a quiet space inside a loud body and the body's loudness leaks in from every pore.

And then there is the weirdness, which is not an effect but the climate.

Time in here is not consistent across the room. The fern is on fast-time. The hand-axe is on slow-time — if you watch it for a while you'll notice the moss beneath it has not been compressed, as though the axe has only just landed, or has not yet landed, or weighs nothing. The mist from the ceiling falls at normal speed in the room's center and at the edges falls upward. The light from the walls casts no shadows from you, only from the fern, and the fern's shadow points consistently toward a wall that the fern is not near.

Occasionally a word will appear in your mouth — a word you don't know, in a language you don't speak — and you'll have the strong sense that the room has placed it there and is waiting to see what you do with it. If you say the word aloud, one of the wall-cells goes dark. If you swallow it, a new fiddlehead unfurls on the fern. There is no third option that has been discovered.

The rules, as best as can be articulated:

- The room is young. It came into being recently. You can feel this — there is a fresh-cut quality to everything, edges not yet softened, the smell of something just opened.
- The room is intimate. It is scaled to one occupant. Two would be a crowd; the room would not permit it, though how it would prevent it is unclear.
- The room is *attentive*. Every surface is paying attention to you. This is not hostile but it is not restful either. You are being read.
- Nothing was built here. Everything grew, or was deposited by growth, including the axe, which grew somehow, which grew the way a tooth grows, pushed up out of the gum of the world.
- The water is incidental — the mist, the condensation beading on the wall-cells, a faint dampness in the moss. There is no pool, no stream. Water is what the room sweats, not what the room contains.

If you stay long enough — and "long enough" is itself unstable here — you begin to suspect the room is one alveolus in something vast. That somewhere there is a creature, or a forest acting as a creature, and this hollow you occupy is one of millions of small breathing chambers inside it, and that the creature is alive in the sense that geology is alive, on its own timescale, and that you have been admitted for reasons of its own.

The fern reaches full height. The axe has not moved. The walls hum their afternoon chord. The ceiling exhales, and warm mist falls on your upturned face, and somewhere — through the leaf-strata, through the kilometers of pressed living tissue, through whatever this is embedded in — something enormous turns over in its sleep, and the sound of it is the sound you have been hearing all along, and have mistaken for silence because it never stops.
