# The Glasshouse Terraces of Meel

It sits on the shoulder of a mountain that no one remembers the name of, above the cloud line but below the snow — a stepped complex of maybe forty rooms, though the count varies depending on which walkways are currently flooded. From outside, if you could stand outside, it would look like a small thing. A hamlet of glass. Four or five hectares of green-tinted panes pitched at clever angles against a rock face still sharp enough to cut a palm. The mountain here is young; the granite shows clean fracture surfaces, no softening, no lichen lip. Everything that weathers in this place does so inside the glass.

The whole structure is submerged in its own weather. This is the first thing to understand. The terraces generate water — from the plants, from the condensers, from the slow boilers down in the service level — and the water never fully leaves. It pools on the floors to knee height in the lower rooms, hangs as warm fog in the middle galleries, beads and runs and beads again on every inner surface of every pane. You walk through it. You breathe it. Your clothes, within minutes, weigh what wet clothes weigh, and then they stop getting heavier because they have reached equilibrium with the air.

The plants love it. They are packed past any sensible density — ferns growing out of the root-mats of other ferns, orchids nested in the axils of tree-ferns nested in the crotches of something like a fig but with leaves the size of a door. You cannot see more than three meters in any direction. You push aside fronds and find more fronds. The walkways are narrow iron gratings, rust-rimed but structurally sound, and the vegetation has grown over them and under them and through their holes, so that in places you are walking on a living floor with iron somewhere inside it.

The temperature holds at what a body reads as just-too-warm. Not hot. The kind of warmth where you stop noticing your skin. The engines are responsible. There are engines — this is the strange part, or one of the strange parts — great slow brass-and-cast-iron engines in the lowest chambers, pistons the length of a man moving at the pace of a tide, one stroke every eleven seconds, every nine seconds, every seven. They drive the condensers. They drive the pumps that lift water from the mountain's interior up through the terraces. They drive something else too, a set of flywheels whose purpose is not immediately legible, turning behind sheets of running condensation.

The engines are nearly silent. This is what makes the place uncanny more than anything else. An industrial plant of this scale should roar. Instead, the pistons move through oil-baths and felted housings, and the loudest sound in the entire complex is the drip — the constant, uncountable, overlapping drip of condensate from leaves and from glass and from the undersides of catwalks. Occasionally a larger drop falls into a standing pool and makes a deeper note. Occasionally steam releases from a valve with a long soft hiss like a sleeper turning over. That is all. No birds. No insects. No animal has ever been introduced here, or if one was, it did not persist. The pollination, such as it is, is done by small brass fans in the ceilings, spinning at the pace of contemplation, nudging the air along.

The light is ordinary daylight, but filtered through green glass and green leaves and the perpetual mist, so that everything inside has the quality of being underwater without quite being underwater. Shadows are soft and doubled. You can read a book in it. You cannot tell what time it is.

Here are the rules of the place, as best as they can be stated:

Nothing is natural. The granite underfoot was cut and placed. The soil was carried up in sacks, decades ago, and has been turning itself over ever since. Every plant descends from a cutting someone brought. The water is pumped, the air is stirred, the warmth is manufactured. Even the mist is metered — there are humidistats behind panels, their needles trembling in the middle of their dials.

Change is slow. A frond that unfurls today began unfurling a week ago. The engines have been running in their current configuration for longer than any living memory. Rust advances at the rate of rust. The mountain, despite being young, is not going anywhere within a human frame. The place is not static — it is deeply dynamic, but on a clock that makes human time look frantic.

The complex is small enough to walk across in an afternoon if the walkways cooperate, and large enough that no one has ever quite mapped it, because the vegetation keeps redrawing the interior. You can be lost and safe at the same time. This is, perhaps, the point of the place, or one of the points. The boundaries are hard — glass, iron, granite, cloud — and within them the density is absolute, and within that density there is nothing that wants to harm you, only wet leaves and warm air and the slow, slow turning of machinery that someone long ago decided should keep turning.

On the uppermost terrace, where the glass meets the raw rock of the peak, there is a single pane that has been left unplanted. If you stand there and press your forehead to it, you can feel the cold of the outside through the heat of the inside, and you can see, through the running water, the tops of other mountains poking through the cloud, sharp and young and unbuilt, and you can remember that somewhere below the cloud there are places without walls.

Then you turn back into the green, and the mist closes, and a drop falls from somewhere far above into a pool somewhere far below, and the piston moves once more through its long stroke, and nothing else happens, for a while.
