# The Canopy Combine

You come up on it from below, through the long sloped meadows of moss that cover the worn shoulders of the range. The mountains here are old past speaking — they have settled, the way an old animal settles before sleep, into low broad humps that barely remember being peaks. Everything above the moss-line is plant. Not forested; *plant*. There is no soil to speak of. The substrate is the vegetation itself, layer upon compressed layer, a fibrous mat thousands of years deep that the wind has shaped into ridges and shallow basins. Where you press your boot, water beads up around the sole and then sinks back in. The whole upland breathes moisture without ever quite being wet.

The town — if it is a town — rises out of the mat at about four thousand meters, where the air starts to taste mineral and the clouds run through the streets like livestock.

It is called, in the local manner, the Combine. It is built almost entirely of living material that has been trained, woven, fused, milled, and pressed into the shapes of industry. The mill houses are great barrel-vaulted things made from the trunks of a treelike lichen that grows in vertical staves; you can see the staves still breathing slightly when the wind shifts. The smokestacks are hollow stems of something between bamboo and bone, and what comes out of them isn't smoke but a slow pale exhalation of spores that drift down the valley in long visible currents and settle on the rooftops of the next mill over, where the rooftops eat them. The factories feed each other.

The streets are wide and mostly empty. This is a sparse settlement for its scale — the buildings stand far apart, separated by aprons of pale green turf, by long settling-ponds where condensation collects off the night-side of the structures, by yards full of cordwood that is not wood. You can walk for ten minutes between one humming workshop and the next and meet no one. The fauna here is thin: a few long-legged grazers picking at the eaves, a kind of tongueless bird that lives inside the mill-walls and hums in resonance with the looms. Once in a great while something larger moves at the edge of vision and is not there when you look. The town does not need many animals. The town is animal enough.

The sound is enormous. This is the thing that strikes first and never quite stops striking. Every building is at work. The looms in the textile-house clack in 7/8 time; the press-house compresses fiber into board with a deep wet *whump* every nine seconds; somewhere a great vegetative turbine is spinning, driven by sap-pressure or by the slow tectonic creep of the mountain itself, and it produces a bass note you feel in your sternum more than your ears. Layered over all this: the chittering of the spore-flues, the rustle of the staves adjusting to load, the long sigh of the cooling ponds. It is industrial noise produced by living throats. It does not feel hostile. It feels — and this is the difficult part — *occupied*. As if the town is doing the listening, and you are the sound.

This is where the world starts to come apart.

The reality breakdown in the Combine is not dramatic. There are no impossible geometries, no skies that bleed. It is subtler and worse. The breakdown is in continuity. You walk past a mill, turn the corner, walk back, and the mill is the same mill but you have the distinct sense that some intermediate version of it has been edited out — that between *passing* and *returning* there was a third state you weren't shown. The streets sometimes deliver you somewhere you were not walking toward, and you accept this for a few seconds before noticing. A worker leans out of a doorway to wave at you and you wave back, and only later does it occur to you that the worker was a bundle of vines arranged in the shape of a person waving, and that this was true the whole time, and also that they did wave.

The locals — there are locals, sparse and slow-moving, dressed in undyed fiber the same color as the buildings — speak of the Combine as though it is a single process rather than a place. They will say *the Combine is thinking about rain today* or *the Combine ran late last night, you can hear it catching up.* They are not metaphor-prone people. They mean it. The whole mountaintop is one slow industrial organism that has been running, by their accounting, since before the peaks wore down. The mills are its organs. The townspeople are something between technicians and symbionts; they were born into the work and the work into them, and neither party is entirely sure which is the substrate of which.

The light up here is good — clean high-altitude light, filtered through running cloud, the kind that makes every fiber of every wall visible in detail. You can see, if you look closely at the boards of a mill-wall, that the grain of the wood is full of tiny moving figures. They are arranged like the figures in a frieze, going about their work: weaving, pressing, hauling. They are the size of a fingernail-paring. They are the Combine remembering itself, or planning itself, at a smaller scale. If you watch one long enough it will eventually look up at you. Don't let it.

Rain comes through in the late afternoon, brief and thin, more a passing condensation than weather. The mills drink it through their roof-fibers and the bass note from the deep turbine deepens by a quarter-tone for an hour afterward. The cooling ponds rise; the spore-currents thicken; the streets fill briefly with a smell like crushed fern and hot iron, though there is no iron here and nothing is hot.

By evening the light goes the color of old paper and the Combine slows — not stops, never stops, but slows, the looms going to half-time, the press-house to one *whump* per minute. This is when the workers come out into the streets, sparse figures crossing the empty aprons between buildings, going home to whatever a home is in a place where the houses are also coworkers. They nod to you as they pass. Their faces are perfectly ordinary. You have the strong and unshakeable feeling that this has all happened before and will happen again, that the Combine has been running this evening for a very long time, and that you are part of how it remembers how the evening goes.

You sleep, if you sleep, in a guesthouse made of the same living staves as everything else. The walls breathe with you. In the morning the looms start up again at full speed, and you go down the mountain, and the Combine continues without you, and also — somehow, in some smaller frieze somewhere on some wall — with you, still walking its streets, forever, at the size of a fingernail-paring, never looking up.
