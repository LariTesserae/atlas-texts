# The Cupped City

High up, where the air thins to that sweet blue ache just before true mountain cold, there is a hollow in the rock no wider than a threshing floor. You would not find it from below. You would not find it from above either, unless you were looking, because the ridge folds over itself here like a hand half-closed around something warm.

Inside the hollow: a city.

Not a ruin exactly. Ruins imply something ended. This is more like a place that reached its final shape a very long time ago and has been breathing quietly in that shape ever since. The stone of the basin is old beyond old — some smooth grey matrix flecked with garnet, worn concave by water that still, now, slides down every surface in slow beaded sheets. The rock weeps. It has always wept. The weeping is what carved the bowl, and the weeping is what fills the bowl, inch by inch over centuries, with a shallow warm pool that the builders learned to live inside of rather than drain.

The buildings stand in the water. Ankle-deep, most of them. They are made of the same grey rock as the basin, quarried from the basin itself so that the city and its floor are one continuous body. Towers no taller than a child. Arcades you would have to kneel to enter. Staircases that climb four fingers' worth of rise before turning. The whole settlement could fit beneath a spread cloak, and does fit, tucked under the overhang of the ridge so that the sky shows only as a slot of pale blue-grey above.

The density is what strikes you, once your eye adjusts to the scale. Every surface is built on. Every ledge has its little house. Roofs nearly touch roofs; the alleys between them are capillary-thin, and in those alleys the warm water moves in slow currents, carrying pollen and the occasional drowned moth from one quarter of the city to another. There is no open ground. There is no unused stone. The place is saturated with itself.

Vegetation: almost none, and what there is grows deliberate. A cushion of dark moss on the north-facing towers. One fern in a cracked cistern at what might be the city's center, a fern that is almost certainly older than any ruling dynasty the city ever had. A scattering of pale algal threads in the warm pools, swaying.

Light comes down the slot overhead and strikes the water first, so that the undersides of arches and eaves are lit from below in wavering reflections. Everything is dim and shifting. Noon in this place is a kind of underwater dusk. The warmth surprises you — the basin traps the heat that rises off the mineral water, and the air inside is humid and close and smells of wet stone and something faintly sweet, like the inside of a seedpod.

It is nearly silent. The weeping of the walls is continuous but too soft to register as sound after the first few minutes; it becomes the shape of the silence rather than an interruption of it. Occasionally a drop falls from a roof-edge into the pool and the sound travels across the whole city. Occasionally a tile, loosened by a thousand years of damp, slides down and clicks against another tile. That is the music.

Fauna: rare, and strange. A single species of pale shrimp, nearly translucent, lives in the pools and moves through the streets in small grazing herds. A beetle the size of a grain of rice, iridescent, that walks on the surface tension and seems to map the city in patterns no one has deciphered. Once in a season, a bird comes down through the slot — always alone, always briefly — drinks, and leaves. The shrimp scatter and regroup. Nothing else.

Who built it: unclear, and this is where the weirdness settles in. The tools that did this work were simple — chisel marks are visible on every stone, the marks of small hand tools, patient work, nothing that required a forge more sophisticated than a cookfire. But the chisel marks are sized for hands smaller than yours. Considerably smaller. And the doorways, when you kneel and look through them, show interiors that are finished — plastered, tiled, fitted with miniature benches and shelves and, in some of them, cups the size of a thimble, still set on the table, still half-full of something dark that has not evaporated in whatever number of centuries it has been sitting there. The air in the basin does not let water go. Nothing dries here.

The builders are not present. There are no bodies, no bones, no signs of violence or flight. The city seems to have been left the way a person leaves a room for a moment and then, distracted, never comes back. Except that the moment has lasted long enough for the fern to outgrow three cisterns and for the ridge itself to settle another hand's-breadth into the range.

There is a dynamic to it, though, despite the stillness. The water rises and falls on a slow cycle — some days the lowest streets are flooded to the second story, some days they drain enough to show the steps. The shrimp follow these tides. The moss advances and retreats. A crack in one of the towers opens a little further each year and a new kind of mineral efflorescence, pale green, has begun to bloom from it. Something is still happening here, just at a pace that would require you to sit still for a human lifetime to see it properly.

If you lie down at the lip of the basin and put your face close to the water, you can see your breath fog on the tiny rooftops. You can see your own eye reflected in the pool, enormous, looking back at you from between the towers, the way a god's eye might look down into one of our cities if a god ever paused long enough to notice.

And then — this is the part that stays with people, afterward, the part they are never sure they didn't invent — sometimes, if you are very still, you will see one of the shrimp stop. Turn. And look up at you. Not startled. Just looking. Taking you in the way you have been taking the city in. Considering.

And after a while, going back to its grazing, as if whatever it needed to confirm has been confirmed.
