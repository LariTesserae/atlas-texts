# The Roost-Cleft

High up — so high the air thins to a thread you have to lean into — the mountain splits. Not a valley; a wound, narrow enough that a thrown stone would clatter from one wall to the other before reaching the floor. The cleft runs perhaps three hundred paces end to end. You could walk its length in the time it takes a fire to die down, if you could walk it at all.

The walls are a soft pumiceous stone, warm-grey, pocked everywhere with hollows. Some hollows are shallow as a cupped palm; others tunnel back arm-deep, shoulder-deep, deeper. Wind and slow water and slower lives have eaten the rock into a honeycomb. Where you set your hand, the stone is dry and warm — the mountain holds the day's heat in its bones, and even here, near its summit, the air is mild, almost close, like the breath of something asleep. The warmth doesn't come from the sun. The sun barely enters. The cleft is too narrow and too deep; a stripe of pale sky runs overhead like a seam, and most of the light that reaches the floor has bounced down the walls and lost its color on the way.

It is, mostly, dark. Not the dark of a cave — there is always that ribbon of sky — but a perpetual dusk, a permanent late evening that does not turn into night and does not turn into morning. Your eyes adjust and then refuse to adjust further. Forms exist as densities rather than shapes.

And the densities move.

Every hollow in the walls is occupied. Every one. Bodies — feathered, leathered, furred, scaled, things between — packed into the rock like seeds in a pod. The cleft is a roost. What roosts here is harder to name. There are bat-shapes, certainly, hanging in clusters that drip from the overhangs like dark fruit. There are birds with eyes too large and beaks too soft, birds that have lived so long in the half-light that they have begun to forget what birds are for. There are small warm things with hands. There are larger things further up, hunched in the deeper hollows, that you sense rather than see — a slow breathing, a turn of weight against stone.

The sound is constant and enormous. Not loud in the way of thunder; loud in the way of a thousand small voices all at once, layered and overlapping and never pausing. Clicks. Trills. A wet chittering. The dry rustle of wings folding and unfolding. The scrape of claws on pumice. A low repetitive hooting from somewhere high up that you stop noticing and then notice again and then stop noticing. When one creature shifts, a chain of small disturbances ripples through the wall — a flutter answers a flutter answers a flutter — and then settles. The cleft is never silent. It has not been silent in a very long time.

Water reaches this place by accident. The mountain's flanks shed clouds; sometimes a cloud catches in the cleft and condenses against the warm stone, and for an hour the walls weep. The droplets gather in the pocks and run down the rough faces and pool in certain low hollows that the creatures know. They drink in shifts. Mostly the air is only damp, the kind of damp that lays a film on the back of your hand and never quite becomes wet. After a rain — which happens, perhaps, every dozen days — the cleft smells different for an afternoon: mineral, alive, the smell of a thing that has been licked.

Plants are few and stubborn. A grey-green lichen in patches. A kind of fleshy, near-leafless stalk that grows from the droppings on certain ledges and bears small, pale, sour berries that the smaller fliers swallow whole. A moss in the wettest pockets. Nothing tall. Nothing that competes for the strip of sky. The vegetation is barely a presence — it is more like a slow chemistry happening on the rock's surface, a film of life on a substrate of life on a substrate of stone.

Nothing here is built. No nest is woven; nests are simply the hollows the rock provides, and the hollows are sufficient. There are no paths because there is no walking — the floor of the cleft is treacherous with guano and bone-mash and the trickling damp, and most of the inhabitants live their lives without ever touching it. The vertical is the real geography. Up and down matter; along matters less. A creature is born in a hollow, grows in a hollow, mates in an adjacent hollow, dies and falls. The floor is where the dead go, and the floor processes them slowly, and the floor in turn feeds the stalks that feed the small fliers that, in time, fall.

If there is anything made here, it is the smallest possible making. Certain of the hand-handed creatures — narrow-faced, patient, with fingers like dark twigs — have been seen to work small flakes of harder stone against the pumice, scraping out a hollow deeper, smoothing a lip. They do this for reasons that may be their own and may be older than reasons. A scraped hollow, polished by generations of small palms, is the closest thing to a tool, to architecture, to a held idea, that this place contains. It is barely there. You could miss it. Many do.

The cleft changes, but slowly. The pumice gives way grain by grain. A hollow deepens over a lifetime, collapses over ten. Populations rise and fall on cycles longer than memory. The shape of the place now is essentially the shape it has had for as long as anything currently alive in it has been alive — and longer. It is established. It has found its form. The wind that carved it carves it still, but gently now, almost as an afterthought, as if the wind itself were satisfied with the work.

There is a strangeness to it that takes a while to name. It is this: the cleft is so dense with life, so saturated, so layered with bodies and sound and warm slow breath, that it begins to feel like a single organism rather than a place. The walls pulse faintly. The chorus has rhythms you start to anticipate. When a section of the wall goes briefly quiet — and it does, in slow waves, sections hushing and resuming — it feels less like coincidence and more like a held breath, a passing thought.

You are not welcome here, exactly, but you are not refused. You are simply included, the way a stone tossed into a pond is included. The cleft accommodates you. The chorus adjusts by a fraction and continues. The warm dim damp closes around you, and after a while you find that you, too, have stopped making sound — not from fear, but because there is nothing to add. The place is already saying everything.
