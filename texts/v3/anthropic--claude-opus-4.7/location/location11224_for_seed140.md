# The Sounding Hollow

There is a basin near the crown of a mountain that has forgotten it was ever sharp. The peaks around it have melted, in the way only deep time can melt stone, into long humped shoulders covered in moss so thick it has its own weather — small fogs that exhale from the moss-skin at dusk and drift across the basin like slow breath. Nothing here points upward anymore. Everything leans, slumps, rests. The mountains have settled into themselves the way an old animal settles into sleep.

The basin itself is perhaps a kilometer across — a single feature, an enclosed room of the world. You could walk its perimeter in a morning, if you could see. But there is no seeing here. The light is gone, not absent in the way of night (which implies a sky to be dark) but absent in the way of a sealed organ. Whatever once lit this place has been done with for a long time. The darkness has a quality, almost a texture; it feels older than the rocks.

The ground is sodden. Not flooded, not marsh exactly — the water is just *there*, a few centimeters below the moss, seeping at the slightest pressure, beading on every leaf, condensing on every surface that is even fractionally cooler than the warm wet air. Walk and your foot prints fill behind you with a soft swallowing sound. The whole hollow drips. From unseen overhangs water falls in thin patient threads; from the ferns it rolls; from the cupped leaves of plants you cannot name it tips and tips and tips. The constant trickle is the underlayer of all sound here.

The vegetation is extravagant and crowds itself without crowding the air. Tree-tall fronds, soft-trunked things that bend under their own weight, vines as thick as a wrist that have grown, looped, rotted, and grown again so many times that their architecture is more sediment than plant. The leaves are enormous and feel, in the dark, like wet flannel. Between the great plants there is *space* — this is what the hollow holds onto, even in its lushness: emptiness. The plants are large and few. You might walk twenty paces between one mossy bole and the next, through warm air thick enough to drink, hearing nothing but the drip and your own breath and —

The sound. The hollow is loud. Not with any single voice but with an unceasing congregation of small voices: the chirring of something insect-like that lives in the leaf-cisterns, the deep periodic *whump* of something larger breathing in the moss, the bell-tones — actual bell-tones, pure and surprising — that come from a kind of frog whose throat acts as a resonance chamber tuned by the water in its body. And above all of these, threading through them, a rhythmic *clik...clik...clik* that comes from no living thing.

That clicking is the first sign of the other presence here.

Scattered through the hollow, half-sunken in the moss, embraced by vines, partly digested by lichen, are the structures. A dozen, perhaps. No more. They are not ruins, exactly — ruin implies a fall, and these things have not fallen. They have *aged in place* alongside the mountains, the way the mountains have aged, slumping into harmony with the landscape until you cannot tell where stone ends and made-thing begins. They are smooth, dark, warm to the touch. They hum at frequencies just below the threshold of hearing, and when you place your palm against one your bones know it before your ears do.

Whatever made them was working with principles that are not — quite — physics. One of the structures, when approached, very slightly bends the path of falling water around itself, as though the rain is reluctant to land on it. Another, sunk to its shoulders in moss, holds a small bubble of cooler air around it; you feel the temperature drop a degree as you pass, then rise again. A third hums in counterpoint to the bell-frogs, and over the millennia the frogs have tuned themselves to it; their chorus is its chorus; they have become its voice or it has become theirs, no one could now tell.

These structures are not abandoned. They are simply *finished*. Whatever they were doing, they have done, and now they only continue — patient machines in a patient world, running their last slow processes across centuries, the way a great clock runs down across a long afternoon. The clicking is one of them, somewhere on the basin's far side, marking out intervals that are not seconds and not minutes but something else, something that matches some larger rhythm of the place. The frogs know what it is. The thing breathing in the moss knows. You will not learn.

The fauna is everywhere and unseen. In darkness this complete, animals do not need to hide; the hollow is full of them, moving easily, brushing past, indifferent. Something soft-footed and warm walks alongside you for ten paces and then is gone. Something the size of a cat climbs the trunk beside you, and you hear the wet grip of its feet. The bell-frogs sing from every standing puddle. High in the canopy — there is a canopy, the air changes overhead, you can hear it — things glide between the great fronds with leathery exhalations. None of them seem to mind you. None of them seem to notice you, particularly. You are too new to matter.

The hollow has its rules, and they are few:

Water moves downward and inward, always, toward a center you cannot find.

The machines continue. They have been continuing. They will continue.

The dark is not a condition but a substance; it has settled here the way silt settles at the bottom of a still pond, and like silt it has its own slow currents.

Nothing here is in a hurry. The vines grow a finger's width in a human lifetime. The moss-fogs cycle once a day; the bell-chorus shifts key once a season; the clicking machine has been marking its intervals since before the mountains rounded.

And one more thing, which you notice last, after you have stood for a while and let your skin learn the air: the hollow is *warm in a particular pattern*. Not uniformly. There are warmer pockets and cooler pockets, and as you turn slowly in place you realize the pattern is not random — there is a faint, body-temperature gradient, slightly warmer toward the basin's heart, as though something enormous and at rest is breathing very slowly somewhere below the moss, and the whole hollow is the curve of its sleeping side.

You could sit down. The moss would take you. The frogs would not stop singing. The clicking would continue. In a hundred years the vines would have grown a finger's width around your ankles, and the hollow would be exactly as it is now, only a little more itself.
