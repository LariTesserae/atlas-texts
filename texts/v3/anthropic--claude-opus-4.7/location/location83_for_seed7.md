Below the surface, though not deeply — perhaps the length of a freight car beneath whatever lies above — a chamber has opened. It is new. The word *cavern* feels wrong; caverns are slow-carved, smoothed, domed by millennia. This is something that happened recently, geologically speaking. The walls still carry the sharp edges of their breaking. Basalt cleaves at fresh angles, and along some of the fracture planes the rock is still glassy, still holding the heat of whatever event made this space.

The heat is the first fact of the place. It sits at the edge of the bearable, a dry weight pressing against the skin, broken only in certain zones where moisture finds a way in. Water seeps along a single seam in the northeast wall — if compass directions mean anything down here — and travels the slope in a thin, lazy sheet, evaporating before it crosses the floor. In the middle distance it becomes a faint mist, a breath-thickness of humidity hanging between the drier air and the hotter air. Condensation beads on cooler surfaces: the underside of a metal flange, the back of a leaf, a length of iron pipe that disappears into rock.

And there are leaves. This is the strange part.

Vegetation has found the chamber and claimed most of it — not a jungle, not a carpet, but a scattered, confident growth that seems to know exactly where it can live. Broad waxy fronds splay from fissures where the water runs. Pale vines, almost colorless in the dark, climb the sharp rock faces and hang from unseen anchors above. Something like a fern but fleshier, more muscular, grows in clusters around the warmest stones. The plants are lush where they grow, but they do not crowd. Between one stand and the next there are meters of bare, glassy rock, and the emptiness is part of the composition — a sparse ecology, deliberate-seeming, as if someone had planted a garden and then gone away.

No animals. No insects ticking in the leaf litter, no bats, no pale spiders. The plants have the place to themselves. Whatever pollinates them, whatever moves their genes around, is not visible and may not be alive in the ordinary sense.

The light is almost nothing. What there is comes from two sources. First, a faint luminescence in some of the plants themselves — not a glow exactly, more a reluctance to be fully dark, a green-gray suggestion along the midribs of the larger leaves. Second, and stranger: machinery.

Scattered through the chamber are industrial artifacts. A squat iron housing the size of a small car, riveted, with ports and valves, sits half-buried in one wall as though the rock had grown around it or broken open to reveal it. A cluster of pipes runs along one fracture line, joining a manifold that hums — the only sound in the place, a low continuous hum, below speech, almost below hearing. A catwalk of grated steel crosses a narrow part of the floor at ankle height, bolted to nothing obvious, leading from nowhere to nowhere. A single filament bulb in a wire cage burns dimly above the manifold, casting an orange that doesn't reach far. Further off, the silhouette of what might be a turbine, or a tank, or a kiln.

None of this is new. The machines have the patina of long use — scale on the pipes, rust blooms along the rivets, a film of mineral deposit where the seep-water has found them. But they are also not abandoned. Something still runs. The hum is evidence. The warmth in certain pipes is evidence. Whatever industrial process this chamber is part of, or was part of, has not entirely stopped. It has slowed. It continues at the pace the chamber itself continues: gradual, patient, a long exhalation.

The plants seem to know about the machines. The vines favor the warm pipes; the ferns cluster near the manifold's waste heat; one of the broad-leafed things has wrapped itself almost tenderly around a valve wheel, and the valve wheel shows no sign of having been turned in a long time. There is a negotiation happening here that predates any witness. The chamber is small enough — grove-sized, room-sized — that one could walk its length in a few minutes, and yet it feels sufficient unto itself, bounded, a complete small world with its own logic.

The sounds, beyond the hum: the faint hiss of the seep where it meets hotter stone and flashes briefly to steam. The soft dry click of a leaf shifting against rock. Nothing else. No wind, because there is no weather here, only the slow convection of heat rising toward a ceiling one cannot quite see. The silence is not empty — it is attentive, the silence of a room where something is being done slowly and carefully, out of sight.

The weirdness sits in the relationships. A fresh-broken chamber should not already contain industry; industry should not already contain a garden; a garden without animals should not thrive; and yet. One possible story: the machines came first, buried, forgotten, and the rock broke open around them recently and let in water and seed. Another: the plants are the machines' doing, cultivated by whatever the manifold is processing, a deliberate small biosphere kept warm and damp as a side-effect or a purpose. Another: the chamber is younger than any of its contents, and emerged already furnished, the way a dream room emerges already lived-in.

There is no obvious way in and no obvious way out. A visitor, if one came, would have to have arrived by the same means the machinery did — pressed into the rock by forces that do not explain themselves. They would stand on the glassy floor, feel the heat through their soles, watch the luminous midribs of the leaves describe a soft topography in the dark, listen to the hum, and understand that the place was doing something. Slowly. Had been doing it. Would continue.

And then — because nothing here is in a hurry — they could sit down on the catwalk, and wait, and after a long time notice that the mist had shifted slightly, that a droplet had fallen from a leaf to a pipe and hissed once, that the light in the filament bulb had dimmed by a degree and brightened again, that the chamber was breathing, just barely, at the rate of something very large and very patient, and that they had been included in it without being asked.
