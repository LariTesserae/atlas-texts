# The Cairn-Makers of Lower Anneth

They were small. This is the first thing to understand, and almost everything else follows from it.

Not small as a child is small — small as a thing that has decided, over many generations, that smallness is the correct answer to a particular question. An adult stood perhaps to the waist of the surface-dweller who wrote the account above. Wiry, long-armed, with the deep ribcage of creatures that breathe warm thick air, and skin the matte color of the lume-light itself — not pale, exactly, but *unshadowed*, the color of something that has lived its whole life inside an even glow. They would have been almost invisible against the walls if they held still. They rarely held still.

Their eyes were small and dark and set deep, and the pupils did not contract or dilate; there was no need. The light of the caverns never changed. What their eyes had learned instead was *texture* — to read the grain of new rock, the chevroned grazing-tracks of the wall-sheets, the subtle banding that meant a pillar was four days old rather than four hours, the faint discoloration above a pool that meant the drip was about to shift. They could glance at a gallery and know its age the way a surface-dweller knows the season.

Their hands were the most developed part of them. Six fingers, the outer two opposed, the inner four long and very strong. The pads were ridged and tough — they spent their lives touching the new rock, and the new rock, soft as it was, would have worn smooth hands to nothing. With those hands they could climb the walls through the layered animals without disturbing them; could feel which seam of fresh mineral was edible (yes, edible — they ate the rock too, in measured amounts, the way the wall-sheets did) and which was not yet ready; could peck a spiral into a cairn-stone with another stone and feel through the haft when the peck had taken.

---

They lived in groups of perhaps a dozen. No more. The cavern would not support more in one place — not because food was scarce (food was *everywhere*) but because the cavern itself would not hold still long enough for a larger group to coordinate. A dozen could move together. A dozen could read the same set of unfolding conditions and agree, mostly without speaking, on where to be next.

They moved constantly. This was their fundamental fact. A shelter was not a home; a shelter was a *pause* — four slabs leaned together for a few days, a week, until the rock around it began to close or the animals shifted or the drip moved or the belling from the Pan came too near. Then they took what little they carried (the bone scrapers, a few hide bundles, fire-makings that they used sparingly because the air down here was precious and they knew it) and went somewhere else. The cleaned floor inside the shelter was cleaned by patience and by a particular smell they could produce — a secretion from glands behind the jaw, rubbed onto the stone, that the wall-sheets and scuttlers found intolerable. It lasted a few days. Long enough.

The tally marks were not days. They were *shifts* — the count of times the cavern had reconfigured around the shelter since they made it. Forty-seven shifts was a long stay. Forty-seven shifts meant something unusual: a stable pocket, a confluence of conditions, perhaps a birth. After forty-seven they would have left because nothing here stayed stable for fifty.

---

They spoke, but lightly. Their language was mostly gesture and touch and a small repertoire of clicks made with the tongue against the hard palate — sounds that carried well through the wash of small clickings already filling the air, that were *of* that sound rather than against it. To a listener on the surface their conversation would have been inaudible inside three meters; it rode on the cavern's own noise like a swimmer on a current. They had names for each other but the names were touches, not sounds — a particular pattern pressed into the shoulder or the back of the hand. To name someone you had to be near them. There was no calling across distance. If you were far enough away to need to be called, you were already gone, and they had a different set of touches for that.

They sang only once in their lives, and only to the Pan. More on this in a moment.

---

What did they make? Very little. The bone scrapers, the hide wraps, the cairns, the ochre (from a particular vein in the deepest gallery they would travel to, a half-day's careful movement away, where the rock turned red and the wall-sheets would not graze). They did not weave. They did not pot. They did not build beyond the leaning of slabs. The cavern made everything else for them — light, warmth, water, food, even, in a sense, walls. To build was to compete with the rock, and the rock would always win. They had learned, over however many generations, to let the cavern do the work and to move with it.

The spiral on the cairn was their only persistent symbol. It meant, near as can be reconstructed, *here passed someone*. Not a marker of territory — they had no territory — but a marker of *witness*. To peck the spiral was to say: a person was in this place when this rock was new. The weathered spiral on the old cairn means that someone, long ago, stood on a ledge that no longer exists in the shape it had then, and saw what they saw, and pressed the fact into the stone before moving on. The cairn-stones themselves were chosen for being already-old — pieces of the black glassy rock that did not grow, that the cavern had finished with. A spiral on growing rock would be erased within a season. A spiral on the black rock might last a lifetime, or three, or ten.

---

They knew about the surface. This is important. The fissure in the dry upland was not unknown to them; some of them, the bravest or the most curious or the most foolish, climbed up to it and looked out. What they saw — the unfiltered sun, the moving air, the horizon — was uninhabitable to them, literally; their eyes could not narrow against that light, their skin could not hold its moisture in that dryness, their lungs could not work that thin cold air. But they knew it was there. They knew the surface-dwellers existed. There are, in one of the deeper galleries that the account-writer did not reach, a few ochre marks that depict — crudely, but unmistakably — a tall figure with a wide head, standing under a circle. A surface-dweller under the sun. The mark is old. They had seen, somehow, and remembered, and passed the memory down.

Whether any surface-dweller ever came down and stayed, in their time, is not clear. There are no mixed burials because there are no burials at all. Which brings us to the last thing.

---

The cairn-makers did not bury their dead. They could not. The rock would have entombed any grave within weeks, and besides, the cavern was already a kind of slow flow, and to add a body to it was only to return what had been borrowed.

When one of them was dying — and they knew, as such people know — they walked, while they still could, down toward the Pan. The others went with them as far as they were willing to go, which was further each generation, but never all the way. At a certain point the dying one continued alone. Down into the lower galleries where the lume-nodes thinned and the rock turned black and glassy and the belling cries came from very close. And there, alone, they sang — the one song of their lives, a long sustained note held as long as the breath would hold it, pitched to match the bell.

Whatever lived in the Pan answered. Whatever lived in the Pan came up. And the dying one was — taken, eaten, dissolved, absorbed; the word does not matter; *returned* — into the slow flow that fed the whole cavern, the mineral cycle that the wall-sheets grazed at the top and that something very large closed at the bottom.

This was not tragedy to them. This was correctness. They were of the cavern; the cavern was of the flow; the flow had a mouth at the bottom; the mouth was fed. They had names for the thing in the Pan but the names were touches, and you cannot touch the thing in the Pan, and so the names were used only among themselves, to remember it by.

---

Why did they leave? They did not leave. They ended.

The cavern shifted, as the cavern always shifted, but at some point the shifts moved against them faster than they could read. Perhaps a generation grew up in a stretch of unusual stability and lost the deep skill of constant motion. Perhaps a pocket of bad air opened from below and took a whole group at once. Perhaps the thing in the Pan grew, or moved, or hungered differently, and called too many of them down too soon. Perhaps it was simply that they were always small in number, and smallness has a floor, and one season they fell through it.

The bone scraper leaning against the wall belonged to the last of them, or near enough. It was set down to fetch water. The one who set it down did not come back to it. The cavern continued without them, as it had continued before them, and the wall-sheets grazed past the scraper and around it, and the rock began, very slowly, to grow over its base.

In another century the scraper will be inside a pillar, and the spiral on the old cairn will be the last legible thing they made, and after that the black rock too will be buried, and the cavern will not remember them at all.

But the flow will continue. The light will be constant. The bell will ring from below. And somewhere, deep in the genome of the wall-sheets or the long pale eels or the great unseen thing in the Pan, there may be a trace of the minerals that were once a cairn-maker, moving through the slow circuit, doing the work that they did in life — being of the cavern, being of the flow, being fed into and feeding in turn.

They would, I think, have considered this sufficient.
