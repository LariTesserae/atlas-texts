They are called, by no one, the *lumen-clingers*, and there is no one to call them anything, so the name does not matter. What matters is what they are.

Begin with the body. A finger's length, yes, but the proportions are wrong for a salamander when you look closely — the head is not really a head, only a slight thickening at one end where two dark spots sit beneath the skin. Not eyes. Light-wells. They register direction and intensity but do not resolve images, and they do not need to: the creatures have lived for so long in a place whose geometry never changes that vision in the ordinary sense would be a waste of tissue. The dark spots are calibration. They tell the animal where on its body the light is falling and how to turn so that the falling is correct.

The skin is the interesting part. Four layers, if you could section one — though no one will. The outermost is almost glass, a clear cuticle kept moist by the wall's seep, refractive enough that a beam passing through the animal is bent very slightly, the way light bends entering water. Beneath this lies the green layer, the working tissue, threaded with what are not quite chloroplasts and not quite anything else: small captive partners that the lineage took in so long ago that the question of separateness has stopped applying. These are the pulse you can see. They contract and expand in a rhythm coupled to the slow chemistry of the cell, and the rhythm is the animal's true metabolism. Below the green is a layer of fine mineral platelets, secreted from within, which act as mirrors — what light is not absorbed on the way down is reflected back up through the green for a second pass. Almost nothing escapes. The faint tinting you see in the emerging beam is only the residue, the wavelengths the green did not want.

The fourth and deepest layer is the wall-foot. It is not a foot. It is a fringe of microscopic processes that interpenetrate the limestone, drinking the film of water on the stone and, with it, the dissolved minerals the water carries — calcium, a little iron, traces of the salts that the deep passages exhale. This is what they mean by *drink the wall*. The animal does not have a mouth. It has not had a mouth for an interval of evolutionary time that cannot be casually stated. The mouth atrophied when the light became sufficient and the wall became enough.

They are hermaphroditic and they reproduce rarely. Rarely in their terms — perhaps once in a clinger's life, which is itself difficult to bound, because senescence in them is slow to the point of being ambiguous. When two of them have drifted close enough on the wall over a sufficient span of months, and when the light has been good, they exchange a small packet of cells through a pore that opens only for this purpose and closes again as if it had never been. The young emerge from the parent as motile larvae, the only motile stage — pale almost-tadpoles that swim briefly in the basins, in the only standing water they will ever know, and then climb out and find a patch of wall and settle and never truly move again. The basins are nurseries. This is partly why the moss-ring marks the old waterline: the moss tolerates what the larvae excrete during their swimming weeks, and grows where they have been.

There are perhaps eight hundred of them. The number is very stable. When one dies — and they do die, occasionally, dropping from the wall as a small soft husk that the basin-water dissolves within a season — another has, somewhere on the wall, recently settled. The chamber's carrying capacity is fixed by the light, and the light is fixed by the flues, and the flues were drilled to a count and an angle that someone, once, chose.

Now the clicking.

The clicking is not communication in the sense that a bird's call is communication. There is no message in any individual click. But the chamber as a whole maintains, through the clicks, a kind of distributed sense of itself. When a clinger shifts position to catch a beam at a new angle, it sometimes clicks; another, far across the chamber, registers the click and — not in response, but in something like sympathy — adjusts its own posture by a fraction. Over hours, over days, the whole population orients and reorients in a slow collective drift that tracks the sun's seasonal path through the flues. No single animal knows the pattern. The pattern emerges. If you could speed time enough to watch a year pass in an afternoon, you would see the wall ripple — a soft, coordinated turning, the entire furred surface breathing with the year.

And this is where the question of what they are becomes uncertain.

Because the chamber is also doing something. The air rises, the light tracks, the basins fill at their hairsbreadth pace, the clingers turn, the clicks pass and answer. These processes are coupled. The warmth of the animals contributes a fraction to the rising air. The rising air affects the condensation pattern, which affects which seams stay wettest, which affects which clingers thrive. The clinging pattern affects how the light scatters, which affects the general radiance, which affects the ferns at the flue-mouths, which affects what falls through and what does not. The chamber is not a container with creatures in it. The chamber is a slow body, and the clingers are one of its organs, and the dripping is another, and the breath up the flues is its respiration, and the light is what it eats.

Whether the drillers understood this when they came with their copper picks, or whether they only suspected it and built the flues to test the suspicion — that cannot be recovered. What can be said is that the chamber as it now exists could not exist without the flues, and the flues required hands, and the hands belonged to people who chose this place and this work over whatever else they might have done with their lives. They drilled for a long time. Long enough that the earliest flues show more weathering than the latest. Long enough that the project must have been inherited.

The clingers do not remember them. The clingers do not remember anything; memory in the ordinary sense is not what their tissue does. But the population as a whole has a shape that was set by the drillers' choices, and the shape persists, and in this attenuated sense the drillers are still present — not as ghosts, not as anything you could speak to, but as the geometry of an arrangement that continues to hold.

If the drillers became something, if the long sitting at the bowl's center did to them what the description hints it might do, then perhaps what they became is not separate from the clingers and not separate from the chamber. Perhaps the drillers learned, slowly, to keep the chamber's time so completely that the distinction between keeping its time and being part of its time stopped being meaningful, and they let go of the parts of themselves that could not be carried across that threshold, and what remained was small enough and quiet enough and patient enough to settle on a wall and turn very slowly toward the light.

This is speculation. The clingers will not confirm it. They are busy, in their fashion, which looks like stillness. They are eating the morning. A drop falls into a basin. One of them clicks. Another, three paces away on the curving wall, answers, and the answer is also a small adjustment of the body, and a thin rod of light that had been passing through empty air now passes through a finger-length of translucent flesh and emerges, on the far wall, very faintly green.
