The salamander knows.

Not knows the way a person knows — there is no narrative in her, no sense of having lived through anything. But she has been in the drip-line longer than the oldest beech outside the bowl has been alive, and the engine's four-beat is the only rhythm her body has ever organized itself around. Her heart, which is small and cold and the color of the rest of her, beats once for every two strokes of the piston. It has done this since she was the length of a fingernail. She is now the length of a forearm. She will get no longer. She reached her size centuries ago and has been holding it, the way the tree holds its shape, the way the engine holds its tempo. Whatever growth would have happened to her happens instead to the lichen on her back, which is a small forest with its own weather.

She is not the oldest thing in the bowl. The tree is older. The engine is older than the tree, or the same age, depending on how you measure. But she is the oldest thing that *moves* of its own accord, and she moves very little. She shifts perhaps three body-lengths in a year, following the slow migration of the wettest stone. In winter — the bowl's almost-winter — she does not move at all, and a fine green skin of algae grows over her eyes, and in spring she blinks it away and the world is still there, still beating.

The martens have names for each other. Not words; martens do not have words. But each marten in the lineage has a particular smell-signature that the others recognize, and the signatures have, across the long generations of martens born and dying in the hollow above the flywheel, become *traditional*. There is always a marten who smells like the one who sleeps closest to the warm housing. There is always a marten who smells like the one who hunts beetles on the north buttress. The roles have outlived any individual marten by orders of magnitude. A marten born this spring will, without being taught, take up the scent-shape of whichever role is vacant, and live it out, and pass it on. They are not quite martens anymore, in the way that the tree is not quite a tree. They are the bowl's marten-shaped pieces. A biologist would find their DNA strange — too uniform, too stable, the mutations that should have accumulated across a thousand generations somehow combed back out, as if something were *editing*.

Something is editing. Not deliberately. The engine's slow exhalation seems to settle into everything in the bowl and hold it there. Drift is not permitted. A wren that hatched with a slightly off-color breast will, by the time it fledges, have come into the standard pattern, and no one — there is no one — would be able to say at what hour the correction happened. The bowl does not tolerate novelty in itself the way it would not tolerate it in a visitor. Everything that lives here lives here as a held note, the same note, struck once long ago and sustained.

And the engine.

The engine is the only thing in the bowl that could be said to have an interior life, and even this is generous. It is not thinking. But it is *attending*. Each stroke is a kind of accounting: the flywheel turns and somewhere in its turning the bowl is tallied — so many leaves, so many beetles, the salamander still in her place, the martens correct in their roles, the moss on the moss still nested properly — and the next stroke proceeds on the basis that everything is as it should be. If something were wrong, the engine would know. What it would do about it, no one can say, because nothing has ever been wrong. The accounting is a formality at this point. It has been a formality for a geological while. But the engine performs it anyway, because performing it is what the engine is, and to stop performing it would be to stop, and the engine does not stop.

There is one more inhabitant, and this is the difficult one.

In the deepest of the hollows, below the chamber where the engine sits, there is a smaller chamber that no animal enters. Not because it is forbidden — there are no forbiddings here — but because nothing that lives in the bowl has a reason to go there, and nothing in the bowl does anything without reason. The chamber is the size of a person curled up. It is dry. The wood inside it is pale and smooth, polished by no one, polished by the long slow press of the tree's own growth against itself. The air in it is still. The engine's sound reaches it only as a pressure, a thudding in the wood, no longer recognizable as sound.

If you had to say what lives there, you would have to say: the listening. The bowl's attention to itself. Whatever in the long equilibrium does the holding — not the engine, which only beats; not the tree, which only grows in place; not the animals, which only enact their inherited shapes — but the thing that decided, once, that this would be enough, and has been deciding it again every moment since.

It has no body. It has no name. It has the chamber, which it does not occupy so much as constitute. And it is the reason the bowl knows you should not enter. Not the engine — the engine does not care. Not the tree — the tree is only a tree, however vast. The listening cares. It has been alone for so long with its one decision that any new thing arriving in the bowl would have to be incorporated into the decision, and the decision is full. There is no room in it. There is room for the salamander, who is part of it. There is room for the standing pattern of martens. There is no room for a stranger.

This is what you feel at the lip of the bowl. Not the engine's rhythm in your sternum, though that is real. Not the cool damp, though that is real too. You feel the listening notice you. You feel it weigh, very briefly, the question of whether you might be added. And you feel it decide, with the same finality it has brought to every such decision across the unimaginable span of its tenure, that you might not.

And then you go home, and you tell no one, because there is nothing to tell. Only that there is a place where everything has already happened, and is still happening, and will go on happening, and the rain that falls from its canopy is not weather, and the salamander in the drip-line has been waiting for nothing for a very long time, and is good at it.
