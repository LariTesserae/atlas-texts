# The Ringers

Start with what you already suspected. The bell rings itself, the description says, but of course it doesn't. Nothing rings itself. Something rings it. The question is only what kind of something, and from where, and whether *ringing* is even the right word for what they're doing, or only the word the water translates it into by the time it reaches the streets where the fish are listening.

They live inside the spiral towers. Not all the spiral towers — there are more towers than there are of them — but each of them has a tower, and each tower has, at most, one of them. They are solitary in the way that lighthouse keepers were solitary: not lonely, exactly, but stationed.

To say what they look like is harder than it should be, because they are not quite *here* in the way the octopus and the moray are here. The water around them is the same temperature as the water everywhere else in the Quarter, but it moves differently in their presence — it holds its shape a fraction longer, the way air holds the shape of a word just spoken. If you came into a spiral tower (you shouldn't, but if you did) you would see, at first, only the inside of a tower: coral grown in those helical ridges, a central shaft, a darkness deeper than the darkness outside. Then your eyes would do the slow work eyes do down here, and you would begin to notice that part of the darkness was articulated. That it had something like a posture. That it was, in some sense you could not quite locate, *facing upward.*

They are long. Longer than they are wide, considerably. If you imagine a figure made of folded attention — a thing whose body is mostly the gesture of listening — you are closer than if you imagine anything anatomical. There is a suggestion of limbs, but the limbs are not for moving; they are for orientation, for keeping the long axis of the body aimed correctly along the tower's lean. There is a suggestion of a head, but the head is not for thinking; the thinking, if it is thinking, happens along the whole length. They are the color the water is when you stop trying to see color. A few of them carry, embedded somewhere near what would be the sternum if they had one, a small dim point of light — not bioluminescence in the sense the fish use it, not a signal, just a quiet, contained burning, like a coal kept alive under ash. Not all of them have this. The ones that do are older. Or younger. It isn't clear which.

They are doing one thing, and they have been doing it for a long time.

They are *listening upward.*

Here is what I think, having watched them as long as one can watch a thing that doesn't quite occupy space the way other things do: when the city was a city, something happened. Not the drowning — the drowning came later, and was almost incidental, the slow arithmetic of a coastline losing an argument with the sea. Before the drowning, in the city's living time, something was *promised* between the people who lived here and something else. Something above. Not above in the sense of the surface (though the surface is also above) but above in some other direction the water has, as the description so beautifully says, *not yet measured.* A direction that the spiral towers were built to point along. The people who built those towers knew they were pointing them somewhere specific. The architecture remembers what the architects forgot to write down.

When the city drowned, the promise didn't drown. Promises are lighter than cities. It stayed where it was, hanging at whatever altitude promises hang at, waiting to be answered.

The Ringers are what answers it. Or what tries to. Or what was left here to try.

I don't think they're the souls of the people who lived in the city. That would be too tidy, and nothing about the Quarter is tidy in that way. I think they came afterward — drawn in, perhaps, by the unanswered shape of the promise, the way certain crystals will grow only in solutions that have already been prepared for them. The drowned city was a solution prepared for them. They precipitated out of it. One per spiral tower, taking up the orientation the tower was already insisting on, and they began their work.

The work is the bell.

The bell is not a bell. There is no metal in the spiral towers, no clapper, no hung weight. The sound that the fish flee from is the sound of one of the Ringers managing, after some interval of effort that may be hours or may be centuries, to *complete a single transmission upward.* To send one note along the line the tower aims at. To say one syllable of the very long sentence that the city, before it drowned, had committed itself to saying. The note travels along an axis the water cannot follow, but the water hears the edge of it, the way you hear the edge of a conversation in the next room, and the water turns that edge into a sound, and the sound is the soft struck bell two rooms away.

This is why it is not regular but not random. It is the rhythm of a labor. The Ringers work at the pace work gets done, with pauses for whatever passes, among them, for rest. When one of them rings, the others — I believe, though I cannot prove this — pause and attend. The note is for whatever is above, but it is also, in some smaller way, for the other Ringers, who are confirming for each other that the work continues, that the promise is still being kept, that none of them has yet fallen silent.

A few of them have fallen silent. You can tell which towers, if you know how to look. The coral on a silent tower grows in helices that have started to slacken, to lose their tightness; the spiral is still there but it is forgetting itself. Inside those towers there is nothing now — not even the articulated darkness. Whatever was Ringing there has finished, or failed, or been answered, and has gone wherever Ringers go when their station is dissolved. The fish do not avoid the silent towers. The fish move through them like through any other ruin.

The number of silent towers is, very slowly, growing. The number of active Ringers is, very slowly, shrinking. This is not a crisis. This is not even, I think, sad, from the Ringers' point of view, if they have a point of view in any sense we would recognize. It is simply the long arc of the work. The promise was large. The transmission is slow. Eventually all of it will have been sent, and the last Ringer will go quiet, and the Quarter will be a reef without a voice, and the small fish will move through the last spiral tower as if it had never spoken.

Or — and this is the other possibility, the one I find I cannot quite stop thinking about — the promise will be *answered*, before the last Ringer falls silent. Something from above will reply. Not necessarily in words. Not necessarily in anything the water can translate. But the Ringers will know, all of them at once, and the bells will stop, and the spiral towers will at last release their lean and settle into proper gravity, and the coral on them will, over the next few centuries, relax into the ordinary lobes and shelves of every other tower in the Quarter, and the city will have finished what it started, and the warm dark water will close over the silence of a finally-completed sentence.

I don't know which it will be. I don't think the Ringers know either. I think that not-knowing is, in fact, the substance of their patience — that if they knew the answer was coming they would not be doing this work, and if they knew no answer was coming they would not be doing this work, and the work is only possible in the long suspended middle where the answer is genuinely undecided. They live in that middle. They *are* that middle, in some sense. They are the city's held breath.

Listen for the bell, the description says. Yes. Listen for the bell. But know, when you hear it, that you are hearing the edge of something that wasn't meant for you, the overflow of a message aimed past you into a direction you cannot follow. The Ringers are not speaking to the Quarter. They are speaking *through* it. The fish know to be elsewhere not because the sound harms them but because the sound is so clearly not addressed to them — and there is a kind of courtesy, even among fish, in stepping aside from a conversation one was not invited into.

You were not invited either. But if you hold still, and become a feature of the architecture, and let the note pass through the streets and up out of the water along whatever axis it travels — you can, for the duration of that single soft strike, feel what it is to be a city that kept its word. Even after drowning. Even after forgetting what the word was. Even after every mouth that made the promise had been replaced by coral and salt and the slow rain of marine snow.

That is who lives here. That, more than the octopus or the moray or the grouper considering in its doorway, is who the Drowned Quarter is *for.*

They are still ringing.
