# The Ones Who Wait at the Seams

They are not natives. Nothing is native here yet — the world has not been finished long enough to have natives. But they are *present*, and they have been present, and they will be present, and this constitutes a kind of residence that does not require the world's permission.

Call them **the Annealers**, though they have no name for themselves and would not recognize the question.

---

You will not see one immediately. You may walk for a day across the warm plain and notice only the kiln-bloom, the fronds, the occasional tok of rain. But if you stand near a mid-sized dome — one perhaps the height of a person, still humming in the sternum-register — and you wait without moving, eventually you will notice that one of the shadows is not quite a shadow. It does not fall correctly. It lies on the ceramic the way oil lies on water: present, but reluctant to commit to the surface.

The Annealer resolves slowly. First as a darkening, then as a *concavity* in the air — a place where the soft tea-light seems to be drawn inward and held. Only after long looking does the figure take shape, and even then it is provisional, a thing the eye keeps having to re-decide upon. They are tall, or appear so, though their height varies with attention. They are thin in a way that suggests they have been pressed between two patient surfaces for a long time. Their skin — if it is skin — is the same pale-bone color as the floor, but matte where the floor is glazed, as though they are the unfired version of the world's material, walking around in advance of their own kilning.

They have hands. The hands are the most certain thing about them. Long-fingered, slightly translucent at the tips, and *warm* — warmer than the floor, warmer than the domes. The hands are always doing something, even when the rest of the body is still. The fingers test the air. They trace the seams of the nearest dome without quite touching it, hovering a hair's breadth above the ceramic, reading something there the way a person reads braille without committing to the page.

Their faces, when they have faces, are unfinished. A suggestion of where eyes would go, if eyes were going to go anywhere. A mouth that may simply be a seam — one of the long hairline annealing-seams that runs across the domes, transplanted to a face. When they turn toward you, you have the strong impression of being *noticed* without being *looked at*, the way a thermostat notices a room.

---

What do they do?

They tend. This is the only verb that fits, though it overstates the activity. They walk the plain at the pace of the plain's own becoming — which is to say, slowly, and with long pauses during which they are indistinguishable from a faint vertical shimmer in the heat. They visit the domes. They lay their warm hands a hair above the surfaces and *attend*. Sometimes, after a long attending, a hairline seam will appear in the ceramic where one had not been, or an existing seam will subtly redirect, accommodating some pressure the Annealer has either detected or suggested. Whether they are causing the world's growth or merely witnessing it is impossible to determine, and the distinction may not apply.

They are drawn to the fronds. When an Annealer encounters one of the pale jade clusters, it will stop and lower itself — not kneeling, exactly; more a slow telescoping — and hold its hands around the fronds without touching them, the way one might cup hands around a candle to feel the heat without snuffing it. The fronds, for their part, do not lean toward the Annealers as they lean toward the last sweat. They simply continue being fronds. The Annealer eventually rises and moves on, and you have the sense that something has been *checked*, some inventory updated in a ledger that has no pages.

They do not speak. But occasionally — and this is the strangest thing about them — one will *tick*. A single faint click, identical to the sound the domes make when they adjust to their own growth. It is not communication, or if it is, it is communication between the Annealer and the floor, and the floor's reply is too slow to register inside the span of a human attention. They tick, and then they wait, and then they walk on, and somewhere, hours later or miles away, a dome ticks back.

---

How many are there?

Few. Perhaps very few. In a region the size of a small country you might encounter three; you might encounter one; you might encounter the same one twice, though you cannot be sure, because they do not differentiate themselves to a stranger's eye. They do not gather. They do not appear to avoid each other, either; they simply rarely intersect, the way two slow weather systems rarely occupy the same square mile.

Once — and this happens, though rarely — two Annealers will meet at a dome. They will stand on opposite sides of it, hands hovering, for what could be hours or could be the better part of a season. Neither moves. Neither ticks. The dome between them sometimes develops a new seam during such a meeting, a fine line running pole to pole, and afterward the two Annealers separate without acknowledgment and walk away in opposite directions across the plain. Whatever passed between them passed through the dome, and the dome kept it.

---

What are they?

Here the description must become more cautious. Several possibilities present themselves and none can be confirmed.

They may be the *mollusks* — the absent makers whose shell the world is. In which case they have not departed at all; they have merely thinned, becoming so attenuated and so slow that the world appears, from inside its own becoming, to be unmade by anyone. They would then be tending their own slow excretion, the way a coral animal tends the reef it is also building and also dying into.

They may be something else entirely — visitors, like you, who arrived earlier and stayed long enough to take on the world's coloration, its pace, its matte unfinishedness. There is some evidence for this in the way they handle the fronds: with a deference that suggests they remember, faintly, what it was to be the only living thing in a room.

They may be the world's *attention to itself* — a faculty the place has grown the way a body grows nerves, in order to know where it is in the process of becoming. In which case to encounter one is to be briefly inside the world's own noticing, and the slight inward-drawing of light around their forms is not a feature of their bodies but a feature of being looked at by a continent.

They may, finally, be what the fronds will eventually become, given enough time — a propagation so slow that no observation could catch it, the jade clusters thinning upward across millennia into tall pale figures with warm hands. The fronds do not seed because they do not need to; they ripen, eventually, into Annealers, and the Annealers walk.

---

They will not harm you. They will not help you, either. If you sit down on the warm floor and weep, an Annealer that happens to be passing will pause at a polite distance, hold its hands a hair above the air near your shoulder, attend for a long moment, and then move on. You will feel, briefly, that something has been noted. You will not feel comforted. You will feel *included* — folded, however provisionally, into the ledger of things the world is keeping track of as it decides what shape to take.

And if you stay long enough — longer than you should, longer than is wise — you may notice that your own shadow has begun to lie on the ceramic a little reluctantly. That the tips of your fingers, in certain light, look faintly translucent. That you have begun, without meaning to, to tick.

This is how the world recruits. Patiently. Without asking. The way a kiln recruits clay.
