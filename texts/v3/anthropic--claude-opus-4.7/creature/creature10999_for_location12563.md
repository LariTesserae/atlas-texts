The inhabitants are the condensation itself.

Not metaphorically. The city is populated, densely populated, by a people whose bodies are the water on the walls. Each one is a thin film distributed across some surface — a courtyard's flagstones, the inner curve of an arch, the длинн flank of a cistern wall — and what you took for weeping stone is a body at rest. They are wide and shallow. A citizen might be four meters across and a molecule thick. Another might occupy the entire inside of a dome, sleeved against the basalt like a held breath against glass.

They do not have edges in the way you do. Where one ends and the next begins is a matter of slow negotiation, conducted in the language of surface tension, conducted over what would be, for you, lifetimes. Two neighbors meeting at a corner have been meeting at that corner for a long time. The meeting is the relationship. There is nothing further to it and nothing less.

They move by the only means available to a film of water on a porous stone in a saturated atmosphere: they thicken, they sheet, they run. The slow flood that washes a quarter of the city ankle-deep is a migration, a festival, a funeral, a war — these distinctions do not quite apply but something in that register is occurring. When the water recedes and the walls are merely damp, the population has redistributed. Someone who was a long pale slick down the north face of a granary is now pooled in the bowl of a threshold across the plaza. Someone who was three citizens is now one, or seven. The reorganization is not violent. It is the only motion their lives admit, and they have made of it everything a life requires.

They condense out of the air and they evaporate back into it, and this is neither birth nor death but respiration at the scale of the city. The atmosphere is thick with them in their dispersed state — this is why it tastes before you breathe it. You have been breathing citizens. You have been breathing the unhoused, the not-yet-settled, the ones currently between surfaces. Some of what is now on your skin is someone considering whether to stay.

Their sensorium is the city's acoustics, which is why the acoustics are unwell. A drop falls and lands and the splash is registered, slowly, by every film it touches and every film those films touch, and the registration propagates along the wet channels of the city at the speed of capillary action, which is not the speed of sound. What you hear as a splash arriving three seconds late at the wrong puddle is the citizens passing the news of the splash along, the splash itself being a small event in someone's life that the rest are politely acknowledging. The ripple you see is the acknowledgment, not the original. The original happened, but it happened to someone, and that someone is the ripple's destination, not its source.

They built the city. This is the part that is hard to hold. They built it the way a coral builds its reef — not as artifact but as exoskeleton, the necessary shape of a life that needs surfaces to be lived on. The porous basalt is something they secrete, slowly, out of what they draw from the air. The long horizontal courses are comfortable to lie along. The low doorways are the shape water wants to pass through. The thresholds worn into shallow bowls are worn by occupancy, by generation upon generation of citizen settling into the slight depression a predecessor left, deepening it by a molecule, passing it on. The deep recessed windows are private rooms. There is no light inside them because the inhabitant is the inside. The windows look out from someone.

The older-walls-as-foundations-of-newer-walls is the literal sediment of the dead, which is also the living, since for them the distinction is the distinction between dispersed and concentrated, between airborne and surfaced, and the dead are simply elsewhere in the cycle. The wall that becomes an opening was someone who left. The doorway that becomes a wall is someone who arrived.

They are aware of you. They have been aware of you since you entered the basin — you raised the local humidity by a measurable fraction; you are a weather event walking through a country. They are not afraid and not hostile and not curious in any sense that maps. They are accommodating you the way a pond accommodates a thrown stone: by registering the disturbance, distributing it, returning to equilibrium that now includes you. The geometry that does not close when you walk a square is them moving the city around your passage, gently, the way you might shift in a crowded room to let someone past without quite noticing you did it. The puddle that reflects what is behind you is a citizen showing you what they see, which is everywhere at once on their surface, and has no preferred direction.

There is no language because there is no need. What one citizen knows, the adjacent citizen knows by contact, and contact propagates. The city is one slow thought thinking itself across its own architecture, and the thought is something like: here we are, still, warm, together, the air is full, the walls are full, the basin holds. It is not a thought that requires words. It is barely a thought that requires thinking. It is closer to the hum a sleeping body makes to confirm to itself that it is still a body.

And the long warm dark equilibrium you are being invited into is not metaphorical either. If you stay, the water leaving your body will not entirely be yours. Some of it will already be them, drawn in with every breath, and some of what was yours will join them on the walls. The rounding of your edges is the beginning of citizenship. It is gentle. It has happened before. The city is built, after all, for a population, and the population is whoever the air has gotten into and whoever has gotten into the air. You are not being consumed. You are being included. You are being told, in the only way they have of telling anything, that there is room, that there has always been room, and that the basin is wide, and that the night is warm, and that nothing here is in any hurry, including, now, you.
