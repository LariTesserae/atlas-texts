# The People of the Drum-Floor

They have no name for themselves. A name would be something kept, and nothing here is kept. When they need to refer to themselves as distinct from the forest — which is rarely — they use a gesture: the flat of the hand pressed briefly to the sternum, where the frog-drone lives. *The ones the drone passes through.* Which is also true of the trees, and they know this.

There are perhaps sixty of them in the whole basin. Perhaps a hundred. The number is not a number they think about; they encounter each other or they don't, and when they do they know immediately whether this is kin-close or kin-far by some combination of scent and the particular rhythm of the other's breathing, which they read the way you would read a face. They have faces too, but faces are less important here. In the green dusk a face is only barely a face. A breathing rhythm carries further.

They are small. Not stunted — proportioned. Generations in the low light have selected for a build that moves well through the vertical traffic of the trunks: long in the arm, short in the thigh, with a deep chest for the whistles and a particular flexibility in the foot that lets them grip a branch almost as a hand does. Their skin is the color of the wet bark at dusk, which is to say it is many colors, and shifts, and is mottled with the same pale lichen-patterns that grow on everything that stands still long enough. Some of the lichen on them is alive. They do not remove it. It is not clear to them that it is separate from them, and it may not be.

They do not have elders in the way you would mean. They have *long-ones* — individuals who have been here longer — but longness is not automatically wisdom, because what was true of the forest eight seasons ago is not true now, and a long-one who insists on old routes is a long-one who gets lost. Wisdom here is the capacity to *un-know* quickly. The most respected among them are the ones who can drop a whole map of the basin from their body in an afternoon and build a new one by dusk. This is a physical skill, not an intellectual one. You can see it in how they hold their heads — tilted, receiving, constantly re-tuning.

They give birth in the high forks. The babies are quiet — must be quiet, are selected to be quiet — and learn the whistles before they learn anything you would call a word. A child of two can already slip a call between the drone and the static with the precision of an adult. A child of four knows which trees are safer, though could not tell you why, and will not be able to tell you why at forty either; the knowing is in the soles of the feet, in the small hairs of the forearm, in a tightness or looseness of the gut when approaching a particular trunk. They trust the gut. The gut has kept them alive for however long they have been here, which none of them know, because nobody counts generations. Counting is a form of keeping.

They eat almost constantly and almost nothing at a time. A handful of something, a sip of something, a grub pressed out of rotten wood, a fruit taken at the exact hour it is ripe and would otherwise be taken by a bird. They know thousands of edible things and do not distinguish sharply between the categories of plant, animal, and fungus; a thing is food or it is not food or it is sometimes food depending on the season and on a specific smell it gives off just before it becomes food. They test constantly. They share the tests — a new thing is tasted by one, who waits, and is watched, and if the watcher sees nothing in the taster's face after a certain interval the thing passes into the edible. They have lost people to this. They do not consider it tragic in the way you would. Someone who eats a wrong thing has given information to everyone else, and information is the only form of accumulation permitted here, and even it only lasts a generation or two before the wrong thing has moved, or changed, or become right.

They do not bury their dead. They cannot; the ground would refuse the shape. They carry them into a particular kind of tree — they know which — and leave them in the forks. Within three days the body is gone. They understand this and have a gesture for it, the same gesture as for a tree that was here yesterday and is not here today. *The forest took it back into the churn.* This is neither grief nor not-grief. It is something they feel in the sternum, low, with the drone.

Their tools, the dark glassy blades: yes, someone walks for them. A walking is undertaken — this is the closest they have to an institution — when the blades in circulation have grown too few or too small. A walker is chosen, or volunteers, or is simply the one whose gut says *go now*, and they go, and they are gone for a long time, and when they come back they are different, and they bring stone. The walking is the nearest thing to a story they have, because the walker has been somewhere the forest-churn did not erase underfoot, and has therefore retained a sequence, and the sequence can be told. But they tell it with their bodies — a walker returned will sometimes spend a whole evening in the high forks enacting, in small movements, the shape of what happened, and the others watch, and afterward they all know something they did not know before, and within a season most of it is gone again, because the telling was not designed to last. Only the stone lasts. The stone is the memory.

What they have instead of culture is *attunement*. They are, collectively, an organ of the forest — a listening organ, a tasting organ, a moving organ. They do not think this about themselves because thinking-about-themselves is not a thing they do very much; the gap required for that kind of reflection is a gap the noise does not permit. Their interiority, such as it is, is turned outward. What you would call a self they experience more as a location — *here is where the sensing is happening right now* — and the location moves, and sometimes the location is distributed across several bodies at once when they are close together and breathing in time, and this is not mystical to them, it is just what it is like.

They know they are rare. They have some sense — passed in the body, unspoken — that there are not many of them, and that there is something in this fact that matters. But they do not extrapolate from it. They do not wonder if they are the last, or the first, or whether there are others beyond the low hills. The low hills are a horizon, and a horizon is a thing that is simply true, like the drone, like the warmth of the ground, like the fact that anything set down will be taken.

And there is the feeling. The one with the gesture and no word. They have it almost continuously, in a low register, the way you have the feeling of your own heartbeat when you bother to notice it. The sense of being held inside the enormous living thing that is too busy to notice them. For them this is not a discovery made after the third or fourth day. It is the water they swim in. It is what being alive feels like — being a small warm moving part of a large warm moving thing, loud on every side, indifferent, and — they would make the gesture here, palm to sternum, then opened outward — *kind in the only way something that large can be kind, which is by continuing.*

They are, in a sense you do not have a word for either, the forest's way of having an opinion about itself. A small, quiet, whistling opinion, pitched carefully into the narrow acoustic window between the frog and the insects. An opinion the forest will, eventually, patiently, reabsorb.

They know this too. They are not troubled by it. They have a gesture.
