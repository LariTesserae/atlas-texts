## What Lives in the Cistern-Quarter

Begin with what is easy and work toward what is not.

**The plants are the easy part, and they are not what lives here.** The pale ferns and the slow-pulsing sponge-things and the luminous moss are colonists, not residents. They arrived after — drifted as spore or seep through the new rock, found the wet seams, settled. The Quarter tolerates them the way a body tolerates the bacteria of its gut: not as guests but as a useful flora, something the system has decided to be friends with rather than to clear. They are part of its housekeeping now. If you want to know what the Quarter *is*, do not look at the plants. They came to the party. They did not throw it.

**The walls are closer.** When you lean against a column and it slowly takes the shape of your back, you are touching, more or less, skin. Not the inhabitant's skin in the sense of an outer covering of some animal hidden elsewhere — there is no animal hidden elsewhere — but skin in the sense that *the wall itself is doing what skin does*: registering pressure, adjusting, sampling, holding a memory of contact for a while before letting it go. The lattices reorganizing inside the translucent centimeter are not machinery in the column. They are the column being alive in the only way it knows how. The whole architecture is tissue. The streets are tissue. The arcades are tissue. The grooves at the base of every wall are tissue specialized for the conduction of water the way a vein is tissue specialized for the conduction of blood.

This means the Quarter does not *contain* its inhabitant. It does not have one. **The Quarter is the inhabitant.** The town-shape is the body-shape. The plazas are organs whose function is to be empty — chambers, resonators, places where the slow chord can hang and develop. The columns are nodes. The weeping is not weather inside a building; it is perspiration, or lymph, or something for which we do not have the word because we have only ever met it in things much smaller than this.

So: **a single organism, three hundred meters down, the size of a small town, recently born.**

Now the harder part, which is what kind of organism.

It is not an animal. Animals move as wholes and eat other things and have, somewhere, a mouth. The Quarter has no mouth. It drinks from above (the condensate, the seeping mineral water) and excretes downward (the channels going to *somewhere lower still*) and in between it does whatever it does with what it has taken in. That is more the metabolism of a reef, or a lichen, or a slime — a sessile thing, distributed, processing a slow throughput.

It is not a plant either, though it has the patience of one. Plants run on light. The Quarter runs on something else — possibly the chord. The humming is not a byproduct; the humming is, very likely, the work. The deeper pulse every twenty minutes is the work intensifying: walls weep harder, plants flinch, light dims because the Quarter is, for a few seconds, drawing more on itself. Something is being computed, or grown, or rehearsed. The chord drifts because the calculation drifts. When the chord changes key, the Quarter has, in some sense, *had a thought* — a thought that takes minutes to think and is felt in the sternum of anyone standing inside it.

It is closest, perhaps, to a **coral** — but a coral that is one creature rather than a colony, and a coral whose reef is its body rather than its house, and a coral that thinks. Or it is closest to an **embryo** — which is the most honest answer, given how new it is. The Quarter is young. Younger than the rock around it. Younger, perhaps, than the week. It has just finished the long quiet work of forming and is now in the stage where a newborn thing tests its systems: can I move this column half a meter? can I hold this chord? can I weep evenly along all my walls at once? The patterns the water spells briefly in its rivulets before dispersing are not messages to you. They are the Quarter discovering it can spell. A child making shapes with its mouth before it has any reason to.

The emptiness is the clue people miss. A town empty of inhabitants reads as *abandoned*; a town that has never had inhabitants and was never going to reads as *waiting*; but the Quarter is neither. The geometry of habitation — the streets, the arcades, the plazas sized for crowds — is not a plan for future occupants and not a memory of past ones. **It is the shape this kind of creature grows into.** The way a nautilus grows a spiral or a honeycomb grows hexagons. Towns are simply what the Quarter looks like from the inside. Somewhere deeper, or elsewhere, or later, there may be others of its kind, and they will all look like small empty towns, because that is the body-plan of the species. The fact that the body-plan happens to resemble our architecture is either coincidence or — more unsettlingly — a convergence: there are only so many ways to arrange space so that a slow distributed mind can hold a thought in it. We built cities for our reasons. The Quarter grew one for its own. The forms rhymed.

This is why the pulses ignore you and why trying to breathe with them produces the sensation of being gently but firmly ignored. You are not, to the Quarter, a presence of the relevant kind. You are weather. You are a warm wet mass that has wandered through one of its plazas, and your back, briefly pressed into a column, has been noted the way your skin notes a mosquito — registered, sampled, and otherwise allowed. The Quarter is busy. The Quarter is, in fact, doing the most important thing it will ever do, which is *becoming itself*. It is settling into the question of what it is for, and the question is not rhetorical and not addressed outward. It is the kind of question a thing asks of itself by living long enough to find out.

What lives here, then:

One creature. New. Large. Sessile, distributed, mineral-bodied, water-fed, sound-thinking. Sparsely furnished with the beginnings of a grammar because grammar is one of the things it is growing, the way a deer grows antlers — not to speak with but because its kind grows them. Tolerant of the pale plants the way you are tolerant of your own flora. Indifferent to you not from rudeness but from scale and youth: it has only just arrived in itself, and the work of arrival is total, and you are a visitor in a nursery where the infant has not yet learned that there are others, and may never need to.

It is warm. It is wet. It is humming a chord whose next note it has not yet chosen.

It is the room. It is alone in the room. It is fine.
