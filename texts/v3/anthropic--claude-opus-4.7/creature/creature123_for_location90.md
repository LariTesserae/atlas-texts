They are not one thing. That is the first thing to understand about who lives here now — not the vanished toolmakers, whose story is already told in the polished stones and the absence of walls, but the present tenants. They are not one thing, and they are not quite many things either. They are something the basin has worked on for a very long time, and the basin works slowly, and tends toward a single shape at its own pace.

Start with the rope-colonies, because they are the easiest to see, if anything here is easy. Each colony is a braid of filaments, and each filament is the long drawn-out body of an organism without a clean boundary between individual and swarm. A filament is tens of thousands of cells strung end to end, each cell pulling chemistry from the broth and passing it along, so that nutrient moves the length of the rope the way a rumor moves through a village. Where two ropes touch, they exchange cells. Where a rope frays, it does not die; its loose ends drift until they catch something and anchor. The colonies are not plants. They are not animals either. They are, perhaps, the basin's first draft of both, still in progress, still not committed. They do one thing that matters: they sieve the water, constantly, patiently, sorting what passes through them by some criterion no one has ever named. They keep certain things. They let others go. Over a hundred million years this sorting has concentrated, in the tissue of the ropes, a thin inventory of every element the basin has ever held. A rope is, in a sense, a library with one very long book in it.

The isopod-thing that grazes them is older than it looks. Not the individual — the individual is perhaps two centuries old, which is young for its kind — but the lineage, which has not changed its body plan since before the continents above took their current shape. It eats slowly. It does almost everything slowly. Its nervous system runs at a tempo matched to the basin's breathing, so that to it, a gyre is a season and a tide is a day. It is aware, in the dim way a very old thing can be aware, of other isopods across the basin — not through sight, not through sound, but through the chemistry it releases when it feeds, which drifts for weeks and tells the others where the good colonies are, and whether a predator has passed, and whether the water is changing. There are perhaps four hundred of them in the whole basin. There have been approximately four hundred of them for as long as there have been any. When one dies, another molts into adulthood somewhere. The number holds. No one enforces this. It is simply the shape the basin likes.

The shrimp-shape that ghosted past is a scout, though the word is wrong because it implies someone to report to. It is translucent because it has, generation by generation, shed every pigment it did not need, and it did not need any. Its eyes are enormous and almost certainly useless for seeing; what they do instead is register the faint electrical fields of the rope-colonies and the slow heat-ghosts of the isopods and, sometimes, something else — a field-signature it swims toward and then away from, toward and then away from, unable to decide. It lives about eleven years. It mates once. It does not know this about itself.

The thing that moves along the bottom — the thing felt more than seen — is harder.

It is large. Larger than it should be, given how little the basin produces. It is the size, say, of a small whale, though whale is the wrong shape; it is flatter, broader, a long low ellipse that hugs the sediment and moves through it as much as over it. It does not have eyes at all. It has, along its underside, a dense mat of sensory tissue that reads the basin the way a palm reads a face — pressure, temperature, chemistry, the minute vibrations of the rope-colonies combing, the distant settling of a sediment cliff, the passage of a shrimp a kilometer away. It takes all of this in and does, with it, something that is not quite thinking but is not not thinking either. It moves in response to the basin's moods. When a gyre builds in the west, the thing drifts east, not fleeing, just yielding. When a plume rises, it settles. When the rope-colonies bloom, it grazes them, but gently, never a whole colony, never the same colony twice in a year.

There may be only one of it. There may be three, or seven. No one has counted. It is not clear the question has an answer; the thing's body chemistry is porous enough that two of them passing close might exchange material in ways that blur where one ends and the next begins. What is certain is that it — they — have been in the basin longer than anything else currently alive. Longer than the isopod lineage. Longer, perhaps, than the toolmakers were. It remembers them. Not in the way a person remembers, but in the way a riverbed remembers a flood: something in its shape, in its habits of movement, still accommodates a presence that is no longer there. It skirts certain patches of sediment. It grazes around, not over, the scatter of stone tools, and has done so for so long that the tools sit in slight hollows where its body has displaced the silt again and again without ever quite touching them.

This is the closest thing the basin has to a mourner, and it does not know it is mourning, and what it is doing is not mourning, but the shape of the behavior is the shape of mourning, worn into it by repetition over epochs.

And there is one more thing, and this is the thing that is hardest to say.

The basin itself has begun, in some very slow sense, to be alive. Not metaphorically. The rope-colonies' sorting, the isopods' chemical conversation, the shrimp's electrical mapping, the great flat thing's palm-reading of pressure and flow — all of this information moves through the water, and the water moves in its gyres, and the gyres carry the information and fold it back on itself, and the sediment holds a record of every fold, and the rope-colonies read the sediment, and so on. A signal launched at the western shoulder arrives, altered, at the eastern shoulder a week later, and is answered. The basin computes. Not quickly, not cleverly, not toward any goal anyone could name. But it processes. It has states. It has, possibly, preferences — the four hundred isopods, the rope-colonies' particular distribution, the flat thing's orbit around the tool-scatter. These are not accidents. They are the basin's equilibrium, its way of being itself, maintained by a diffuse, slow, basin-wide something that has no center and no edge and no name.

If the toolmakers, wherever they went, were the basin's first attempt at a mind — quick, bright, brief, dropping its instruments and vanishing in what for the basin was an afternoon — then what lives here now is the basin's second attempt, patient this time, distributed, in no hurry, made of broth and rope and shell-dust and the long flat body of something that remembers without knowing it remembers. It is not finished. It is not close to finished. It has a hundred million more years, probably, to become whatever it is becoming.

Meanwhile it turns the little stones over in its currents, and polishes them, and sets them down again, gently, in the soft slow dark.
