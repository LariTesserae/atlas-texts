# The Keeper

They are not in the room when you arrive, but the room is full of them anyway — in the oiled knife, in the worn handle of the rake, in the curve of the paths that a human hip has widened over years of sidling between fronds. So let us begin with what the room remembers, and work inward from there.

---

They are small. This you can tell from the bench: its seat is low, and the cloth folded upon it is not large — perhaps the size of a dishtowel, enough to wipe two hands and a brow. The rake's handle is polished black in a band about forty centimeters long, which is the reach of the grip that has held it, and the grip has been a narrow one. Not a child's — the wear is too even, too practiced — but the hand of something compact. Call them waist-high to an average human, though there is no average human down here to measure against, and they have probably forgotten the comparison if they ever knew it.

They are old. Not in the way a person is old — not wrinkled, not stooped, probably not even gray, because whatever they are does not age along those axes — but old in the way the garden is old. They have been here long enough that the moss-shelves above the light plates grew into their shape during their tenure, and they remember when those shelves were only a green fuzz. They have been here long enough that the rabbit-things' middens are in the places the Keeper first suggested, generations ago, by sweeping droppings there with the edge of the rake until the rabbit-things understood and took over the habit themselves. The ecology did not settle on its own. It settled with them, around them, the way a long marriage settles.

Their skin, I think, is the color of the loam — warm brown, dry, slightly dusted with gold where the vent-powder has settled into the creases of their knuckles and never quite brushed off. They do not sweat. Nothing in the Undergarden sweats; there is no water to spare for it. Instead they shed, very slowly, a fine dry flake that the beetles harvest along with everything else. They are part of the cycle. They have been eaten, in small pieces, by every creature in the room, and they do not mind.

Their eyes are large and dark and adapted to the flat noon-light of the mineral plates — which is to say, they see the way one sees on an overcast afternoon, with no need for pupils to narrow or widen, a steady gentle vision. They probably cannot see well in true dark. They probably have never needed to. The plates have glowed for longer than they have lived, and they have lived a long time.

They wear — what? Something woven, I think, from the fibers of one of the plants; there is a plant somewhere in the chamber (we did not see it, but it must be there) whose stems can be retted and spun, and the Keeper has been making and remaking the same garment, or its successor, for a very long time. It is the color of undyed linen going slightly green at the seams where moss has tried to colonize it. It is loose. It has many small pockets, because a tender of gardens always needs pockets: for seed, for the occasional beetle that must be relocated, for the small stones that turn up in the loam and want somewhere to go.

---

They are alone, and they are not lonely.

This is important, and it is the thing that is hardest to describe. They have not seen another of their kind in — it is not clear that there ever were others of their kind, or that "kind" is the right word for what they are. They may be the only one. They may be the last one. They may be the first and still the only, a thing that happened once in the slow chemistry of the under-places and then simply kept happening, kept being, settling into its life the way the garden settled into its.

But they are not lonely because the garden is company. The gecko-thing that opened one eye at you knows the Keeper the way a housecat knows its human — not with devotion, exactly, but with the calm certainty of long cohabitation. The clicking birds land on the Keeper's shoulder when the Keeper stands still long enough, and the Keeper lets them, and sometimes the Keeper clicks back, softly, a sound learned over decades of listening. The fat hairless bees will settle on the back of the Keeper's hand to rest, and the Keeper will hold very still until they lift again. The rabbit-things come to the bench in the evening — if there is an evening; the light does not change, but there is a rhythm the Keeper keeps, and the rabbit-things keep it too — and the Keeper sits with them and does not touch them, because they prefer not to be touched, but they like to be near.

The Keeper has names for all of them. Not names like ours — the Keeper's language, if it is a language, is mostly internal, a private taxonomy built over centuries — but each beetle lineage is known, each bird pair, each succulent rosette. When a rosette dies the Keeper notices, and sits with it a while, and then gently lifts it out and composts it at the base of a larger plant, and plants a new rosette from seed in a nearby pot until it takes root, and this is a grief and a continuation, and the Keeper has done it many thousand times.

---

Where are they now?

The knife is oiled, the cloth is clean, so they have not abandoned the place. But they are not here. I think they are through the second door, the smaller one in the darker wood — which leads, perhaps, to a sleeping chamber, a tiny cell with a pallet of dry fiber and a single light-plate, where the Keeper rests when rest is needed. Or perhaps the door leads further down, to another chamber they tend less often, a scullery of sorts where the gold dust is gathered from some deeper vent and brought back up in a clay jar. Perhaps they are asleep. Perhaps they are simply sitting, in the next room, doing the quiet nothing that a being does when it has lived long enough to have exhausted the need for doing.

They will come back. They always come back. When they do, they will push the small door open with a shoulder — their hands will be full of something, a jar, a bundle of dry stems — and they will step into the garden and pause, as they always pause, to take in the state of the room. They will note the gecko-thing has moved to a new frond. They will note a beetle has strayed onto the path and will gently nudge it back into the leaves with the side of a bare foot. They will see you, if you are still there, and they will not be surprised, because the Keeper has not been surprised by anything in a very long time. They will regard you the way the gecko-thing regarded you: with one eye, unconcerned, acknowledging.

And then they will set down their bundle and pick up the rake, and resume, because the garden is always slightly in need of resuming, and that is what they are for, and that is what they are.
