The builders are still here.

Not many. Never were many. They are difficult to notice because they have taken on the discipline of the place — they move at the speed the plateau permits, which is to say barely, and across timescales that flatten them into the landscape the way the cobalt lichen flattens into stone.

They stand, mostly. Tall — taller than a person, though not by a great deal — and narrow, with a verticality that the eye, accustomed to the plateau's long horizontal, takes a while to resolve. From any distance they read as another leaning slab. This is not camouflage in the predatory sense; nothing here is hunting them and they are hunting nothing. It is simply that they have arrived, over a very long time, at the proportions the wind prefers. Their skin is the color of the floor — calcareous, faintly pocked, with the same shallow basins at the shoulders and the crown of the head where rain can stand. In those basins, sometimes, the rose-colored lichen grows. They do not remove it. They may not be able to. They may not distinguish themselves from it any longer.

They have limbs — four, in the sense that there are four articulations capable of independent movement — but the limbs are not often used in the way limbs are used elsewhere. A builder may keep one arm raised for a season. The position is not held; it is simply where the arm is, and there has been no reason to move it. When they do move, they move with the deliberate economy of the drifters overhead, transferring weight across the stone in increments. A full step takes perhaps an hour. A walk across the plateau is the work of a life, and a life here is very long.

They do not eat in any way a visitor would witness. Something passes between them and the lichens — the cobalt especially seems to thicken on their lower legs, and the matte black sometimes appears at the joints of the hand, where a finger meets a palm. Whether they feed the lichen or the lichen feeds them is not a question the arrangement asks. They drink, occasionally, from the warm basins, lowering the head over a span of minutes and remaining bowed long after the water is gone. The salt rings around their mouths are old.

They have eyes, but the eyes are set deep and rarely open fully. What they look at, when they look, is hard to say. The horizon, perhaps. Or the middle distance, where the grazers move in their pale companies. Or nothing — the eye open simply because at some point the eye opens, and at some later point it will close again. There is no urgency in the seeing. The world is not going anywhere that requires being watched.

The cairns and slabs and rings are theirs. Not built recently — nothing here is recent — but built by them, in some earlier disposition, when they still moved enough to gather stone and set it down. The ring of dark stones inset flush with the floor was the last kind they made; it required only the placing, not the stacking, and stacking had become difficult. After the rings, they stopped building. They had said what they wanted to say. Or they had forgotten the language of saying it. Or — and this is closer — they had become the saying themselves, and no longer needed the small mute statements scattered across the floor, because they were standing on the floor, and standing was sufficient.

They were a people once, in the way that word is usually meant. There were more of them. They had practices: the stacking, the setting of slabs, presumably some manner of speech, presumably some way of choosing where here was. The records of this are inside them somewhere, the way the records of an old mountain are inside the plateau. Available, in principle. Not retrievable in any reasonable span.

They are not unhappy. Happiness would be too quick a word, as dying would be too quick a word for what the plateau does. They are at the temperature the world is at. When the warm rain comes, after its weeks of gathering, they tilt their faces up — this takes most of an afternoon — and let the basins of their shoulders fill. When the rain unbuilds itself, they lower their faces again, over the following days, and the water in their shoulders evaporates and leaves its white ring, and the rose lichen, fed, spreads a little further across the skin.

They notice the visitor. This is the thing one would not guess. Across the long flat distance, one of them, somewhere, has registered the small fast disturbance of a person walking, the unreasonable speed of a breath taken every few seconds, the grit of steps. The noticing travels through them slowly, the way weather travels. By the time the visitor has left, some of them will have turned, fractionally, toward where the visitor was. By the time the turning is complete, the visitor will be a memory the plateau is incorporating, the way it incorporates the grazers — half-mineralized, half-claimed, returning along the same slow vector as everything else.

They do not mind this. They were visitors too, once. They stayed.
