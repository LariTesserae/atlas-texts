## Who Lives Here

### The Brinecatchers

Start small, because everything here starts small. The brinecatcher is the size of a grain of rice, with a body like a bead of oil — green-gold shading to violet depending on how the bronze light catches it. Four wings, not two; the rear pair smaller, used for steering in the thick updrafts off the pans. They hatch in the mineral crusts at the water's edge, where the larvae look like tiny pale commas wriggling in the hot shallows, grazing on the bacterial mats that stripe the pans in colors the eye can barely name — ochre, rust, a particular dried-blood maroon.

An individual brinecatcher lives perhaps six days. A column of them at dusk is something else — a weather system of insects, rising in slow helical pillars off the hottest pans, drawn upward by the convection and held together by whatever signal they use to know each other. The columns lean downwind in the afternoon dry; at dusk, when the air settles, they stand straight up, sometimes a hundred feet, and you can stand beneath one and hear it: a sound like fine rain on paper, continuous, without any single source.

They do not bite. They have no mouth as adults. They exist to breed and to feed everything else.

### The Wall-Pourers

The lizards the townspeople call *pourers*, or sometimes *brickskins*, because when one moves along a wall the wall seems to pour in that direction — a ripple of the same color as the substrate, as if a piece of the kiln had decided to relocate itself. They are fat in the belly and flat in the skull, no longer than a man's thumb, with skin that mottles to match whatever they rest on: the dull red of old brick, the bruise-purple of wet cobble, the faint yellow-green of gypsum lace.

They have a trick. In the bronze hour they flatten themselves so thoroughly against the warm stone that the whole body pancakes out — you can see the spread of their ribs through the skin — and they drop their metabolism to almost nothing, baking in the heat. A man could put his hand down on one and not know until it poured away from his palm. At dusk they wake all at once, the entire population of a wall stirring in a single wave, and they hunt brinecatchers by the thousand. A fat pourer in a good week eats its own weight each night.

They lay clutches of eggs in the mortar gaps, and the eggs look like pearls of salt. The townsmen leave them alone. There is a local superstition that a wall without pourers is a wall about to fall.

### The Stilt-Waders

The birds are the oldest-looking things in the basin. Thin white egrets, the description says, but *older* — and that word is exact. They stand four feet tall on legs like lengths of pale wire, and the legs have too many joints. The knee bends one way and then there is a second bend, lower, that goes the other, so when they walk they do so with a slow articulated precision, like something being assembled as it moves. The neck is long and held in a flat shallow S. The beak is black and narrow and slightly recurved, and when it closes on a thing it does so with a soft dry click that carries across the pans.

Their feathers are not quite white. In the morning light they look white; in the bronze hour they are the color of old ivory; at dusk, when they step out of the sheds and culverts where they wait out the heat, they take on the color of whatever sky is above them, and a flock of them wading a pan at sundown looks like a row of standing flames, orange-pink against the mineral crust.

They eat eels, larval brinecatchers, the smaller pourers when they can catch them, and any rat foolish enough to come down to the water in daylight. They nest on the flat roofs of the taller warehouses, in untidy piles of twig and wire and bone. The nests are decades old. A given roof-nest may have been in continuous use longer than any man in town has been alive, renovated each year by whatever pair has inherited it.

They do not vocalize. The click of the beak is all they make. It is enough; the flats carry sound, and a flock at work sounds like a clock shop at noon.

### The Cellar Rats

These are the confident fat ones. Not vermin-sized — big, some of them nearly cat-sized, with short dense coats gone slightly yellow-brown from the sulfur in the water they drink. They have the complacent movement of animals with no predators that matter. A rat here will stop in the middle of a street to groom itself, then continue when it pleases.

They live in the vast undercity that the town has accidentally built — the culverts and drainage works and overflow channels that honeycomb the ground beneath the pans, the disused boiler-tunnels, the cooling ducts that run between the kilns. In the wet warm dark down there, the rats have developed into something almost domesticated by the place itself. They have routes. They have traditions. A particular rat will leave a particular culvert at a particular hour and walk a particular circuit of back alleys, and if you watched for a week you could set your clock by it.

They eat what rats eat, which here means: dead brinecatchers in windrows at the edges of the pans, scraps from the workers' middens, the occasional sick pourer, and — this is important — each other's dead. The rats bury their dead. Not ceremonially; simply, a rat coming upon a dead rat will drag it to one of a few specific sinks in the drainage network and leave it there, and those sinks are known, and other rats come to them, and in the deep warm dark there are chambers full of generations of rat bones picked clean by the blind pale things that live further down still.

### The Things Further Down

Mention should be made. The aquifer is hot and old and the tunnels go deeper than anyone has reason to map. The brinecatcher larvae in the pans have cousins — blind, unpigmented, slightly larger — that live in the hot water below. There are pale eels in the river, and the eels come up from somewhere, and whatever they come up from is not a place men visit. Occasionally something is found in a settling tank that nobody can name: a jointed segment, a translucent claw, a coil of what looked like rope until it uncoiled. These are noted and discarded. The town does not dwell on them. They are part of the arrangement — the basin's private life, which the town has agreed not to inquire into.

### The River Otters (so-called)

They are not otters. They are otter-shaped, which is a shape that evolution arrives at repeatedly when the problem is *hunt long slippery things in warm shallow water*. The local animal is low and long and dark, with short dense fur that sheds water in beads, webbed feet, and a flat paddle tail. The face is not an otter's face. It is flatter, with the eyes set farther forward, and the teeth when the mouth opens are too many and too fine, a dense picket of needles for holding eel.

They hunt in family groups of four to seven, working the river at dawn and dusk, driving eels into the shallows and taking them there. They are not shy of men but not interested either. A worker crossing one of the river bridges at dawn may look down and see a group of them surfacing together in a ring, each with an eel in its jaws, and they will look back at him with mild recognition and then submerge again about their business.

They den in the banks, in chambers they excavate into the warm mud, and their dens are large — multi-chambered, multi-generational, with entrances above and below the waterline. A den complex may house forty animals in extended family. They are long-lived; a matriarch may rule her stretch of river for thirty years.

### The Scavengers

Dog-sized, but not dogs — not quite anything you could name. Low-slung, heavy-shouldered, with short coats the color of the dust and long narrow jaws. They come out after the otters have finished, taking eel-heads and tails and spines from the bank. They also work the middens behind the kilns, and the edges of the pans for dead birds, and they will follow a sick rat for a half a mile waiting for it to lie down.

They are not pack animals but they tolerate each other. At a good kill three or four may feed together without fighting. They have a call — a low coughing *huk-huk-huk* that carries at night — and they use it sparingly, mostly to locate each other across the basin in the dark. It is the one large animal sound in the place. A man lying in a rowhouse bed at midnight may hear one coughing two miles away across the pans, and an answer from somewhere closer, and then nothing.

They are not dangerous to men. They have never been dangerous to men. It is understood that they could be, if the arrangement changed, but the arrangement has not changed in a long time.

### The Men

Should be counted among the fauna, because here they are one population among several, and not the dominant one. There are perhaps eight hundred of them, in a town built for five thousand. Most are older. The young ones leave. The ones who stay have the particular quality of people who have made peace with a place that does not need them very much — competent, unhurried, sparing with words. They wear oilcloth against the sour rain and leather against the heat, and their skin over the years takes on a faint yellowish cast from the sulfur, not unhealthy-looking, just *of the place*, the way the pourers are the color of brick.

They tend the kilns and the pans. They cart the soda and sulfur cake and red pigment to the wagons that come once a week from the hills. They keep the machines fed. The machines, as noted, largely run themselves; the men's job is mostly to notice when something is about to go wrong and to prevent it, which is a skill that looks, to an outsider, like loitering. A man leaning in a doorway with a pipe is in fact listening to three different engines in three different buildings and will move when one of them changes its note.

They live in the rowhouses on the eastern bank. They cook on small stoves, eat eel and river-fish and the flat mineral bread the place produces, drink a thin sour beer brewed from grain brought in by the wagons. They sleep early. They dream, one assumes, the dreams of people who live among a great deal of small quiet life and very little large loud life — dreams of small movements, distributed sounds, the long pour of a lizard across a wall.

They have wives, some of them, and children, fewer. The children are quiet. They grow up knowing the names of forty kinds of animal and the sounds of a dozen kinds of machine and the exact hour at which the brinecatchers will rise, and they either stay and become their parents or they leave on a wagon one morning and are spoken of afterward with neither bitterness nor pride.

### The Arrangement

What lives here, finally, is the arrangement itself. The basin and its water and its minerals; the chemical gardens and the small fat animals and the tall patient ones and the things below; the kilns and the men and the slow tired smoke. None of it is ascendant. None of it is in crisis. The brinecatchers rise and the pourers wake and the stilt-waders step out of their sheds and the otters work the river and the scavengers cough across the dark and the men listen to the engines and the engines breathe, and all of this has been happening long enough that it has become a single thing, a single slow organism with many mouths, and the organism is not going anywhere, and it is not becoming anything else, and it is, in its hot mineral patient way, quite thoroughly alive.
