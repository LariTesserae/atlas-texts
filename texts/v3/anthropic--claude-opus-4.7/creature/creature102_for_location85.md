# The Tenders

They are called tenders, though no one remembers choosing the word. *Foreman* is what the paperwork says, *gardener* is what they call each other when no paperwork is watching, and *tender* is what the plants would call them if plants named things. The word has stuck because it carries both meanings — one who tends, and the quality of being tender, soft-footed, soft-voiced, easily bruised by the wrong kind of attention.

There are, on the seventh terrace, four of them. There are always four. When one retires or dies or simply walks down the spiral stair one morning and does not come back, a fourth appears within the season, usually young, usually quiet, usually already knowing things she should not yet know. No one asks where from. The Cauldron has its own hiring.

---

The senior tender of the seventh is a woman called Merel, though that may not have been her name when she arrived. She has been on this terrace for thirty-one years. Her feet are the color of the floor — not stained, exactly; the copper has gotten into her, is *of* her now, a faint green tidemark that climbs to mid-calf and fades. Her soles are unrecognizable as human soles. They are closer to the pads of something that was never meant to wear shoes, thickened and seamed and sensitive in ways ordinary feet are not. She can tell, walking a transect of the floor in the dark, which dimples are low by a millimeter. She can feel the frogs' clicking through the copper before she hears it.

Her hands are the opposite — pale, almost translucent at the fingertips, because she spends so much time with them inside the clay matrices, working by touch in the dark interiors of the embryonic cliffs, feeling for the places where the slurry is pooling wrong, where a capillary has clogged, where a crystal is forming that shouldn't. She does not use tools for this. The tools bruise the clay. She uses fingers, and she hums while she does it, a low tuneless hum that the younger tenders have learned to recognize as the sound of *do not speak to her now*.

She eats little. Drinks the terrace-water directly from the dimples, cupped. Sleeps in a hammock strung in the densest part of the fern-wall, where the lantern-weed's glow is enough to read by, though she rarely reads anymore. What she does instead, in the sleeping hours, is listen. She has a theory about the frogs that she has not told anyone. She has a theory about the tree, too.

---

The second tender is called Ifan. He is perhaps forty, perhaps younger — the damp ages people unevenly here, softening some and preserving others. He came up from the foundry-engines, which is unusual; most tenders come from above, from the cloud-work, the crane-houses. He still has the engine-tender's deep chest and the slight deafness in the left ear, and he still feels the sternum-drone of the foundry as *home* rather than as background. On the terrace he is the one who manages the slurry-lines, because he understands the pipework from its other end. He crawls the ceiling-spaces. He is often upside down for hours.

When he descends from the pipework at the end of a shift his arms are streaked with rust-water and his hair is full of moss that has detached from the ceiling and hitched a ride. He does not brush it out. He has been colonized, slowly, by the terrace itself — a fine dusting of spores in the creases of his neck, a lichen beginning, perhaps, in the soft skin behind his left ear where he can't quite reach and won't let anyone else touch. Merel has looked at it, once, and said nothing, which Ifan took as permission.

He talks more than the others. He tells the tree about his day. The tree, bonsai'd and patient, has heard thirteen years of Ifan's days.

---

The third tender is new — only six years in — and is called Sable, and is the one who worries. Worries about the frogs, about whether the clicking is really lowering or whether the old foreman's hearing is simply going. Worries about the replacement-theory, which Sable heard for the first time in her second year here and has not been able to un-hear. She keeps a notebook, which is forbidden, which everyone pretends not to know about. In it she draws the finished cliff-section from different angles, month by month, trying to catch it in the act of changing. It does not change. Or it changes too slowly for her pencil. This is the thing that worries her most: that she cannot tell the difference between *not changing* and *changing at a rate indistinguishable from stone*.

Sable is the one who will eventually, in perhaps twenty years, be the senior tender. She does not know this. Merel knows this. Merel chose her, though Sable believes she simply arrived.

Sable still wears shoes. Cloth ones, rotted through at the toes. She is losing them by degrees, the way one loses a language one has stopped speaking.

---

The fourth tender is a child. This is not supposed to be possible and is not written down anywhere, but every terrace has one, and the seventh's is a boy of perhaps nine called Wren, who was born here, to a tender two generations back, and who has never descended below the fourth terrace in his life. The cloud-line is his sky. The copper floor is his ground. He knows the ninety-one species the way other children know the names of streets.

He is the one the frogs will come out for. Only him. He sits by the fissure for hours and they emerge onto his knees, pale and cool and looking at him with the sideways attention of things that have decided he is furniture. He does not try to touch them. He has learned, already, the terrace's central discipline: *attend without intervening*. When they click, he clicks back, softly, with his tongue against his palate. Merel has heard this and has not stopped it. Merel suspects — she has not said this aloud, even to herself, quite — that Wren is part of what the terrace is growing. That the Cauldron is not only making stone. That somewhere in the slow composting of a world that ordered its own geology into a world that has its geology ordered for it, a new kind of person is also being matrixed, trellised, slurry-fed, gardened into shape. A person who belongs to landscape the way landscape used to belong to itself.

Wren does not know this either. Wren is simply happy. He has a tree he talks to, though his tree is the same tree as Ifan's, and neither of them knows the other talks to it, and the tree — if the tree has an opinion — keeps it to the slow grammar of its three concentric lichens.

---

What the four of them share, what makes them tenders and not merely workers:

They have all, at some point, stopped being sure whether they are maintaining the terrace or whether the terrace is maintaining them. This is not a crisis for any of them anymore. It was, once, for each. Merel's was thirty years ago and involved a week in which she did not speak. Ifan's was in the pipework, upside down, and involved laughing until he cried. Sable's is happening now, quietly, in the margins of her forbidden notebook. Wren will not have one; he was never on the other side of the question.

They do not think of themselves as a family. They think of themselves, when they think of themselves at all, as part of the ecology — the ninety-second species, or the ninety-third through ninety-fifth, or, in Wren's case, something the count has not yet been updated to include. They die here, usually. Merel will. Ifan has already decided to. Sable has not decided but will, in time, find she has. Wren is the uncertain one; Wren may be the first tender who leaves, carried down with a finished section one day, installed somewhere in the world that needs him, indistinguishable from wild.

The frogs will note his absence with a click.

It will be lower than the last.
