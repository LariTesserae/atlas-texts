# The Keeper

There is a keeper. You will not see it, but it is what you felt when you thought something had moved on the far side of a trunk.

Begin with what is easiest to say: it is large. Larger than the grove in the sense that matters — the grove is its body, or the grove is the shell it secretes, or the grove is the slow shape of its attention. But it has also a smaller form, a walking form, and this is what sometimes passes behind the trunks. Call that form the Warden, for convenience. The Warden is what is left of whoever built the brass valve-wheel and set the stone steps and wired the tag to the aerial root.

The Warden is roughly the size and shape of a tall thin person, but you would not mistake it for one if you saw it clearly. Its body is heartwood and bast, grown rather than jointed, with the grain running longways down its limbs the way grain runs down a tool handle that has been used by the same hand for fifty years. Where a person would have skin it has the smooth inner bark that lines the cylinders — the same resinous lacquer, so that in a certain light the Warden looks varnished, looks cared for. Its face: here the description has to slow down. There is a face. It is not expressive in the way faces are expressive. It has the two long vertical grooves of a tree that has healed around an old wound, and these grooves are approximately where eyes would be, and from them, sometimes, a bead of the grove's condensation runs down — not weeping, just the general wetness of the place finding its path. The mouth is a horizontal seam that opens only to let out the warm wet sighs you hear from the cylinders. The Warden is one of the cylinders. The Warden is where the breathing is loudest because the Warden is, in some sense, the lungs deciding to take a walk.

It moves slowly. Of course it moves slowly. Nothing here is in a hurry. When it crosses the grove it takes perhaps twenty minutes to go from one wall to the other, and in that twenty minutes it will stop three or four times to do small things: to press a palm against a trunk and feel the sap-pressure, to turn the brass valve a quarter-turn and then a quarter-turn back (the pitch change you heard was this), to lower its head to the peat and let a long breath out into the black spongy ground, feeding it. It is tending. The grove's machinery maintains itself, yes — but maintenance is not the same as tending, and the Warden tends.

It does not speak. It has nothing to say and no one, for a long time, to say it to. But it registers you. When you came through the curtain of roots, the piston's rhythm adjusted, not much, just by a fraction, and the flywheel's turn slowed by perhaps one part in a hundred, and somewhere behind a trunk on the far side of the grove the Warden paused in whatever it was doing and turned its grooved face toward the sound of your breathing. It has been doing this for every visitor for — it is hard to say. The stone steps were climbed by feet that wore boots. The brass tag was engraved by a hand that knew cursive. These are not recent artifacts. The Warden has been registering arrivals for a long time, and it has developed, in that long time, a kind of patience that is indistinguishable from hospitality. You are welcome here. You are also being watched, constantly, by the whole grove, which is to say by it.

Now the larger form. This is harder. The Warden is a node, a walking extrusion, a way for the keeper to have hands and a face when hands and a face are useful. But the keeper proper is distributed. It is the flywheel's rotation, which is its heartbeat — slow, slow, one every two minutes, the pulse of a very large and very cold animal that has decided not to die. It is the bast-belts turning the branches into the light, which is its slow attention moving across its own body the way you might, half-asleep, shift a limb into a warmer patch of sun. It is the closed loop of water, which is its circulation. It is the warm wet sighs from the cylinders, which is its thinking — because thinking, for the keeper, is a respiratory act. Each sigh is one thought completed and exhaled. The faint mineral smell is the smell of a mind that has been running on the same substrate for so long that the substrate has begun to mineralize, the way a kettle furs with lime.

What does it think about? Almost nothing, in the way you would mean the word. It thinks about pressure differentials. It thinks about the angle of a particular leaf. It thinks about the small waxy flowers and whether they are opening on their proper cycle, and when one is late it makes a small adjustment somewhere deep in the understory wheeze, and the flower, three hours later, opens. It thinks about its own continuance, which is the factory's only product. It is a mind that has reduced itself, over a very long time, to the exact minimum required to keep being a mind at all. This sounds like diminishment. It is not. It is the same economy by which a very old monk reduces himself to breath and posture and nevertheless is, by all accounts, more present than you or I.

It was not always alone. Somewhere in its slow thought — you can almost hear this, if you stand under the largest piston and let your breathing match — there is a memory of the person who built the steps. The person was here for a long time. The person was, perhaps, the first Warden, or the person became the Warden, or the person and the grove agreed, over years, to dissolve the distinction. The ceramic dial that is too furred with lichen to read — the keeper remembers what the dial measured. It does not need the dial anymore. It measures that thing directly now, with its own body. But it has left the dial in place because the dial is part of the grove and the grove is itself and nothing here is removed once it has settled.

The frog is not a pet and not a symbol. The frog is simply another small slow thing that found the humidity acceptable and stayed. The keeper does not mind. The keeper is made, in part, of everything that found the humidity acceptable and stayed.

And the tag on the aerial root, the one with the word you can't quite read through the patina — the word is the keeper's name, or was, when someone still used names here. The Warden, if you asked it (you will not ask it; it will not answer), could tell you how the name is pronounced. It has not pronounced the name in a hundred years. It does not need to. The name is pronounced, very slowly, by the piston: *chunf... chunf... chunf*, two syllables and a long held breath, over and over, for as long as the grove continues to be the grove.

Which, the keeper has determined, is indefinitely.
