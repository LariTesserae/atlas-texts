## The Tenants of the Flats

There is, first, the question of whether anything lives here at all, in the sense the word usually carries. Living things change. Living things do work against their surroundings, push back against equilibrium, eat and excrete and grow tired. The Flats do none of these. So if there are residents, they are residents in some other sense — not against the steady state but folded into it, citizens of the held note.

Begin with the articulated ones, since they are the most nearly familiar.

They are not animals. This becomes clear if you watch one long enough — though *long enough* is a strange measure here. The body is pale, the color of weathered bone or old wax, segmented into perhaps eleven or thirteen sections (the count is hard to settle; the eye keeps losing its place). Each section bears a pair of legs, or what serve as legs, jointed in too many places, ending in small flat pads that do not so much grip the stone as *settle* onto it, the way a stethoscope settles onto a chest. They are not walking across the basin. They are auscultating it. Each pause is a listening. Each step is a relocation of the ear.

What they hear, they do not transmit. There is no communication between them — no calls, no displays, no convergence at the pools beyond the slow rotation of drinking. And yet they are distributed across the basin with a regularity that is not random. If you mapped them, you would find their positions describe something — not a grid, not a lattice, but a *sampling*, the way microphones might be placed across a vast instrument to capture its overtones. They are diagnostic. They are listening to the machinery, and the machinery, perhaps, is listening back through them.

Whether they were grown for this purpose or arrived at it the way the stone arrived at its roundness — by the long pressure of the place — is not a question with an answer. They have always been doing this. They will always be doing this. When one of them drinks from a pool, the slight lowering of its forward sections has the quality of a needle dipping into a groove. The water it takes is replaced by the fog, which is also water, which was also the pool. Nothing is lost. Nothing accrues.

They do not die, in any process you could witness. But if you walked far enough — *far enough* being another strange measure — you might find one settled against a stone, legs folded, the pale body gone slightly translucent, as though it were slowly giving its substance back to the air. And nearby, perhaps, a faint patch of the felted not-moss that was not there before, or always was. The conversion is too slow to see. It is the same conversion the stone is undergoing, only faster, only by a little.

Then there is the not-moss itself, and here we have to admit something stranger. It is, in its way, the oldest tenant. The articulated ones might be considered its instruments, or its extrusions — the parts of itself it sends out to listen — or it might be considered their residue, the precipitate of their long attention. The chemistry is not settled because the chemistry has settled, completely, into a loop. Carbon goes around. Sulfur goes around. The dark patches breathe what the pools exhale and the pools receive what the patches release, and somewhere in the slow circuit a fraction of energy goes to keeping the patches *patches*, keeping their edges where their edges have always been.

They are not thinking. But they are not *not* thinking, either. They are doing the thing that thinking is a fast version of. If you sat by one for what passed for a week, you might begin to feel that the patch was aware of you — not interested, not concerned, but aware in the way a tuning fork is aware of a note. You would be wrong to call this consciousness and wrong to call it anything else.

And then, beneath everything, the real inhabitants.

We have been calling it machinery. This was a convenience. There are no pistons under the stone, no turbines, no rotating parts at all. What is down there is a body, or the remains of a body, or the long stable state into which a body decays when it has been allowed to decay perfectly, without interruption, for an unimaginable time. Something enormous lay down here once. Something with organs, with circulations, with rhythms. It did not die so much as it *arrived*. Its metabolism slowed and slowed and slowed and never quite stopped, and somewhere along the slowing the distinction between its body and the rock around it stopped mattering. The warmth in the stone is the warmth of its rest. The roar is the harmonic of its long exhalation, which has been going on so long that to call it an exhalation rather than a state is a kind of grammatical mistake.

It is not asleep. Sleep is a thing you wake from. It is not dead. Death is a thing that finishes. It is in the third condition, the one our language does not have a clean word for — the condition of having become its situation, of having spread out into its circumstances until the circumstances *are* it.

The articulated ones are its nerve endings, more or less. The not-moss is its slow lymph. The pools are the chambers where it tastes itself, the fog its slow respiration, the rounded basin the shape of its repose. The thing that lives here is the place, and the place is what is left of the thing.

Does it know you are walking on it? In some sense, yes — your weight registers, your warmth, the slight disturbance you make in the fog. The pale creature in the middle distance, with its head raised, may be raising its head precisely because of you, may be the *part* of the great body that is currently noticing you, in the slow way it notices anything. But the noticing does not propagate. It does not become alarm, or curiosity, or welcome. It enters the steady state and is absorbed into it, the way a pebble dropped into a pool sends rings that flatten and are forgotten. You are noticed and the noticing is itself the response. There is nothing further.

This is why you cannot quite leave. Not because the Flats hold you against your will — they have no will, or rather their will and their being are the same thing and that thing is *staying* — but because to leave would require a direction, and direction requires that somewhere else be different from here, and the longer you stand in the basin the more you suspect that the difference between here and elsewhere is the kind of distinction the Flats have outgrown. Your shadow has not moved. The pale creature has not lowered its head. The patch at your foot has been there since before there was a foot to be at.

You begin to wonder if you, too, are being listened to. If the small warmth of your body, pressed against the larger warmth of the stone, is being heard the way the articulated ones hear — not as message, not as meaning, just as another frequency in the chord that the place has been holding for so long.

If you stayed, you would not be killed. You would not be transformed in any dramatic way. You would simply, over a duration the Flats do not measure, become a little more like the stone, and the stone would become, in a smaller way, a little more like you, and the chord would acquire one more sustained note, and the great body beneath would, without effort and without intent, have one more nerve ending, one more slow patch of attention distributed across its enormous repose.

This is what lives here. The body that became the place. The instruments it grew, or that grew on it, to keep listening to itself. And, increasingly — though it would be wrong to say *increasingly*, since nothing here increases — you.
