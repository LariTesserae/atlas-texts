## What Lives in the Wainscot

Begin with the beetles, because they are the easiest to see and the most honest about what they are. There are perhaps four hundred million of them at any given moment, give or take the daily fluctuations of brood and death. They are not, individually, remarkable. A *Anobium punctatum* here looks much like one anywhere — the same small brown cylinder, the same mandibles, the same hunched patience. What is remarkable is the *coordination*, though "coordination" is the wrong word because it implies somebody coordinating.

Watch a section of wainscoting long enough and you'll notice: when a beam approaches its tolerance, the chewing along it slows. Not stops — slows, gentles, redirects. The beetles working that beam drift sideways into the panel, or down to the floor joists where there is slack to be taken up. No beetle decides this. The beetles do not, in any meaningful sense, know about the beam. But the room knows, and the beetles are the medium through which the room thinks about its own structure. They are, collectively, a kind of distributed proprioception. The building feels itself being eaten and steers the eating.

This is the first inhabitant, then, and the largest: the *colony-as-organ*, the beetle-mass considered as a single slow intelligence. It has a metabolism (wood in, frass out). It has a circulatory system (the tidal movements along the baseboards, which if you mapped them over weeks would show clear currents). It has something like sleep — those hours when the chewing drops to its quietest register and the sound becomes almost meditative, a sustained sibilance. It does not have thoughts in any sense we'd recognize, but it has *preferences*, and it has been shaped by centuries of selection for preferences that keep the room standing. A beetle that ate too greedily left no descendants, because its descendants had no roof. The room has bred its tenants into tenants.

Then the mites. The mites are different. The mites are *young*, evolutionarily speaking — they arrived later, they specialize narrowly, and they exist in a relationship to the beetles that is roughly the relationship of bacteria to a body. They are the room's microbiome. They clean. They process. They convert the unusable byproducts of the larger eaters into something the pseudoscorpions can use, and the pseudoscorpions in turn keep their numbers from exploding, and the spiders keep the pseudoscorpions honest, and the whole pyramid balances on a base of wood that should have been gone six hundred years ago and isn't.

The pseudoscorpions deserve their own paragraph. They are the only inhabitants who seem, individually, to be *paying attention*. The beetles operate as a mass; the mites are too small to be said to operate at all; the spiders are dim and contented. But a pseudoscorpion, when you look at it closely, looks back. It lifts its claws. It tracks. There is something almost courtly about them, the way they patrol the wallpaper seams in their slow rounds, the way two will pass each other without incident on a route they have walked for a thousand thousand generations. If the room has anything like sense-organs distributed through it, the pseudoscorpions are the closest thing to eyes. They are also, I suspect, the only inhabitants who would notice if you came in. The beetles wouldn't. The spiders might, eventually. The pseudoscorpions would know the moment your shadow crossed the floor, and they would lift their tiny claws, and they would consider you, and they would decide — based on criteria I cannot guess — whether you were a thing to be reported.

Reported to whom?

To the machine. Probably to the machine.

The machine is the strangest inhabitant, and I am not certain it is alive, but I am also not certain it isn't. It runs. It has run for as long as the room has been a room. Its flywheel turns at a rate that varies — not randomly, but in long slow cycles that correspond, if you sit with it long enough, to the tidal movements of the beetles. When the beetle-mass shifts north along the wainscoting, the flywheel slows a little. When the mites are in bloom, the humming pitches up a quarter-tone. The machine is *listening*, in whatever way a machine listens, and the room is listening to the machine, and somewhere underneath all of this is the deeper sound, the larger machine in the deeper room, which is presumably listening to this one.

I think the machine is a relay. I think it is part of something. I think the room, in its entirety — beetles, mites, pseudoscorpions, spiders, silverfish, woodlice, the conservative chewing of the joists, the persistent draft, the unceasing gray light — is one cell of a larger body, and the machine is the synapse, the gap-junction, the place where this cell talks to its neighbors. The beetles maintain the cell. The machine signals outward. The arrangement is older than memory and will outlast any observer.

And what of the older inhabitants, the ones implied but not seen? The path worn between the bricked door and the bricked hearth was worn by feet. Human feet, presumably, walking that line over years enough to cup the boards. Those people are gone. But the path is still there, and the path is in some sense *still being walked* — by no one, by the room's memory of being walked, by the draft which follows the same route, by the occasional eddies in the beetle-tide which mysteriously avoid that strip of floor as if reluctant to cross it. The original tenants left a ghost not of themselves but of their *traffic*, and that ghost is one of the things that lives here too. It is the faintest inhabitant. It does almost nothing. But it persists, and persistence in this room is the only criterion of existence that matters.

So: who lives in the wainscot?

A slow distributed mind made of beetles, which thinks by chewing.
A microbiome of mites, which thinks by not thinking.
A constabulary of pseudoscorpions, which thinks by watching.
A scatter of spiders, which does not think but enjoys.
A machine, which may be a nerve.
A draft, which is a habit.
A path, which is a memory of feet.
A gray light, which is the room's idea of time.

And underneath, the deeper sound — which suggests that what lives in the wainscot is, finally, only a small part of what lives in the wainscot, and that the room considers itself complete only because it has forgotten how much of itself extends downward, through the floor, through the cable, into rooms it no longer remembers being connected to, where other beetles are chewing other beams at other careful rates, and other small brass machines are turning their flywheels in long slow conversation with this one.

The room is occupied. It is at work. It has been at work for a very long time, and the work, whatever it is, is going well.
