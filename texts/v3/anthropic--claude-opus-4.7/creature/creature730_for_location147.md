The people of Lowdamp are called, by themselves and no one else, the sumpers. There are perhaps two hundred and forty of them, give or take a birth, give or take whatever the shaft takes back. They are not a race apart — their grandparents came down from the surface, most of them, answering postings that promised warm work and steady pay and did not mention the frogs. But two or three generations in the wet heat have done things to them, and you can tell a sumper from a surface-man at a glance now, even when the sumper has gone up for a funeral and is standing in a black coat on dry ground.

They are pale the way cavefish are pale, but not white — a kind of waxy tallow-cream, because the piped gas-light has something yellow-green in it and the skin takes a tint from what light it knows. Their hair grows slow and fine. The men go bald early at the crown and keep a fringe around the sides that they wet down with palm-water and comb back. The women wear theirs long and braided close to the skull because loose hair in this air becomes a sodden rope by noon. Everyone's hands are wrinkled as laundresses' hands, permanently, from the fingertips to the second knuckle — the cavern pickles them from infancy — and the wrinkling goes further up the older a sumper gets, so that you can guess a man's age by how far the pruning has climbed his forearms. The foreman's is past his elbows.

They are not large people. The heat does not favor bulk. They run to the lean and sinewy, with long flat muscles under skin that shines always with a film of sweat and the cavern's own condensate, so it is hard to tell where the man ends and the weather begins. Their eyes are wide and dark-adapted, the pupils big even in the greenish gaslight, and they have a habit of not quite looking at you when they speak — looking instead at a point just past your shoulder, as if listening for something behind you. They are listening. They are always listening, underneath. The chorus tells them things. When a run of toads goes quiet in a corner of the town, every sumper within earshot turns their head without knowing they've turned it, and someone goes to see what's wrong.

Their voices have adapted to the chorus. They speak in a register that sits above the croaking — a thin, carrying tenor in the men, a clear high alto in the women, pitched to ride over the low pulse the way a flute rides an organ drone. A sumper speaking on the surface sounds, to surface ears, faintly querulous or sing-song, and they know it and hate going up for this reason among others. Among themselves they also use a second language, which is not words: a clicking of the tongue against the palate, a thumb-snap, a knuckle-rap on brick or pipe. This language carries through the wet air and through the stone when the chorus is at its loudest, and it is how foremen call across the foundry and mothers call children home. A child of Lowdamp learns to rap before she learns to speak.

They dress in very little and what they wear is pale. Cheesecloth shifts, as you noted, and canvas trousers cut off below the knee, and rubber galoshes always, always, up to the calf, with the tops rolled down in a cuff when a sumper is at ease and rolled up tight when he is working. The galoshes are a kind of currency. A good pair lasts four years and is handed down. Children inherit their older siblings' boots and grow into them by stuffing the toes with moss. Everyone has a second garment for going up the shaft — a surface-suit of proper wool and proper leather, kept in a cedar chest sealed with wax against the damp, brought out maybe once in a decade and smelling, when unsealed, of a country the wearer has half-forgotten.

They are patient the way the rock is patient. This is the deepest thing about them, and the hardest to describe without making them sound slow-witted, which they are not. A sumper will think about a question for a full minute before answering it, and the answer when it comes will be exact. They do not interrupt. They do not hurry. A conversation in Lowdamp, between two old sumpers at the end of a shift, can last four hours and cover three topics, with long silences between sentences in which both parties are genuinely considering, and neither feels the silence as awkward. To a surface visitor this is unbearable. The sumpers know this and are mildly amused by it but do not alter their pace for company. Their pace is not a manner. It is the metabolism of the place, and they are of the place.

They are, in a quiet way, religious, though no priest has ever come down the shaft and no chapel stands in the town. Their religion is the toads, or is expressed through the toads, or uses the toads as its grammar — it is hard to say which, and they themselves will not say. The unwritten courtesy you noticed is the visible surface of something deeper. A sumper passing a wall of toads will touch two fingers to his lips and then to the brick, not every time, but often, and without self-consciousness. A sumper who has killed a toad outside of rendering season — it happens, by accident, a misplaced boot, a dropped crate — will stop work for the rest of the shift and sit by the pump-house and not speak to anyone. No one requires this of him. He requires it of himself. During rendering season the old toads are taken by a man called the taker, a position that rotates among the senior sumpers and is held for a year. The taker does not speak for the duration of his year except when absolutely necessary. He eats alone. His wife sleeps in a separate bed. When his year ends he is embraced by every adult in the town in turn, and then he may speak again, and for a week afterward he will often weep without warning, which everyone pretends not to notice, because the heat, you understand, the heat.

They have their small pleasures. There is a card game called sink, played with a deck that has six suits instead of four, the extra two being toads and stones, and a hand can take an hour. There is a still behind the foundry that produces a clear spirit called damp-gin, flavored with the fern that grows by the gaslights, and it tastes of iron and green and is drunk warm from clay thimbles. There is singing, late, after shifts, when a few sumpers will sit on the steps of the pump-house and sing in their high carrying voices against the chorus, and the chorus — this is what visitors never quite believe — will sometimes seem to answer, the pulse shifting, the waves re-timing themselves around the human melody, so that for a few minutes the song and the croaking are one music. The sumpers do not make much of this. It happens. It doesn't always happen. You can't force it.

The children are the strangest of them, to a surface eye. There are eleven at present, ranging from an infant to a girl of fourteen. They are grave little creatures, wide-eyed and slow-moving and startlingly articulate when they do speak, because they have spent their short lives listening to adults who think before they talk. They play, but their play is quiet — games of stones arranged in patterns on the cobbles, games of holding very still while a toad climbs across a bare foot, games of rapping messages pipe-to-pipe across the town. They do not run much. Running is for the surface. The oldest girl, the fourteen-year-old, has been up the shaft twice and came back both times subdued and thoughtful, and has told the younger ones that the sky is a lie and that the wind is worse than anyone says. The younger ones believe her. She may be right.

And then there is the matter no one in Lowdamp will quite address, though all of them know it, which is that the sumpers and the toads are becoming, over generations, a little more like each other. The pale skin. The wide dark eyes. The patient pulse of thought. The listening. A young sumper's throat, when he sings, swells slightly at the base — not a goiter, the company doctor who came down once was very clear it was not a goiter — and pulses in time with the low notes. The oldest sumpers, the ones whose pruning has reached their shoulders, sit very still for very long periods and breathe in a way that is not quite human breathing, a slow shallow pulse that matches the chorus. They do not seem unhappy. They seem, if anything, to be settling, the way a feature of the cavern settles, the way the young rock is learning to be rock. Something is accreting in them. No one knows what it will be. No one is in a hurry to find out.

Above, the world does whatever the world does. Down here, the sumpers pulse and the gaslights burn green and the brick weeps and the people — slowly, slowly — learn how to be whatever they are becoming.
