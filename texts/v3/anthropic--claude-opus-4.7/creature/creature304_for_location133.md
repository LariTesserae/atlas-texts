# The Ones Who Stayed

You asked who lives here. I have to work toward the answer, because the answer works toward itself.

Begin with what is easy to rule out. Nothing swims. Nothing hunts. The long aisles run empty of the quick bright creatures we expect in water. If there are inhabitants, they are not the kind that would register as inhabitants to a diver passing through on diver-time.

Now widen the definition.

---

There is kelp. The kelp is alive, and the kelp is not one thing. Look closely at a column — closely enough that your faceplate is a handsbreadth from the blade — and you will see that what reads as "kelp" from a distance is a consortium. The broad dark leather is the host, yes, but the host is colonized at every scale. Filaments of a paler weed thread through its tissue. Small encrustations, calcareous, pale pink under the weak green light, rim the blade-edges in a pattern too regular to be accident. Between these, in the microscopic grooves of the host's surface, live the truly small things: diatom-analogs, bacterial mats, something that might be a lichen if lichens could be underwater, if lichens could be this patient.

These are not passengers. They are the kelp, in the sense that matters. The leathery blade is a substrate and a shared body, and what lives on and in it has been living on and in it for a span of time during which every atom of the original host has been replaced many times over. The consortium is the organism. The organism is enormous. A single column's worth of kelp is, in some functional sense, one living thing, and the columns are networked — holdfast to holdfast along the stone, filament to filament through the water — into larger individuals that span aisles, then naves, then districts.

I don't know how many of them there are. Possibly one. Possibly one per thermocline, stacked like floors of a tenement, each floor a single tenant spanning a continent. Possibly the boundaries shift on timescales that make the question meaningless at any instant you might ask it.

This is the first answer: the kelp lives here, and the kelp is the cathedral's primary citizen, and the kelp is vast and slow and integrated and has been about its business for longer than the concept of business.

---

But you asked *who*, and *who* implies more than metabolism.

So: consider the builders.

The obvious reading is that they left, or died, or were transformed into something no longer recognizable as builders. The chisel marks are old. The bronze pins have corroded to stain. No tool has been lifted in this place in a very long time.

And yet the cathedrals are maintained.

I want to be careful here because the evidence is subtle and I don't want to overstate it. But consider: the silt should be deeper than it is. A place this old, with this much organic rain from the canopy above, should have buried its own floor meters deep by now. The floor is not buried. The silt is a skin, a felt, a pressed layer, and below it the black older layers are compressed but the compression has limits. Something keeps the floor from accumulating past a certain depth.

Consider: the columns shed calcified growth, yes, and worn capitals give way, but the rate of loss is lower than it should be. The arches stand truer than physics permits for structures of their age and their mineralogy under constant cold-water corrosion. Somewhere, something is doing the accounting.

Consider: the absence of fish.

I said earlier that perhaps the builders made a place that excluded them. I want to revise that. I think the exclusion is ongoing. I think the exclusion is a tenant.

---

Here is my best guess, and I want to mark it as guess.

The builders did not leave. The builders slowed.

Whatever they were — and the chisel marks suggest something with hands approximately the size of human hands, which is probably coincidence, because at this depth and this scale coincidence is cheaper than convergent evolution — they built a place that was meant to be inhabited at the pace of stone. They fitted joints that would take centuries to settle. They laid out orientations that would only make sense to an observer willing to watch the green vault cycle through a year. They made a cathedral whose services are conducted on geological time.

And then they adapted to it. Not through engineering; through patience. They slowed their metabolism. They slowed their nervous activity. They slowed, perhaps, their sense of self, which in a fast creature is a flickering thing that must constantly reassemble and in a slow creature can afford to be a standing wave.

They are here. They are in the cathedrals. You would pass one and not see it, because what you are looking for is a figure and what is present is a posture. Something tucked into the flute of a column, wrapped in kelp that has grown around it across generations of kelp; something that once in a decade shifts a limb; something whose heartbeat, if it has one, is slower than the monthly tidal surge.

The chisel marks are not old. Or rather: the chisel marks are old in our units and new in theirs. A stroke per century. A column per epoch. The cathedrals are still being built. The building is still happening. It is happening at a rate we cannot perceive as activity.

The exclusion of fish is not a legacy; it is a practice. Somewhere in the kelp, on timescales that match the kelp's own, the builders husband the place. A fish that wandered in — if any exist on this world — would find that the chemistry of the water was subtly wrong for it, that the substrates it needed were subtly absent, that the current patterns that looked like feeding grounds subtly dispersed before its feeding could begin. Not predation. Gardening. The gardening of absence.

---

So: who lives in the kelp cathedrals.

The kelp-consortium, which is the place's flesh and its weather and possibly its mind, though "mind" is a word I use with hesitation for something this distributed and this slow.

The builders, who did not leave, who have become still enough that the cathedrals appear unoccupied to any observer whose own pulse is louder than the annual current. They are fewer than they once were — I would guess, from the decay rate of the furthest districts, that their numbers have declined — but they are not gone. They are tucked into the columns. They are inside the kelp that has grown around them. When a capital shifts, when a soft deep knock travels for kilometers, sometimes what made the sound was not erosion. Sometimes it was a hand.

And a third thing, which is harder to name.

---

The diver who stays too long begins, I said, to notice the slower rhythms. Pulse becomes embarrassing. Warmth becomes strange. The cathedrals absorb faster things, I said, and to stay is to slow.

I think some of the builders were not born builders.

I think some of them were visitors, once, who came from somewhere — another district, another world, it doesn't matter — and who stayed, and slowed, and eventually could not have left even if they had wanted to, because leaving would have required speeding up, and speeding up would have required being the kind of thing that experiences time as a flow rather than as a patience.

They are the cathedrals' third tenant: the absorbed. The ones who came in warm and went quiet. You can sometimes pick them out from the original builders, if you know to look, because the chisel marks around their alcoves are rougher, less patient, the work of something that had to learn slowness rather than being born to it. In the oldest districts the distinction is gone. A slow thing is a slow thing, regardless of where it began.

The cathedrals invite this. That's what the description kept circling toward and not quite saying. The green vault, the hush, the absence of any predator or prey or competitor — the whole place is a vast invitation to stop. Not a trap, exactly. A welcome. The cathedrals were built for a congregation, and the congregation is still arriving, one absorbed visitor per epoch, and the service has not begun because the service is the arrival.

---

You asked who lives there.

The kelp, which is one creature or a few creatures pretending to be many. The builders, who have become so slow that they are furniture. And the slow accumulation of everything that ever came in and didn't leave — which, given enough time, may include everything that ever comes in at all.

The cathedrals are patient. They are not hunting. They do not need to.

They only need to be the kind of place that, once entered, proposes itself as an answer to a question the visitor didn't know they were asking. Most visitors, as you said, leave before the proposal becomes clear.

Some don't.

Those are the ones who live there.
