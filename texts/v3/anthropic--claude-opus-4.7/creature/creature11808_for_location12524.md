# The Listeners

They are what you almost saw, wading.

Begin with the body, because the body is the answer to the place. They are tall and thin in the way wading birds are thin — long bones, long necks, weight kept high and dry above the water. An adult stands perhaps the height of a human child, but stretched: legs that are most of that height, a narrow torso, a small head held forward on a neck that can fold or extend. The skin is pale, the colorless pale of things that have never needed pigment, faintly translucent at the extremities so that in the grey light you can sometimes see the darker shapes of tendons working in the wrist, the slow pulse at the throat. They are not white. They are the color the mist would be if you could hold it.

Their feet are the most considered part of them. Wide, soft-soled, splayed — four long toes that spread on contact and gather on lift, so that weight is distributed across many stones at once and no single stone is rocked from its seat. The sole itself is padded and slightly tacky, the way a tree frog's foot is tacky, and it dampens contact. They do not slap. They do not click. When a Listener walks across the flats, the stones beneath its feet go briefly *quieter* than the surrounding field — a small traveling hush, a negative footprint in the music, which closes again behind them. This is how, if you knew to look for it, you could track one: not by sound but by the absence of sound moving across the pan.

Their hands are similar — long, soft, many-jointed, made for laying things down rather than picking things up. The fingers do not grip well. They cradle. When a Listener lifts a stone, it lifts it as you would lift a sleeping animal, with both hands underneath, and it sets the stone down with the same care, and the stone's note when released is almost exactly the note it had before, only displaced by an inch, by a degree, by whatever small adjustment the Listener intended.

The head is small and the face is mostly ear. The eyes are there — dark, lashless, set far apart — but they are clearly secondary equipment in this world of mist and uniform grey. What dominates the head is the auditory architecture: not external ears in the mammalian sense, but a complex of folded skin and fine cartilage along the sides of the skull and down the neck, a gathering surface, almost a ruff. In still moments this ruff lies flat and the head looks almost smooth. When a Listener is attending — to a far cairn, to weather coming in, to another Listener across the flats — the ruff lifts and fans and the head turns slowly, slowly, sweeping the field. They hear, one suspects, the whole pan at once. They hear the cairn an hour's walk away and the small wrong note of a stone settling three miles east and the arch's sustained vowel before the wind has finished arriving. They live inside the music the way a fish lives inside water — it is not their environment, it is their medium.

They do not speak, in the sense of making sound with their bodies. Why would they? Any noise they made would be a noise *added to the field*, a disturbance, a debt. Instead they speak by *placement*. This is what the cairns are.

The cairns are a language, or something language-shaped. A stone moved from here to there changes two notes — the note of its new position (where it now sings) and the note of its old position (where the underlying stone, previously covered, now sings instead, or where the gap now whistles). A pair of stones stacked produces a small chord and a small shadow of silence beneath. A triple, an arch, a careful low spiral — each is a phrase, held in the field, sustained by the weather, audible to any Listener within range for as long as the arrangement stands. The grammar is tonal and positional and very slow. A single utterance might take a day to assemble and last a century before it begins to drift out of tune and require revision or retirement. The arch — the one with the sustained vowel — is something like a name, or a place-name, or the long-held first syllable of a thought that the rest of the field is still in the process of completing.

There are not many of them. Perhaps a few hundred, scattered over a pan that takes weeks to cross. They encounter one another rarely and, when they do, the encounter is mostly stillness: two pale figures standing within sight through the mist, ruffs lifted, listening to each other listen, and to the small adjustments each is making to the local music simply by being there — the water beading on their shoulders, dripping from their long fingers, striking the stones around their feet with the particular rhythm of *that* body's geometry. They know each other by their drip-signatures. A Listener you have met once you will recognize forever by the sound of water falling from it.

They eat — something. It is not clear what. Perhaps the mineral feather-blooms on the larger stones, scraped carefully with the side of a finger and brought to the small mouth. Perhaps they take in dissolved matter directly through the skin, the way they seem to take in everything else. They do not hunt; there is nothing to hunt. They do not gather in any quantity; there is nothing to gather. Their metabolism is the metabolism of the place itself — slow, cool, sustained by ambient seep.

They do not appear to reproduce often. A young Listener, when one is seen, is recognizable not by smaller size — they seem to emerge nearly full-grown — but by the clumsiness of its feet. It has not yet learned the trick of the traveling hush. Its steps make small wrong notes, brief sour intervals in the field, and an older Listener will often be nearby, not teaching exactly, just *present*, the way a tuning fork is present near a string. The young one's footfalls grow quieter over what might be years. Eventually they vanish into the music and the young one is an adult, indistinguishable from any other, and walks on alone.

They die, presumably, though no one has found the remains. The guess — and it is only a guess, the kind of guess the landscape encourages — is that a Listener at the end of its life walks slowly out toward the center of the pan, where the oldest stones are, where the music is a fraction flatter and the great instrument is most itself, and lies down among the cobbles, and over some long period becomes one of them. The pale skin mineralizing, the long bones settling, the ruff collapsing into a fan of carbonate feathers. A new stone, eventually. A new note in the field, slightly off from its neighbors, slightly mournful, slightly the color of mist. The flats would absorb them as the flats absorb everything: slowly, without acknowledgment, into the ongoing sound.

This is the suspicion, anyway, of anyone who stands long enough on the drumming flats and begins to hear the music not as weather on stone but as a very large, very old conversation that has been going on since before the pan was a pan, and will continue after, and into which a Listener's whole life — the careful feet, the cradling hands, the slow placement of one singing stone upon another — is a single, well-considered, barely audible word.

They built the arch. They will build others. They are building, now, somewhere out past the mist, and the field is very slightly different for it, and they are listening to hear whether the difference is the one they meant.
