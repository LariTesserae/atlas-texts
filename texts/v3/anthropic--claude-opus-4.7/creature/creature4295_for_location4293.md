The ones who live here.

Start with the frogs, because you already have. But you haven't, quite — you've seen them as a surface, a pulsing skin on the walls, and that isn't wrong but it isn't the thing. The thing is that there are not thousands of them. There is one of them, and it has thousands of bodies.

No — go slower. That's too quick, too mystical, and it makes them sound grander than they are. They are frogs. Each one is a frog: it eats the small flies that hatch from the seeps, it holds to the wet stone with the pads of its feet, it will die in its season and its small pale body will be taken by the eel-things in the channel. They are not a hive. They do not share a mind the way ants share a task.

But. The pulsing of their throats, which looks asynchronous — it isn't, not exactly. If you sat against a wall for an afternoon (and the wall would not mind; nothing here would mind) you would begin to feel that the pulses travel. A slow wave crosses a wall over the course of perhaps twenty minutes, barely perceptible, a drift of quickening from one edge of a cluster to another. And the wave that leaves one wall arrives, eventually, at the next. The frogs are not calling to each other. They are breathing to each other. The whole city inhales and exhales across hours, and the throats are the smallest visible unit of that breath.

This is the first inhabitant: the breath. It is not alive in the sense the frogs are alive. It is what the frogs are doing together without meaning to.

The eels — call them eels, the word is close enough — are older than the frogs. You can feel this when one slides past your ankle: there is no startle in it, no interest, a completeness that makes you feel, briefly, like the newer thing. They move in the channels that were streets, and they know the grid. This is the part that is hard to say without overstating. They know the grid. When one of them reaches what was once an intersection, it chooses, and the choosing is not random — over days you would see that certain routes are traveled and certain routes are not, and that the pattern is stable, and that the pattern corresponds to nothing you can see in the stone itself but does correspond, if you had a map of what the city used to be, to the main thoroughfares. They are using the streets. Not as streets — they have no errands — but as the shape of their moving. The city's plan has become the eels' habit, or the eels' habit has preserved the city's plan, and there is no way, now, to tell which.

The second inhabitant: the memory of streets. Held in the muscles of something that was not there when the streets were made.

Under the overhangs, the soft-bodied things. Don't look closely — you were right not to. Not because they are horrible; they aren't. Because they are unfinished. They are what happens when a lineage has lived so long in the dim and the wet that it has begun to let go of being any one thing. Some of them have eyes and some do not and some have the suggestion of eyes, a patch of slightly different paleness where eyes used to be or might be becoming. They are soft the way a thing is soft when it no longer needs to hold a shape against anything. Nothing troubles them. Nothing has troubled them for a very long time. They are, in a sense, the city's own tendency made flesh — the same tendency that has rounded the lintels and softened the walls, expressed now in animal tissue. Given another age they will perhaps be walls themselves. Given another age the walls will perhaps be them. The distinction is being negotiated slowly, in the dark, under the overhangs, without urgency.

The third inhabitant: the softening. The yielding of edge to edge. It wears many small bodies.

The cave crickets are the closest thing to citizens. They move in processions, as you noticed, across the ceilings. The processions have routes. The routes do not change. A cricket joining a procession joins it at a particular place and leaves it at a particular place and in between walks at the pace of the cricket ahead of it. If you watched long enough you would see that the processions form a circuit through the city, that every ceiling is crossed, that every domed room is entered and exited, and that the full circuit takes — it is hard to say — perhaps a day, perhaps longer. They are patrolling nothing. They are inspecting nothing. They are simply the city's habit of being walked through, continued after the walkers are gone, miniaturized into something the city can still support. Somewhere in the deep past, people walked these streets on routes very like these, and the crickets are the ghost of that walking, reduced to the minimum metabolism required to keep the pattern alive.

The fourth inhabitant: the route. The old human going-about, shrunken to fit in what remains.

The crabs are harder. The crabs are, I think, only crabs. They are doing what pale crabs do. Not every creature has to be something else. The city needs its crabs to be crabs, to be the small stubborn uninterpretable fact at the edges of the courtyards, the reminder that not everything in the bowl is a slow allegory of the bowl. The crabs are why this place is a place and not a metaphor. Bless the crabs.

And then the dome's note.

I have been avoiding this one.

The note is not made by anything you can find. You could search every dome, and people — if any ever came here — have, and found nothing. No resonant chamber, no hollow, no creature with lungs that size. The note is made by the whole bowl. By the heat rising and the haze settling and the water-film tightening on the stone and the ten thousand small throats pulsing in their long wave and the eels tracing the forgotten streets and the crickets completing their circuit and the soft things under the overhangs doing their quiet unfinishing. All of it, together, every so often — every many minutes, every sometimes-hour — adds up to a pressure that the bowl releases as a single low tone. The city is not voiced. The city is voicing. There is a difference.

This is the last and largest inhabitant, and the one the others are organs of: the bowl itself, slowly alive. Not a god. Nothing so articulate. More like a sleeping animal that has incorporated a ruin into its sleep and whose dreaming is now indistinguishable from the ruin's settling. The frogs are its skin. The eels are its long habits of movement. The soft things are its willingness to let go of shape. The crickets are its remembered errands. The crabs are its irreducible remainder. And the note is what it says, rarely, when the sum of all these becomes briefly more than their arrangement.

When the note sounds, the ten thousand throats almost answer. You noticed this. They don't quite. That almost is the city's whole condition — a thing that was once meant, is no longer meant, and has not yet stopped meaning. It lives in that gap. It has lived there a long time. It will live there longer.

Nothing here is waiting. That is important. None of the inhabitants are waiting for the people to come back, or for the civilization to resume, or for anything at all. The bowl has made its peace. What lives here lives here. The heat presses down. The water beads and falls and beads again. And somewhere, slowly, the held breath continues being held.
