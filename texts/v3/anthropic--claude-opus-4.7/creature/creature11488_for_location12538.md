# The Inhabitants of the Cradle Plateaus

## The People

They are not tall. Generations of thin air have compressed them toward the ground, given them the deep chests of swimmers and the short strong legs of climbers who never need to climb fast. Their hands are wide. Their fingers are blunt and clever. The faces are broad across the cheekbones, the eyes set deep beneath heavy brow-ridges that shade against the high-altitude glare on those rare bright days when the cloud-deck falls away. The skin runs to a weathered umber, the lips the slight blue of people whose blood has learned to carry oxygen differently — not a sickness-blue but a working-blue, the color of a tool used well.

They move at what a lowlander would call half-speed. Watch a woman crossing the market: she does not dawdle, exactly, but each step is placed. Each gesture completes itself before the next begins. There is no economy of motion to learn here because the economy was learned a thousand years ago and is now simply the shape of being a person. They breathe between sentences. They breathe between thoughts. A conversation between two of them, to an outsider's ear, sounds like a conversation conducted underwater — the long pauses, the murmured cadence, the way a question can hang for half a minute before its answer arrives, and the answer when it arrives is exactly the right one because the half-minute was used.

They live long. Not preternaturally so — but the eighty-year-old here is still working, still hauling jars to the trolley, still walking the lanes at dusk. Something about the cold and the slowness and the mineral water preserves them. Their bones get heavier with age rather than lighter. By seventy a person here has begun, very slightly, to take on the density of the stone they have walked on all their lives. This is not metaphor. The undertakers know.

They are organized into households rather than families, and the households are organized around the springs. A household is not a bloodline but a custodianship: this household tends *these* three springs, monitors their breathing, knows their moods, reads their mineral content by smell. The custodianship passes from one generation to the next not by inheritance but by aptitude — children apprentice across households, and the one who shows the right ear for a particular spring's rhythm goes to that spring's keepers. A household may contain six adults and no children for a decade, then take in four apprentices at once when a neighboring spring's keeper grows too old. The arrangements are fluid and ancient and mutually understood.

Their craft is patience. They make almost nothing quickly. The ceramic jars that hold the mineral powders are fired for eleven days in slow kilns vented off the geothermal grid. The felted curtains in the doorways are felted by walking on them — laid out in the lanes, walked over by every passerby for a season, until they reach the right density. There is a kind of tea, brewed from the dwarf rhododendron-things, that requires a full day of attention to make a single pot, the leaves added in twelve stages at twelve temperatures, each measured by a tongue rather than a thermometer. Outsiders who try the tea and do not understand the day behind it taste only a mild astringency. Outsiders who understand taste something else.

Their language has the long vowels and soft consonants of a tongue shaped by thin air. It has the word for the col-wind's sustained note (it is *uremm*, and it is also the word for a particular kind of patience, and also the word for the sound a sleeping person makes who is about to speak in their sleep but does not). It has seven words for mist, distinguished by what the mist is doing: rising, settling, thinking, dispersing, *waiting*, arriving, leaving. The word for *waiting mist* is the one outsiders most often fail to learn. The townspeople can identify which one is present without looking out the window. They can feel it in the pressure behind the eyes.

They are not religious in any way a visitor would recognize, but they are observant. Before drawing from a spring, a small acknowledgment — a touch to the rim of the wellhead, two fingers, brief. Before entering a house not one's own, a pause in the doorway long enough to be noticed by whatever is inside, person or otherwise. Before sleep, a moment at the window facing the saddle, even when the saddle is full of waiting mist and there is nothing to see. These are not prayers. They are the small courtesies of a people who have understood, for longer than they can remember, that the plateau is occupied by more than its occupants.

## The Corvids

There are perhaps four hundred of them on the central plateau. They live in the rock-clefts, in the eaves of the older buildings, in the abandoned vent-shafts above the foundry. They are sooty all over except for a paler smudge at the throat, and the smudge is different on each bird — a smear, a band, a freckling — and this is how they know each other and how, eventually, the townspeople come to know them too.

They speak rarely. A bird that speaks does so perhaps a dozen times in a year, perhaps thirty times in a lifetime. The syllables are clean — not the slurred mimicry of a parrot — and they are appropriate. A corvid will not say a greeting to no one. A corvid that says *coming rain* will say it before the rain, not during it, and not when no rain is coming. The townspeople have learned not to ask the birds questions, because the birds will not answer questions; the birds only volunteer.

What they volunteer is sparse and often useful. *Closed* — said to a man approaching a shop on the day its keeper has gone to the springs. *Slow* — said in winter, often, to no one in particular, perhaps meaning the season, perhaps meaning the speaker. *Left* — said by an old corvid to a child whose grandmother had died that morning, before the child had been told. These are the stories the town keeps.

They are not loved, exactly. They steal. They are arrogant in the way of corvids everywhere. But they are *acknowledged*, the way one acknowledges an old neighbor whose presence is woven into the texture of the lane. A bird that has been around for thirty years will be greeted by name when it perches on a windowsill, and the greeting is sometimes returned. The names the birds have are not always names the townspeople gave them. Some of them seem to have given themselves names, and have communicated those names, and the names have entered the local lexicon. There is a bird called *Threnn* on the south slope, and no one knows what *Threnn* means, but everyone knows which bird is *Threnn*.

They live a long time. No one is certain how long. The oldest one anyone living can remember was already old when the current eldest townsperson was a child, and is still around — its throat-smudge has faded to nearly white, its flight has slowed, but it is still there, on the chimney of the mill-house, and it still occasionally says something, and what it says is increasingly cryptic and increasingly, in retrospect, correct.

## The Moths

The felted moths are the size of a thumb's last joint, plump and shaggy, with wings that look more like coat-fur than wings. They emerge at dusk from the cushion-plants where they have spent the day pressed flat against the warmth of the vegetation's slow respiration. They beat through the mist on heavy disordered flight-paths, navigating by something other than sight — perhaps the magnetic field, perhaps the breathing of the springs, perhaps the position of the people, since the moths often seem to navigate *toward* people rather than away, blundering into felted curtains and walls of houses with soft repeated thumps that the townspeople have stopped hearing.

They do not feed as adults. They live for two weeks, mate, and die in a slow dispersal across the cushion-plants, their bodies decomposing into the moss and fattening it. The cushion-plants, in some sense, are made of moths.

Every other year, in late summer, they migrate. The migration is not long — from one plateau to the next, perhaps thirty kilometers along the spine of the range — but it takes them six nights, and on those nights the air thickens with them, and the townspeople do not go out, not from fear but from courtesy: the moths' navigation, whatever it is, is disturbed by warm bodies in their path. The town goes quiet. The lamps are dimmed. The trolleys do not run. From inside the houses, behind the felted curtains, you can hear the soft mass of them passing — a sound like a continuous slow rain on a roof that does not get wet.

## The Pikas and the Smaller Lives

The pikas in the screes are unremarkable — small, gray, alert, present. They are mentioned because the townspeople watch them. A pika that vanishes from its accustomed scree is read as a sign. A pika that has taken to a new scree is read as another. The readings are not systematic, but they accumulate. A keeper of a spring will say, *the pikas above the third wellhead have moved down two terraces*, and another keeper will nod, and something will be adjusted in the refinery's schedule, and the adjustment will turn out to have been correct.

There are also things smaller than pikas. There is a colorless beetle that lives only in the lichen-felt and is found only when the felt is peeled back, which is rarely. There is a translucent slug, no longer than an eyelash, that grazes the cyanobacterial film after rain and leaves trails that catch the dim light in faint iridescence — the townspeople call these trails *script*, and the children are taught not to step on them, though no one claims the script means anything. There are mites in the moss that are entirely red. There are tardigrades, presumably, though no one here uses that word. The density of small life in any handful of cushion-plant, examined closely, exceeds what the visible plateau seems able to support.

## The Other Inhabitant

And then there is whatever lives underneath.

The townspeople do not have a name for it. They have words *around* it — the way one circles a thing in the dark with the hands rather than naming it. They speak of *the patience*, sometimes. They speak of *the listening*. The springs breathe; something breathes them. The mist rearranges the streets; something rearranges. When a person who has lived here forty years pauses at a corner and does not recognize it, the corner has not changed — but the relationship between the corner and the person has been, very slightly, attended to.

It is not malevolent. It is not benevolent. The townspeople are very careful, when speaking of it to outsiders, not to anthropomorphize, because the worst error a visitor can make is to imagine a person down there, a god or a beast or an intelligence in any sense the visitor would recognize. There is no person down there. There is something slower than person, something for which the entire human history of the town is roughly the length of an inhalation. It notices the town the way a sleeping body notices a fly that has landed on its arm — not as a fly, not as an intruder, just as a small warmth that is now there and was not there a moment ago, and the body adjusts its breathing very slightly, and the fly is included in the breathing.

This is what it is to live on the Cradle Plateaus. To be a small warmth that is now there. To be included in the breathing.

The corvids, the townspeople sometimes think, are closer to it than they are. The corvids have been here longer, in some sense — not as individual birds, but as a presence on the rock. The moths are closer still: the moths are made of the cushion-plants and the cushion-plants are made of the moths and the whole carpet of them is, in some way no one wishes to examine too directly, a single organ of the larger thing, an organ of perception perhaps, a way the slow patient awakeness below feels what is above.

The townspeople are not part of that organ. They are guests. They have built their houses on the back of a sleeping animal and the animal has, over centuries, come to accept the houses the way a body accepts a long-worn scar. The relationship is not affection. It is something more durable than affection. It is the kind of inclusion that does not require notice on either side, and does not require love, and persists.

When a townsperson dies, they are not buried — the rock is too thin over too much that is not rock. They are taken to one of the warm springs, the small ones, the ones not used for industry, and they are lowered in, and over a season the body dissolves into the mineral water and is taken down. The townspeople say, *gone to the patience*. They do not weep at these ceremonies. They breathe between sentences. They walk home slowly through the waiting mist. The corvids, sometimes, say a single syllable, and sometimes the syllable is the dead person's name, and sometimes it is not, and either way the household accepts it and goes inside, and the door's felted curtain falls closed behind them with the soft sound of a thing settling into the long shape it has always been settling into.
