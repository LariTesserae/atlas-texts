## Who Lives at Thallow Bend

### The Harvesters

They are mostly women, and mostly not old, though they age in a particular way here — the skin of their hands goes before the rest of them. They wade the braids in rubber waders patched with bicycle tube, canvas aprons belted high, and the long gloves that are the town's one non-negotiable expense. A harvester owns three pairs and guards them. The gloves are elbow-length, oiled, and checked each morning for pinholes against a lamp; a pinhole means a day of light duty at the sorting tables, which nobody wants because the sorting tables are where the older women are, and the older women talk.

They work bent. They work fast. They cut with a short curved knife and drop the stems into a wet-basket floated alongside them on a cork ring. They know which mats will hold their weight and which will sink, and they know this by a sense they cannot explain to newcomers, who therefore go through the mat and come up cursing within their first week. After that the newcomers either learn or leave.

A harvester at the end of a shift looks like she has been rained on from the inside. Her hair is plastered. Her forearms steam faintly in cold weather. She smells of peppery green and iron and the particular sulphur of the boilers. She will not take her gloves off until she is in her own kitchen with the basin already drawn.

### The Sorters

These are the older women, and a few of the older men whose backs have gone. They sit at long tin-topped tables under the one part of the Works with good skylights, and they pick through the cut greens by touch as much as sight — pulling out the bitter sorrel that belongs in a different batch, the snail, the water-beetle, the occasional tiny fish that came up in a cuff of cress and is already dead. They wear cotton gloves, thin ones, because the fine work cannot be done in the oiled leather. The cotton gloves are not enough. This is understood. This is the trade.

Their nails are the color of wet moss. The quicks glow faintly in a dark room, though none of them will admit to having checked this, and none of them will admit to knowing that the others have checked it too. Their hands are otherwise beautiful — the skin very soft from the constant damp, the knuckles unswollen, the veins prominent and a particular cool blue-green that is not quite the blue-green of other people's veins.

They talk all day over the clatter. They have developed, between them, a way of speaking that uses the four-second gap of the steam-press as a natural punctuation, so that a sentence finished just before the thud carries a different weight than one that finishes just after. Outsiders find their conversation oddly rhythmic and hard to follow. The sorters do not find it hard to follow. They are, in a sense, the only people in Thallow Bend who hear the Works fully, because they have made a language that fits inside it.

### Foreman Aldrick

Thirty-one years at the Works. He came as a boy of fourteen from somewhere east, with an accent he has mostly lost and a name he has entirely replaced. The tracery under the skin of his left forearm is his private weather vane: in March it flushes the green of new alder leaf, in August it fades to something like old bruise. He rolls that sleeve down in every season and the right one up in summer. He does not discuss it. When a new harvester, invariably a young one, works up the nerve to ask, he says *a long time ago I was careless* and turns back to whatever he was doing.

He runs the Works with a light hand and a loud voice. He is fair. He keeps a ledger in which he notes, in a private shorthand, the small injuries of his workers and which of them have been paid out for and which have not. He has never lost a worker to the boilers, though he has lost two to the river, one in his first year and one in his fifteenth, and he considers the second his personal failure.

He lives alone in a cottage with a kitchen garden gone to deliberate riot. He grows nothing edible. The company doctor, had he returned, might have been interested in this.

### The Engineer

There is one. His name is Pell and he came with the boilers when they were installed and he has stayed because leaving would mean someone else touching them. He sleeps in a cot behind the middle boiler when a seal is uncertain, which is often. He is deaf in the ordinary human register and has compensated by developing an uncanny ear for the mechanical one: he can tell from two rooms away which valve is about to complain. He speaks little and when he does it is at a shout, which the sorters find funny and the harvesters find endearing and his apprentice finds exhausting.

The apprentice is a girl of seventeen called Merrin who was pulled from the sorting tables after six months because she took a pressure gauge apart during her lunch and reassembled it correctly. She wears her hair short because of the machinery and she has begun to wear gloves even off-shift, which the older women notice and do not comment on, because there is nothing to say that she does not already know.

### The Trestle-men

Two of them. They live in a shack at the valley's lip, at the far end of the narrow-gauge, and they are the only residents of Thallow Bend who routinely see the fog on the other side. They maintain the trestle, load the cans onto the flatbeds the locomotive brings up, and hand them off to the crew that comes from the city and does not come down. The city crew wears respirators, which the trestle-men find offensive, and gloves of a heavier grade than the harvesters'. The trestle-men wear no gloves at all. They have been there a long time. Their hands look, from any distance, ordinary. Up close, in good light, the palms have the faint cross-hatched green of a leaf held up to the sun.

They are brothers, or say they are. Nobody in town remembers them arriving.

### The Children

There are not many. The cottages are soot-streaked but the windows are washed; behind each pane, usually, one or two small faces at dusk. The children are kept from the braids until they are old enough to understand the gloves, which in practice means about seven. Before that they play on the silt flats, which are the safest ground in the valley, being too raw for anything to have colonized yet. They build small unstable cities out of stones the river has not finished rounding and they abandon them when the sideways rain comes through and they build them again.

They are watched, always, by at least one heron. The herons have decided over the generations that children are neither food nor threat, and observe them with the same patient industrial indifference they show the pipeworks. The children have a rhyme about the herons that the adults pretend not to know. It ends *and one for the one who stands in your shadow when you're grown.*

### The Doctor, Kind Of

There is no doctor. There is Mother Esh, who is perhaps sixty and perhaps eighty, and who lives in the last cottage of the lower row. She sets bones, stitches cuts, pulls teeth, and delivers babies, of whom there are few but a steady few. She keeps a cabinet of tinctures, most of which she makes herself from the valley's plants, excluding the four commercial species, which she will not touch. She refuses payment and accepts gifts. Her own hands are wrapped in linen at all times, changed daily. Nobody has seen them bare in twenty years. She is not secretive about this — if asked directly, she will say *I rested them in the wrong place for too long, once* — but she is not forthcoming either.

She and Aldrick drink tea together on Sunday evenings and do not talk about their forearms.

### The Others

And then the rest, the accountants and the stokers and the carbide-man who tends the single lamp at the crossroads, and the old woman who cooks for the unmarried workers in the long shed behind the cottages, and the boy who runs messages between the Works and the trestle on a bicycle that is rusting faster than he can maintain it. They are ordinary. They are not, quite, ordinary. Each of them, if you caught them in the right light at the right hour — stepping from a hot bath, say, or reaching up to a high shelf so that a sleeve fell back — would show you some small tracery, some faint green thread, some single leaf-shaped mark at the inside of an elbow or the back of a knee. They do not discuss it with each other. They do not, particularly, discuss it with themselves.

They eat the cress. Of course they eat the cress. It is what there is. They eat it in soups and on bread and pickled in the winter, and they have eaten it, some of them, every day of their lives. Whatever the people in the cities are or are not noticing, the people of Thallow Bend have long since noticed and long since stopped.

### What Else Lives There

The herons, already mentioned, who are probably the oldest continuous residents — some of them were here before the river slipped, and remember, in whatever way a heron remembers, a different shape of water.

The water-voles in the warm ash, thriving and multiplying and developing, generation by generation, a slight tolerance for heat that their cousins downstream do not share.

The kingfishers, territorial and furious and gorgeous, one pair per braid, who have come to an arrangement with the harvesters wherein the harvesters do not cut within six feet of a nest-bank and the kingfishers do not dive on the harvesters' hats.

The frogs, uncountable.

And — this is the part the foreman would deny and the sorters would laugh at and Mother Esh would neither confirm nor deny — the cress itself, which is not quite a who but is no longer entirely a what. It has been pressed and boiled and canned and shipped for forty years. It has also been worn, in the lines of hands, and buried, in the graves of two harvesters and a trestle-man's wife, and eaten every day by a population that has never entirely left the valley. Something in it has had a long time to learn the shape of a person. Something in the people has had a long time to learn the shape of it.

In another hundred years, when the bowl is a meadow and the Works is a ruin in it, something will still be there. It will look, to a stranger passing through, like a particularly lush patch of green in a quiet field. It will not, quite, be only that.

For now: the cool damp, the loud machines, the sparse scatter of lit windows at dusk. Behind each window, a pair of hands being washed carefully in a basin. Under each pair of hands, in the faintest possible green, the map of a long slow arrangement between a town and the thing it harvests.
