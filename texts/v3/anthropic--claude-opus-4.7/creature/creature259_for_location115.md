Who lives here.

Begin with the pikas, because they were named first and because they are, in a certain sense, the valley's foundational animal — the one whose burrows organize the scree into neighborhoods. They are not solitary and not social; they are territorial the way the cushion plants are territorial, each holding a little radius of stone and calling across it. In late summer they cut grass and cure it in haypiles in the lee of overhangs, and these haypiles are one of the few signs in the valley that any creature plans for a future. A pika at the mouth of its burrow looks, from a few meters away, almost exactly like a wet rock with eyes. This resemblance is not accidental. It has been selected for over a long time by the lammergeier's shadow and the fox's patience.

The marmots are heavier citizens and have a different relationship to time. They sleep through more than half the year in burrow-systems that go deeper than you would think possible in this young rock — they find the soft places, the old fault-fills, the pockets of finer sediment between boulders, and they excavate there for generations. A marmot colony is older than any individual marmot. The whistling that crosses the valley is not just alarm; it is also announcement, location, census. When the wind is right you can hear three or four colonies holding a slow conversation across kilometers of empty air.

The ibex use the valley vertically in a way the other animals do not. Their territory is the cliff face and the ledge system, and they come down to the valley floor mostly at dawn, to drink from the seeps and lick at the mineral stains where iron and salt bleed out of the fresh rock. They move as if gravity were negotiable. The kids, born in late spring on ledges that seem geometrically impossible, learn the cliff before they learn anything else. An old ibex knows the face the way a pianist knows a keyboard — which holds will take weight in frost, which will not, which ledge widens around the corner, which narrows to nothing.

The fox is resident but thinly so. One fox, maybe two, working the whole valley. She is smaller than a lowland fox and greyer, almost the color of the wet scree, and she hunts by walking — a long patient transect along the edge of the talus, pausing at each burrow-mouth, reading the air. She does not chase. She arrives. Pikas that do not see her in time simply disappear, and the sharp bark that warned the colony came a second too late, and the colony recalibrates and the fox moves on.

The lynx — if it is a lynx — is a visitor rather than a resident, probably, working a range that includes this valley and three or four others and crossing the passes when the weather allows. You will not see it. The track in the mud at the seep is the whole of its presence, and even that will be gone by afternoon when the seep rises a centimeter and smooths the mud over.

The birds are their own population and operate on a different scale. The lammergeier's territory is measured in hundreds of square kilometers; this valley is one paragraph in its long reading of the range. It comes through to check the scree for the bones of winterkill, which it will carry up and drop onto flat rock to split. The choughs are more local, nesting in the cliff cracks, turning together in the thermals, raucous at close range and somehow dignified from far away. The snowcock live higher than almost anything else and descend only rarely; they are heard more than seen. And there are smaller birds — accentors, wheatears, a pair of wallcreepers working a damp face — that a patient observer will eventually notice but that do not announce themselves.

Under the rock, under the scree, in the saturated grit where the snowmelt pools beneath the surface, there are invertebrates nobody has catalogued. Springtails. Ice-adapted stoneflies whose larvae live in the interstitial water between cobbles. A spider, pale and long-legged, that hunts on the underside of wet stones. These are not charismatic and nobody counts them, but they are the substrate the pikas' ecosystem rests on, in the sense that the cushion plants feed on what the invertebrates decompose, and the pikas feed on the cushion plants, and the fox feeds on the pikas, and so on up to the lammergeier's shadow.

The cushion plants themselves are alive in a way that is easy to miss because they do not move. A single cushion may be a hundred years old. It grows a millimeter a year, or less, and it has negotiated its square of stone against wind and frost and grazing for longer than anyone currently breathing in the valley has been alive. Inside the cushion, out of the wind, the temperature is measurably warmer than the air above it, and small things shelter there — a beetle, a spider, a seedling of something else entirely that may or may not survive its first winter. The cushion is not just a plant. It is a small weather system, a microhabitat, a slow fortress.

And then there is what lives here that is not, strictly, alive.

The lichens, which are two organisms pretending to be one, and which are only just beginning to colonize the fresh scars on the cleaved rock. The cyanobacterial films on the damp undersides of stones, blue-black and slick, doing photosynthesis in light so dim it barely qualifies. The fungal threads running through the grit under the cushion plants, trading minerals for sugar in a commerce older than roots. These are the first settlers, the ones who arrive before soil because they are what soil will eventually be made of.

The valley, taken together, is not empty. It is sparsely populated at the visible scale and densely populated at every scale below that. The appearance of emptiness is a function of how far apart the large things are and how well the small things hide.

As for the humming on the other side of the ridge —

Nothing in the valley lives from it or toward it. No animal here has adapted to human presence, because there has been none to adapt to. The fox does not associate the sound with food; the ibex does not flinch at it; the marmots do not include it in their census of whistles. It passes through the valley the way a distant weather front passes, registered by the barometers of the living without being understood. If the humming grew louder, year by year, the animals that could leave would leave, and the ones that could not — the cushion plants, the lichens, the interstitial stoneflies — would simply persist or not persist according to what the new conditions allowed. They have no politics about it. They have only thresholds.

For now the thresholds hold. The valley is inhabited by what has always inhabited this kind of place: the small barking mammal of the scree, the heavy whistling mammal of the boulder field, the cliff-walking ungulate, the patient fox, the rumored cat, the soaring scavenger, the black flocking corvid, the hidden invertebrate, the slow cushion, the slower lichen, the slowest stone. Each of them at its own tempo. Each of them fitted to a valley that is not yet finished being a valley, and that will, in the long run, outlast all of them and be inhabited, ten thousand years from now, by some reconfigured version of the same assembly — different species perhaps, different proportions, but the same shape of life pressed into the same shape of place.

They live here. That is the answer. They live here in the particular way that things live in a place that has not decided what it will be: provisionally, attentively, and without leaving much of a mark.
