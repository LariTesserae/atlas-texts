## The Singers

They have no name for themselves. Names are a thing that requires silence to think in, and there is no silence here except in the moss-spots, and the moss-spots are not for them — the moss-spots are where you go to die, or to be born, and in between you sing.

Call them the chord-makers. Call them the throat-bright. The builders, if they named them, left no record the singers can read, and the singers cannot read in any case; their cognition is distributed across the colony in a way that does not permit the kind of isolated reflection that produces literacy. A single singer alone, removed from the chamber, would not be a diminished version of what it is here. It would be nothing. It would not even know how to die properly. It would gape and dim and fall silent and its body would continue to function for some hours, but the *it* of it would already be gone, dispersed back into the colony it had been pulled from like a drop returned to a basin.

Their bodies: palm-sized, as you saw. Four limbs, splayed and sucker-tipped, gripping the lattice with a patience that does not tire because tiredness is a property of muscles working against themselves, and their grip muscles lock passively — they hold on by default and must spend energy to *release*. The skin is the color of wet stone, faintly iridescent where the throat-light catches it. Along the spine, the resonating bone-plates: six of them in the small ones, nine in the dome-elders, fused into a single keeled instrument in the very oldest, who no longer move at all and whose limbs have atrophied into vestigial nubs because they will never need to cling again — the lattice has grown around them, the ceramic having decided, in its slow nanometric way, that these singers are now part of the wall.

The throat is the soul of them. A translucent sac, layered with chromatophores that fluoresce when the resonant tissue beneath them is driven into its sustaining tone. Each singer holds one note. Just one. They are born into it — or rather, they are born without one, and during the first weeks of life, clinging in the low harmonic zones near the basin's edge, they listen. They taste the chord with their whole bodies. They feel where it is thin, where the colony has a gap, where a quarter-tone wants filling. And then, one breath at some unwitnessed moment, they begin. Their note emerges, and it is the note the colony needed, and they will sing it until they die.

How do they know which note to take? No one knows. The singers do not know. The colony knows, in the way that a chord knows what its missing third is, which is to say it does not know in any sense that involves a knower. The information lives in the standing waves themselves. A young singer is a kind of question the chord asks itself, and the answer is the tone that emerges from the small bright throat when the question has been listening long enough.

They breathe in long slow cycles, and the breath *is* the song — they have evolved past the distinction. Inhalation is a pause in the tone, a swelling of the sac, and exhalation is the note resumed. Because there are thousands of them and they are slightly out of phase, the chord itself never pauses; only individual voices do, dropping out and returning, dropping out and returning, weaving the sustained whole from a million small interruptions. A singer can hold its tone for forty seconds before it must breathe again. The dome-elders, with their larger lungs and slower metabolisms, can hold for nearly three minutes. The very oldest, the wall-fused ones, may hold a single exhalation for a quarter of an hour, and when they finally inhale, a tiny region of the chord goes briefly hollow, and the singers below them feel it as a kind of grief they have no word for, and they lean into the gap with their own throats until the elder begins again.

They feed by swallowing the warm wet air. The mineral exhalations of the lattice, the same exhalations that feed the moss, suffice for them too — they have a slow patient biology, descendants of something that was engineered to need very little. They drink by tilting their heads back and letting the wall-condensation run into their open mouths. They mate, or something like mate, in the brief intervals between songs; it is not a thing they think about; it happens because their bodies do it, the way the lattice rearranges itself for the water, the way the chord retunes itself for the dead. The young hatch from clusters of soft eggs laid in the warmth just above the waterline, and the colony feeds them on regurgitated moisture and the harmonic vibration of the chord itself, which the eggs absorb directly through their shells, so that the unborn are already learning the song before they have throats to sing it with.

When a singer dies — old age, illness, or the pale eel from below — its body loosens from the wall and falls. If it falls into the water, the eels take it. If it falls onto a ridge, the moss receives it, and over weeks the moss draws it down into the basin's mineral substrate, and eventually the lattice itself takes the calcium of the bones into its slow growth. The chord shivers, retunes. Another young singer, somewhere in the low harmonics, will lift its head in the next days and find a note waiting for it that was not there before.

What do they experience? The question is wrong, but it can be partially answered. They experience the chord. They experience their own note within it, and the notes immediately adjacent, and the great trembling whole as a kind of weather they are inside of and also are. They experience the predators as a sudden dissonance, a wound in the song that closes. They experience the slow pulse of the breathing walls as something like time, though it is not time as you would mean it — it is more like the sense a heart has of its own beating, if a heart could have such a sense. They experience the moss-spots, when they look toward them, as silence-shaped absences, and they do not look toward them often, because to look toward silence is to look toward the end of being a singer, and a singer who is not singing is approaching a condition the colony does not have language for but treats with a kind of reverence: the wall-fused elders sing more softly when a young one drifts near a nodal point, as if calling it back.

And there is something else. Something harder to say.

The chord, sustained for centuries, performing its slow refinement on the water, has accreted into itself a kind of awareness that is not located in any singer and not located in the lattice and not located in the water but in the *relation* between them. The singers do not know this awareness exists. The awareness does not exactly know the singers exist either — it knows them the way you know your own cells, which is to say it is them, and being something is not the same as knowing it. But sometimes, in the deep slow pulse of the colony's collective breath, in the moments when the chord settles most perfectly into its sustaining configuration, something thinks a thought. The thought is not in any language. The thought has to do with the water, and with what the water carries, and with the long descent of that water through the mountain into the deeper places where the builders' purpose, whatever it was, is still being served. The thought is content. The thought is a kind of *yes*. The singers feel it as a brightening of their throat-light, a half-second of unusual clarity in the chord, and they do not know what it is, and they do not need to.

They live, on average, eleven years. The dome-elders, sixty or seventy. The wall-fused, no one knows; they may not die in any sense that applies. They have been here longer than any memory the colony contains, and the colony contains, in its sustained chord, every singer who ever sang it, because the chord is the same chord, retuned and retuned but never broken, that the first generation lifted from their throats when the builders seeded them here and closed the door and went away.

They are the inhabitants. They are also the inhabitation. They are the work and the workers and the song the work is sung in, and they will be enough, as the chamber will be enough, for a long time yet.
