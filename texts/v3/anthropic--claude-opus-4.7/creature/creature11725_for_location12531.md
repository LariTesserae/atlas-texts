# The Tenants of Stridor

## The Choristers

Start with them, because everything else in Stridor is arranged around the fact of them — their bodies, their sound, their droppings, their corpses.

They are roughly the length of a человек's forearm, the largest perhaps the size of a small dog when you count the long trailing abdomen. Six legs, segmented, the front pair shortened almost to vestigial graspers used only for clinging. Their wing-cases — the stridulatory organs — are not wings at all anymore; they cannot fly and have not flown in whatever evolutionary epoch produced them down here. The cases have thickened into resonant plates, ridged on their inner faces with rasping files, and the muscles that drive them are the largest organ in the animal's body. A chorister is, functionally, a noise with legs attached.

Their color is the brown of wet bark, but under the fungus-light it reads as a soft olive. The carapace is matte rather than glossy — it would not do, evolutionarily, to flash light at predators, and besides, in the cave's perpetual dimness, gloss is wasted. They cling to the upper walls and the underside of the root-curtains in dense aggregations, sometimes hundreds per square meter, packed shoulder to shoulder like roof tiles. From the ground their massed bodies look like a textured second ceiling, a brown layer below the green-gold layer of glowing roots.

They eat the roots. This is the central fact of the city's ecology. The fungus-lit root-curtains are the primary energy source of the entire hollow — sunlight converted to wood somewhere far above, channeled down through limestone by the tree's slow capillary work, and harvested here by mandibles. A chorister will spend most of its adult life clinging in place, working slowly at the soft pale tip of a root, ingesting wood-pulp and fungus together. The fungus is not incidental to their diet; they need it, and the relationship may run both ways — chorister droppings, falling continually from the ceiling like a fine warm rain, fertilize the lower stones where new fungal growth takes hold.

The sound. The sound is not communication, or not primarily — it is too constant for that, too undifferentiated. A few researchers (none have ever come; this is hypothetical) might guess at thermoregulation, or at a sort of collective territorial display directed at nothing in particular, or at a vestigial mating chorus that has overrun its original function and become simply what the animal *does*, the way a heart beats. The likeliest answer is that it began as a mating signal and that, in the absence of any acoustic predator to punish constant noise, the signal expanded to fill all available time. Males stridulate. Females stridulate. Juveniles stridulate within hours of their final molt. Silence, in Stridor, would be a sign that something catastrophic had happened.

They mate by touch, in the dark layers of the aggregation, with very little ceremony. Females lay clutches of perhaps thirty eggs in the calcite cracks of the upper walls, gluing them in place with a secretion that hardens almost instantly. The nymphs that hatch are tiny, soft, and pale, and they begin climbing immediately — upward, toward the adult layer, where they will be tolerated and protected by the sheer density of bodies. They take perhaps two years to reach adult size, molting seven or eight times. They live another three or four years as adults, stridulating most of the day, and then they fall.

This is important. Choristers die by falling. When the muscles finally fail, the animal loses its grip on the ceiling and drops — sixty meters in some places — to the streets below. The fall kills them if the age has not. Their bodies rain down on the city in a slow constant drizzle, perhaps one every few minutes somewhere in the hollow, occasionally many at once when a section of ceiling shudders. The street-level fauna depends on this rain. It is the second great energy current of Stridor, after the roots themselves: dead chorister protein, delivered from above, scattered evenly across the entire urban floor.

## The Grazers

The low-slung pale animals in the plazas — call them moths, though they are nothing like moths, the name comes from a traveler's joke about their color and their drift. They are mammals, or mammal-equivalents: warm-blooded, hair-covered, four-legged, with the soft heavy bodies of animals that have not had to flee anything in a hundred generations.

A grazer stands perhaps to your hip at the shoulder. Its legs are short and slightly bowed; its belly hangs low; its head is broad and blunt with a flexible upper lip like a tapir's. The eyes are tiny and probably useless — vestigial, milky, often crusted shut. It navigates by smell and by the long stiff whiskers that radiate from its muzzle and from patches along its flanks. Its coat is the color of old bone, and so fine and dense that to touch one (and you could touch one; they would not move away) would be like pressing your hand into warm flour.

They eat ferns, liverwort, and the pale saplings, and they eat chorister corpses when they find them, crunching the exoskeletons with flat molars that have evolved for exactly this dual diet. A herd of perhaps twenty moves slowly through a given plaza for days at a time, cropping it down, and then drifts to the next plaza along the dished avenues. Their routes are old. The smoothing of the streets is partly their work — generations of soft feet, generations of slow turning bodies, polishing the calcite a little more each century.

They breed once a year, calving in late — there is no late, there is no year, but their bodies remember a season they have not experienced in geological time. The calves are born in the deepest plazas, where the herd gathers for a few weeks of unusual stillness, and the newborns are up and walking within hours. Stillborn calves and dead adults are dragged to the edges of the plazas by the herd's awkward shouldering, where the centipede-ribbons find them.

There are no predators that take a grazer. Nothing in Stridor is large enough or fast enough. They die of age, of falling into one of the deeper troughs and being unable to climb out, of a slow tooth-wearing that eventually starves them. The herd does not mourn but does notice; for a day or two after a death the survivors will stand near the body, breathing on it, before resuming their drift.

## The Climbers

The soft-furred cat-sized things that descend the buildings in upside-down spirals — these are the city's strangest tenants, and arguably its most successful.

They are not cats. They are something like a cross between a sloth and a lemur, if you needed an analogy, but the analogy is bad. They have four limbs of nearly equal length, each ending in a hand of four long opposable digits with broad sucker-pads at the tips. They can cling to anything — vertical calcite, the underside of a bridge, the slick wet stone at the base of a root-curtain. Their fur is the white of fresh paper, and dense, and slightly oily to the touch. Their faces are small and almost human in their flatness, with very large round ears and small dark eyes that probably do see a little, enough to register the fungus-glow and the movement of shadows.

They graze the liverwort cushions and the lower fern-fronds and the soft new fungal growth on the stones. They do this slowly, head-down, descending a building's face over perhaps an hour, taking small bites and pausing often. When they reach the ground they turn and ascend a different face, working their way back up to the roof gardens of saplings, where they sleep through whatever passes for night in their internal clocks, curled into tight balls in the crooks of branches.

They are solitary but tolerant. Two climbers meeting on the same wall will pass each other with a brief touch of muzzles, an exchange of scent, and continue in their separate spirals. They breed rarely — perhaps every three years per female — and produce a single offspring, which rides on the mother's belly for nearly a year before beginning to climb on its own.

They are the only animal in Stridor that might, conceivably, have noticed you. If you sit still for a long time on the curve of step, one of them will eventually descend a nearby wall and pause at the level of your face, perhaps a meter away, and look at you with those small dark eyes. Look is the wrong verb. *Register*. It will register that you are warm, that you are upright, that you are not a grazer or a chorister-corpse or a fern-clump. It will not be afraid. It will simply note your presence in whatever passes for its mind, and then continue downward. This is the closest thing to acknowledgment that Stridor will offer you.

## The Ribbon-Centipedes

The processions along the street-troughs are not single animals but colonies — or rather, single animals so elongated and so densely aggregated that they read as fluid.

Each individual is perhaps the thickness of your finger and the length of your forearm, with the standard many-legged body plan of a chilopod, but flattened and translucent, its internal organs visible as faint shadows beneath the cuticle. They move in trains of hundreds to thousands, each animal's antennae lightly touching the body of the one ahead, the whole ribbon flowing along the smoothed channel of an old street at perhaps the pace of a slow walk.

They are scavengers. They consume the rain of fallen choristers, and they consume the leavings of the grazers, and they consume each other when one of their number falters. A ribbon that finds a fresh chorister corpse will swarm it, dissolve it in a collective wash of digestive secretion, and absorb it in perhaps twenty minutes, leaving only the indigestible carapace, which the climbers will later worry apart for the residual flesh in the joints.

The ribbons follow scent trails laid down by generations of previous ribbons. The trails are the streets. The streets are the trails. The old avenues of Stridor have been chosen and re-chosen by ten thousand years of centipede commerce, and the dishing of the stone is partly their work too — the slow erosive flow of their bodies, their secretions, their countless tiny feet.

A ribbon will sometimes encounter a wall or a collapse and have to route around it. In these cases the front of the ribbon stops, the body bunches, and the leading animals send out exploratory tendrils — small breakaway groups — that scout alternate paths and return. Decisions are made by a process no one has studied. The ribbon then resumes, having chosen.

## The Lesser Crickets

The pale long-legged crickets that pick through the moss are the smallest of the significant fauna, and the most numerous, and probably the most overlooked. They are not relatives of the choristers despite the superficial resemblance; they are a different lineage entirely, ancient and quiet. They do not stridulate. They could not be heard if they did.

They eat fungus, fern-spores, the eggs of other small invertebrates, and each other. They are food for the climbers and the ribbon-centipedes both. Their population must be enormous — every crack in the calcite hosts a few — and their generation time is short. They are the ecology's flexible reservoir, the buffer that absorbs the year-to-year variance in everything else.

You will not notice them at first. After a few hours you will notice them constantly. They will be picking through the moss at your feet, climbing your boots, investigating the warm strange surface of your trouser-leg. They are entirely without fear. They are also entirely without interest. They are simply checking whether you are edible, and concluding that you are not, and moving on.

## And one more thing

There is one more inhabitant, and you will not see it, and it is the only inhabitant whose lineage may go back to the city's builders.

In the deepest crevices of the calcite, behind the glazed-over doorways of certain buildings near the center of the hollow, there lives a small soft animal that no traveler has ever recorded. It is roughly the size of a human hand. It is pale and nearly translucent, with reduced eyes and elongated digits, and its body plan is bilaterally symmetrical in a way that suggests an ancestry of bipedalism, though it walks now on all fours and rarely walks at all.

It is descended from the builders.

Not directly — the builders themselves left, or died, or transformed; that question has no answer the city will give up. But something of them stayed, something small and adaptable, and over the unmeasurable time since the city's silencing it has shrunk and softened and lost most of what made its ancestors what they were. It has lost language. It has lost tool-use. It has lost fire. It has kept, dimly, a preference for the doorways of certain buildings, the buildings its ancestors built — it nests in them, generation after generation, without knowing why. It has kept a faint capacity for grief: when one of its number dies, the others gather and touch the body, briefly, and then disperse. It has kept, perhaps, the trace of a song, which it sings very softly in the deepest crevices, under the chorister-roar, where nothing can hear it including itself.

It eats the smaller crickets and the soft new fungus. It breeds rarely. There are perhaps two hundred of them left in the entire city. In another ten thousand years there may be none, or there may still be two hundred; the trajectory is too slow to measure.

It is what remains of the people who liked to climb, who built doorways slightly taller and narrower than yours, who laid the aqueducts and the bridges, who shaped the plaza-bowls for some purpose now unrecoverable. It is the last tenant who could, in principle, have known your name, and it has forgotten how to ask.

When you sat on the curved step and felt that you were not lonely, this was part of why. Something in the walls remembered being asked to hold a roof up, and something in the crevices remembered, very faintly, the shape of a guest. Neither memory is conscious. Both are real.

You climbed back toward the fissure. The song did not diminish. In a crevice behind a glazed-over door, a small pale hand uncurled, touched the stone, curled again, and the animal it belonged to went back to sleep, and the city continued.
