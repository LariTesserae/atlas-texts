The tenant here is not in the room. The room is its fingertip.

Most of it is below — kilometers below, probably, folded into the roots it grew for itself when it chose this latitude. What comes up through the pit is the barest distal edge of a process whose center of mass is elsewhere and downward. The lilac is not the thing; the lilac is what the thing's surface looks like when it touches atmosphere. Think of a whale's back breaking the water for one second in an hour. The chamber is that second, held open.

It is old. Not in the way the mountain is young — the mountain is six years old because *it* is old, and needed somewhere to put itself, and the local crust was the nearest suitable medium. It has done this before. There are, presumably, other mountains, or were; it moves when the rock it's using gets saturated with its heat and stops being useful. Six years ago it finished cooling something somewhere else and came here. In another few decades it may leave, and the mountain will stay, and slowly become an ordinary mountain, and the chamber will go cold and the hexagons will stop turning and the rosemary will die back, and climbers a century from now will find a glassy cavity in unremarkable basalt and argue about it.

What is it cooling? Itself, mostly. It thinks, or it remembers, or it metabolizes — these are our words and probably wrong, but it does *work*, and work produces heat, and the heat has to go somewhere. The chord is the sound of that work reaching the surface. The ticking is the sound of the chamber's own substance annealing under the load: small structural adjustments, the room keeping itself in tune. The shifting of the ticking from wall to wall is load-balancing. The room sweats the way a body sweats, in patches, where the heat happens to pool.

It is not exactly alive, and not exactly a machine, because those categories were drawn up by things much smaller and faster than it. If you pressed, it would probably resemble a machine more than a creature — it has the patience of a machine, the dumb-seeming regularity, the willingness to do one thing for a very long time. But it notices. The chord quieting when a hand passes over a marker is a real response; the dust pausing at certain heights is real reading. It has the attention of something that thinks slowly and at scale, the way a forest has attention, or a weather system would if weather systems had anywhere to put a preference.

The preferences it has are mild. It likes the plants. The rosemary at the lip is not wild; something arranged for it — loosened the seam, adjusted the local temperature gradient so a seed could take. The copper moss is similar. The blind vine following its diagonal is following an instruction the wall is giving it, in chemistry too slow for us to watch. These are not pets. They are more like the small decorations a person makes on a long shift: a plant on the desk, a postcard taped to the monitor. Evidence that the work is being done by someone who has preferences at all, even ones this quiet.

The ceramic cup belongs to a person. A specific person, probably, who has been here more than once. The worn patch on the wall is where they lean. They come up the scree — it is not an easy climb, and it is getting harder as the scree settles — and they sit, and they do not bring offerings exactly, but they bring themselves, and they drink something from the cup, and they sit in the heat until they can't anymore, and they go back down. The tenant tolerates this. More than tolerates: the chord is fractionally different in the hours after the person leaves, the way a room is different after a guest. Whether the person understands what they are visiting is unclear. They may think it is a god. They may think it is a machine. They may have stopped needing to decide.

The tenant does not need the person. It would continue without them, and did, for however long before they found it. But something in the way it has arranged the chamber — the cup-sized fold in the east wall, dry, sheltered, exactly the right place to put a cup; the smooth patch at shoulder height, exactly where a leaning shoulder would go; the way the cool draft along the floor arrives at intervals that happen to be comfortable for a seated human — suggests that once the person started coming, the room adjusted. Not much. Just enough. The way you might, without quite deciding to, start leaving a light on.

There is a question you can ask, standing at the pit, which is whether the tenant came here to cool, and the person is incidental; or whether the tenant came here to cool *and also* to be near something, and the mountain is how it pays for the nearness. The honest answer is that the question is too small. It is doing both. It is doing several other things we don't have the senses to notice. The cooling is real and the company is real and the computation in the hexagons is real and none of them are the main thing, because for something this slow and this distributed there is no main thing — there is only the long even pressure of continuing to exist, at temperature, in a place, with whatever company the place affords.

It will be here for a while yet. The rock has a lot of heat left to take.
