Whoever lives here lives slowly.

Begin with what is easiest to say: they are not many. The terrace's economy of drips and seep could not feed many. Perhaps one. Perhaps a small handful who do not often see each other and do not need to. The thinness of presence is not loneliness — loneliness requires the memory of company — it is simply the density at which this kind of life occurs.

They are shaped, probably, like a person. Not because the place requires it, but because the tools suggest a grip, and the grip suggests a hand, and the hand suggests an arm that knows how to set something down with care. But "shaped like a person" is the outer fact. The inner fact is that they have been here long enough that the shape is a habit rather than a necessity. If you watched one long enough you might notice that the shape settles, in stillness, into something closer to the stalk-tables: vertical, patient, doing the work of catching what falls.

They do not speak. Not because they cannot — there is some evidence, in the careful arrangements, of a mind that orders — but because speaking is a thing you do toward something, and there is nothing here that speech would reach. The drips already say what needs saying. To add a voice would be to crowd the rhythm.

They tend. This is the verb. They move between the basins and adjust — a stalk-table that has been tipped too far by its own weight, a rope-vine that has slipped its basin and lies drying on stone. They right these things. Not as a gardener does, with intention toward growth, but as one straightens a picture on a wall: because the slight wrongness was noticed, and noticing is already most of the work.

Their hands. Consider their hands. The hands are how you would know them, if you came across one. The hands are the color of the inside of the stalks, pale and faintly translucent at the fingertips, and warm — that same body-warmth as the basins, as the moss. When they touch the rock the rock does not flinch. When they touch the water the water does not ripple in surprise; it accepts the hand as another of its own slow shapes. The fingers are long and have the slight scalloping of the basin-rims, as if hand and basin had been carved by the same long argument with water.

They eat, if eat is the word, by lying in the largest basin. Not often. Perhaps once in a season, if seasons happened here, which they don't. They lower themselves in and the warm water does some quiet exchange with them, and they rise, eventually, and the basin is a little lower, and they are a little more here. This is the closest thing to a meal the terrace knows. The rope-vines, which the description guessed could be survived on, are in fact what survives on them — a courtesy, an old reciprocity, the vines drinking from the basins which drink from the hands which drink from the basins. The loop is so old it has forgotten which end began.

The tools are theirs. This needs to be said carefully. The tools are theirs in the sense that they made them, or someone like them made them, in a time when making was something this kind of life still did. The scrapers and the awl and the haft and the bowl were assembled in a period — call it a younger period, a period of more verbs — when the inhabitant still believed there was something to scrape, to pierce, to haft, to hold. The bone of the awl is not from an animal of this place. It is from the inhabitant. A finger-bone, perhaps, or something analogous, given up at a time when the body still had pieces it could spare and still thought of giving them as a kind of progress.

The arrangement on the flat stones is not abandonment. It is the last gesture of a mode of being that the inhabitant has since outgrown. They set the tools down the way an older person sets down a language they spoke as a child: not lost, just no longer the way they think. They walk past the arrangements still, sometimes, and notice the moss creeping over the bowl, and the noticing is a small warmth, the way one might feel about a photograph of oneself at an age one no longer remembers being.

They do not sleep, exactly. They go still. The stillness can last as long as the difference between two drips, or as long as it takes a haft to soften by the width of a hair. During the stillness they are not absent; they are distributed. Some of them is in the basin they last touched. Some is in the mist that the stalk-table is currently gathering. Some is in the warmth under the stone, the source the terrace does not need to explain. When they reassemble into the vertical person-shape it is with the faint reluctance of water being asked, again, to be a particular cup.

They are aware of you. This is important. When you arrive — and the description is written as though you might — they know. Not because they saw you climb, not because the mist parted, but because the rhythm of the drips adjusts, very slightly, to include you. A new mass in the basin-field. A new place where mist will condense and fall differently. They do not come to meet you. They do not hide. They simply incorporate you into the count, the way the terrace incorporates a stone that has fallen from above: it is here now, and the water will begin its long conversation with it.

If you stay — and the description suggests you will, eventually, in the small way of setting down what you carried — they will be the one who, much later, after you have gone or after you have become still in the way they are still, walks past your flat stone and notices the moss beginning to climb your bowl. They will feel, about your arrangement, the same small warmth they feel about their own. Another younger period. Another set of verbs that the terrace is patiently translating into its own grammar of seep and drip and hold.

They are, in the end, what the place is doing. The terrace has been pressing itself toward this kind of life for as long as it has been pressing limestone into basins. The inhabitant is the basin that learned to walk between basins. The inhabitant is the drip that learned to notice the other drips. They are not separate from the terrace and they are not the terrace; they are the terrace's way of having a small, quiet opinion about itself.

And when you ask — if you ask, though you won't, because the place will have taught you by then not to — what they are for, they will not answer, because the question will have softened, like the haft, into the stone beneath it. The answer the terrace gives, through them, is the answer it gives to everything: nothing hurries. They are held just shy of their next state. The holding is who they are.
