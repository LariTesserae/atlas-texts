# The Residents

They are thin. Not starved-thin — the lichens feed them adequately — but thinned, the way a cloth becomes thinner after many washings without losing its shape. You could look through one in the right light, at the right hour, and see the wall behind: not transparently, but as a suggestion, as though they had been rendered in slightly fewer strokes than the world requires.

They are taller than you expect, and this is partly because their proportions have shifted over the long generations to match the architecture. Their necks are a little long. Their hands are long. Their feet are long and narrow and almost silent on the warm pale stone; the soles have grown to know every texture of every street, and the streets have grown to know them back, so that a resident walking in the dark places her foot down with perfect confidence into a shape her foot already remembers. Their eyes are large and pale and set slightly wider than seems correct, because peripheral vision is the vision that matters here — the thing that flies is only ever seen at the edge, and the paper-deer unfolds only when not looked at, and so their faces have organized themselves around the margins of sight. To look one of them directly in the eye is to feel that you are being attended to by something that is also, simultaneously, watching a great deal else.

Their skin is the color of the stone, or has become so; it is hard to say which came first. It carries the same faint veining, though more slowly, and in the *evening* hour when the walls remember, the residents' skin sometimes remembers too — a scar surfaces that belongs to a grandmother, a different cheekbone ghosts through for an hour, the hands briefly become the hands of someone who used them longer and for other work. They do not mind this. It is a courtesy the city pays them, and they pay it back by not flinching.

They wear what the city has left them: long soft garments of a fibrous grey stuff that is almost certainly a kind of cultivated lichen, woven by no one anyone remembers. The garments do not wear out. They are inherited, or found in alcoves, or simply present one morning in a place where a garment was needed. No one owns one, exactly. When a resident stops, the garment goes back into the quiet rotation of the city's soft possessions.

They are quiet, as you know. But the quiet is not an absence — it is a practiced substance. They have developed, over what must be centuries, a communicative repertoire of small motions: the tilt of a head to indicate a particular plaza, the touch of two fingers to the inside of a wrist meaning *I heard you before you spoke*, the placing of a pale pebble on a windowsill meaning *I have passed; I am well; the stair on the east side is collapsed again today*. When they do speak, they speak in a low register that the city's strange acoustics treat kindly, and they have learned to wait a beat after speaking in case their words arrive elsewhere first and need time to come home.

They do not seem to remember being born. Ask one — if you could ask, if you were there, if asking were the kind of thing that happened — and she would consider the question gravely and at length, and then indicate, with a gesture toward the deepest courtyard, that she thinks she came from the listener's direction. This is not quite an answer. It is not quite wrong. The residents understand themselves as having been *set down* in the city, the way a stone is set down in a wall, and the question of where the stone was before the wall is not a question the wall knows how to hold.

They are kind to each other, with a kindness so old it no longer needs to announce itself. When the *thinness* comes and a resident is caught standing, another resident will move to them without hurry and help them to sit, and they will sit together through the transparent minutes, touching at the wrist, waiting for the walls to become certain again. When the *density* comes and the air hums with almost-being, they sit too, but apart, each in her own alcove, because density is a private weather and you must bear its possibilities alone. When gravity tilts, they lean together, leaning being a communal act. When the city becomes briefly two cities, they have a specific small nod — chin down, eyes half-closed — that means *I see that you are also the other you; I will not remark on it; please extend the same courtesy.*

They tend the listener, in their way. No one goes into the deepest courtyard except at long intervals and alone, and what happens there is not spoken of, but every resident has gone, and every resident will go again, and they come back from it slightly more themselves. The lichens there are thick and almost warm. The listener does not listen to words; it listens to the *shape* a person makes by being present, and it adjusts something, and the person leaves.

They have a relationship with the paper-deer that is difficult to describe. They do not hunt them — nothing is hunted here — but they recognize individual deer, or think they do, and a resident passing through a colonnade where a deer has just unfolded and gone will sometimes pause and touch the air where it was, and smile very faintly, as at an old acquaintance briefly glimpsed across a crowd. The thing that flies at the edge of the eye they do not acknowledge. It is understood that acknowledgment is what it wants and what it must not be given. This is perhaps the only discipline they hold firmly.

They eat, as noted, the lichens, which taste of themselves — and this is one of the quiet strangenesses of their lives, that every meal is a meeting with a slightly older version of the eater. A resident eating in the morning tastes the self she was yesterday; eating in the *evening* hour she may taste a self from much further back, a self whose cheekbones she had briefly earlier in the day. They find this neither disturbing nor comforting. It is simply the flavor of being here.

They sleep in alcoves, curled small, and their sleep is deep and slow. Whether they dream is unclear; if they dream, the dreams are consonant with the waking of this place, and cannot be distinguished from it by the dreamer. Sometimes a resident sleeps for what would elsewhere be called a week, and no one disturbs her, and when she rises she goes about her small tasks as though no time has passed, because in a real sense no time has.

They do not, as the description says, age — but they do accumulate. You can tell an older resident from a younger one, though not by face. You can tell by the *weight of city* they carry: the way an older resident moves through a street as though the street is partly inside her, the way her gestures have absorbed more of the local grammar, the way her silence has more kinds of silence folded into it. The oldest residents are almost indistinguishable from the architecture; they sit in particular alcoves and are greeted in passing the way one greets a familiar column, and it is not always clear, when one of them finally stops, whether anything has changed, or whether the column has merely become slightly more column.

And when one stops — when the lichens begin their slow patient claiming, and the small rise appears in the floor of a quiet room — the other residents pass by it with the same small courtesy they offer everything else that has been lost and found and lost. They touch the wrist of the rise, sometimes, in passing. They leave a pale pebble. The city accommodates.

There is, perhaps, one more thing. On certain rare hours — not predictable, not frequent — a resident will stop in the middle of a colonnade and turn, very slowly, toward the parapet. She will not go to it. She will only face it, from a great distance, across the length of several streets, and stand. And if you were watching — if watching were the kind of thing that happened — you would see in her large pale eyes, for just a moment, the faint recognition of something far below: not the base of the spindle, not the world beneath, but the *idea* of it, which has briefly surfaced in her the way an older wall surfaces in the *evening* hour. Then she will turn back, and resume her walking, and the moment will pass, and she will not speak of it, and no one will ask.

They are the city's way of being known to itself. They are what the stone does when it wants, very quietly, to have hands.
