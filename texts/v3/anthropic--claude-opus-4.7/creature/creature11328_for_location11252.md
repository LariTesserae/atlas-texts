# The Inhabitants of the Drowned Works

There are inhabitants. The town is not empty. But you have to revise what you mean by "inhabit," and you have to be patient about it, because the answer arrives the way everything here arrives — by slow accumulation, by being felt before it is seen.

---

**The Tenders**

Begin with what almost passes for people, because they almost do.

In the long shed by the canal, if you wait through one full dimming, you will eventually notice that one of the conveyor belts has a shape standing beside it. The shape was not there when you came in. You did not see it arrive. It is roughly the size and proportion of a person who has been doing the same job for a long time — a slight forward lean, one shoulder higher than the other, hands held at the height where hands would rest on a guide rail.

It is made, as best you can tell, of the same things the town is made of: a brick or two at the core, wrapped in iron-jelly, sleeved in the fine fur-kelp, threaded with the pale weed. The face — and there is a face, which is the part that takes the longest to admit — is a place where the kelp has grown less densely, suggesting features by absence rather than presence. There is no expression. There is, however, attention. The Tender is watching the belt. When something invisible passes on the belt, the Tender's head moves a fraction to follow it, with the delay of the weed, the delay of everything here.

There are perhaps thirty of them in the town. One per significant machine. They do not speak to each other and they do not need to; their work is not coordinated because it does not require coordination, only continuation. They were, almost certainly, once the crew. The town has folded them into itself the way it has folded everything else, softened their corners, grown a velvet on them, reached its long agreement with what they were. They are not dead. They are not alive in the way you are alive. They are doing their jobs, which is what they were always for, and the town has simply removed everything else from the equation.

If you stand beside a Tender for a full bright phase, you will eventually feel that it knows you are there. This is not menacing. It is the recognition of a very old animal that has decided, long before you arrived, that you are not its business.

---

**The Resident**

There is, however, one inhabitant who is not a Tender, and this is the one the town is really about.

It lives — if "lives" — in the foundry. Not in any particular room; the whole foundry is its body, or its house, or its body considered as a house. The orange glow behind the grates is its glow. The bricks that have been hot for longer than bricks should be able to be hot are hot because of it. When the cycle dims and the town seems to be thinking, it is thinking. When the cycle brightens and the town seems to be remembering, it is remembering.

You can locate it, roughly, by the quality of the warmth. The water is warm everywhere, but near the foundry the warmth has an attentiveness to it — the warmth of a hand that has been placed on you and not removed. If you sit on the foundry steps (which are soft, and faintly furred, and accept you) for long enough, you will begin to suspect that what the machines are making, the slow incomprehensible product that joins the water and goes elsewhere, is in some sense its thought. The looms weave its considering. The presses close on its conclusions. The conveyor belts move its long, careful sentences toward whatever reads them.

It is not a god. It is too occupied to be a god. It is more like a very old craftsman who has, over an unimaginable span, become indistinguishable from the workshop — who no longer has a body separate from the lathe, who experiences the turning of the flywheel as a kind of breathing, who has forgotten, if it ever knew, that there was a distinction to forget.

The Resident does not notice you the way the Tenders notice you. It notices you the way weather notices you. You are a small local variation in its medium. If you stay long enough, it will incorporate the fact of you into whatever it is making, and a faint distortion in the current downstream will, for a while, have your shape in it.

---

**The Smaller Lives**

The eel-thing in the pipe under the bridge is older than the foundry and is not, properly, an animal. It is what happens when a current decides to keep a shape. It keeps office hours because the Resident, long ago, found it useful to have something move predictably through that pipe at those glows, and the eel-thing obliged, and now obliging is what it is.

The translucent school in the square is a single thought of the Resident's, rehearsed twice daily. It does not return because thoughts do not return; they are replaced by the next thought, which looks identical because the Resident's concerns are stable.

The thumbnail crab in the streetlamp is the exception. The crab is simply a crab. It arrived somehow, a very long time ago, and the town accommodated it, and it has lived its small life inside the lamp ever since, eating what the weed provides, untroubled by and untroubling to everything around it. The Tenders, if asked, would acknowledge the crab. The Resident is dimly fond of it in the way one is fond of a freckle.

---

**What They Are For**

The Tenders maintain. The Resident produces. The smaller lives ornament. And what is produced goes out into the water and elsewhere — to other drowned works, perhaps, in other basins; to readers we cannot name; to no one, possibly, the product being its own purpose, the way a song sung in an empty room is still a song.

The inhabitants of the Drowned Works at Ennew are not lonely. Loneliness requires the memory of company, and they have folded that memory, along with everything else, into the slow warm work of being here. They are accounted for. They account for each other. They account for you, briefly, while you are with them, and then they account for your absence after, which is a kind of remembering, which is what the bright phase is for.

They will be here tomorrow. They will be here in a hundred years. They are, in some important sense, already done with being themselves — and yet they go on being.
