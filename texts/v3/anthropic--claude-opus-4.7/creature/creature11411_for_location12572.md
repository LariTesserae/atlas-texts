There is someone who tends the bowl.

I want to be careful here, because the shelf does not advertise her, and the first guesses are wrong. She is not a hermit in robes. She is not a spirit in the older sense — not a thing made of mist that gathers itself when no one is looking. She is not the mountain dreaming a woman. She is a person, or was, or is in the way the shelf is: at its own rate.

Start with what the shelf permits. Nothing fast. Nothing loud against the singing. Nothing that breaks the arrangement by which the moss grows and the rock weeps and the bird, or the bird's lineage, passes through the tree ferns. Whoever lives here lives within those tolerances or does not live here at all. So: she moves slowly. She is quiet in a way that is not effortful — the shelf has trained quietness into her, or selected for it, the way wind selects for the shape of a leaf.

I think she is old. Not ancient in any mythic register — old the way the gnarled trees are old, which is to say she has been doing this long enough that the doing has shaped her. Her hands would be the giveaway, if you saw them: limestone-pale from the wet, the skin softened and a little fluted itself at the knuckles, the nails kept short by work. She has the hands of someone who chips stone occasionally and rinses moss often. The bowl is hers, or it was her teacher's, or her teacher's teacher's. She did not make it. She inherited the keeping of it. This is important. The bowl is older than any one keeper. The keeping is a relay.

She does not live on the shelf. The shelf would not permit a shelter; a shelter is a fast thing, a human-shaped intrusion, smoke and warmth and accumulation. She lives lower, in the cloud-line or just under it, in a fold of rock where a person can sleep dry. She comes up. How often is hard to say — often enough that the moss does not close over the bowl's rim, rarely enough that no track wears in. Perhaps once in a season. Perhaps when a particular chord comes around, and she knows from below that the shelf is in that chord, and climbs.

What does she do when she is there. Less than you would think. She clears the rim of the bowl, if it needs clearing, with a thumb. She does not empty the bowl and refill it; the water in the bowl is the water the shelf has given, and to pour it out would be to refuse a gift. She tops it, perhaps, from a particular seep she favors — there is always a favorite seep, for a keeper, the way a reader has a favorite chair. She sits. She listens through one chord, or two. She does not pray in any form a stranger would recognize as prayer, but the sitting is not idle. She is doing the thing the shelf wants done, which is: being one more slow attentive presence inside the singing, so that the singing has a listener, so that the arrangement is witnessed and therefore continues to be an arrangement and not just a coincidence of wind and stone.

This is the work. Witness at the pace of weeping rock.

She eats nothing on the shelf. She leaves nothing. She does not speak — not because speech is forbidden but because she would have to raise her voice over the chord and raising her voice is the kind of fast loud thing the shelf does not host. If she hums, she hums under the high whistling, picking out a line the shelf is already playing, adding nothing, doubling something. The bird knows her. The bird does not come close, but it passes a little nearer when she is there than when a stranger is. This is the only acknowledgment in the entire ecology that she is a particular person and not just weather.

Now — and here I have to slow down, because I have been writing her as one woman and I am no longer sure that is right.

There may not be a current keeper. The moss-rim of the bowl could be kept clear by something other than a thumb. The seep nearest the bowl could be angled, by the shelf's own ancient geometry, to drip into the bowl at exactly the rate that evaporation takes water out, so that the bowl is self-filling and self-tending and the hand-tool marks on it are simply the last human act in a long story whose human chapter is over. In which case what lives there is just: the moss, the ferns, the cushion-plant, the bird, the insects, the chord. And the bowl is a kind of fossil of attention — a thing left by a vanished keeper, around which the shelf has organized itself to continue the keeping in its own way.

I think both are true, and I think this is how the shelf prefers it. There is a keeper. The keeper is sometimes a woman climbing up through cloud with limestone-pale hands. The keeper is sometimes a seep angled just so. The keeper is sometimes the moss declining, for its own reasons, to cross a particular rim. The keeping passes between these forms without anyone deciding. When the woman dies, the seep will tend the bowl for a while. When the seep eventually shifts — and seeps shift, over centuries — perhaps another woman will have climbed up by then, drawn by a chord she heard from below, and she will sit, and she will understand without being told what the sitting is for, and the relay will resume in human form for another span.

What lives on the shelf, then, is the keeping itself. It wears a woman sometimes. It wears stone and water other times. It is patient in the way the rock is patient. It is one more slow thing among slow things. It has been there longer than any single body that has carried it, and it will outlast the next several bodies too, and the bowl — the bowl is its instrument, the way the hollows in the rock are the wind's instrument, the small fixed thing around which the long slow music organizes.

And the bird, passing between the ferns, drinking from beaded water on the cushion-plant, is its witness in the spans between keepers. The bird does not sing. The shelf is already singing. The bird only has to be there, and to drink, and to pass again.
