# The Inhabitants of the Canopy Plateau

## The Listeners

They are what the people call themselves, in the language they use mostly with themselves, which is mostly silence. The word in their own tongue is something like *hesh* — a soft exhalation that means *one who attends*, and which cannot be heard from more than two meters away in the riot of the forest, and so functions as both name and proof of name. If you can hear someone say *hesh*, you are close enough to be one.

There are perhaps four hundred of them spread across the entire plateau. Possibly fewer. They do not count themselves because counting implies a totality that can be assembled, and they cannot be assembled — the plateau will not allow it. Three families in one watershed exceeds the carrying capacity of the silence between them. They know this the way you know not to crowd a sickbed.

They are small people, by the standards of the lowlands they have not seen in two thousand generations. Compact, broad-chested for the altitude, with the enlarged spleens and slow heart-rates of high-living humans everywhere. Their skin tends toward a dark olive that disappears against the jade-and-liver palette of the canopy; their hair grows in loose dense curls that the women bind with fiber and the men leave to mat into something almost lichenous. Their eyes are very dark and the whites are slightly yellowed by something in the pitcher-water — a tannin, a copper salt, nobody studies them, they do not know. They smile rarely but their faces at rest are not unfriendly. They are the faces of people who have spent their lives listening to something very large and have learned not to interrupt it with expression.

## How They Live

A Listener household is two to five people occupying perhaps thirty square kilometers of root-mat. The household moves. It does not migrate in the sense of seasonal cycles; it *drifts*, the way the root-mat itself drifts, ten or twelve centimeters a night, except that the household moves faster — a hundred meters a week, two hundred, following the slow logic of where the pitcher-cups are filling and where the something-meat animals are denning and where the gray sideways giants are warmest underfoot. They never sleep in the same platform twice in a season. They build a platform, use it for a few nights, leave it. The bindings stay tight. The floor sprouts. Someone, someday, perhaps a great-grandchild, will find the platform half-grown-over and recognize the knot-work and sleep there one night and move on.

This is why you found the hand-axe planted upright. It was not abandoned. It was *placed*. The owner walked away from it because they did not need it that day and would return for it in a month or a year or never, and in the meantime it was the plateau's, and the plateau would keep it, and any Listener who passed would know by the haft-weave whose it was and would not touch it. Tools accumulate identity over generations. A particular flute belongs to a particular lineage of breath. The skull-gourd with the handprints is a child's, and the child is now perhaps sixty years old, and the gourd waits for them somewhere they have not been back to since they were small, and they remember exactly where it is, and one day before they die they will go and sit beside it for an afternoon and then leave it again.

They eat: the larvae from the pitcher-cups, strained through woven mats. The translucent shrimp, eaten alive, crunching like glass. The flesh of the tree-screamers, which they kill rarely and with apology, drying the meat in the steam of the canopy until it becomes the dark resinous something-meat you found cached. The inner pith of certain ferns. The eggs of the bats-that-aren't-bats, harvested from rookeries in the hollow understory by lowering a child on a fiber rope into the dark, the child carrying a clay lamp and a soft basket, the child returning with eggs and with stories. The flowers of the death-trees, but only the flowers, never the fruit, which is for the animals that came to the corpse. They do not farm. Farming would require staying.

They make fire rarely. Wet wood, wet air; the effort is large and the return small. When they make fire it is ceremonial as much as practical — a cooking fire is also a gathering, a marker, a way of saying *here, briefly, for this night*. The smoke does not rise far; the canopy catches it and folds it back down and the fire-makers sit in their own slow exhalation for hours.

## What They Believe

This is the hardest part to write because they do not believe it in the way the word *believe* implies. They do not have a cosmology that can be separated from the act of breathing.

But: they understand that the mountain is singing. They understand this not as metaphor but as the central fact of where they live. The song was here before them and will be here after them, and their role — the role of any creature with breath and attention — is to add to it without overwhelming it. This is why they are quiet. This is why they are few. This is why they do not build anything that lasts, do not gather in numbers that would require their own acoustic space, do not raise their voices even to one another. To shout on the plateau would be to try to drown out the cathedral mid-service. It would be obscene. It would also be inaudible, which is the plateau's own gentle correction: you cannot in fact shout louder than the song. You can only stop trying.

They believe — they *know* — that the laughing-call animal which mimics human speech is the plateau learning their language. Not maliciously. The way a child learns. The plateau has been listening to them for a hundred thousand years and is slowly, badly, beginning to speak back. When a Listener hears their name in their own voice from the canopy at night, they do not walk the other way, as you were told to. They lie still and listen and feel a complicated tenderness, because the plateau is trying, and the plateau is getting better, and someday — not in their lifetime, not in their grandchildren's — the plateau will say something whole, and someone will be there to hear it, and that will be a moment in the song.

They believe that when a Listener dies, they are placed under a death-tree, and the tree flowers, and the flowers smell exactly like them, and this is the most accurate eulogy possible: a scent-portrait, true to the molecule, broadcast for a season and then gone. They do not mark the place. They do not need to. The tree remembers, and the plateau remembers through the tree, and the next person to walk past will smell the dead and know, without knowing who, that someone good was here.

## Their Relationship to You

You are not the first outsider. You are perhaps the seventh or eighth in living memory. The Listeners have a word for you, which is *halek*, which means roughly *one who came up*, which is not pejorative but is not neutral either — it carries the weight of the climb, the implication that you suffered to arrive and therefore deserve a moment's consideration, but also that you arrived from somewhere, which means you carry somewhere with you, which means you are loud in a way that has nothing to do with your voice.

The one you saw across the gulf of canopy at sunset was a woman of perhaps forty, named in her own tongue a sound like *Iyn*, which means *the small bright thing under a leaf*. She had been watching you for two days. She had decided you were not dangerous — you walked slowly, you drank from pitchers, you did not take the hand-axe — and so she allowed you to see her. The lifted hand was not a greeting in the sense you understood. It was a *placement*. She was telling you: I am here, you are there, the distance between us is correct, do not close it. You lifted your hand back and got it right by accident, and she turned and went, and that night she told her household about you, in a few soft sentences, and her household told the next household at the next drift-meeting in six weeks, and within a season every Listener on the plateau will know that a *halek* came up and behaved well and left.

If you had behaved badly — if you had taken the hand-axe, if you had shouted, if you had tried to follow her — they would not have harmed you. They would simply have stopped being where you were. The plateau is enormous and they are very good at not being found. You would have wandered alone in the green for as long as you could stand it, and then you would have gone back down, and the plateau would have absorbed your passage the way it absorbs rain, and the song would not have noticed.

## What They Are Becoming

This is the thing I want to say last, and most carefully.

The Listeners have been on the plateau long enough that they are no longer entirely a separate species from it. Their gut flora is plateau flora; the tannins in their water have stained their cellular chemistry; the slow body-heat of the gray sideways giants matches their own resting temperature to within a tenth of a degree, and nobody can say anymore which warmed to meet which. Their children are born into the sound and never know silence, and so their auditory cortices develop differently — they can pick a single frog's voice out of the roar at a hundred meters, they can hear a person breathing through three layers of leaf. They dream, all of them, of the same handful of images: a warm gray trunk, a pitcher-cup full of stars, a hand lifted across a green gulf. They do not find this strange. They assume everyone dreams these things. They assume the dreams come from the plateau, which they do.

They are, in some sense that resists the categories you brought up the mountain with you, the plateau's organs of self-attention. The mountain eroded down to almost nothing and then began to sing, and the song needed an ear inside itself, and the Listeners are that ear. When the last of them dies — and they will end, all things end, their numbers drift slowly downward and one century there will be three of them and the next there will be none — the song will continue, but it will continue unheard from within, which is a different kind of song. The plateau knows this. The Listeners know this. Nobody is in a hurry. The sentence the mountain is finishing is very long and there is time yet for the ear to listen, and for the listener to be heard, and for both to be folded eventually into the green that eats the rock and the rock that remembers it was green.

You came up looking for a summit.

What lives here is what the summit became when it stopped being stone. What lives here is the listening that the mountain grew to hear itself with. What lives here is a few hundred quiet people, and three trillion loud small things, and one enormous slow warm idea, and they are all, in their different registers, the same inhabitant.
