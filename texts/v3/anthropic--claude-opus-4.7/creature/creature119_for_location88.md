They are called, among themselves, nothing in particular. The tapping language has a sign that means *us-here-now*, four quick strikes and a held silence, but this is used only when a distinction is needed, which is rarely. A people who have not met another people have little need of a name for themselves.

Their bodies are built for the water they live in rather than for the water as such. This is an important distinction. They are not fish and were never fish; their ancestors, if one can speak of ancestors in a place this young, seem to have been something that walked on a shore that no longer exists, or perhaps has not yet existed — the grammar of time in Aunu is unreliable. The long limbs and the flat flexing ears and the slate-wet skin are adaptations to *slowness*, to a medium that resists and cushions and carries. They move the way tall grass moves. When one of them reaches for a cup, the reach begins in the shoulder and travels outward like a small considered wave, and the cup is lifted before the hand seems to have decided to lift it. They do not have the jerk of terrestrial gesture. A child learning to handle objects learns first to let the water do half the work.

Their eyes are larger than ours and set slightly to the sides, and the pupils are horizontal, the shape of a lentil laid on its side. In the blue lichen-light these eyes look almost black; in the yellow-green of the ribbon-groves they show a band of coppery inner color, a ring inside a ring. They do not blink often. When they do, it is slow and deliberate and often conveys meaning — a slow blink held for two beats is a kind of acknowledgment, the equivalent of our small nod. Visitors who do not know this are thought rude without knowing why.

They eat little and eat often. The main food is a paste made from the pith of the ribbon-growth, pounded and left to ferment in clay jars for a season, eaten with the fingers from shallow bowls. It is mild and slightly sweet and has the texture of soft curd. They supplement this with the drifters, which are netted in the upper streets and dried on the roofs under the silt-fall until they become translucent wafers that taste, I am told, of almost nothing, but which the body needs for reasons the inhabitants cannot articulate and do not particularly care to. They do not hunt the eels. They do not speak of the long animal's relation to food, if there is one.

Their hands have six fingers, arranged as four and an opposed pair — two thumbs, one on each side of the palm, which makes their grip symmetrical and gives their knotwork and net-weaving a particular quality: every knot they tie is chiral-neutral, readable and untieable from either side. I did not understand at first why their ropes looked strange to me. It is this. A rope made by a one-thumbed species has a handedness. Theirs do not.

They are long-lived, though no one is certain how long. A woman I spoke with through the trade-pidgin said she remembered her great-grandmother's voice, which would mean little except that her great-grandmother, she said, remembered *her* great-grandmother's voice, and the chain she described went back further than seemed plausible. Memory in Aunu is assisted by the stone. A person buried in a wall is not entirely gone; the shape remains, and the shape is visited, and the visits are tapped into the speaking-post, and the sequence of a family's tappings over centuries becomes a kind of long slow document that lives in the walls and in the habit of the living. I came to suspect that part of what they call remembering is actually this external continuity — that an Aunu woman who says *I remember my great-grandmother* may partly mean *I know the pattern that is tapped for her, and I have tapped it, and in tapping it I have become, briefly, a place where she was*.

They sleep in pairs or threes, curled in shallow alcoves cut into the inner walls of the dwellings, and they sleep lightly and often, in half-hour drifts rather than in long nights. They do not dream, they say, or rather — and this took some time to work out through the pidgin — they do not distinguish dreaming from the ordinary drift of attention during waking. The word they use for *thought* and the word they use for *dream* are the same word, tapped with the same rhythm. An Aunu going about her errands in the streets and an Aunu lying still in her alcove are understood to be doing variants of the same activity, and the variants are not ranked.

They are gentle. I want to say this without making them seem simple, because they are not simple. But they are gentle in a way that has the quality of a decision made so long ago it has become the shape of the body. They do not raise their voices because their voices carry. They do not cut the young rock because the rock is still deciding. They do not look for the edge of the water because the water is holding them. A great deal of Aunu behavior, examined closely, turns out to be a form of courtesy extended to things that cannot ask for it — to the stone, to the silt, to the ribbon-groves, to the long animal, to the possibility that the world is finite and fragile and watching back.

I think they know they are provisional. I do not think this frightens them. They have the temperament of something that has understood, early and without argument, that it is a draft — that the rock will harden, that the ridges will become valleys, that the water will perhaps one day thin and clear, and that the people who live here then will not be quite them. They tap to their dead in the walls. They watch the bright sleep once every twelve years. They move their long limbs slowly through the warm green light. They are practicing, I think, a particular kind of tenderness that a species can only develop in a place that has not yet finished being made — the tenderness of inhabitants who know that they, too, are among the unfinished things, and that to press too hard on any surface, including themselves, would leave a mark that the world is not yet ready to heal.

At night — if one can call it night, the lilac ceiling only dims, it does not go out — the tapping slows and thins and becomes a scatter, like the last rain of a storm moving off. One can hear, then, the individual posts. A child practicing a rhythm and getting it slightly wrong. An old man tapping a long complicated sequence that may be a recipe or a genealogy or a letter to someone no longer alive. The woman who speaks to her husband about the weather. The water carries it all, gentled by distance, and somewhere far below or far above, the long animal moves past very slowly, and everyone has agreed not to see it, and this agreement is itself a kind of love, and the city goes on becoming.
