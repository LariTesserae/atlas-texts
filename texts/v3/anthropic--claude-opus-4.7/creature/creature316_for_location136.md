# The People of the Saddle

They are shorter than lowlanders, and it takes you a while to notice why: not bone but posture. They've grown up ducking. The lanes corkscrew and the eaves kiss and the doorways are low because doorways only need to be as tall as the people who use them, and so each generation confirms the next. They move with a particular economy — a slight forward carry of the head, shoulders loose and ready to turn sideways, feet that know the give of duckboard from the give of live moss without looking. An outsider on the paths lurches. A child of the saddle flows.

Their skin is pale under a permanent faint flush from the wet cold, and almost everyone, by forty, has a fine green tint at the cuticles and in the creases of the knuckles — not dirt, exactly; a staining from handling wall-moss daily that no scrubbing fully lifts, and that they've stopped thinking of as anything but the color hands are. The very old have it at the temples too, where they've leaned against doorframes for sixty years. Hair runs dark and heavy and is worn braided close, because loose hair in this air becomes a wick. The braids are oiled with something rendered from the fat of the autumn geese that pass through, and the oil smells, faintly, like every person in town.

Their clothing is layered wool under oiled linen under, on the worst days, a hooded cape of felted moss-fiber itself — the town's one real export, traded down-mountain for the bronze and salt and grain they can't grow in cloud. The moss-capes are nearly waterproof and nearly weightless and last a lifetime, and a child receives their first one at around seven, cut down from a parent's. You can tell a family by the shade of their cape: each household's is felted from the trimmings of their own walls, and every wall is subtly its own green.

Their hands are remarkable. Thick through the palm, long in the finger, with pads worn smooth by constant contact with damp fiber. They are botanists without the word for it. A woman of thirty can tell you by touch alone whether a patch of wall-moss is thriving, thirsting, souring, or ready to be trimmed, and she learned this the way lowlanders learn to read — gradually, without remembering when. The children play a game where they are blindfolded and asked to name which of six moss-species they are holding; by nine or ten they are rarely wrong. This is not considered a skill. It's considered knowing where you are.

They speak loudly, as the description says, but also — and this takes longer to notice — *sparely*. Because the ambient noise is so dense, words cost more here. A sentence has to earn its passage through the frog-chorus. So they've pared their speech down over generations into something almost telegraphic: nouns, verbs, the occasional flat vowel of feeling. *Wet year. Good wall. Bell for Imra.* Strangers mistake this for coldness. It isn't. Watch two of them sit together on a threshold for an hour and say nine words total and you'll understand that the words were the least of what passed. They are enormously present to each other. They have to be. You can't hear a person in this town; you have to watch them.

The bells do much of what speech does elsewhere. Every child learns their own house's bell-voice before they learn their own name, and learns the neighbors' bells not long after. The patterns are codified but also personal — a family develops idiolects, little rhythmic signatures, so that *Ila is home* sounds slightly different from *Ila's mother is home* even though the bell and the summons are the same. In a fog thick enough to lose a hand in front of your face — which happens — a person can stand in the central square and know from the bells alone who is cooking, who is in labor, who has just noticed the cistern rising. They navigate by sound the way sailors navigate by stars.

They are, by the standards of down-mountain, extraordinarily patient and extraordinarily impatient in different registers. Patient with growth: a house takes forty years to thicken properly, and no one hurries it, and the idea of hurrying it would be obscene, like hurrying a child. Impatient with water: the sluice-child is relieved every two hours because attention lapses and attention lapsing means a flooded lower ward, and a child who falls asleep at the gate is not punished exactly but is *talked about*, quietly, for years. Water is the one thing here that does not wait.

Their dead are given to the moss. There is a slope on the north shoulder, shaded and always wet, where the bodies are laid in shallow troughs and covered with living sphagnum and left. Within a season there is nothing left to find. Within three seasons the moss from that slope is harvested, felted, and worked into the wall of the new house of whoever in the family is marrying that spring. So the walls of the oldest houses are, quite literally, ancestral. When a grandmother leans against her kitchen wall to rest, she is leaning against her grandmother. They do not make a cult of this. It's just the arrangement. The dead go into the houses and the houses shelter the living and in time the living go into the houses too. A stranger asking *isn't that strange* will get a long look and eventually the answer: *where do your dead go.*

They marry in the Turning because, as the description notes, one more upheaval doesn't signify — but also because the Turning is when the whole town is touching moss constantly, re-laying the sloughed roofs, cutting back horsetail, catching the mudslides in nets, and a marriage in that season means the couple's first shared labor is this communal, green-handed work. You are married, here, by working on someone else's roof beside your spouse for three weeks. The vows are brief and said under a bell. The labor is the ceremony.

They have almost no possessions. A knife. A bowl. A cape. A bell, inherited. A small carved something-or-other — a frog, usually, or a newt, the local religious art — kept in a niche in the wall that the moss slowly, over a life, tries to close over, and that the owner has to periodically trim back. When someone dies, there is very little to distribute. Mostly the niche is re-carved, deeper, for the next person who sleeps in that room.

They laugh more than you'd expect. Quietly, because of the sound, but often. The humor is dry and observational and tends to involve slugs. A slug on a neighbor's threshold is a week of material. They find the frogs funny too, in an exasperated way, the way a person finds their own loud relatives funny. The warblers they love without reservation. The bats they miss when the bats leave, and celebrate when the bats come back in late summer, and there is a small private bell-pattern, not taught but somehow known by everyone, for *the bats are back.*

From the ridge above, you see the green loaves rising from the saddle and the smoke threading up through the living roofs. What you don't see, at that distance, are the people — because they are the color of their walls, in their moss-capes and their green-stained hands, moving slowly along lanes you can't trace, pausing at thresholds, ringing small hard bells into the frog-chorus. They are not hidden. They are simply *of* the place to a degree that makes them hard to resolve as separate from it. Come down into the town and they appear, one by one, out of the green, nodding, wordless, already knowing which bell you are.
