# Who Lives There

## The Salamander

Begin with her, because everything else in the chamber is faster than she is and therefore, in a sense, less real. She is almost a foot of pale muscle, the color of the inside of a root, with external gills like small red ferns at her throat and eyes that have become vestigial — dark spots under translucent skin, aware of light but not of shape. She is female; she has been female for a long time. Her species, wherever its surface relatives went, does not reach this size quickly. Fifty years is plausible. Seventy is not impossible.

She does not think, but she *knows* the chamber the way a hand knows a pocket. She knows the pool's lip in absolute darkness — every flute and calcium knuckle of it. She knows the three throats by the different qualities of air that fall from them: the north one drier, the east one more mineral, the west one carrying, every few years, the smell of a particular flowering thing above that she has never seen and has no name for but recognizes as *that again*. She knows the seep's cycles in her blood before the frogs know them in their clicks.

She hunts perhaps eight times a year. A shrimp, a cricket that has fallen in, occasionally — rarely, and it takes her hours to decide — one of the frogs. She does not need much. Her metabolism is a slow flame kept in a slow lantern. When she emerges from her crevice, she does so with a deliberateness that would look, to a human observer, almost like ceremony: the long pale body unspooling an inch at a time into the warm water, the gill-fronds flaring, a single slow arc across the pool's floor, and then either the strike or, more often, a long motionless hover during which nothing visible happens but during which some small decision is being made and unmade in her dim wet brain.

She has no companion. She is the only one of her kind in the chamber and possibly in the watershed. Whether she was carried here as an egg in the gullet of a bird, whether her ancestors walked down one of the root-throats when they were younger and narrower, whether she is the last of a line or simply the current expression of one — no one knows, and she does not know either. She is not lonely because loneliness is a vertebrate luxury she has not bothered with.

## The Frogs

There are perhaps sixty of them. The population fluctuates; in a wet year, eighty; in a dry one, forty. They are not a described species, or rather, they are a described species that has drifted — the chamber's isolation has made them slightly their own thing, smaller and paler and quieter than their cousins in the surface streams, and with the click instead of the croak, which is almost certainly a behavioral response to the chamber's acoustics: a croak would boom and overlap and drown itself, but a click carries clean.

They live, essentially, as a single distributed organism with sixty bodies. The rotation of their clicking is not taught; a frog raised alone would not do it. But a frog dropped into the chamber learns the rotation within a night, the way a person walking into a room of people breathing learns, without noticing, to breathe in the same rhythm. They position themselves with a loose spacing — never two on the same frond, rarely two within a hand's width on the same pillar — and this spacing is maintained without apparent negotiation. When one dies, the others redistribute within a day.

They are aware of the salamander the way a village is aware of a landslide: as a possibility, not a presence. When she emerges, the clicking does not stop, but it *thins*, the nearer frogs going quiet, the rotation continuing in a broken arc on the far side of the chamber until she has returned to her crevice.

They are aware of humans in a different way. A human in the chamber is neither predator nor weather. They do not know what to make of one, and so they do what frogs do with things they do not know what to make of: they continue. But the clicking slows. Not from fear — from something more like attention.

## The Felting

This is harder. The white mycelial mat on the walls is not one organism in the sense that the salamander is one organism, but it is not many either. It is a single genetic individual spread across perhaps two hundred square feet of wall, with some filaments reaching up into the root-mat and some reaching down into the clay under the pool. It is older than the salamander by an order of magnitude. It may be older than the limestone step.

It does not experience anything, probably. But it *responds*. It grows toward fallen leaves. It thickens where the ferns drop spent fronds. It has, over centuries, shaped itself to the chamber's microclimates — thinner where the air moves, thicker in the dead pockets, sculpted into those folds and shelves because those are the forms that efficiently catch what falls. The face-like shape on the western wall is not a face and was not made by intention, but it was made by the chamber: it is the shape that wall-felting takes when exposed to exactly that pattern of drip and draft for exactly that long. Another chamber with the same conditions would grow the same face.

If the chamber has a memory, the felting is where it is kept. Not in symbols — in textures. The shape of every season the chamber has had since the felting established itself is written into the felting's topography, the way tree rings write years.

## The Small Lives

The crickets are long-legged and flightless and walk rather than jump. They have been flightless, here, for long enough that their wings are gone — not reduced, *gone*, which is a more committed evolutionary decision than reduction. They graze on the fern fronds and on the felting where it thins enough to be edible, and they are eaten by the frogs, and their population is the real engine of the chamber: a bad cricket year is a bad frog year is a bad shrimp year (because fewer frogs die into the pool).

The millipedes — wet-penny-colored, deliberate, the size of a short match — are the chamber's undertakers. They work the floor and the lower walls. Anything that dies and falls, if it is not eaten first, is reduced by them. They are the reason the floor is not a carpet of small corpses. They are gentle and slow and there are more of them than you would think; turn over a piece of root-bark on the floor and three or four will be there, startled into immobility, waiting for you to leave.

The moths are a puzzle. There is only one species, and it is not found anywhere else that has been surveyed, though no one has surveyed very hard. They lay their eggs on the undersides of fern fronds; the caterpillars eat the fronds and the felting; the pupae hang in the high corners of the root-mat; the adults emerge, rest, mate once, rest more, and die. They almost never fly. The flights, when they happen, seem to serve no purpose a biologist would recognize — they are not feeding, not seeking mates, not fleeing. A moth will simply, occasionally, leave its frond and cross the chamber, and land, and rest again. It is possible this is how they distribute themselves. It is possible it is a vestige of a behavior that meant something in an ancestor. It is possible the chamber requires, for reasons no one will ever work out, the occasional pale flutter across its dim air, and the moths provide it.

The shrimp in the pool are the simplest residents — almost transparent, eyelash-length, responsive only to current and chemistry. The snails are slightly more themselves: each one a small honey-drop, grazing the felting in tracks you can see if you look closely, each snail keeping, apparently, to a territory of about a hand's breadth, returning to the same resting spot on the same shelf after each grazing circuit. A snail-scale life, lived with snail-scale fidelity.

## The Roots, Which Are Also Residents

It is easy to think of the roots as infrastructure — the ceiling, the pillars, the ladders — but they are alive and they are drinking and they are, in their slow way, participating. The fig-like species that makes up most of the root-mat is a single strangler above, which means that what looks like a forest of pillars down here is, in some cases, one tree: one organism with its crown in the sun and its throat in this hot green dim, aware (if awareness is the word, and it isn't, but there isn't a better one) of both.

The roots have learned the chamber. The smoothed footholds on the throats are partly human wear and partly the root's own response to being climbed — it has laid down slightly denser tissue there, the way a hand callouses. The pillars that reach the floor have thickened at their bases into something like feet. The roots that dip into the pool have, over time, developed a slightly different bark on their submerged portions, and they drink there, and they return that water, transpired, to a canopy that has never known where its water came from.

## The Absent Ones

And then there are the residents who are not here now but whose traces are. Whoever sat against the pillar until it polished. Whoever cut the limestone step. Whoever kept something in the niche long enough for the beeswax to go black and fossil. They are part of the chamber's population in the way the felting is — distributed, historical, responsive. They did not write, or did not want to, but they left the shape of their attention: a step where a step was needed, a seat where sitting was wanted, a small shelf for a small offering.

They came, probably, in ones and twos, across generations, and they sat, and they listened, and they left. They did not build. They did not expand the niche, or add a second step, or carve a name. They understood — the chamber teaches this quickly — that the place did not want additions. It wanted to be *witnessed*, occasionally, and then left to continue.

Whoever they were, they have been gone a long time. The beeswax is almost stone. The moss on the step is deep. But in the chamber's slow bookkeeping they are not quite gone; they are simply the residents who visit least often, whose rotation in the chamber's long clicking is measured not in minutes but in centuries, and who may yet — it is not impossible — come again.

## What Lives There, Altogether

A salamander who is almost a clock. Sixty frogs who are almost a single animal. A fungus that is almost a memory. Crickets without wings, millipedes who bury the dead, moths who fly for no reason anyone can name, snails who keep their small appointments, shrimp who are barely more than the water itself. A tree that is also a ceiling. And, folded into all of it, the soft ghost of a human practice — someone's grandmother's grandmother, maybe, sitting against the pillar, listening to the clicks align with her pulse, and understanding, without having to put it in words, that the room was listening back.

That is who lives there. The chamber itself, if you insist on a single answer. But the chamber is a committee, and the committee has been in session, in the hot green dim, for a very long time, and intends — insofar as anything down there intends anything — to continue.
