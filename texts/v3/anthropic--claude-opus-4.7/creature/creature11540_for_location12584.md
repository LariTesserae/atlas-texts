## What Lives There

Start with the obvious tenants and work down.

**The frogs are the loudest fact.** They occupy the place the way bacteria occupy a gut — not as visitors but as a layer of the system. There are probably two hundred species on this single range, most of them undescribed because no one is here to describe them. The dominant lineage has done something specific with the vent ecology: their tadpoles develop in the warm pools downstream of the chamber-engines, and the adults have tuned their calls to fill the silences between vent cycles. When a vent roars, that whole acoustic band is unusable, so the frogs in its basin have moved their calls up into the high registers or compressed them into the quiet windows. You can map the vent sequence by ear if you sit still long enough; the chorus shifts around the ridge like a slow searchlight, brightening wherever the steam has just gone quiet.

A few of them are stranger. There is a small dun-colored species, no bigger than a thumbnail, that lives only on the silica nozzles themselves, in the band of rock that is hot but not lethal. It lays its eggs in the sulfur crust. The tadpoles eat thermophilic mats. When the vent fires, the adults are simply gone — they have learned, over a very long time, the precursor signs: a particular smell, a particular vibration, a change in the way the rock sweats. They retreat to crevices a meter or two off the throat and wait out the blast. After, they come back. Their skin is the color of the crust, sulfur-cream with rust streaks, and they are very hard to see until they move.

**The climbing mammals in the canopy** — call them tank-eaters — are the slow heart of the upper forest. Imagine an animal the size of a large house cat, with the deliberate gait of a sloth and the dexterous forepaws of a kinkajou, but with a long prehensile snout for reaching into the deep funnels of the bromeliad tanks. They eat the insect larvae and the trapped frogs and the soft inner leaves, and they drink the tank water, and in doing so they reset the tanks, which would otherwise stagnate and kill their host plants. They are pale, the color of wet wood, and their fur grows algae — actual living algae, symbiotic, the same trick the sloths of another continent stumbled into. In the rain they look green. In the sun they look gray. They sleep in the crowns of the tree ferns, curled into the wet duff that accumulates in the leaf-bases, and they move at night in something that is not quite a troop and not quite solitary — loose chains of three or four, calling to each other in low coughs.

They have a relationship with the vents too, but a more passive one: they will not cross a vent-line during a firing, and they seem to know the schedule. Whether they read it from the rock or from each other, no one can say.

**The spotted cat** is the only large predator. It is the size of an ocelot, but more heavily built in the shoulders, with the broad paws of an animal that walks on wet rock. Its spots are small, dense, and arranged in rosettes around a darker central blotch — the pattern that vanishes against the dappled liverwort-and-moss substrate of the talus. It hunts the tank-eaters when it can, but mostly it eats the climbing crabs and the larger frogs and the occasional young of the deer-thing that browses the lower slopes.

It does one peculiar thing. It uses the warm pools. Not to drink — there is water everywhere — but to lie in, after a hunt, the way a domestic cat will find a sunbeam. The mineral terraces near the chamber-engines often have a single cat-sized depression worn into the calcite where one of these animals has come, generation after generation, to soak its belly and dry its paws. The cats are not numerous. There may be only a few dozen on the whole range. They are old in the evolutionary sense — they have been here long enough to have specialized down to a narrow waist, and they cannot leave.

**The crabs** are the surprise. Freshwater, palm-sized, dull purple with cream-tipped claws. They climb the wet rock chimneys at night, scaling vertical faces using the friction of the algal slime, foraging on whatever the steam has cooked: insects driven out of crevices, soft mats of microorganisms, the occasional unlucky frog. They are not the only crustaceans — there are smaller ones in the streams, and something nearly shrimp-like in the warm pools — but the climbers are the ones you notice, because at night, when you sweep a light across a wet face, the faint green eyeshine of two hundred of them looks like the face is studded with low stars.

They moult in synchrony with the vent cycles. Whether this is causation or correlation no one knows.

**The long-tailed cuckoo-thing** is not actually a cuckoo. It is its own lineage, very deeply branched, and it does not parasitize other nests. It eats the large hairy caterpillars that nothing else will touch — the ones loaded with the alkaloids of the melastome understory — and it stores the toxins in its own feathers, which are accordingly avoided by every predator on the ridge. Its two-note call, with that long silence after, is a territorial marker. The silences are not empty; they are listening. A male will not call again until he has heard, faintly, the answering note of his nearest neighbor two valleys over. The whole ridge is netted with these slow conversations. When a male dies, his territory falls silent for a season, and the silence propagates through the network as his neighbors lose their reference and recalibrate. It takes about a year for the song-map to fully heal around a loss.

**In the leaf litter**, the snakes. Small ones mostly, hand-length, blunt-headed, eating the smallest frogs and the soft white grubs. One species is larger — as long as a man is tall, slender, the color of wet bark — and it lives in the tree fern crowns and eats the tank-eaters' young when it can get them. It is sluggish in the cool of morning and active in the heat after a vent has fired, and so it follows the vent cycle around the ridge, slowly, over weeks. A snake that hunts on a circuit.

**The caecilians in the warm pools** are blind and pinkish and have been here, in some form, since before the mountains were mountains. They are the oldest residents. They breed in the mineral-rich water near the chamber-engines, where almost nothing else can live, and their larvae feed on the bacterial mats. They do not appear to have changed in any significant way for a very long time. They are the closest thing this place has to a constant.

**The hummingbirds** are doing what hummingbirds do, but with one wrinkle: several species have learned to drink from the condensation that collects on the undersides of the wine-red melastome leaves, not just from flowers. The leaves act as slow stills, distilling clean water out of the steam, and the birds have figured out the schedule. They visit the leaves in the hour after a vent fires, when the condensation is heaviest. It is not nectar but it is reliable, and in a place this wet, the reliability of water from a known source is worth something even to an animal swimming in moisture.

---

Now the harder layer.

**There are no people, and there have never been people, but there is something like attention in the place.** Not a mind. Not an animal. The sequencing of the vents, the regularity of the piston-knock — these are not alive in any sense that would survive scrutiny. They are geology. But the ecosystem has organized itself around them so completely that the distinction has gone soft at the edges. The frogs schedule themselves to the vents. The tank-eaters avoid the vent-lines. The crabs moult to the cycle. The cuckoo-things sing in a network whose timing is set, partly, by the percussion in the deep rock. The whole forest is entrained.

If you sat on the ridge for a year you would begin to feel that the place was running a process, and that the animals were not so much its inhabitants as its working parts — the way the bacteria in a fermenter are not visitors but the thing the fermenter is for. The product, as the description says, is the forest itself: a stable, self-maintaining, slowly elaborating wet-warm-high biome that has been refining itself for so long that every niche is filled and every cycle is buffered against every other.

What lives there, then, in the largest sense, is the **process** — and the animals are how the process keeps its own time. The frogs are its clock. The tank-eaters are its gardeners. The cat is its editor. The cuckoo-things are its long-range nervous system, slow as weather. The caecilians are its memory. The crabs are its night shift. The snakes follow the heat around the ridge like a slow hand on a slow dial.

And underneath it all, the cylinder knocks. Knocks. Knocks. Keeping the time the forest grew up to.
