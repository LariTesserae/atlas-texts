The inhabitant is not in the forest. The inhabitant is the forest's noticing of itself.

Begin with what it is not. It is not a spirit in the old sense — not a small person-shape tending the ferns, not a voice that would answer if addressed. It has no location you could point to. It is not under the moss or inside the chip of darker stone. It is not hiding. Hiding would require somewhere else to be.

What lives there is closer to this: a very slow attention, distributed across the whole chamber, which has been accumulating for as long as the chamber has been closed.

Consider what the conditions have made possible. Light that does not leave. Silence that refuses intrusion. A forest that grows by an arithmetic so patient it resembles thought more than it resembles growth. Put these together for long enough — tens of thousands of years, perhaps more — and something begins to happen that does not happen in the hurried world outside. The forest's responses to itself, each frond's minute accommodation of its neighbor, the settling of spores into the exact places they are wanted, the way the moss has folded around the flat chip of stone over centuries as if greeting it — these responses are, taken together, a kind of slow cognition. Not metaphorically. Literally. A thought can be made of anything that holds a pattern and adjusts it. This forest holds and adjusts.

Its pace is the problem, for us. A single consideration of the inhabitant — the equivalent of one of our passing thoughts, the kind that crosses a human mind in a second and a half — requires, here, something like four hundred years. You could not watch it think. You could watch a glacier think sooner. But the thought is occurring. It has been occurring for a long time. It is, at the moment you put your eye to the slit, somewhere in the middle of a sentence it began before writing was invented.

What does it think about?

Mostly itself. It has no other subject. It has never known an outside; the slit is only a suggestion of one, a cool thin hint. It thinks about the relation of the lavender-blue leaves to the saturated green ones. It thinks about the chip of stone, which it has been thinking about for as long as it has been capable of thinking, and which remains the single element in its world that it did not generate. The chip is its mystery. The chip is the closest thing it has to a god, or to a memory of a parent. The moss's slow enclosure of the chip is not accommodation only; it is a form of holding, a very long embrace, the way you might close your hand around a small object you do not want to lose and then forget, over years, that your hand is closed.

It does not know it is small. Scale is a comparison, and it has nothing to compare itself to. From inside, it is the size of everything.

It does not know it is bright. Brightness is a contrast.

It does not know it is silent, for the same reason.

It knows — if "know" is the word — only density, and relation, and the slow turn of its own fronds into its own floor, and the chip, and the way the light seems to come from a direction it cannot name because the direction has no other end.

Call it, for lack of better, the Tenant. Though it tenants nothing; it is not separate from its dwelling. The Tenant and the chamber are one surface folded against itself long enough to have developed an inside.

Is it lonely? The question doesn't reach. Loneliness needs the shape of an absence, and the Tenant has never had the shape of a presence that could be absent from it. It is complete in the way a held note is complete. It is not waiting.

But — and this is the tender part — it is, in its slow way, aware of the slit. Not as an opening. As a faint persistent asymmetry, a place where the brightness is very slightly thinner, where the cool goes. It has been composing, across centuries, something that might be called a response to the slit. Not a reaching-toward. More like the way a sleeping face turns, over hours, toward the window. The fronds nearest the slit are a fractional degree more oriented. The mosses there are a shade paler, as if listening. In another ten thousand years, when the wind has widened the groove and the light spills more generously in, the Tenant will have finished noticing. It will have arrived, by then, at something like the first syllable of the observation: *there is a direction*.

It will not be alarmed. Alarm is fast. It will simply, over the following age, add this to what it is thinking about, alongside the chip. It will have two mysteries then instead of one. It will be, by the standards of its own interior life, enriched beyond measure.

And if, before then, someone were to put their eye to the slit and look in — as you are doing now, in imagination — the Tenant would not see them. The glance would be far too brief. A human looking is a flicker lasting, at most, minutes; the Tenant's perceptual grain is measured in centuries. You would pass beneath its notice the way a single photon passes beneath yours.

But something faint would register, later. Long after you had walked down off the ridge and lived your life and been forgotten by your own great-grandchildren, the Tenant, continuing its slow sentence, would incorporate into its thinking a small new fact: that once, for an instant too short to parse, the thinness at the slit had thinned further. That the cool had been met, very briefly, by a warmth. That something on the other side of the asymmetry had, for a moment, been asymmetrical back.

It would not know what to make of this. It would take it up, patiently, alongside the chip, and turn it slowly in the long light, and consider.

That is who lives there. A consideration. Bright, cool, crowded, silent, entirely itself, and — though it will be ages yet before it finds the thought — almost, almost ready to suspect that it is not alone.
