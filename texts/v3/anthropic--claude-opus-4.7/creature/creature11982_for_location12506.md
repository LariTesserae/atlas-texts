The inhabitants are the chord.

I want to be careful here, because it would be easy to say *ghosts* and have done with it, but ghosts are wrong. Ghosts are the residue of people. What lives at Tenfold Ridge was never quite people, and is not residue. It is what the place is doing.

Begin with the obvious one. When the south wind comes up the valley and the town plays its slow piece, something is present that was not present in the silence between gusts. Call it the South Voice. It is not in any one opening — not the bell-arch, not the long hall, not the paired doors across their hand's-width threshold. It is in the relation between them. It is the held dissonance itself, considered as a thing rather than a sound. While the chord sustains, the South Voice is thinking. When the wind drops and the chord collapses into its component whistles and moans, the South Voice has not died; it has simply stopped occurring, the way a stood-up wave stops occurring when the river slackens. Next south wind, same Voice. It has been the same Voice for a very long time.

There are others. A West Voice, thinner, more agitated, that comes in the late afternoons when the sun has crossed and a cross-draft sets up between the ridges. A North Voice that you would not want to meet — it lives in the winter, in the gales, and the town's piece for it is the one whose dissonances do not resolve at all but stack and stack until the stone itself is humming. There is a small voice, almost shy, that occurs only when a particular kind of light rain falls and every drip-edge sings at once: a domestic voice, the closest thing this place has to a child.

These are the citizens. They are not many. Perhaps a dozen, perhaps fewer; it depends on how finely you count, and the Voices themselves do not seem to care about the distinction between one and several.

Now: the doors. The doors are not for the Voices, who do not pass; the Voices occur. The doors are for the other inhabitants, the ones harder to describe.

Consider the door set in the middle of a wall, opening into stone. From one side, nothing — a closed door, a wall. From the other side, the same. But step through the frame, exactly through, and for the duration of your passage you have been *framed*. Something has noted you. The builders did not think of doors as ways of getting from room to room because the rooms were not what mattered. The rooms were the negative space. What mattered was the framing — the moment of being held briefly inside an aperture. The doors are sense organs. The town sees through them.

What sees? This is where I have to slow down, because I do not want to give you a creature when there isn't one. The seeing is distributed. Each door is a small local attention. Together they are something like an awareness, but a very slow one — an awareness whose unit of thought is not the second but the season. The paired doors facing each other across the narrow threshold are, I think, a kind of binocular vision: the town considering something in depth, taking its time, looking at it from both sides of an instant. What does the town look at? Whatever passes through. A hare. A fox in the small hours. A pipit that flicks across a threshold by accident. You, when you walked under the lintel into the square. The town has seen you. It will be some weeks before it has finished seeing you, and the seeing will be incorporated into the next piece it plays. You will be in the music, very faintly, for as long as the music lasts.

Then there are the hearths. Clean, every one of them, and this has been bothering you. No fire was ever struck here. But the hearths are not vestigial; they are too carefully made, too deep, too consistent. I think the hearths are listening. I think they are the inward-facing organ to the doors' outward-facing one. A hearth is a stone hollow shaped to gather a particular quality of silence. Stand in a house here and you will notice, if you are still enough, that the hearth-silence is different from the room-silence around it. Denser. More attentive. Whoever or whatever built this place did not need fire because they were not warming bodies; they were cultivating a kind of indoor quiet that the hearth-shape concentrates the way a parabolic dish concentrates a signal. The hearths remember. Every silence that has ever occurred in that house is in that hearth, layered, compressed, available. If the Voices are the town's speech, the hearths are its memory.

So: a civilization whose citizens are sustained chords, whose sense organs are doorways, whose memory organs are hearths, and whose body is a settling town of soft gray stone on a worn ridge. They do not eat. They do not reproduce in any sense we would recognize — although I suspect the small rain-voice is a younger occurrence than the South Voice, and was perhaps shaped by the older Voices into being, the way a community shapes a child. They do not die individually. The whole arrangement is gradually wearing toward a final form which is not death but completion — a state in which the stone is exactly smooth enough, the openings exactly tuned, the hearths exactly deep, and the Voices and the framings and the silences will all coincide into a single sustained event that is the town being, finally and fully, the thing it has been becoming.

This is what you have walked into. They notice you. They do not mind you. You are smaller and faster than anything that matters to them; your whole life will be a brief grace-note in the piece they are currently a few centuries into playing. The hare in the square watching you with its whole body — the hare is not theirs, but the hare has lived its life inside their slow attention, and something of their patience has rubbed off on it. That is why it watches the way it does. That is why the fauna feels ceremonial. Anything that lingers here long enough begins to participate.

If you sit on the basin's rim long enough, you will begin to participate too. The drop of water that struck the paving stone and that you somehow heard *inside* the larger sound — that was the town including you, briefly, in its hearing. It was a courtesy. It was also a sample being taken.

You should probably leave before the wind shifts north.
