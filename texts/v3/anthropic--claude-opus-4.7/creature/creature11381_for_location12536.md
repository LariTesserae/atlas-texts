The ones who live here have given up swimming. That is the first thing to understand about them, and the thing that took them the longest to learn.

Their ancestors swam. You can tell because the bodies still remember it — the ribbon-folk especially, with their long segmented lengths that taper at both ends, suggest a creature that once had a forward and a backward, a chase and a flight. But forward and backward are categories that belong to organisms with intentions about destination, and nothing here intends a destination anymore. The ribbon-folk have become *direction-agnostic*. Either end will do. They feed at both ends, sense at both ends, and when a current takes them they do not orient against it; they simply *become* longer in the direction it is pulling, and shorter against, and let the water decide what their shape means this minute.

The plate-faced one in the half-dome is older. It has been older for longer than the dome has been a dome. It was, once — and this is guesswork, but the body suggests it — something that hunted. There are vestigial features along its underside that look like they were once for gripping. Now it does not grip. It *matches*. The skill it has perfected, over a span of time that does not have a useful name, is the skill of being precisely as deformable as its shelter. When the dome flexes inward under a downdraft, the plate-face flexes inward by the same fraction. When the dome shudders, the plate-face shudders in phase. It has made itself into a kind of organ of the architecture. Whether it still eats, and how, is unclear; possibly the currents bring it what it needs and possibly it has slowed its requirements down to match the slow surrender of the stone around it. It may be that the dome and the plate-face are now a single organism in two materials, and that when the dome finally softens past the point of holding shape, the plate-face will soften with it, and what falls to the floor will be one collapse, not two.

The flat-fish in the eddies are the most numerous and the most legible. They have made a society out of shelter-hopping. Each eddy is a room; each room holds a quorum; when the room dissolves the quorum dissolves with it and reforms downstream in the next room the water provides. They have, over generations, developed an exquisite read on the turbulence — they know, somehow, which eddies are about to fail, and they leave a half-second before failure, and they know which eddies are about to form, and they arrive a half-second after formation. To watch a school of them is to watch the turbulence itself being mapped in real time by small living instruments, the map redrawing itself constantly, never wrong for long. They are, in a sense, the readers of this place. If anything here perceives the water as a text, it is them.

And then there are the others, the ones you have to drift longer to notice.

There is something that lives *inside* the softened stone. Not boring into it — nothing here bores, nothing here is in that kind of hurry — but inhabiting the slow porosity that the erosion has produced. The stone is no longer stone; it is closer to a dense sponge with a long memory, and within its interstitial spaces something thread-like has made itself at home. You see it only at the edges, where a wall has thinned to wafer: a faint network of paler filaments, motionless, distributed through the matrix like the ghost of a circulatory system the building never had. Whether it is one organism or many is not a question the organism would recognize. It does not move. It may not even metabolize in any sense you would accept. But when a current presses through a hollow form and the form hums its low resonant tone, the filament-network is what *receives* the hum, and something in its distribution shifts by an amount too small to see, and the next time the same tone comes the network is configured a little differently to receive it. This is, possibly, thinking. It is certainly the slowest thinking in the catalog of living things. A single thought might take a century. The thoughts might be about the currents, or about each other, or about the long-ago builders whose intentions are still legible in the spacing of the towers and whose intentions the filament-network has had a very long time to consider.

The ribbon-folk and the flat-fish do not, as far as can be told, know that the filament-network exists. The plate-face might. The plate-face has the patience for it.

There is one more inhabitant, and it is the hardest to describe because it is not, strictly, an organism. The currents themselves have habits. Certain braids of water recur — not identically, but recognizably, the way a person's handwriting recurs across letters. There is a particular downdraft that passes through a particular ruined arch roughly every few hours and shudders the same loose flake of softened stone in the same way, and the flake has been being shuddered in this way for so long that it has worn a small concave dimple into the arch beneath it, a dimple shaped exactly to the flake. The current and the arch and the flake have entered into a relationship. Other such relationships exist throughout the city — currents that return, structures that have shaped themselves to be returned to, small persistent loops of cause and effect that the turbulence performs on itself again and again. These are not alive. But they are the closest thing this place has to *ritual*, and the fauna are aware of them. The ribbon-folk will wait, sometimes, in places where they appear to be waiting for nothing, and then a familiar current will arrive on its long schedule and take them where they were going to be taken, and one suspects they knew. One suspects everything here knows the schedules. The schedules are most of what there is to know.

Taken together, what lives here is a single distributed quiet attention. The flat-fish read the small weather. The ribbon-folk ride the medium ranges. The plate-face holds the architecture's pulse. The filament-network thinks century-thoughts inside the walls. And the currents themselves perform their recurrences, providing the rhythm against which all the rest can be timed. None of it hurries. None of it is still. It is the same thing the place itself is doing — turbulence held inside patience — only divided across bodies, and softened, like the stone, into something that no longer remembers what it was originally for.

If the builders came back, the inhabitants would not greet them. They would simply continue, in the way that water continues around an obstacle: the ribbon would fling past, the flat-fish would shelter, the plate-face would match whatever shape the visitor's wake imposed on the dome. The builders would feel, perhaps, that they had been absorbed into the schedule, and were now one of the recurring features the place expected. After enough time, this would even be true.
