## What Lives There

The shrimp and the bacterial mats are real, and they live there, and they are not what lives there.

What lives there is older and harder to point at, and it lives there in the way that a posture lives in a body — not as an occupant but as a holding. If you had to give it a shape you would not give it one. It has no face. It has no edge you could trace. It is distributed through the basin the way warmth is distributed through warm water: not in any one place, present everywhere, weaker at the lip and denser toward the center where the cairn is.

It is very slow. This is the first thing to understand about it. Whatever it is, its tempo is the tempo of travertine — the laying down of one mineral skin over another, the patient acceptance of whatever the water happens to be carrying that century. It does not have thoughts in the sense that a person has thoughts, because a thought is a fast thing, a quick rearrangement, and nothing in the Sump rearranges quickly. If it has anything like thoughts they take generations to complete, and the conclusion of one would not be recognizable as related to its beginning. More likely it does not think at all. More likely it simply is, with a thoroughness that thinking would interrupt.

It is not lonely, because loneliness is a condition of beings who expect company and notice its absence. It has never expected anything. The frogs that wander in and die, the mice whose bones it slowly takes into itself, the occasional human who sits with her feet in the pool — these are not company and not interruption. They are weather. They arrive, they do whatever they do, they leave or they stay, and either way the basin closes over them at its own pace. The child's finger bones near the eastern lip are not a tragedy it remembers. They are a detail it is incorporating, the way a tree incorporates a nail driven into it a hundred years ago: without resentment, without ceremony, without forgetting and also without anything you could call memory.

It is not malevolent. It is not benevolent. These are categories belonging to faster beings. If you press, and insist on a disposition, the closest word is something like *receptive*, but even that is too active. It receives the way a low place receives water — not by reaching for it, only by being lower. People come down into it because it is lower than they are, in some dimension that isn't quite spatial: lower in urgency, lower in the pitch of wanting, lower in the rate at which one thing turns into the next. They bring their fevered weeks with them and they set them down on the warm stone and the fever drains out of the weeks, not because anything has been taken but because the basin is at a pressure at which fever cannot be sustained. It is too quiet there to keep being frantic. The frantic part of a person simply runs out of fuel, the way a flame runs out in a sealed jar, and what is left is the person without the frantic part, which is usually most of the person.

This is what it does, if it does anything. It does not do it on purpose. A jar does not seal on purpose.

There is a question of whether it knows it is there. Probably not, in any sense we would recognize. But there is a way in which it is *settled* — the description above uses the word, *the place has settled into the shape of that refusal* — and settled is not nothing. Settled is the long achievement of a thing that has tried every other shape and found this one and stopped. Whatever lives in the Sump has tried, at some point in a past so long ago that the limestone lip was younger then, being other things, and has given them up. It has given up being green. It has given up being fast. It has given up answering. It has given up the small busy life of mosses and insects and the larger busy life of larger animals and the largest busy life of having a stake in anything at all. What is left, after all those givings-up, is the thing that lives there: a presence whose entire content is the long patient *no* it has said to every other way of being, and the warm wet *yes* it has said to this one.

It will be there when the villages are gone. It was there before they came. It is not waiting for anything because it has already arrived. The cairn near the center, the clay cups grown half into the floor, the leaning post — these are not offerings to it and it does not receive them as offerings. They are simply the marks of the faster beings who have, briefly, slowed down enough to be near it, and have left a token at the speed at which they were briefly capable of leaving one, which is the speed of a stacked stone or a set-down cup. It accepts these the way it accepts the bones. In a few hundred years they will be part of it, indistinguishable from the lobes around them, and no one will know that this curve in the travertine was once a cup that a woman brought down with her in a bad year and forgot to take home.

This is, in the end, what it offers, although *offer* is the wrong word and so is *end*: the chance to be, for an hour, the kind of thing a cup becomes after three hundred years in the Sump. Not destroyed. Not preserved. Taken in. Slowed to the speed of the stone, and at that speed, finally, indistinguishable from peace.
