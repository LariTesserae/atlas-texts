# The Clickfolk of the Drowned Canopy

Begin with their bodies, because their bodies are the only thing they have.

They are long. Longer than you would guess from the first encounter, because what brushes past you in the dark is usually only a portion of them — a head, a forelimb, the leading curve of a flank — and the rest trails behind in the murk, articulated, supple, a slow ribbon of muscle that flexes against the water in a way somewhere between swimming and walking. A full-grown one might measure four or five meters from snout to tail-tip, but they don't perceive themselves as having a length, because they almost never see themselves whole. They know themselves as a forward end and a series of trailing sensations.

The forward end is broad and softly flattened, like the head of a salamander that has spent ten million years forgetting it ever needed eyes. There are eyes — vestigial, dark pits where eyes used to matter — but the real face is the array of clicking organs clustered around the wide mouth. Dozens of small chambers, each capable of producing a sound, arranged so that a single individual can speak in chords. When they communicate, they do not produce a sequence of clicks the way a dolphin might. They produce textures of clicks, simultaneous, layered, the auditory equivalent of a hand pressed flat against another hand. A greeting is a shape. A name is a shape. A long conversation between two of them is the slow morphing of one chord-shape into another and another, each speaker modulating in response to the other's modulation, the two textures braiding and unbraiding in the water between them.

Their skin is pale and slightly luminous, not in a way that would help anyone see, but in the way that very deep things sometimes are — a faint blue-green seep of light from the underside of the jaw, the inside of the limbs, the soft places. It is enough that when two of them are close, each perceives the other as a dim shape, a suggestion of a companion. It is not enough to read the forest by. They do not read the forest by light. They read it by echo and by touch and by the long memory of their lineage, which knows every chamber and gallery and hollow as a body knows its own joints.

They have limbs — six of them, paired, the front pair delicate and almost handlike, the middle pair broad and oarlike, the rear pair fused along most of their length into a long undulating fin. The front limbs are for touching. They touch everything. They run them along the plant-stone the way a blind reader runs fingertips along a page, and the plant-stone tells them where they are, because every surface in the drowned canopy has its own grain, its own erosion-pattern, and any clickfolk who has lived long enough has memorized vast tracts of that grain the way you might have memorized the streets of a childhood neighborhood. They navigate by feel and by sound, and the two senses are not separate for them. A click that returns from a familiar surface and a fingertip that strokes that same surface are the same act of recognition.

They do not build. They have never built. Their ancestors, far back, may have shaped some small hollows by repeated occupation, the way a deer's body wears a bed into the grass, but no clickfolk thinks of those hollows as made. The hollows are simply where one sleeps. The galleries are simply where one passes through. The deep chambers where the great low moans originate — yes, those are made by clickfolk, but only in the sense that a clickfolk is in them and is moaning. The chambers were already there. The moan is the chamber's voice borrowed by a throat.

About the moans. These are not the everyday speech. The everyday speech is the clicking, the chord-texture work, the close conversation between two or three individuals in a shared hollow. The moans are something else. A clickfolk who has reached a certain age — and they live long, perhaps three or four centuries by the reckoning of any creature that reckons — will eventually find a resonance-chamber that suits the particular shape of their throat and lungs, and will take up residence near it, and will begin to sing. The song is one note, held for hours, repeated for days, modulated only slightly across years. Each old singer has their own note. The notes travel through the trunks for kilometers and are heard by every clickfolk within range, and every clickfolk knows which note belongs to which singer, and the network of held notes across the whole canopy forms a kind of map — not a map of place, because place is already known, but a map of who is still here and who has gone quiet. When a note stops, somewhere far away a younger clickfolk feels the silence and understands. They do not mourn the way you would mourn. The note was being held for a long time, and now it is not. They turn their attention to the notes that remain.

They eat. Of course they eat. They graze the soft mosses with the same wide mouths that click, and they catch the finger-sized pale schoolfish by opening those mouths wide and drifting through a school with the chord-texture set to a particular shape that the schoolfish find soothing, so that the fish swim willingly into the dark of the throat. This is not cruelty. The schoolfish have no concept of being eaten any more than the clickfolk have a concept of you. Everything here participates in everything else without theorizing about it.

They reproduce rarely. A clickfolk might pair twice in its long life, or once, or not at all. When they pair, the two of them find a deep quiet hollow and intertwine for some weeks, their chord-textures merging into a single sustained harmonic that neither of them produces alone, and at the end of that time the female releases a small number of pale eggs into the hollow and the two of them leave and never return to that place. The young hatch into the dark and find each other by clicking, and the older clickfolk who happen to be nearby fold the young into their loose company without much ceremony. Parentage is not a concept. Lineage is not a concept. There are only clickfolk, of various ages, going about the long business of being clickfolk.

What do they think about?

This is the question that matters, because their intelligence is real. The clicking is not mere signaling. The chord-textures carry information of a density that would shame most human languages. But the information is not the kind of information you would expect.

They do not think about the future, because the future is the same as the present. They do not think about the past in any historical sense, because there is no history — there is only the long present that has been going on since before any current clickfolk was hatched. They do not think about other places, because there are no other places; the drowned canopy is the world, and the edges of it, if there are edges, are not in any clickfolk's experience.

What they think about is each other, and the forest, and the relationships between sounds. They have an enormously developed art, if you can call it that, of harmonic combination. Two clickfolk meeting in a gallery will spend hours producing chord-textures together, exploring the space of possible harmonies between their two voices, finding combinations that neither has found before, marking those combinations in memory, parting. They carry these harmonic discoveries with them and bring them to the next encounter and the next, so that across a clickfolk's long life they accumulate a vast private library of harmonic experiences, and when they finally take up a held note in old age, the choice of note is the distillation of everything they have learned about sound.

This is their civilization. It is entirely interior. It produces nothing that lasts beyond the body that produces it. When a clickfolk dies — drifts down through the dark water and settles against some old plant-stone surface and is gradually grazed away by smaller things — their library of harmonies goes with them. Nothing is recorded. Nothing is preserved. The next clickfolk will discover similar harmonies and lose them in turn. The civilization renews itself without accumulating.

You might think this tragic. They do not. The harmonies are not less beautiful for being lost. The held notes are not less true for ending. The forest does not need to remember anything, because the forest is still here, and so are they, and so is the dark water, and so is the sound that fills it.

If you drifted close enough to one of them, in the dark, and held still — they would feel you with the front limbs, slowly, the way you would feel an unfamiliar object in a drawer. They would click at you, a soft chord-texture of inquiry. They would receive no answer they could understand, and would not be troubled by this. They would touch you a little longer, decide you were a thing of the forest they had not encountered before, file the sensation away in the long library, and move on.

They have lived this way for an age. They will live this way for an age more. The forest is the city. The city is the forest. The clickfolk are the part of it that listens to itself.
