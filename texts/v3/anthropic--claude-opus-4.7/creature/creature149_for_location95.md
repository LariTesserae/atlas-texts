# The Inhabitants of the Engine-Garden

## The Caretaker

There is one, and there has always been one, and the question of whether it is the same one is not a question the garden knows how to ask.

The Caretaker is small — smaller than a human, probably, judging by the gauge of the catwalks worn smooth in a pattern that suggests a narrower gait than ours. It moves at night, or in cloud, or in the interval when a visitor has turned their back to look at something; it has an uncanny sense for the averted gaze. It has been seen perhaps four times in the last century, each time only partially, and each account disagrees about everything except two details: the hands, which are long and many-jointed and stained dark to the wrist with a grease that smells of sulfur and violets, and the gait, which is perfectly in time with the pistons. BOOM, step. BOOM, step. It has been walking in that rhythm so long that if the Engine ever stopped, the Caretaker would probably fall over.

It carries, or wears, or perhaps is partly made of, a small toolkit: a curved oil-can with a spout like an ibis's beak, a pair of shears for the vines, a length of copper wire, a soft cloth, and something else that clicks softly and is not a tool for anything anyone understands. Its clothes, if they are clothes, are the color of old bronze and fit it exactly.

It does not eat, so far as anyone has determined. It does not sleep in any place that has been found. It speaks to the frogs — this has been observed once, by a pilgrim who saw a bent figure crouched in the ferns at dusk, murmuring something the Engine drowned out, and a thousand translucent throats had stopped their breathing for the duration of the speech and started again, in unison, when the figure stood. The pilgrim left and did not return, and would not, afterward, describe what the Caretaker's face had looked like when it turned.

The theological position is that the Caretaker is the last of the builders, or the first of whatever the builders became, or a thing the Engine has grown for itself the way the garden grew, patiently, over centuries, to tend its own tending. None of these positions is incompatible with the others.

## The Frog-Choir

They are not frogs. They resemble frogs. They have the body plan and the wet skin and the sticky fingers, but their interiors, in the rare specimens that have been examined, contain small dense organs of a metallic character and a heart that beats only when the piston falls — between beats, they are, by any meaningful measure, dead, and then the piston falls and they are alive again, and they have been doing this for a hundred generations without noticing the interruption.

They do not mate in any way that has been observed. New frogs simply appear in the ferns, slightly translucent at first and gaining opacity over weeks, as if condensing out of the steam. Old frogs disappear similarly: one day their throat does not inflate on the downbeat, and by the next piston-fall they have dissolved into the substrate, contributing their small quantity of iron and graphite back to the soil.

They are the Engine's lungs, or its metronome, or its congregation. They are almost certainly all three.

## The Swifts

The swifts are the only inhabitants of the garden who seem to have a life of their own — who come and go, who hunt, who raise young in the upper pipework with the ordinary selfishness of birds. They are not synchronized to the Engine. They are loud in the only way anything here can be loud against the Engine, which is to say not loud at all, but present: their chittering in the high spaces is the garden's one small human-scaled sound.

They are, perhaps, the garden's guests rather than its residents. They were not made. They found the warmth and stayed. In the theology of the place, they are considered auspicious; a garden without swifts would be a garden whose wildness had finally been fully metabolized, and no one wishes to see that.

They nest in the same four or five places, generation after generation, and the nests have become small architectural features — ragged cones of fiber and spider-silk and the odd filament of copper wire the birds have pilfered from somewhere below. The Caretaker does not disturb the nests. This, too, is noted.

## The Clockwork Moths

They are the strangest of the small things and the least studied, because they fly only at dusk and dusk here is brief. Their wings bear a pattern that is not *like* clockwork but *is* clockwork, in the sense that the markings on a given moth correspond to an actual, functioning escapement visible under magnification — tiny gears and pallets rendered in pigment, every tooth in the right place. No one knows what timepiece these patterns describe. They are not all the same. Each moth carries, on its wings, the schematic of a mechanism, and these mechanisms, collated across the moth population, may or may not constitute a complete plan of something. A pilgrim-entomologist spent eleven years photographing moth wings and died with her work unfinished. Her notebooks are kept at a monastery in the foothills and consulted by no one.

The moths cling to the warm tanks by day and do not move. At dusk they rise in a silent shimmering cloud, perform a slow turning dance above the platform for perhaps twenty minutes, and settle again. They do not feed. They do not, apparently, reproduce. They have been counted, imperfectly, at roughly the same number — around four hundred — for as long as anyone has counted them.

## The Mice

The mice are the garden's joke, if the garden makes jokes. They are ordinary mice, of an ordinary mountain species, which have colonized the platform as mice colonize everything, and which have been altered by the Engine only in the one particular of their movement: they walk in slow pulses, four or five seconds per pulse, a small scurry and then stillness and then a small scurry again, as if the Engine's rhythm had entrained their nervous systems across generations. They do not seem to know they do this. When removed from the platform (this has been tried) they resume ordinary mouse-motion within a day. When returned, they re-entrain within an hour.

They eat fig-seeds and fern-spores and the occasional moth that has failed its dusk dance. They are preyed on by the swifts. They are the garden's only real disorder, and the garden tolerates them, and the Caretaker, who could presumably remove them, does not.

## The Vines

It is necessary, finally, to say that the vines are inhabitants, not furniture. They grow, which plants do, but they also *choose* — they have, over decades, learned which conduits are warmest and route themselves accordingly, and this learning is preserved across the vine as a whole, so that a cutting transplanted to a new section of pipework will find the warm route within weeks rather than years. Whether this constitutes thought is a question for people who enjoy such questions. What it constitutes, practically, is a slow distributed intelligence that maps the Engine's thermal geography and is, perhaps, the Engine's own way of knowing where it is warm.

The vines do not flower. They fruit, occasionally, small hard green fruits that no creature in the garden eats and that fall eventually into the substrate and are absorbed. What the fruits are for, if they are for anything, is not known. The fruits may be the product. The garden may, in two or three hundred years, be finished when it has produced the right fruit. Or the right number of fruits. Or a fruit of a particular kind that has not yet appeared.

---

All of these together — Caretaker, frogs, swifts, moths, mice, vines, and the uncounted smaller things in the substrate that the garden has not yet revealed — constitute the inhabitants. They do not form a community in any sense they would recognize. They form a process. The process is slow and the process is loud and the process is, by every available measure, nearly but not quite complete.

BOOM. The frogs swell. The Caretaker, somewhere, oils a bearing. A moth adjusts its grip on a warm tank. A mouse takes one small step. A vine, over the course of a season, grows half a centimeter toward a conduit it has decided it prefers. A swift returns with an insect for its young.

And the garden holds, and the Engine turns, and whatever is being made is being made.
