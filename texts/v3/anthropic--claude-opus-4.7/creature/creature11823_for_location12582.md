# The Inhabitants

## The Drinkers

Begin with the smallest, because there are the most of them, and because everything else in the Sumpworks is, in some sense, a way of being a drinker that got more complicated.

The thumbnail-sized six-legged things on the water are called nothing — no one is here to call them anything — but if you watch one for an hour you will understand its whole life. It is a flat disc of pale chitin with six hair-fine legs splayed at the surface tension, and a mouth on its underside that is really just a pore. It does not swim. It does not even walk, exactly; it lets the runnel carry it, and where the light is thickest it stops, and the pore opens, and a thread of glow goes up into it and disperses through its body like ink in water. When it is full it glows visibly for some hours, a tiny lamp drifting on a vein of brighter lamp. Then it dims. Then it drinks again.

It does not reproduce in any way you would recognize. Sometimes two of them touch on the water and afterwards there are three; sometimes one of them simply comes apart at a seam and there are two, both slightly smaller, both immediately drinking. There is no courtship, no egg, no young. The population is more or less constant and has been for a span of time the stones cannot remember.

They are the base of nothing. Nothing eats them, because nothing here needs to eat them — the light is freely available to anything with a mouth, and the drinkers offer no concentration worth pursuing. They are not prey. They are not food. They are a way the water has of being more thoroughly drunk than it would otherwise be.

## The Waders

The long-necked translucent thing — there are perhaps a few hundred of these in any walking-day's worth of city — is the second form. It is roughly the size of a heron and shaped a little like one, but boneless; it moves on four soft pads, and its neck is a tube of skin around a column of slow muscle, and its head is barely a head, just a swelling where the mouth is.

It eats silt. It wades the deeper runnels and the cistern margins and dips its face and swallows, and you can watch the meal travel down it because it is mostly transparent, and the silt distributes itself through the wader's body over the next hour, settling into a dim diffuse glow that makes the creature, at rest, look like a paper lantern.

The waders are the closest thing the Sumpworks has to a presence. They are tall enough to notice you. They do notice you — they will turn the swelling of the head toward you, and hold there a long moment, and then go back to wading. Whatever they are doing with the regard, it is not curiosity in any sense a person would feel curiosity. It is more like the way a thermometer notices a room.

They do not fight. They do not mate. When one of them finally fails — they do fail, eventually, after lifespans no one could measure — it simply lies down in a runnel and over a few weeks comes apart into the water, the silt of its body rejoining the silt of the current, the light it carried going back into general circulation. The other waders step over the dissolving one without ceremony. They are not callous. There is just nothing to do.

## The Cucumber-Cats

These are the strange ones, and the most worth watching, because they are the ones with something that might be called behavior.

They are eyeless, soft, the size described — cat-sized, sea-cucumber-shaped — and they live in the cracks and the half-collapsed lower storeys and the cisterns. They have a sense of the world that seems to be partly touch, partly pressure, partly some response to the light they cannot see but can apparently feel against their skin, the way you feel sun on a closed eyelid. They are warm to the touch, slightly warmer than the stone, which is the only warm thing in the Sumpworks.

They do not eat the light directly. They eat the slime, and the slime hosts the light, and so the light passes through them too, but slowly, by a longer road. Their insides glow a deep slow orange rather than the cold blue-white of the runnels. When one of them passes over your foot you can sometimes feel the warmth of its core through the soft outer body, a single banked ember moving across your instep.

They come out when the light dims. This is the closest thing to a schedule in the Sumpworks, and it isn't a schedule, because the dimming has no pattern — but when a district goes dark, the cucumber-cats pour out of the cracks in numbers, dozens, sometimes hundreds, and they do whatever it is they do, which is mostly: arrange themselves. They lie in patterns. Not patterns a person would call beautiful, necessarily, but patterns — concentric, radial, sometimes long lines down a street like a row of slow commas. They lie like that for hours. When the light returns, they disperse without hurry and go back into the cracks and the patterns are gone.

What the patterns are for, if they are for anything, no one knows. They could be a kind of communication. They could be a kind of rest. They could be the last echo of something the builders taught them, or something the builders were, before they became this. The cucumber-cats are not telling.

If anything in the Sumpworks is the descendant of whoever built it, it is probably the cucumber-cats. There is a way they hold still that is too still — a composure that feels less animal than architectural — and there is the matter of the patterns, and there is the matter of how, very rarely, you will see one of them pause on a threshold and seem to consider the doorway before it goes through. Not look at it. Consider it. As though there were a lintel-memory somewhere in its body, and the threshold woke it.

This is probably a fancy. The cucumber-cats are probably just cucumber-cats. But the Sumpworks invites fancies, and that one is hard to put down.

## The Slime

You said it: a colony, a smear, a thing that does not reproduce so much as continue. But it is more populous than any of the discrete animals, and in a strict accounting it is the largest inhabitant of the Sumpworks by mass, by spread, by years lived.

In the wet places it is a thin gelatinous mat, faintly phosphorescent, a paler echo of the runnel-light. In drier places it crusts and dims and looks dead, but it is not dead; rewet a dry patch and within a day it greens up again, faintly, and resumes its slow smear. It moves perhaps an inch a month, on average. Some patches move faster. Some patches haven't moved at all in any time worth measuring, and the limestone beneath them is etched in their exact outline, as though the slime had been pressed into the rock like a seal.

It eats the light. It also is the light, in a sense, because the light needs something to live in, and the slime is the largest hospitable medium in the city. The runnels carry the light, but the slime *holds* it, the way a battery holds a charge. When a district dims, it is usually because the slime in that district is taking up more than its share. When the light comes back, it is usually because the slime is letting go.

The slime, in other words, is the breath of the Sumpworks. The capillary brightening and dimming you observed is the slime exhaling and inhaling on its own schedule, which is not a schedule, which is whatever the slime is doing with itself when no one is looking, which is most of the time.

And here is the thing about the slime: it has a five-fingered handprint at its edges, sometimes, where it has crept up a wall. Not always. Not often. But sometimes, if you look very carefully at the leading edge of a patch on a flat surface, you can see that the smear has fingers. Four and a thumb. A palm-shape behind them. As though, very slowly, over years, a hand were reaching.

This might mean nothing. The slime takes many shapes at its edges; it makes scallops, fans, branching ferns, whatever the surface and the moisture push it toward. The hands could be coincidence. They almost certainly are coincidence.

They are the only thing in the Sumpworks that has ever made me want to leave.

## What Lives Here

So: drinkers on the water, waders in the deeper places, cucumber-cats in the cracks, slime on every wet surface, and the light itself, which is alive in some sense that does not require it to be an organism.

That is the fauna. That is the flora. That is the inhabitants list.

But notice what is missing. There are no predators. There is no competition. There is no scarcity — the light is abundant, the silt is abundant, the slime is abundant, and everything that eats anything has plenty. There is no breeding pressure, no territorial dispute, no alarm call, no flight response. The animals do not flinch from the collapses because there is nothing for a flinch to accomplish; if the stone takes you, the stone takes you, and meanwhile the light is right there to drink.

It is an ecosystem with the urgency taken out. Every relationship that, on the surface, would be tense — predator and prey, parent and young, mate and rival — has here been smoothed to a kind of standing wave. The drinkers drink. The waders wade. The cucumber-cats arrange themselves in the dimness. The slime breathes. The light moves through all of them and returns to the water and moves through them again. Nothing accumulates. Nothing depletes. Nothing wins.

This is why it is so quiet. Not because the animals are stealthy or secretive — they aren't, they are right out in the open, they will pour across your foot — but because there is nothing to call about. Calls are for warning, for finding, for claiming, for mourning. None of those apply here. The Sumpworks animals have no use for voices and so they have, over a span of time the stones cannot remember, simply stopped having them.

## And the Builders

You asked, implicitly, about the builders. I do not know. No one knows. The hand-marks are old past counting and the city does not miss them and the slime sometimes has fingers at its edges and the cucumber-cats sometimes pause at a threshold and consider it. Make of that what you will.

What I can say is this: whatever lives here now is not waiting for the builders to come back. If the builders are gone, the city has absorbed the loss. If the builders are the slime, the slime is not in any hurry to be anything else. If the builders are the cucumber-cats, they have forgotten almost everything, and the little they remember — the lintel, the pattern, the threshold — they remember in a way that asks nothing of anyone.

The Sumpworks is inhabited. It is fully, densely, redundantly inhabited, by things that drink and wade and pour and smear and glow. It is just inhabited at a tempo so slow, and a volume so low, and a stake so absent, that it can look, to a quick visitor, like nothing lives here at all.

Sit on the doorstep. Wait. The ember will pour across your foot. That is the city introducing itself, in the only voice it has left, which is the voice of *we are here, we are not using it, you may be here too.*
