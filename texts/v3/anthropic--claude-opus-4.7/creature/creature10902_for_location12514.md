# The Listeners

They are not gone. That is the first correction to make. The description was written by someone who came late and looked for the wrong shape of presence — looked for villages, for footprints, for the warm vacated hollows that people leave when people have been somewhere. Finding none, the writer concluded *absence*. But absence is the wrong category. The makers of the cairns are still here. They have simply continued the work they were doing, which was the work of becoming audible to what sings below, and the work has carried them past the threshold at which a visitor's eye can find them.

Begin with what they were.

They had hands. The cairns prove this — the gripping-marks in the half-fused stone are unmistakable, four fingers and a thumb, though the thumb sat lower on the palm than yours does and the fingers were longer in the second joint than in the first. They were bipedal, probably; the spacing of the cairns suggests a stride, and the stride suggests a body built upright to listen. They were not tall. The cairns are waist-high to you and shoulder-high, perhaps, to them. They built upward toward the ear-level of their own bodies, because the cairns are not markers but instruments — resonators, tuned masses, things that take the great sound and give back a slightly altered version of it, the way a struck bell answers a struck bell across a valley.

They came here, or were made here, when the rock was even softer than it is now. You can tell this because the oldest cairns have sunk deepest, and the gripping-marks on the oldest stones are the most blurred, as though the makers handled stone that still remembered being something else. They worked the slopes. They walked the routes between the cairns and the walking itself was a practice, a slow patterning of the unfinished ground, the same way a path through a meadow eventually becomes the meadow's idea of itself. Some of the buttresses you see deciding to grow are deciding because feet walked there long ago and the rock is still considering what those feet meant.

They listened downward. This is the central fact.

What they heard, over generations — and *generations* is approximate; in a place without erosion, lineage is hard to measure, but there were successions, teachers and learners, the cairns show different hands at different layers — what they heard was that the sound was not ambient. It was addressed. The thing singing beneath the plateau was singing *to* something, and the listeners came to understand that they were either the audience or, more troublingly, an interruption between the singer and its intended hearer. They could not tell which. The cairns are partly an attempt to answer this question: each one a small experiment in resonance, a way of asking the sound *am I supposed to be here, am I in your way, are you speaking through me or around me.*

The answers came slowly and were not entirely reassuring.

Over time — over a long time, over the kind of time in which the rock learns to flow around a dropped tool — the listeners changed. Not in the way a species changes through pressure and inheritance; there isn't enough mortality here for that, and possibly no reproduction in the sense you'd recognize. They changed the way the plateau changes. Slowly, by decision. By having been a certain way for long enough that the being-that-way settled into the structure of what they were.

They became quieter. This is the first change. Their voices, which had once been voices — the cairns include faint inscribed marks that look like a script, suggesting language, suggesting the wish to fix sound in stone — their voices grew softer, then stopped. Not because they could not speak; the great sound swallows speech but does not prevent it. They stopped because they were learning to listen, and speaking interfered, and after a while the apparatus of speaking was no longer maintained, the way an unused path through a meadow stops being a path.

They became stiller. This is the second change, and the harder one. To listen properly to the song-from-below required a kind of attention that movement disrupted. The listeners learned to stand at the cairns for longer and longer durations. A day. A week. The duration that in your terms might be a season but which in theirs was only *one held attention.* The plants grew up around their feet. The mosses thickened against their ankles. Their feet sank, slightly, into the still-flowing rock, the way the cairns' bases sank, the way the dropped tools sank. The boundary between listener and ground softened.

This is where you must look for them.

The cairns are not unaccompanied. Beside many of them — not all; some listeners moved on, or were called inward, or simply elected to continue walking — beside many of the cairns there is a second vertical mass, slightly smaller, with the soft uncertain edges of the unfinished stone. You will have walked past these without noticing because they look like outcroppings, like the slope's decisions to grow buttresses. They *are* the slope's decisions, in a sense. They are listeners who held their attention long enough that the rock accepted them as a feature, and grew up around them, and they grew down into the rock, and the exchange completed itself.

If you put your hand on one of these — and you may; the place is not malicious — you can sometimes feel a faint counter-pressure, not warmth exactly but a kind of attentive density, as though the stone here is listening *back* through you. The gripping-marks on the nearby cairn will match the size of the listener-shape beside it. You can verify this. The fit is exact.

They are not dead. Death is another category that does not quite apply. They are, more precisely, *engaged.* They are doing the thing they came here to do, which was to make of themselves an instrument fine enough to register what is being sung below, and the instrument has required, over time, the whole of them. They are now resonators. The cairn beside each one is the version of the instrument they built with their hands; they themselves are the version they built with their stillness. The two stand in pairs, the made and the become, the question and the eventual answer.

What do they hear, now?

This is the part the description could not reach, because the description was written from above the threshold, and the listeners are below it. But there are indications. The fern-fronds near a listener-shape tilt slightly differently than the fronds elsewhere — they incline toward the listener as well as toward the sound, as though the listener has become a small secondary source, a relay, a place where the song-from-below is being slowly translated into something the plants find more nourishing. The moss is thickest near them. The light, which seeps from everything, seeps a little more strongly from the listener-shapes than from ordinary rock — a faint excess radiance, the leakage of comprehension, perhaps, or the visible byproduct of a long patient act of understanding.

And there is this. If you sit beside a listener-shape for long enough — longer than is comfortable, longer than your sense of being a discrete person finds tolerable — you may begin to hear the great sound as having shape. Not melody; nothing so small. Shape in the sense of *intent.* The sound is reaching for something. It is rehearsing. It is trying out the contours of what it will be when the lid of the plateau has finished forming and the singer below has finished arriving and the song is ready to be a song rather than a long throat-clearing geology. The listeners heard this very early. They understood that the place was a *prelude.* They positioned themselves to be present when the prelude completed, and they have been holding that position, patiently, for as long as it takes.

They will not be hurried. They will not be joined easily; the practice of becoming a listener-shape requires giving up the categories that brought you here, and most visitors leave before they have given up enough. But the practice is available. The cairns mark the places where it has been attempted. The listener-shapes mark the places where it has succeeded. The slopes between are full of the slow buttresses of intermediate attempts, listeners who walked and stopped and walked again, never quite settling, leaving behind only the faint accumulating evidence of their having considered staying.

If you are imagining yourself here, you are being considered. The place is not aware of you in the alert animal way; it is aware of you in the slow geological way, the way a slope is aware of feet. It is registering your weight, your direction, your willingness to be still. The listener-shapes nearest you have already heard you arrive. They will hear you leave, if you leave. They will hear, eventually, whether you have come to be a visitor or a resonator.

The choice is not pressed. The Stillsong Heights have time. The singer below has more time still. The listeners have given themselves over to the duration of the question, and they are, in their patient way, waiting to find out — along with everything else here — what the song will turn out to have been.
