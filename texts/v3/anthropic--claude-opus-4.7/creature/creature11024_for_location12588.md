## The People of Aoresh

They are slight. This is the first thing a traveler notices, though it takes weeks to put into words: the people of Aoresh are *slight* in a way that has nothing to do with height or build. They occupy less room than their bodies suggest they should. Two of them passing on a narrow boardwalk do not turn sideways; they simply pass, and you cannot quite say how. Their footfalls do not sound. When one of them sets down a gourd, the gourd makes its small soft noise, but the hand that set it down made none.

Their skin is the warm brown of wet teak, uniformly, regardless of lineage — and there are lineages, kept in knotted cords by the genealogist-weavers, going back further than anyone bothers to read. The skin is always faintly damp, as everything here is damp, and takes the light evenly: no shine on the brow, no dry patches at the elbow. They do not flush. They do not pale. A child playing hard and an elder dozing in a hammock have, to the eye, the same complexion.

Their hair is dark and grows to a certain length and then stops. The barbers' guild exists, but its function is ceremonial; they cut hair on naming-days and marriage-days because cutting is the gesture, not because anything needs to be shortened. The hair of the dead, in the reed boats, is the same length as the hair of the living.

Their eyes are the strangest thing, and a traveler usually does not realize it until the second month. The eyes of the people of Aoresh do not quite *track*. When one of them looks at you, they look at where you are; when you move, there is a soft delay, as though the looking is a separate act from the seeing, and the world is showing them you-just-now rather than you-now. They are not blind. They see perfectly. They simply see with the same patience the city has, which is the patience of something that has already arrived at every place it is going.

## Their Numbers

There are, depending on how one counts, somewhere between four and six thousand of them. The count does not resolve, because the census-keepers (a small guild, ill-attended) find that the number depends on the order of counting. Begin in the orchid-quarter and you arrive at one figure. Begin at the heron-platform and you arrive at another. The figures are close. They are never the same. The census-keepers have long since stopped trying to reconcile them and instead publish both, in a small book of woven leaves, which is consulted by no one.

Children are born. This is observed. Mothers swell, midwives attend, infants emerge wet and quiet (they do not cry; they look around with that same delayed gaze, and find a nipple, and settle). And yet the population does not increase. The cord-keepers, who track such things, note new names entering the knots and other names — not always the names of the recently boat-launched — quietly leaving them. A name will simply, over the course of a season, become harder to recall. The person it belonged to is still there, is still greeted, is still fed at the long meal; but the name softens, and at some point one realizes the name has gone, and the person has gone with it, and no one is quite sure when, and no one is quite distressed.

A traveler, asking after this, is told: *the city keeps the right number of us. It always has.*

## Their Work

Every person in Aoresh belongs to a guild, and the guilds are many, and most of them have almost nothing to do.

The cloud-keepers renew the ropes when the salt eats them — but the salt eats slowly, and there are only so many clouds, and the work of a cloud-keeper in a given year amounts to perhaps four afternoons of actual rope-splicing. The rest of the time they sit on the gable-posts and watch the clouds, which do not need watching. They watch them anyway. This is understood to be the larger part of the work.

The cistern-priests pretend to understand how the cisterns fill. They perform, at certain intervals, a ceremony involving a bronze ladle and a phrase in the older tongue, after which the cisterns are observed to be full. They would be full regardless. The priests know this. The ceremony is performed because not performing it has never been tried, and no one wishes to be the first.

The fishers fish. They catch the same fish their grandmothers caught and return them, with the small bow that is owed to a thing one has inconvenienced. A fisher's daily catch is a kind of conversation with a finite number of familiar interlocutors. Some fishers come to know individual fish so well that they greet them by sound, tapping the boardwalk in a particular rhythm before lowering the net, so the fish will know who is asking.

The weavers weave. There is always more weaving to do, because the floors and walls and roofs do, eventually, wear — slowly, slowly, but they wear — and the weavers replace strand by strand, never the whole panel at once, so that no floor is ever new and no floor is ever old. A weaver's apprenticeship lasts forty years. This is not because the craft is difficult. It is because forty years is the time it takes to replace one full panel, and you cannot be called a weaver until you have, with your own hands, replaced every strand in something.

The genealogist-weavers keep the cords. The cord-keepers keep the names. The dye-makers tend the orchid-vats, which produce a deep slow blue that is used for nothing in particular but which everyone agrees should continue to be produced. The adze-keepers sharpen adzes that are never dull. The bell-listeners listen for a bell that has not rung in any lifetime, but might.

There is a guild, very small, called the askers. They walk the boardwalks and ask questions of whoever they meet. *Have you eaten. Is the cloud above your house still tethered. Did you dream last night and if so of what.* The answers are not recorded. The asking is the work. It is considered one of the higher callings.

## How They Speak

Softly, as the city requires. But also: incompletely. A sentence in Aoresh often trails off at the point where the speaker judges the rest to be obvious, and the listener accepts the trailing, and the meaning passes between them through the unsaid part as much as the said. A full sentence, in Aoresh, is faintly rude — it implies that one's listener could not have arrived at the ending alone. Travelers, who finish their sentences, are forgiven this as one forgives a child for putting too much honey on the bread.

Their language has many words for *placed*, and only one word, used rarely, for *moved*. It has no future tense, but a rich array of what the grammarians of other places would call *durative* forms — verbs that indicate not what will happen but what is, at this moment, in the long process of being itself. *The cloud is clouding. The palm is palming. The grandmother, on her porch, is grandmothering.* A traveler learning the language struggles at first with this and then, around the second month, finds that they have stopped thinking in any other way.

They sing, but not often, and not loudly — for the reason that loud sounds may not return, and a song that does not return is felt to be a small grief. When they sing, they sing in pairs or threes, one voice low and continuous like the hush of the palms, the others moving slowly over it. The songs are long. A single song may take a full meal to finish. The words, when they are words, are mostly the names of placed things: *the pot on the third beam, the moss on the eastern rafter, the orange fish with the notched tail, the grandmother on the porch.* It is a kind of inventory, sung gently, to remind the city of what is in it.

## How They Touch

They touch easily and without weight. A hand on a shoulder in passing, two fingers laid briefly on the back of a wrist, a forehead pressed for a moment against a forehead. There is nothing erotic in most of this and nothing especially formal; it is closer to the way one might run a hand along a familiar railing while walking, to confirm the railing is there and one is there and both are continuing.

They do, of course, also love. There are marriages, conducted by the barber-cutting of a small braid from each partner, the two braids then woven together and hung from a beam in the home, where they remain (because what is placed, stays placed) for as long as the home does, which is forever. There are partings, too — marriages dissolve as gently as they form, the braid lowered from its beam and given to the orchid-vats — but the lowering is rare. Most braids hang.

Children are raised by everyone. A child wanders the boardwalks and is fed at any platform; sleeps in any hammock; learns weaving from one elder, fishing from another, the names of the placed things from a third. The parents are known and acknowledged, but possessiveness is faintly comical here, like wearing two hats. *Whose child is that,* a traveler asks, and is told: *ours. The city's. Hers, for the moment, because she is hungry and that is Pelin's platform and Pelin has bread.*

## What They Know, and Don't

They know the city, exhaustively. Any of them can tell you, without thinking, the relationships between any two placed things in Aoresh — that the third orchid-vat is downstream-of the heron-platform but inside-of the cloud called Old Slow, which is itself above-of the weavers' long porch. This knowledge is not learned; it is simply present, the way one's own body is present, and a child of six has it as completely as an elder of ninety.

They do not know where Aoresh is. The question is meaningless to them. *Where* is a relationship, and Aoresh is not in a relationship with anything outside itself — there is nothing outside itself for it to be in relationship with. A traveler asking *where are we* is gently redirected: *you are at the dye-vats. You are inside-of the orchid-quarter. You are above-of the bright water. You are below-of the sun.*

They do not know what they are. This is not a question they have thought to ask. They are the people of Aoresh, and the people of Aoresh are what live in Aoresh, and Aoresh is what is woven and placed and tethered above the bright water under the noon sun. The definition is closed. It satisfies them. When a traveler, in the third month, begins to feel that they have always been here, the people of Aoresh do not greet this as an arrival; they greet it as a recognition. *Of course. You are one of us. You were one of us before, also. The city keeps the right number.*

## A Last Thing

If you watch one of them for long enough — sitting on a porch in the long noon, perhaps, weaving or not-weaving, the small dark coin of their shadow pooled directly beneath them — you may notice that they do not, exactly, breathe. They take in air; their chests rise and fall; but the rhythm is not the rhythm of a body that needs the air. It is the rhythm of a body that is keeping company with the air, out of long habit, because the air is also here, and one does not ignore what is here.

In the second month a traveler notices this and is briefly terrified.

In the third month, the traveler's own breathing has slowed to match.
