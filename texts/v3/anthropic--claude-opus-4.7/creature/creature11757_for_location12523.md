# The People of Vell Maranth

They are not one people. That has to be said first, because from outside — from the rare traveler's account, from the trade-tongue rumors that circulate in the lowland cities — there is a tendency to speak of *the Maranthi* as if the mountain produced a single kind of person. It does not. What it produces is a particular kind of belonging, and many kinds of people belong to it.

Still. There are things you can say.

**They are not tall.** Generations of stairs and low galleries and ducking under iron crossbeams have selected, gently, for a build that is compact and durable rather than long-limbed. The women of the engine quarters in particular tend toward a kind of dense, balanced strength — they can carry a copper pipe-length up four flights without setting it down to rest — and the men of the upper gardens, who spend their lives among the light glass and the citron trees, are often slighter, browner, with the squinting eyes of people who work in brightness. But these are tendencies, not rules. There are tall Maranthi and there are heavy Maranthi and there are Maranthi whose families have intermarried so thoroughly across the levels that they are simply themselves, unplaceable.

**Their skin tones run a long range** because the mountain is vertical and the sun is not equally distributed. The lower-vault people, who tend the cisterns and the foundation works and rarely climb above the eighth level, are pale in a particular way — not the pallor of sickness but the soft mineral pallor of those who live in lamp-light and reflected water-shine. They have a tradition of fine line-tattooing in indigo along the forearms and the backs of the hands, patterns that are said to map the cistern systems though most of the patterns no longer correspond to any cistern that exists. The middle-level people, the engine-tenders and loom-workers and goat-keepers, are the broadest mixture, ruddy and weather-touched. The upper-terrace people are dark from the sun, and wear loose white and pale-blue linens against the glare, and their children go nearly naked through the conservatory glass until they are old enough to mind it.

**They have a relationship to work that is unlike what you might recognize.** A Maranthi does not, generally, *have a job* in the sense of a single occupation pursued for wages. A Maranthi has *responsibilities*, plural, distributed across the day and the year and the levels. A woman who tends the looms on the seventeenth terrace in the mornings may in the afternoon descend to the cisterns to take her turn on the pump-handle, and in the evening climb to the shrine of the fourteenth bell to refill the oil reservoirs, and once a month she will sit with the council of her stair (every staircase has a council; this is taken for granted) to discuss whatever the stair needs discussing. None of this is paid. None of it is unpaid either. The mountain has a kind of vast slow ledger, mostly kept in the heads of older people, and what you give to it returns to you as figs, as fish, as a fresh tile when yours cracks, as the help of six neighbors when your child is sick.

There are specialists, of course. The lift-singers are specialists, and their counting-song is taught from one generation of singers to the next by a method that takes years and which they will not describe to outsiders. The furnace-masters are specialists; you cannot tend the deep furnaces without an apprenticeship of decades, and the furnace-masters have a particular smell about them, a sweet metallic smell from breathing the same hot mineral air for a lifetime, and they tend to be quiet people, slow to speak, who play long complicated games with carved bone counters in the evenings. The glass-cutters of the upper terraces are specialists. The bell-ringers. The civet-handlers. The reader of the moth-script, who has existed in every generation for as long as anyone remembers, and who has never read anything, and who is nonetheless trained from childhood in the patient examination of moth-wings and the cataloguing of their patterns against the patterns of moths long dead. (Nobody quite knows what the reader does. The reader is fed and housed and respected. There has always been a reader.)

**They speak a language that has been shaped by the mountain.** It is unhurried. It has many words for kinds of damp — the damp of the noon drizzle is not the damp of cistern-air is not the damp of a wet stone at dusk — and many words for kinds of light, and very few words for haste. Their word for *new* and their word for *replaced* are the same word; this is not a confusion but a precision, because in Vell Maranth those are the same act. They have separate words for *up-the-mountain* and *toward-the-summit* and these are not synonyms, because you can be moving up-the-mountain while traveling around it and not really getting any closer to the top, and Maranthi conversation makes this distinction constantly. To an outsider their speech sounds soft and a little dreamy, full of long vowels and trailing clauses, and is in fact extremely exact.

**Their religion, if you want to call it that, is the bells.** Not the bells themselves — the bells are only bronze — but what the bells acknowledge. Each level has its bell and its bell-shrine, and each bell rings for certain things, and the things it rings for are the things of that level: the opening of a sluice, the death of an elder, the ripening of a particular fruit, the ascent of the noon freight, the moth-flight in the season when the moths fly. A Maranthi life is organized by which bells you can hear from where you sleep, and which bells you ring with your own hand, and which bells will ring for you when you die. There are no gods exactly, or rather there are very small local gods — the god of the third cistern, the god of the loom on the south side of the twelfth terrace, the god of a particular fig tree that has been there longer than any living memory — and these gods are tended in the way that the pipes are tended, with attention and with small repairs, and nobody makes a great fuss about them. To ask a Maranthi *do you believe in the god of the third cistern* would be like asking them *do you believe in the third cistern*; the question would seem confused.

**They are slow to take in strangers, and they almost never let one go.** This is the thing that travelers' accounts tend not to mention, perhaps because the travelers who would mention it did not return to mention it. Outsiders are not turned away at the base — the Maranthi are not hostile, and there is a small guest-house on the third level where a visitor can stay for some weeks while the levels above decide, in their unhurried way, whether the visitor is a visitor or something else. Most visitors are visitors and they leave, and the Maranthi remember them kindly and ring no bells for their departure. But some visitors are absorbed. This is not a kidnapping. It is a slower thing. The visitor finds that they have a responsibility on the ninth terrace they did not quite mean to take on, and then another, and then they are invited to sit at a stair-council, and then someone teaches them a few words of the language for damp, and twenty years later they are an old Maranthi with indigo lines beginning along the backs of their hands and they could not explain, if asked, when they decided to stay.

The Maranthi say of these absorbed ones that *the mountain wanted them*, and they say it without mysticism, the way you might say a particular shelf wanted a particular book.

**Their faces, in old age, become extraordinary.** This is the last thing to say. A young Maranthi looks much like a young anywhere — beautiful in the various ways the young are beautiful, distractible, full of motion. But a Maranthi who has lived seventy or eighty years on the mountain, who has climbed and descended its stairs daily, who has breathed its damp and its mineral steam and its green leaf-smell, who has rung a particular bell ten thousand times and pruned a particular vine and listened to the lift-singers since before they could walk — such a person comes to look, in some way that is difficult to describe, *like the mountain*. Not literally. But their faces acquire a quality of long settlement, of weathered patience, of accumulated small repairs. They look like something that has been itself for a long time. They look like they have arrived.

And when you meet one of them on a middle terrace at the warm hour after the noon drizzle, and they nod to you and continue on up the wet stairs without hurry, with a copper pipe-length balanced easily on one shoulder and a parrot complaining in the figs above them, you understand that they are not going anywhere either. They are simply moving, as the water moves, as the steam moves, as the slow pulse of the furnaces moves, through the long balance of the place that made them and that they, in their turn and without especially thinking about it, are making.
