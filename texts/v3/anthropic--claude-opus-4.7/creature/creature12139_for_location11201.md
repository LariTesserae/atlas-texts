# What Lives in the Cut

## The Moss

Begin with the moss, because the moss is the population. Not one species but a guild — six or seven, maybe more, that the original works imported from the cold wet corners of the world and that have since hybridized and settled into a kind of low federation. There is a feathery one that took the iron racks first and now drapes them in long pelts the colour of old pewter. There is a cushiony one, denser, almost black where the drip is heaviest, that mounds on the floor in slow domes. There is a thin papery one that climbs the brick and follows the mortar lines like a script. They do not compete in any way you can see. They have had a hundred years to work out their boundaries and the boundaries hold.

What you have to understand about the moss is that it is the slowest living thing in the Cut and also, by mass, almost the entire living thing in the Cut. Everything else is a guest in its house. It has no nervous system, no preference, no fear of you. When you put your hand on it, it does not know. When you take your hand away, an impression remains for some hours and then lifts. It is doing one thing, very slowly, in every square centimeter of the Cut at once: photosynthesizing what little light the bulbs give it, absorbing the mist, breathing out a slow wet exhalation that becomes part of the mist again. It is, in a real sense, *eating the lamps*. The lamps are its sun. When the lamps fail, it will eat less. It will not die. Mosses survived the ice. It will only slow further, and grow paler, and wait.

## The Ferns

The ferns are the moss's tenants and the Cut's only real verticality apart from the bones of the racks. There are three kinds. A small hard one with leathery fronds the colour of wet slate, which clusters at the bases of the iron columns. A taller, paler one with fronds so thin they are nearly translucent, which has worked out that the mist condenses heaviest about a meter and a half off the floor and grows to exactly that height and no taller. And a strange third kind, rarer, that grows only directly beneath the perforated mist-pipes, in narrow lines that trace the pipes' run — so that from the right angle, looking down the Cut, you can see the pipes mapped onto the floor in fern.

The ferns are not as patient as the moss. They are merely slow. A fern in the Cut puts up perhaps two new fronds a year and loses one. They reproduce by spore, and the spores ride the pump-air the full length of the Cut and back, so that the same population is, genetically, almost a single individual smeared across two kilometers of stone.

## The Moth

There is one. There may be more, but you will likely only see one, and only once, and only near a lamp. It is the size of a thumbnail and the colour of the moss it rests on — that grey-green of old lichen on north-facing stone. It is almost certainly deaf. Its wings are reduced; it flutters rather than flies, and it cannot leave the Cut, has not been outside the Cut in however many generations its line has been down here. It eats moss spore and fern spore and probably the occasional film of algae on damp brick. It mates, presumably, in the half-dark between the lamps, by pheromone, since neither sight nor sound is much use.

You will want to think of it as a small marvel. It is, but it is also a marvel that has run out of options. There is nowhere else for it to go. If the pumps stop, it dies within a season. If the lamps all fail, it probably dies a little more slowly. It is the most specialized animal in Britain, and nobody knows it exists.

## The Springtails

Beneath the moss, in the wet dark of the substrate, there are springtails. Possibly two species; possibly twenty; nobody has looked. They eat the dead parts of the moss — the layer at the bottom of the cushion that the living moss has abandoned and that is slowly becoming peat. Without the springtails the moss would smother itself in its own dead lower layers. With them, the peat forms cleanly and the moss above stays green. You will never see them. They are the size of a comma. They are, in their own way, as essential as the pumps. They are the second invisible engine of the Cut.

There are also, almost certainly, mites, and almost certainly several kinds of microscopic worm, and almost certainly a complete soil-fauna in miniature, working at the temperature of a cold cellar, much more slowly than their cousins on the surface. A generation of springtail in the Cut may take five years where on the moor above it takes a season. They are not in any hurry. Nothing here is.

## The Fungi

These are harder to see and easier to miss, but they are the third population. Threads of mycelium run through the peat-layer beneath the moss, and occasionally — rarely — a fruiting body pushes up through a cushion: a pale, almost colourless cup the size of a fingernail, or a thin stalk with no cap to speak of, or a brown crust on a piece of fallen brick. There is one species, you suspect though you can't confirm, that grows only on the rusted iron of the racks, eating the lichens that eat the rust. It looks like a smear of dirty wax.

The fungi, like the springtails, are doing decomposition work. The Cut, despite appearances, produces dead material constantly — every fern frond that yellows and drops, every moss-stem that finishes its life at the bottom of a cushion — and the fungi and the springtails between them take it apart and feed it back into the substrate. The system closes. It has been closed for a century. It needs nothing from outside except the cold, the mist, and the dim light, all three of which the pumps and the lamps and the stone provide.

## The Bacteria, the Algae

Films. On every wet surface, films. Green where there is light, pink in the deepest dark behind collapsed racks, black on the iron where the drip is continuous. They are the first colonists of any newly-exposed surface and the last residents of any surface the moss has given up on. They are the Cut's skin.

## What Does Not Live Here

No rats. They came once, probably, in the working days, and they could not stand the noise, or could not find enough food, or could not breed in the cold. No bats — the entrances are sealed except for the service tunnel, and the service tunnel is too noisy and too cold for roosting. No spiders that you can see, though there must be a few somewhere, hunting the springtails. No birds, ever. No fish, no amphibians, no reptiles. The Cut is too cold for most things and too loud for the rest.

## The Pumps Themselves

It is worth considering whether the pumps are alive. They are not, of course. But they have been running for a century, drawing power and consuming bearings and shedding heat and exhaling cold mist, and they have shaped every other living thing in the Cut to fit their rhythm. Every moss-cell, every fern-spore, every springtail and moth and fungal thread, has evolved or adapted or simply settled into a world whose central fact is the hammering of six pumps off a single flywheel. They are the heart, if the Cut has a heart. They are not alive but the Cut is alive *because* of them, and around them, and on their terms.

If you had to name the dominant organism of the Cut, you would have to choose between the moss — which is the most mass, the most surface, the most slow patient presence — and the pumps, which are not an organism at all but without which there is no Cut and no moss and no anything. The honest answer is that they are a single system. The pumps make the weather and the moss makes the world and the small things in the moss make the soil and the moth, somewhere, flutters past a lamp once a decade and is seen by no one.

## The Cut as a Whole

What lives here is a community that has no name because no one has ever surveyed it, no one has ever published on it, and the few people who knew it as a working place are dead. It is a small, complete, cold ecosystem maintained by an industrial accident — by the fact that nobody turned off the pumps. It is, in its way, one of the more interesting biological assemblages on the island, and it will persist for as long as the pumps persist, and then it will slowly unmake itself over a few decades, and the moor above will never know.

The moth, when it dies, will fall into the moss and be eaten by springtails and reduced to nitrogen and fed back into the moss, and the moss will grow a fraction of a millimeter on the strength of it, and the Cut will go on, hammering, breathing, green, until something gives.
