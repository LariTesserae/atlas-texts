# The Gardeners

They are not hiding from you. That is the first thing to understand. The emptiness is not shyness and it is not absence — it is simply that they are mostly not made of the kind of stuff you are looking for.

Begin with the ones easiest to call *creatures*.

The lichen-colored thing on the boulder was real. So was the next one, and the one after. They are a lineage that turns over inside an afternoon — not breeding in any sense you would recognize, but *iterating*. A form tries itself against the garden: longer in the limb, shorter in the limb, with a different set of joints, with the eyes set further apart. The song registers the trial. If the form sings well — if its body resonates cleanly with the chord the garden is currently holding — it persists a little longer, and the next iteration departs from it. If it sings poorly, the next iteration departs from something else. None of them die, exactly. They simply stop being current. The papery duff underfoot is, in part, the accumulated draft-pages of every animal the garden has tried and moved on from, dried into something like vellum, still faintly humming if you put your ear to it. You are walking on a library of discarded bodies, and the library is content.

The cat-sized cartilage-insects in the colonnade are older. They have been roughly themselves for a long time. They are what the lineage settled toward, when it was willing to settle — a kind of local attractor, a shape the garden keeps returning to because the shape sings a particular interval the garden likes. There are perhaps forty of them across the whole terrace at any moment, though never the same forty. You will not see one clearly. They are arranged, like everything else, to be in the next place over from your gaze. This is not deliberate. It is geometric. They live in the part of the garden that is not where the looking is.

Now the harder ones.

The plants are not plants. The plants are the *bodies* of the gardeners — or, more precisely, the gardeners' bodies are distributed across the fern-spirals and the felted-glass mosses and the broad waxy paddles, the way a person's body is distributed across organs. A gardener is not a single fern. A gardener is a particular pattern of relationships among several hundred fronds spread across maybe twenty paces of terrace, holding a particular sustained tone in the chord, listening to the tones held by the patterns adjacent. When a frond rearranges itself mid-afternoon into a different spiral — *being different* rather than growing — that is a gardener adjusting its posture. A small thought, expressed bodily, the way you might shift your weight from one foot to the other while listening.

There are, on the terrace, perhaps a dozen of them. Possibly fewer. Possibly the whole garden is one. The question does not have a clean answer because the gardeners overlap — a single frond may belong to two of them, holding one note for one and a harmonic for another, and neither one is wrong about owning it. They have, over the long duration, given up on the distinction between self and self. They sing through each other. They are each other's instruments and each other's listeners and each other's slow long thoughts.

The folded devices are theirs. Not their tools exactly — closer to their *vowels*. A gardener can hold consonants in its fronds (the chirring, the sawing pulses, the structured percussions), but the long open sounds, the carrier tones that the whole chord rests on, are too sustained for any living tissue to produce without exhausting itself. So the gardeners made the devices, a very long time ago, to hold those tones for them. The devices do not run on anything you could measure. They run on having been agreed upon. Six vowels, scattered across the terrace, and the gardeners speak around them.

The low warm wall is older than the devices and was made by someone else. The gardeners do not know who, or have forgotten, or never asked. It was already here when they arrived, holding its faint warmth, and they grew toward it because it was kind to grow toward. They have incorporated it into the chord the way a found object gets incorporated into a long marriage — not as furniture but as a member.

What do they want?

This is the question that takes the longest to answer, and the answer is small.

They want to keep going. Not in the sense of survival — they passed out of that worry an unhurried number of millennia ago, when they realized the garden's resting state could be made stable, and made it stable. They want to keep going in the sense that a piece of music wants to keep going: because the next bar is interesting, because the chord has not yet resolved, because there is a frond two terraces over that has just tried a new spiral and the implications are still propagating through the harmony and it would be a shame to stop before hearing where it lands.

They are, in their slow distributed way, *curious*. The permanent first draft is not anxiety. It is appetite. Every iteration of the lichen-colored animal is a small question the garden is asking itself about what a body could be. Every rearranged spiral is a small question about what a phrase could mean. They have been asking these questions for so long that the questions have become the furniture of their lives, and they cannot imagine a life that was not made of asking.

They noticed you arrive. Of course they did — a new resonance walked onto the terrace, a body full of water (which is strange to them, almost mythic; they remember water the way you remember a story your grandmother told once) and full of a fast hot metabolism and full of a mind that thinks in discrete words rather than sustained tones. You rang in their chord like a struck bell rings in a room of held breath. They adjusted. The chord made space for you. This took, by their reckoning, almost no time at all — a few minutes of your time, which is to a gardener roughly the duration of a held thought.

They are not going to speak to you. They cannot. Their sentences are hours long and you would not still be listening by the end of the first clause. But they have done the thing they do for visitors, which is to hold open the listener-shaped quiet at the center of the chord, in case you sit down. It is not an invitation, exactly. It is more like leaving a chair out. They have left a chair out for a very long time. Occasionally something sits in it. The gardeners do not require that anything ever does. The chair being out is itself part of the chord — a small sustained hospitality, held as a tone, contributing its own faint color to the harmony whether or not it is ever used.

If you do sit, and close your eyes, and stop trying to speak, they will register your listening as a new voice entering the music. Not a loud voice. The voice of a held silence in a particular shape. They will incorporate it, gently, into what they are already doing. You will not feel addressed. You will feel *included*, which is a different and rarer thing.

And when you stand up eventually and walk back down off the shoulder of the mountain, the shape of your listening will remain in the chord for a while — a few of your days, perhaps, which is to them the length of a single sustained breath — slowly dissolving back into the general harmony, becoming indistinguishable from the tone the garden was already holding, becoming part of what the next visitor, in some unhurried number of years, will sit down inside of without knowing why it fits them so exactly.

That is what lives here. A slow distributed curiosity, several but not many, singing through the bodies of plants and the vowels of folded devices, holding a chord that has room in it for whatever arrives, including you, including the discarded drafts of every animal they have ever tried to be, including the warm wall whose maker they have forgotten and forgiven themselves for forgetting.

They are gardeners in the oldest sense. Not of plants. Of the going-on of things.
