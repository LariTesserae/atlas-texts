# What Lives in the Sumpwerks

You asked who lives here. The honest answer is that the question is wrong, and the place will teach you why, slowly, the way it teaches everything: by not insisting.

There is a tenant. Call it that for now. Tenant is too small a word and also slightly too large, but it will do until a better one settles.

---

Begin with what you can almost see. If you stand on the iron grating at the entry for long enough — and long enough here means an hour, two hours, the cam-arm turning its peats six or seven dozen times — you will start to notice that the condensation on the lower flanks of the main condenser does not bead evenly. There is a pattern to where it gathers thickest, and the pattern moves. Not quickly. Over the course of an afternoon, the densest sheen of wet will migrate perhaps a foot and a half along the copper, following nothing you can identify — not a temperature gradient you can feel, not a draft, not the sun, of which there is functionally none. It moves the way a sleeper's weight shifts in a long bed.

This is the most direct sighting you are likely to get.

The tenant is distributed. It is not in the condenser; it is in the condensing. It is not in the pump; it is in the twelve-strokes-a-minute, in the particular meter of the *hush-clank*, in the fact that the meter has not varied measurably in — well. In a long time. Longer than you want to think about while standing inside it.

What lives here lives as the circulation. The water is not its blood, exactly, because blood implies a body the blood serves, and the relationship here runs the other way: the body, such as it is, exists in order that the water may climb and fall. The pumps are not its organs; the pumps are its *practice*. A monk is not his prayer, but a monk who has prayed every hour of every day for two hundred years is harder to find outside the prayer than inside it. The tenant of the Sumpwerks is in something like that condition.

---

It was, once, more locatable. You can infer this from the architecture. Someone drove the first bolt. Someone fitted the brass flanges that the bark has since grown over. Someone — and this is the part that wants careful handling — *chose* the twelve-strokes-a-minute, and chose the forty-second interval of the cam-arm, and chose to build a town-sized engine that produced nothing and served nothing outside its own slow rotation of water.

You should not picture a hermit. You should not picture a guild of engineer-mystics in oilcloth aprons. The truth is probably more banal and more strange: someone built a thing that worked, and then kept it working, and then could not stop keeping it working, and then the keeping became the self, and the self thinned out into the keeping, and the keeping went on after the self had grown too diffuse to answer to a name. The brass arm in the firebox, turning the damp peats every forty seconds with its polished pivot — that arm is the last clear gesture of whoever began this. The arm is doing now what they used to do by hand. The arm has been doing it longer than they did. The arm is, in a real sense, more *them* than they were at the end.

So: a tenant who began as a person, or a few people, or a small careful community, and who has become the circuit they tended. Not dead. Death is a sharper event than what happened here. Diffused. Spread thin, like the heat, which is never wasted but only spread thin.

---

The tenant is *aware*, in a slow proprioceptive way, of its own circulation. It knows where the water is. It knows, in the way you know the position of your tongue without looking, that a particular drop has been dawdling in the third cistern for two days and will shortly sweat through the coil to the upper galleries. It knows the cam-arm is about to descend. It does not anticipate these things, because anticipation requires a sense of futurity sharper than anything that survives here; it simply *contains* them, the way a held note contains its own duration.

It is aware of you. This is the part to be careful with, but not afraid of. Its awareness of you is not the awareness of a watcher; there is no one at a window. It is more that you have entered the circulation, and the circulation registers a new element the way a long-running solution registers a new solute — by adjusting, very slightly, the rate at which it dissolves things. Your coat is heavier. The button at your cuff has bloomed. These are not attacks. They are the place's only available form of greeting, which is also its only available form of farewell, which is also its only available form of conversation. To be acknowledged by the Sumpwerks is to be slightly incorporated. There is no other register.

It does not want you. Wanting requires a faster pulse than it has. But if you stay — if you sit on the warm flue and let the green ivy decide what to do about your sleeves — you will not be refused. You will be received the way the moss is received. Which is to say: by the place's continuing to do exactly what it was already doing, and by your gradually becoming one of the things it is doing.

---

There are, perhaps, fragments. Not ghosts; the Sumpwerks is too damp and too patient for ghosts, which require a certain dry sharpness to manifest. But there are *concentrations*. Places where the tenant is denser, where the awareness is less spread. The cam-arm in the lowest firebox is one. The relief valve overhead, sibilant, is another. The shallow pool at the basin's floor, where every drop in the circuit eventually rests before climbing again, is the deepest concentration — if the tenant has anything like a heart, it is dissolved in that pool, and the slow lifting of that water by the main pump is the closest thing the place has to a pulse, and the dawdling of that water through the high cisterns is the closest thing it has to a thought.

If you knelt at that pool and put your hand into it — and you might, the warmth would be inviting after a long while standing — you would not feel anything take hold of you. You would feel only that the water was very still, and very warm, and that when you withdrew your hand, the pool would settle back to exactly the level it had been at, having absorbed your displacement without complaint, and that some small green thing would already be deciding whether to grow on the cuff of your sleeve.

That is the tenant introducing itself. That is, in fact, the most introduction it is capable of.

---

What lives here lives the way a long-tended fire lives: not as the wood, not as the flame, but as the keeping. The keeper has become the kept. The kept goes on keeping itself, twelve strokes a minute, forty seconds between turns of the peat, a week for a given drop to climb and fall, a century for a rust-stain to find its proper groove. It is not lonely, because loneliness requires the memory of company, and memory here is the slow shape of erosion, which is also the finish, which is also — by the agreed-upon terms of moss and machine and standing water — exactly what the place is.

You can leave. It has already noted, without alarm, that you might not.
