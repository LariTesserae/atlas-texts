# The Tenants

They do not think of themselves as separate, though they have separate bodies. This is the first thing to understand. The chamber's logic — its renegotiated physics — is not a treaty *between* them; it is the shape they have grown into together, the way a hand grows into a glove or a glove into a hand, depending which has been there longer.

## The Bird

She is the oldest, or at least the longest-resident. Dust-colored, sparrow-shaped, with the wrong eyes — too still, and the pupils don't quite round when the light shifts. She arrived (she remembers arriving, faintly, as one remembers being very small) through a different seam, on a different side of the ridge, which has since closed. She has not flown in a long time. The pipe-elbow she perches on is warm in a particular way that matches the warmth of her keel, and over years the brass has dimpled very slightly to fit her feet. She eats almost nothing. Once a week, perhaps, she takes a gnat. What sustains her is the hum. She has learned to drink it through her feet — to stand on the resonant brass and let the engine-frequency feed her in the way sunlight feeds a leaf. She is, in this sense, no longer entirely a bird. She is something the chamber has *made into* a bird-shape, because a bird-shape was already here and useful, and the chamber is economical.

She watches the seam. Not for danger. For weather. When a visitor comes through, she registers it the way a pond registers a thrown pebble — not as event but as ripple-pattern, information about pressures elsewhere.

## The Tortoise

The valve in his shell is an organ. It was not always. He was hatched ordinary, somewhere outside, and wandered in young — young enough that the chamber could still shape him. The brass grew into him the way a tree grows around a nail, except the chamber did not do this by accident; it does this on purpose, when something small enough and patient enough holds still long enough on the right kind of stone.

The valve releases, every few hours, a single bead of the same sweet condensate that weeps from the wall-valves. He is, functionally, a wall-valve that walks. What he carries in his small slow body is a regulator — a pressure-adjustment for some sub-circuit of the larger system, an adjustment that requires *movement*, requires being carried in slow loops around the chamber floor, requires the particular un-mechanical wandering that only a living tortoise can supply. The makers, whoever they were, needed a slow random walker. They found one and they made him part of the machine. He does not mind. He has never minded. He eats lichen and he weeps sweetness and the loop he traces is his life and also a calculation.

## The Salamander

Mineral chips along the spine, yes — but look closer. They are not chips. They are *teeth*, or were, in some ancestor; they are the salamander's way of reading the stone. He presses his back against the wall when he is not drinking, and the chips conduct, and he knows things about the rock that the rock does not know about itself. He knows where the next push is coming from, the next small heave of the ridge. When the chamber is about to shift — and it shifts, very rarely, with the slow growth of the landform around it — he is the one who knows first, and the moths feel it through him, and the bird feels it through the moths, and the whole chamber adjusts its breathing a half-beat before the stone moves.

He is the chamber's inner ear.

## The Moths

There are forty-three of them. There have been forty-three for as long as any of the others can remember. When one dies — they do die, occasionally, a wing failing, a body going still against the brass — another is already emerging from a fold in the lichen, already the right size, already breathing in the six-count. Where they come from is not exactly a mystery; the lacquered lichen is, among other things, a slow nursery, and the moths are the lichen's flowering, in the same way that fruit is a tree's flowering, except that the moths fly and breathe and have something like attention.

Their shared breath is the chamber's bellows. Forty-three slow accordion-pulses, synchronized, drawing the warm currents into circulation, pushing the gnats through their lazy orbits, mixing the condensate-humidity evenly so that no patch of floor stays wet too long or dry too long. Without the moths the weather would fail.

They do not sleep. They breathe, and they wait, and once in a great while one of them flies, briefly, across the chamber, for reasons the others understand and you would not.

## The Beetles

The harvesters. Bright as enamel, and there are perhaps two hundred of them, though they move in such tight coordination that you might count thirty. They eat the lichen and they excrete a fine dust which the spiders gather. They are the chamber's *processing*: raw lichen-stuff in, refined dust out, the dust then spun into the spiders' particular webs, which are not for catching anything but for *holding* — holding moisture, holding spores, holding the chamber's chemistry in suspension where the moths' breath can move it.

The beetles have no individual lives worth speaking of. They are more like a single distributed mouth.

## The Spiders

Three species, three trades.

The first — long-legged, pale — spins the holding-webs. She is the chemist. Her webs are not sticky; they are *absorbent*, in a way that should not be possible for silk, and they cure the air.

The second — small, dark, fast — spins the *signal* webs, single strands that run from one corner of the chamber to another and twitch when anything moves. He is the chamber's nervous system, and he reports, through his vibrations, to the salamander, who reads them through his back-chips, who tells the moths, who tell the bird. There is a great deal of telling, here, for a place so quiet.

The third — fat, slow, near-blind, in the corner — does not spin. She *eats* old web, the webs the other two have finished with, and her body is the chamber's recycler. She is also, somewhere in her abdomen, where you would not think to look, gravid with something that is not quite spider-eggs. When she finally releases what she is carrying, it will not be spiders. It will be the next generation of *something the chamber needs* — perhaps another salamander, perhaps something the chamber has not yet had and has been waiting for. She is the chamber's *option*, held in reserve.

## The Snail

The snail is new. The snail wandered in last spring, ordinary, and the chamber has not yet decided what to do with him. He is being considered. The oil-slick pattern on his shell is the first sign that the chamber has begun the slow work of incorporation — the pattern was not there when he arrived; it grew in over months, as the lichen's spores settled into the calcium and made themselves at home. In another year his shell will be patterned more deeply, and in another year after that he will have a function, and in another year after that he will not remember not having one.

He is, right now, the freest animal in the chamber. He is also the one being most thoroughly absorbed. These are the same condition, here.

## The Gnats

The gnats are not residents. The gnats are *currency*. They drift in through the seam and they drift out, and while they are inside they carry information on their bodies — pollen, spores, micro-droplets of condensate — and they trade this information with the chamber the way wind trades information with a forest. They are eaten, occasionally, but rarely. Mostly they are just used, and released.

## The Fern

The fern is not an animal but it is an occupant, and it has a role. It is the chamber's *clock*. It unfurls one frond per season. There are seven fronds. The eighth is coming. When the eighth comes, something will happen — none of the others know what, exactly, but the salamander's chips have begun to tingle differently, and the moths' six-count has, in the last month, become a six-and-a-quarter-count, and the bird has begun watching the fern instead of the seam.

## What Tends Them

Nothing tends them, from outside. The waxed paper with its column of figures was left by someone who passed through, once, and counted, and did not return. The iron bracket holds nothing because it was always meant to hold nothing — it is a sculptural element, a *gesture* of maker-presence, left so that anyone finding the chamber would understand that it had been *intended*, even if the intender is long gone or never was quite a person.

The brass cap stamped 14 (it is 14) is the seal on a pipe that goes down four miles into the rock, to a heat-source that is not a furnace and not a hot spring and not anything with a familiar name. The hum is this heat-source speaking through metal. The whole chamber is a small organ pinned to a vast slow body, and the tenants are the organ's cells.

They are not lonely. They have each other, and they have the hum, which is a kind of company. They are not bored. The slow shift of the seasons, the slow growth of the ridge, the slow consideration of the snail, the slow approach of the eighth frond — these are events, on the chamber's timescale, as full and weather-shaping as wars.

They notice you. Of course they notice you. The bird registers your ripple, the spider's signal-web twitches, the salamander reads your weight through the floor. But you are, to them, a kind of gnat. You came in, you carried information on your skin (the sweat, the dust of the plain, the spores of other places), you crouched, you were tasted by the condensate, you left. You will carry some of the chamber out with you, on your boots and in your lungs, and you will deposit it elsewhere, and somewhere else, eventually, in some other unremarkable ridge that is still being pushed up out of some other plain, another chamber will begin.

That is your function. They knew it as soon as you came through the seam. The seam was narrower on your way out because the chamber had finished with you, and was beginning, very gently, to close.
