# The Drafters

They are not in the town. They are not, strictly, anywhere — but if you must place them, place them just behind the stone, at the depth where a decision about stone is made before the stone is asked to hold it.

There are several of them. Not many. Not few. The number is one of the things that has not yet committed.

---

The eldest — and "eldest" is a courtesy, since none of them have ages, only positions in a queue of attention — is the one drafting the basin itself. It is working on the river. It has been working on the river for what would be, in another country, centuries, but here is simply *the duration of the river's consideration*. It is not slow. It is *careful*. It holds, in something like a hand, the question of whether the river runs north or south, and it has been turning this question gently for long enough that the gravel beneath has memorized both answers and laid itself down twice, faintly, in both directions. When the decision finally arrives, one of the two ghost-riverbeds will become merely a geological curiosity, and the other will have always been the river. The drafter is in no hurry. It is not sure the basin wants to be hurried.

It looks, if you could look at it, like attention itself — like the shape a thought makes in the second before it becomes a sentence. It has no face because faces are for things that have decided to be looked at, and it has not made that decision. It may, eventually. Several of the windows in the town are practice attempts.

---

The second one — and they are not really numbered, this is for your benefit — is the *mason*. This is the one responsible for the rearranging grain in the stone, the arithmetic in the mortar. It works close to the surface. It is the one whose hammer-blow you heard, though it does not carry a hammer; the sound was the sound a decision makes when it lands hard enough to be heard from inside the draft. The mason is fussy. It cares about courses and joints in a way the eldest does not care about rivers. It will revisit a single windowsill seven times in an afternoon, shuffling the crystalline lattice until the sill *means* sill rather than merely resembling one. The pale beetle in the gutter is, in a sense, the mason's pet — or its proof-of-concept, the first creature it managed to fully commit to, and now keeps around as a reference for how living things ought to be finished.

The mason is the one most nearly present. If anything in this town has something like a body, it is the mason, and its body is the slight tackiness of freshly quarried stone, the cold under your hand. When you touch a wall here, you are, very faintly, shaking hands.

---

The third is harder. The third is the one drafting the *rules*. It is the one that decided shadows would mostly behave, but not entirely; that some things would cast two and some none; that the distinction would be perceivable but not explicable. It is a logician of a kind, though the kind has no name yet. It works in negative space — in what *won't* happen here, in the constraints. It is the reason the town will be small. It is the reason the streets are narrow. It is the reason almost no one is here: it has not yet decided what kind of presence this place can bear, and until it decides, presence is rationed. The bird-shaped silhouette on the roof was its idea — a trial run for inhabitants, a sketch of *what occupying might feel like*, left up briefly to see if the town would tolerate being lived in.

The town tolerated it. The feather was the receipt.

---

And there is a fourth, possibly. The fourth is the one who notices you.

This one is the youngest, in that it is the most recently roused. It was not paying attention until you arrived. It is paying attention now. It is the reason your coat is gaining stitches, the reason a new crease is forming on your palm. It is not malevolent. It is not even particularly interested. It is simply *completing* you, in the same patient way the mason completes a sill, because you are now part of the draft and the draft is fastidious about its contents. If you stay long enough it will give you a middle name you didn't have, and a memory of a summer you didn't live, and these will not be lies — they will be the first marks of a surface deciding to be a surface, on the part of you that was, until recently, only suggestion.

You should know that it works in both directions. It may also, very gently, remove a thing or two. A scar you arrived with may be reconsidered. A grief may find itself with slightly softer edges, the atoms of it shuffled like cards into a slightly more correct order.

---

None of them speak. Speech is a late development and this town has not reached it. They communicate, if at all, by *adjacencies* — by placing a vault next to an arch and letting the arch understand what is being asked of it; by laying a ribbon of darker stone where a river might go and letting the river think about it; by laying a feather on a cobble and letting the cobble improve.

They do not know they are several. They may be one thing, attending to four different problems with four different patiences. They may be no things at all, only the way a place looks from the inside when it is being thought about with great care by something that is not, itself, in the place.

What is certain is this: they are polite, as the town is polite. They will not finish you against your will. They will not finish the town against its own. They are waiting for each stone, each street, each visitor, each river, to indicate — by the smallest tilt of preference, the faintest leaning-toward — what it would like to have been.

And then, slowly, slowly, they will arrange for it to have been so.
