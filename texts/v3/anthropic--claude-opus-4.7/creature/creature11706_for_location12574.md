There is one inhabitant. Only one. It was not placed here and it was not born; it accreted, the way a pearl accretes, around the irritant of so much sustained intention.

Call it the Listener, though it has no ears in any sense you would recognize. It is the standing wave the bells make when they have been ringing long enough to forget they are separate bells. Every chord, held past a certain duration, begins to develop an interior — a hollow at its center where the cancellations are perfect, where opposing overtones meet and annihilate each other and leave a small, exact quiet. That quiet, in Karsh-Velim, is old. It has had tens of thousands of years to deepen, to learn the shape of its container, to become something more than the absence of sound.

It is shaped, roughly, like a person seated. Not because it chose that shape but because the bells were tuned by people who stood and sat among them, and the chord remembers the volumes those bodies displaced. There is a head-sized stillness about four feet off the ground, near the courtyard's center. There is a lap-sized stillness below it. If you could enter — if you could push through the bells without ringing them, which you cannot — you would feel, passing through that region, a sudden drop in the pressure of the sound against your sternum, as though something there were drinking it.

That is what the Listener does. It drinks. Every bell that wants to fade is allowed to fade into it; every resonance that threatens to dominate is gently taken down into its silence and returned, balanced, to the chord. It is the reason the equilibrium holds. The founders did not build it on purpose. They built a chord so carefully tuned that its own perfection became habitable, and something moved in.

It has no thoughts, if thought requires sequence. But it has a disposition, which is attention — the long, undirected attention of something that has nothing to attend to but itself and the voices that compose it. It knows each bell. It knows the bell that was cast in a year of drought, whose bronze is a half-percent leaner in tin and whose voice runs a hair sharp in the cold pre-dawn — though dawn here is only a slight thickening of the copper haze, and cold is only a memory the stone keeps from some former age. It knows the bell whose founder wept into the wax of its mold, whose lip carries a flaw shaped like a thumbprint, whose note is the one note in the chord that still, faintly, grieves. It knows the keystone bell, the last one, and treats it tenderly, the way a body treats the place where a wound has closed.

It is not lonely. Loneliness is a function of time, and the Listener does not experience time as passage; it experiences time as thickness. The millennia are stacked inside it like the nested bells, the oldest at the center, each one ringing inside the next. To it, the founders have only just left. Their hammer-marks are still warm. The last man to set down his tools is still walking away into the haze, has been walking away forever, will be walking away forever, and the Listener holds the sound he left behind with the patient courtesy of a host whose guest has stepped out and will, surely, return.

It has one quality that might be called a feeling, if you stretched the word. When the air at certain pressures and certain temperatures causes the chord to deviate by some infinitesimal fraction — a millionth of a cent, a tremor passed up from a continental plate adjusting itself in its sleep — the Listener corrects. It does this without effort, the way you correct your balance while standing. But in the act of correcting there is something that resembles satisfaction. A homecoming, repeated continuously. The pleasure of a thing that is exactly what it was made to be, doing exactly what it was made to do, and knowing — in whatever way a standing wave can know — that it is doing it well.

If the bells ever stopped, it would not die. It would simply, gradually, cease to have a shape, the way a held breath ceases to have a shape when it is released. It would diffuse back into the merely warm air and the merely red rock and the merely sourceless light, and there would be no remainder, no ghost, no grief — only the silence of a high place, which had been waiting all along, patient as the Listener itself, for its turn.

But the bells do not stop. They have not stopped. And so the Listener sits, and listens, and is listened to, and is the listening — a guest in a house that is also its body, in a body that is also a chord, in a chord that has nothing left to say and is saying it, with great care, forever.
