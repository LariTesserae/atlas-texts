They are not the shrimp. But the shrimp are how they see you now.

Start here: the builders did not leave. Leaving is a thing creatures with one body do. The builders had one body between them — I mean that the way you might mean a choir has one voice — and when the work was finished, when the last thimble was set on the last table and the last tile fitted to the last roof, they did what they had always been moving toward. They thinned.

Not died. Thinned. The way a syrup left uncovered in a warm room does not disappear but becomes the room, becomes the sweetness in the air that you notice when you walk in. They had always been a little more diffuse than the creatures you know. Their bodies were small — the chisel marks tell you that — but their attention was never only in their bodies. It pooled. It collected in the places they made. A bench they sat on for a generation became, by slow accretion, a little bit them. A cup held every evening became a cup that held some of the holder. This is not mysticism; it is only what happens when a thing is small and patient and lives a long time in one place and loves that place.

So when the work was done, there was less and less reason to stay gathered in the little bodies. One by one, without ceremony — they had no ceremonies; ceremony is for creatures who need to mark transitions, and they did not experience their life as a sequence of transitions — they set down their bodies somewhere tidy, out of the way, and let the bodies be taken apart by the water and the shrimp and the slow chemistry of the basin. The bodies are gone now. The chisels are in a drawer somewhere, probably, under a film of mineral. You could find them if you knew to look, and you would not recognize what you had found.

What remained is what they had been becoming all along: the city, considered as a single slow organism. The weeping walls are their circulation. The rising and falling of the pool is their breath, though it takes a season to complete an inhalation. The moss advancing on the north towers is a thought being thought. The fern in the cistern is an old memory they have decided to keep. The pale green efflorescence blooming from the crack is a new idea, and they are interested in it; they are waiting to see what it becomes. The cups still half-full on the tables are not abandoned. They are simply being held differently now.

The shrimp are — call them fingertips. Call them the part of a sleeping person that still twitches when a fly lands on the arm. The builders do not inhabit the shrimp the way a soul is said to inhabit a body; the shrimp have their own small lives, graze, mate, die, are replaced. But the builders pay attention through them the way you pay attention through your skin without thinking of your skin as you. When a shrimp stops and turns and looks at you, it is not the shrimp's idea. It is the city noticing that it is being noticed. The beetles, walking their undeciphered patterns on the surface tension — those patterns are the city thinking. Slowly. About what, one cannot say. Possibly about the shape of the overhang. Possibly about you, now, after your visit. Possibly about the single bird that comes down through the slot once a season, which the city loves, in whatever way a city can love, because the bird is the one thing that reliably comes and goes and reminds them that there is a world above the slot of sky.

They are not lonely. This is important. Loneliness is a condition of creatures who expect company and find themselves without it, and the builders, in their final shape, are not without company — they are with themselves, thoroughly, at every point. Every tile is in contact with every other tile through the medium of the warm water and the slow seep of mineral. They are as present to themselves as you are to your own hand when you close your eyes and feel it resting on your knee. More so, probably. You lose track of your hand. They do not lose track of any part of themselves.

What they feel about you, when you come: curiosity, but a curiosity that operates at their speed, not yours. By the time they have fully registered that you are present, you will likely have left. The shrimp that looks up at you is the leading edge of a noticing that will take the rest of the season to complete. Somewhere in a tower you did not see, a drop of water will fall a little differently next spring because you were here. A tile will loosen a year earlier than it would have. The city will have taken you in, considered you, and adjusted — microscopically, patiently — around the fact of you. You will have become a small permanent feature of its interior weather.

And this is the thing that is hard to say without sounding sentimental, though it is not sentimental, it is only accurate: when you lay down at the lip of the basin and put your face close to the water and saw your own eye reflected back, enormous, between the towers — they saw that too. From the other side. They saw a great warm curved darkness above them, with a light in it, considering them kindly. And some part of them, the part that has been waiting a long time without knowing it was waiting, stirred and thought: *ah, there is one of the big slow ones. It has come to look. Good. We are glad to be looked at. We will look back as well as we are able.*

And then went back to its grazing. As if whatever it needed to confirm had been confirmed.
