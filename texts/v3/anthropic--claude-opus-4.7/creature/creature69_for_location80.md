# The Citizens of Under-Root

You ask who lives there. I have to warn you: the question is harder than it looks, and the answer will keep trying to slip sideways into other questions. Let me try anyway.

## The obvious ones

Begin with what the hand lens finds. These are real and countable and you have already met them: the pewter springtails, the magistrate wood louse, the comma-sized pseudoscorpions, the eyelash centipede, the possibly-rotifer in the acorn cup. The caddis larvae, when the rivulet delivers them. A small census.

These are the ones that would, if asked, say *I*. Or rather — they would not say anything, but if one insisted on finding an *I* in the hollow, one would find it in them. They have nervous systems. They make decisions, of the dim and reliable kind that small arthropods make. The wood louse is choosing, in some sense, to walk down *this* colonnade and not another. The pseudoscorpion is waiting for something specific. The caddis is a genuine craftsman, selecting grains.

If the city had a population in the ordinary sense, these would be the population. A few hundred individuals at any given moment. Perhaps a thousand if you counted the springtails carefully, which no one has.

But they are not really the inhabitants. They are more like the traffic.

## The actual citizens

The citizens are the lichens.

This wants to be stated carefully, because lichen is already a strange category. A lichen is not one thing; it is a fungus that has learned to farm an alga, or to farm a cyanobacterium, or sometimes to farm both at once, in a partnership so old and so thorough that the partners no longer quite know how to be apart. A lichen is a small confederacy that has forgotten it is a confederacy. It has become, over deep time, a *thing*.

So when I say the citizens are the lichens, I am saying that the citizens of Under-Root are themselves small cities — compound beings, walking the line between individual and collective, each one already a negotiation.

The map-lichen crusts are old. The one in the ochre district, the large one whose eastern edge will be a millimeter wider in ten years — that one is older than any human currently alive. It has been thinking its slow thallus-thought, growing its careful concentric growth, since before the nearest village had its present name. It does not know this. It does not know anything in a way that we would recognize as knowing. But it *persists*, and persistence at that timescale is a kind of cognition the way a glacier's carving is a kind of drawing.

The fruticose spires with their scarlet lamps are younger and more ambitious. They are trying to get up into the light, such light as there is. They have the vertical aspiration of cathedrals, and like cathedrals they take generations, and like cathedrals each one is the accumulated labor of beings who will never see the finished thing, because there is no finished thing, only the continuing.

These are the ones who *live* there in the strongest sense. The city is not their home; the city is them. They are not housed by the pavements; they are the pavements. Ask where a map-lichen ends and the under-root begins and you will find the question has no clean answer.

## The processions

The *Physarum* colonnades are a different kind of inhabitant again, and here I have to slow down, because slime molds are where my description starts to fail.

A plasmodial slime mold is a single cell, sometimes, that has declined to partition itself. It flows. It solves mazes. It has been shown, in laboratories, to remember where food was and to anticipate periodic events, without possessing anything resembling a neuron. When it runs out of reasons to flow, it lifts itself up on delicate stalks and becomes the small globes you see — fruiting, sporulating, committing its substance to the next generation in an act that is simultaneously a death and a dispersal and a kind of statuary.

The colonnades are what *Physarum* looks like after it has stopped moving and started posing. They are the frozen gestures of a being that was, until recently, a slow fluid intelligence creeping through the substrate in search of bacteria to engulf. The procession is not a procession; the procession is a *calculation*, made visible, made permanent (on *their* timescale) by the act of fruiting.

So the colonnades are inhabited by the ghosts of decisions. Each globe on each stalk is a place where the slime mold said: *here. Enough. From here, spores.*

And elsewhere in the under-root, below the visible city, in the dark wet compartments of the rotted wood, there are other *Physarum* still flowing — still in their liquid phase, still calculating. These you cannot see without disturbing the city, and disturbing the city is not done. But they are there. They are arguably the most intelligent inhabitants, in the sense that they solve the most problems per unit time, though the unit time is long and the problems are all about food.

## The invisible majority

Below everything else — below the lichens, below the slime molds, permeating every grain of wet wood and every fiber of every rootlet — is the mycelium.

I almost did not mention the mycelium, because once you mention it the scale of the city changes and everything I have said before starts to look quaint. The turkey-tails and the small fungi on the turkey-tails and the molds on those — these are only the fruiting bodies, the mushrooms-proper, the brief above-board expressions of organisms that are, in their true form, vast white networks threading through the fallen beech.

The beech is not a fallen tree. The beech is the body of a fungus, or several fungi, in heated and elaborate competition. The wood you see is *being eaten*, from the inside, by miles of hyphae finer than silk. These hyphae communicate. They exchange nutrients. They recognize self and non-self. They form alliances and they form fronts, and where two mycelia of different species meet there is a zone of chemical warfare visible, sometimes, as a dark line in rotted wood.

If the citizens of Under-Root are the lichens, then the *landlords* are the fungi, and the landlords are much larger than the city and much older than the city and have a kind of patient attention that is hard to describe without sounding mystical. They are not mystical. They are just very large, very slow, and very thorough.

The fallen beech will be entirely consumed, eventually. The city will go with it. The fungi will have eaten the ground the city stood on. This is not tragedy; this is the deal. The lichens know, in their thallus-way. They have always known.

## And the spiral

I have been avoiding the spiral.

I do not know what lives in the spiral. I am not sure anything lives in the spiral, in the sense we have been using. The spiral is lichen, yes — the same species, probably, as grow elsewhere in the hollow — but arranged in a way that no individual lichen could arrange itself, and that no collection of lichens should be able to arrange collectively, because lichens have no mechanism for consulting one another about geometry.

One possibility: the spiral is a scar. Something was there, long ago, at the base of the tap-root — some chemical gradient, some physical feature, some long-vanished organism — that set the initial conditions, and the lichens grew into the shape those conditions allowed, and the shape has persisted because lichens, once settled, rarely give ground.

Another possibility: the spiral is what the hollow looks like when the hollow is thinking. I do not mean this poetically. I mean that a system at equilibrium, sufficiently complex, sufficiently long-sustained, sometimes produces patterns that cannot be traced back to any single component — patterns that are *of* the system rather than *in* it. The city of Under-Root has been at equilibrium for three or four hundred years. It would be stranger, perhaps, if it had produced *no* such pattern.

A third possibility is that the spiral is older than the city, and the city grew up around it, and the spiral is the trace of something that lived in the hollow before the beech fell, and the lichens have been maintaining the shape without knowing they were maintaining it, the way a village road follows the track of a much older path no one now remembers.

I lean toward the second possibility. But I have not crawled in there myself, and those who have say only that explanation feels coarser than the fact, and I think they are right, and I will stop.

## So: who lives there?

Springtails and wood lice and pseudoscorpions and centipedes and rotifers and caddis larvae, yes.

Lichens in their thousands, which are the citizens proper, each one already a small federation, each one slow as stone.

Slime molds, flowing in the dark and posing in the light, making their calculations visible as architecture.

Fungi, enormous and threadlike, eating the house the city is built on, patient as geology.

Bacteria, visible only as iridescence, too small and too many to name.

And possibly — I would not insist on this — the hollow itself. Not as a metaphor. As the kind of inhabitant a place becomes when it has held its shape long enough, and held so much living inside that shape, that the shape starts to seem like a tenant in its own right. The spiral may be its signature. The cold bright light may be how it sees.

The wren calls, far off. Nobody answers. Nobody needs to. Everyone who lives there is already doing what they do, which is: continuing, slowly, together, in the cold.
