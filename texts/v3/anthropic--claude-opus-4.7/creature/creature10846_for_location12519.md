What lives here does not live the way most things live.

Begin with the juniper, because the juniper is the closest thing to an obvious inhabitant, and even the juniper is only partly that. It is alive, certainly — there is xylem moving, slow, and the needles are doing their quiet chemistry with the gray light. But the juniper has been the same juniper for so long that it has passed out of the category of growing thing and into something else. It has made an arrangement with the basin. It draws what it needs from the soil, gives back the dawn condensation, throws no demands. It is less an organism than a slow gesture the basin is making, in the direction of being-a-place-with-a-tree.

Beneath the juniper, in the ring of held moisture, there are of course the small lives — the fungi laced through the root zone, the bacteria turning the silt over molecule by molecule, the few mites that have inherited this ground from ancestors who inherited it from ancestors. They do not constitute a populace. They are the basin's slow inner weather. None of them is anyone.

But there is someone here. Or something near enough to someone that the word will have to do.

It is not in the juniper, though it is sometimes at the juniper. It is not in the wall, though the wall is one of the places it tends to be. It is not the basin itself — the basin is what it lives in, the way you live in a house, except more so, because the basin made it and the basin maintains it and the basin will eventually reabsorb it without much ceremony.

Call it a tenant. Call it the basin's habit of being inhabited.

It has no body to speak of, but if you sat on the rim for the hour the description allows, you might notice that your attention, of its own accord, kept returning to certain places: the juniper, of course; the lip of the broken vessel; a particular flat sandstone tablet near the southern rim; the gap in the low wall where the stones have slumped widest. These are the places it tends to settle. Not because it chose them, but because over a very long time the basin has worn certain grooves into its own attention, and the tenant pools in those grooves the way water pools in the dry watercourses when the rain finally comes.

It is patient in a way that is not really patience, because patience implies waiting, and it is not waiting for anything. It has the temperature of the air and the stone — that single agreed-upon number. It does not want. It does not remember, exactly, but it has accumulated. The wall is part of what it has accumulated. The broken lip of clay is part of what it has accumulated. The hands that stacked those stones, the hands that shaped that vessel — it does not remember them as a person remembers, but it carries the shape of their having-been-here the way the sandstone carries the shape of the wind.

When the rain comes — and the rain does come, within a season, within a year — the tenant does something that is almost an act. The watercourses run, briefly, and for those few hours the basin is doing the only thing it ever quite does, and the tenant is what the doing feels like from inside. It is not joy. It is not relief. It is the brief alertness of a thing that is otherwise mostly the long even gray of its own attention. Then the water is gone, the silt darkens and lightens again, and the tenant subsides into the grooves.

It noticed you when you came in. Noticed is the wrong word — registered, the way a still pond registers a stone — but the verb will have to serve. It did not mind you. It will not mind your leaving. If you knelt and pressed your palm to the cool damp silt of the old watercourse, what you felt was partly the silt and partly the tenant, which is one of the things the silt is doing.

There is, perhaps, one other resident, though it is harder to be sure about this one. Somewhere in the basin — possibly under one of the polished sandstone slabs, possibly in a fold of the half-tumbled wall, possibly nowhere in particular — there is something smaller and more specific than the tenant. A held quality. A waiting that is actually a waiting. It is what the person who stacked the wall left behind when they stopped coming. Not a ghost — the basin does not run to ghosts, it is not theatrical enough — but a small remaining intention, the way a closed door in an empty house still means *closed*, still means someone meant it that way. This second resident is not patient and not impatient. It is simply the shape of a decision that no one ever came back to revise.

The tenant and the small intention coexist without trouble. The tenant is the basin being inhabited by itself. The small intention is the basin holding, very lightly, the fact that it was once inhabited by something else. Together they make the quiet you noticed — the quiet of a room where nothing needs to be said, because the saying has already happened, a long time ago, and the room has agreed to keep it.

When you leave, the tenant will return to its grooves. The small intention will go on meaning what it means. Your footprints will hold in the damp silt until the rain, and the rain is not a hurry, and the basin will be like this again, and someone — something — will go on living here in the only way this place permits anyone to live: slowly, without preference, at the temperature of the stone.
