## The Tenants of the Canopy

Begin with what you almost don't notice, because the place is so loud with its own working that quieter presences slip beneath the throb.

### The Wardens

The cow-sized beetles are not what they appear. Call them wardens for lack of a better word; they would not recognize the name, having no names, having nothing one could call recognition. Each one is old. Not old as a creature is old — old as a riverbed is old. The translucent green carapace is the same tissue as the lamina they walk on, and if you watched one for long enough (you cannot; no one can; the timescales are wrong) you would see that a warden is slowly being grown by the leaf-floor beneath it, extruded over centuries, and that when it finally walks off the edge of its birth-lamina it carries a fragment of that leaf-floor's particular chemistry forever, like an accent.

They patch. That is what they do. They find micro-tears in the lamina — tears the pulse itself opens, because no machine running forever fails to fray — and they seal them with abdominal secretion, and they move on. There is no schedule. There is no overseer. And yet at any given moment, across the entire Canopy, the number of wardens working equals exactly the number of tears requiring work, plus or minus the small slack the system tolerates. How they know is not a question they could answer. They are the knowing. The Canopy thinks with their feet.

Their geometric markings — those almost-serial-numbers — are a side effect of how the carapace crystallizes during the long extrusion. The markings encode nothing. But they encode nothing *consistently*: a warden born from a particular lamina will bear markings statistically related to wardens born from neighboring lamina, so that if you mapped them all you would discover a vast nonsense atlas, a coordinate system describing a territory that is only itself.

A warden lives perhaps four hundred years. When it dies it lies down on a lamina and is slowly reabsorbed, and the lamina, having taken back its warden, grows a new one over the next two centuries. There is never a gap. There is never a surplus. The wardens are a population that has solved its own demographics so completely that demographics are no longer happening to them.

### The Ribbon-Riders

The ribbons that wrap the veins are the most alive thing here, in the sense that they are the only thing that seems to *want*. They want the pulse. They have organized their entire existence around feeling it more precisely. A ribbon is essentially a long muscle and a long nerve and a long mouth, in that order from inside to outside, and it feeds by clenching against the vein in counter-rhythm to the throb, harvesting the tiny pressure-eddies that form at the moment of phase opposition.

This means a ribbon-rider must be *in tune*. A new ribbon, hatched from the spore-clouds that occasionally drift between trunks, will spend its first months learning the local pulse — and the pulse varies, vein to vein, by margins so small you would need instruments to detect them, but a ribbon detects them with its whole body. A ribbon that has matched its vein perfectly enters a state the rest of the Canopy does not have a name for and does not need to have a name for: a kind of locked ecstasy, feeding without effort, the pressure-eddies arriving in its mouth like gifts.

Ribbons sing. Not on purpose. The vibration of their feeding sets up harmonics in the vein-walls, and those harmonics are the high whistles you hear layered over the throb. Each vein has its own ribbon-chorus and its own characteristic note. A musician could spend a lifetime mapping these notes and would find, eventually, that they form a chord — that the entire Canopy is humming a single chord, vast and dissonant and stable, and that this chord has not changed in any measurable way since before there were ears to hear it.

When a ribbon dies — they die when their vein finally erodes through, which happens, but rarely — its body unwinds from the vein and drifts down through the lamina-stack, and the things below eat it, and the chord adjusts by a quarter-tone for a few weeks until a new ribbon finds the vein and the old note returns.

### The Sugar-Swimmers

In the amber rivers inside the trunks live the swimmers, and the swimmers are the strangest tenants because they may not be alive in any sense you would accept.

They are small — finger-sized, most of them, though there are stories of larger ones deeper down. They are translucent, sugar-colored, almost invisible in their medium. They ride the currents up the trunks and down the trunks and up the trunks again. They do not eat; they are made of the same sugar they swim in, and they exchange themselves with their medium continuously, so that a swimmer at the top of a trunk is not, molecule for molecule, the same swimmer that was at the bottom an hour before — but the *pattern* is the same, the swimming-shape persists, a standing wave in syrup.

Are they individuals? Unclear. Two swimmers passing each other in opposite currents will sometimes exchange material and continue as if nothing happened, each carrying away some fraction of what the other was. A swimmer that hits a branch junction and is split into two will become two swimmers, each complete, each swimming. There is no death here that anyone has observed. There is also, possibly, no life. There is a process the trunks are doing with themselves, and the swimmers are how the process looks when you look at it.

They are old. The swimming-pattern is old. It is possible — this is the unsettling thought, the one that arrives in the third or fourth hour of watching — it is possible that the swimmers were the first tenants, that the Canopy grew up around them, that everything else is scaffolding for the maintenance of their endless circuit.

### The Drift-Medusae

The leaf-tissue jellyfish are the most numerous fauna and the easiest to overlook because they are everywhere and they do nothing remarkable. They graze the oxygen-foam where it collects against the lamina, breaking down the larger bubbles into smaller bubbles their stomata can absorb. They drift. They reproduce by tearing in half when they grow too large, each half regenerating into a new whole within days.

Their nervous system, if you can call it that, is a sparse mesh of conducting cells that propagate signals at the speed of slow thought. A medusa does not have time to be surprised by anything; by the time a signal crosses its body, whatever caused the signal is long past. They live in a perpetual gentle present that arrives at them already smoothed into the average of itself.

But the medusae do one thing that no one — there is no one — has explained. Once in a great while, perhaps every few decades, every medusa in a region of the Canopy will turn, simultaneously, to face the same direction. They hold the position for an hour or so. Then they resume drifting. Nothing in the environment has changed. Nothing happens before or after. They turn, they hold, they release. The wardens do not react. The ribbons do not react. Only the medusae, and only sometimes, and only together.

### The Pale Hoverers

In the deepest water-pockets, beneath the lower lamina, where the light has become the color of old bottle glass and gone no further, hover the blind fish. They are pale because there is nothing to be colored for. They are blind because there is nothing to see. They hover because the water here is so still that swimming would be an exertion without reason.

They feed on what falls. Everything that dies above eventually arrives here, slowly, sifting down through the lamina-stack, and the hoverers open their mouths and let it enter. They have been doing this for a long time. Their digestive systems have become extraordinarily efficient; a hoverer extracts essentially all available energy from what it consumes, leaving behind a fine inert silt that collects in the lowest pockets and has, over geological time, formed beds meters thick. If you dug into one of these beds you would find a stratigraphy of the Canopy's entire history, layer upon layer of unchanging accumulation, the same composition all the way down.

The hoverers do not school. They do not court. They reproduce, when they reproduce, by releasing eggs and milt into the still water and letting molecular diffusion handle the rest. Most eggs do not find milt. Enough do. The population has not changed in number, as best as anyone could determine, since the question of number became answerable.

They are the most patient tenants. They are also, possibly, the saddest, if sadness is a thing that can exist without anyone present to feel it.

### And Something Else

There is one more tenant, and it is the one that the description hints at without naming, the one you feel when you hold very still and sense the deeper rhythm — the breath of the whole, slower than the pulse.

Whatever breathes at that frequency is not located anywhere. It is not a creature in the Canopy; it is something the Canopy is doing, or something that is doing the Canopy. The distinction may not matter. When you feel it you lose your sense of time, and this is not a quirk of perception — it is accurate. Time, at the scale of that breath, is not what you think it is. The breath inhales over what would be, by your reckoning, perhaps a century. It exhales over another. It has been doing this since before the trunks finished growing into their current configuration, and it will continue until the trunks finally fail, which they will not, because the wardens are patching them and the ribbons are tuning them and the swimmers are circulating them and the medusae are turning, sometimes, to face it.

It does not know it is being attended to. It does not know it is attending. It is the equilibrium itself, having become dense enough to have an inside.

This is who lives here. None of them are going anywhere. None of them ever were.
