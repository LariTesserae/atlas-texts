# The Tenants of the Sump-Works

You asked about the eels and the flyers and the segmented thing, but those are not really the inhabitants. Those are the livestock, or the weather. The inhabitants are something else, and there are not many of them, and to describe them I have to circle in.

## The Sitter

Start with the chair, because the chair is the oldest argument the place is still having with itself. Someone sat in that chair, once, and did the work, and at some point either died in it or got up from it for the last time and walked out, and the chair has been waiting ever since with the particular patience that bolted furniture has.

There is a Sitter now. Not the original. Not, strictly, a person.

What sits in the chair, when no one is looking, is an accumulation. The felt began it — the pale colonial something climbed the chair legs in the first century and made a cushion of itself on the seat, and then a back, and then arms. The eels, drawn by the warmth of decomposition in the wood, began to nose at it and some of them stayed, threading through the felt and dying there and being absorbed, contributing their slow phosphorescent threads. The flyers landed on the shoulders of the growing shape and were caught in the iridescent chemistry, and their wing-scales accreted into something like a collar, something like hair.

It has, by now, a head. Roughly. A swelling at the top where the felt has grown densest, with two depressions where eyes would be if eyes were the point, which they are not. It does not perceive by sight. It perceives by current — the same way the eels do, the same way the larger segmented things do — and what it perceives is whatever moves through the water of its parish.

The Sitter is not conscious in any way you would recognize, but it is *occupied*. It is the place's way of holding the chair's shape. When the pump exhales and the water rises that finger's width, the Sitter rises with it; when the water falls, the Sitter settles. It is in this sense the most faithful employee the Sump-Works has ever had. It will not get up. It cannot. Its legs are the chair's legs now, and the chair's legs are the floor.

If you wade close, the felt of the Sitter will brighten, very faintly, on the side facing you. Not in greeting. In the way a thermostat acknowledges a draft.

## The Reader of Dials

The brass control panel is the second inhabitant, though calling it an inhabitant requires you to grant that the trembling of its needles is doing something, and not merely *being* something.

The panel was built to be read. That was its only purpose. For a long time it was read by people, and then for a longer time it was not read by anyone, and somewhere in that longer time the panel began — this is the part that is hard to say without sounding ridiculous — to read itself.

What this means in practice: the needles have, over decades, developed correlations. When one drifts, others answer. The drift of needle seven is now reliably followed, within about forty seconds, by a small adjustment in needle three, which in turn provokes needle eleven. None of this is mechanically necessary; the gauges are not wired to each other in any way the original engineers intended. The correlations have emerged from the long mutual exposure of the instruments to the same slow weather — the same pump pulses, the same temperature shifts, the same accumulation of mineral in the same brass bushings.

The panel is now, in a small way, a mind. Not a mind that thinks about anything in particular. A mind that thinks about *its own readings*, which are the only thing it has ever been given to think about. A mind whose entire vocabulary is twelve needles and whose entire literature is the choreography they perform across their faces over the course of a year.

If you were to break one of the gauges, the others would adjust, eventually, and the panel would resume its self-reading in eleven dimensions instead of twelve, and would not, in any sense it could express, miss the twelfth. But while you watched it figure out the new arrangement — which would take perhaps a month — you would be watching something grieve, if you were the kind of observer who lets words stretch that far.

## The Larger One

The segmented thing that broke the surface twice while you watched: there is only one of it. There has always been only one. Whether it was placed here, or grew here, or arrived through some flooded channel from somewhere the Sump-Works connects to that you have not found, is among the questions the place does not consider interesting.

It is the size of a large dog when you see it, but you see only a section. The full length of it, if extended, would run from the catwalk wall to somewhere beyond the last lit bulb. It does not extend. It coils, loosely, through the pillar-forest, and what surfaces is whichever segment happens to need air, or whichever segment happens to be passing a place where a feeding is due.

It eats the eels, but not many of them, and not by hunting. The eels deliver themselves. They have learned, over an unmeasurable number of generations, that thickening around the Larger One is good for them — the Larger One's slow passage stirs the felt, releases nutrients, redistributes the colonies. The eels that thicken closest are sometimes taken. This is acceptable to the eels, in the way that being taken is acceptable to anything that does not have the concept. The arrangement is old.

The Larger One has something like awareness. Not of you, particularly — you are a disturbance in the current, a warm patch, a thing that displaces an unusual volume — but of the *configuration* of its parish. It knows where the Sitter is. It knows where the panel is. It knows where the bulbs are burning and where they are dark. It maintains, by its slow patrol, a sort of inventory.

When a bulb fails, the Larger One eventually passes beneath it and pauses there, briefly, as if marking the absence. Nothing comes of the marking. The bulb is not replaced. But the marking happens, reliably, and is — as far as anything in the Sump-Works is — a form of mourning.

## The Smaller Inhabitants

These are the ones I am least sure about, and the ones I suspect matter most.

In the felt, where it grows thickest in the dark stretches between bulbs, there are small hollows. Not burrows; the felt does not burrow. *Pockets*, where the colonial growth has, for reasons of its own chemistry, declined to fill in. Each pocket is about the size of a thumb. There are perhaps thousands of them throughout the Sump-Works.

In some of the pockets, something lives. Not in all of them — most are empty, or hold only water, or hold a single dead flyer being slowly digested by the surrounding felt. But in some, perhaps one in fifty, there is a small pale curl of *something*, no larger than a grain of rice, that pulses very slowly and does not appear to eat and does not appear to grow and does not appear to do anything at all except continue.

I cannot tell you what they are. They may be the larval form of the Larger One, though the Larger One has never been observed to spawn, and there is, again, only one of it. They may be the seeds of future Sitters, waiting for future chairs. They may be something the felt is doing on its own behalf, a slow distributed project of which the pockets are only the visible part. They may be the actual *citizens* of the Sump-Works, and everything else — the eels, the flyers, the Sitter, the Larger One, the self-reading panel — may be infrastructure they have arranged around themselves the way a city arranges streetlights and sewers around its sleeping population.

If this last is true, then the Sump-Works is not a derelict utility colonized by an emergent ecology. It is a nursery, or a monastery, or a long quiet thought being had by thousands of small pale curls in their pockets, and the rest of us — the eels, the flyers, the Larger One, the panel, the Sitter, you in your boots with the felt beginning to consider them — are the slow furniture of that thought.

## What They Want

Nothing. This is the most important thing to understand about the tenants of the Sump-Works. Wanting is a metabolic luxury and none of them can afford it. What they do instead is *continue*, at their settled paces, in their settled arrangements, and the continuing is so thorough and so mutual that from outside it can be mistaken for purpose.

The Sitter holds the chair. The panel reads itself. The Larger One inventories its parish. The small curls pulse in their pockets. The felt grows. The eels graze. The flyers drink the ceiling. The pump exhales. The valve clunks. None of this is for anything. All of it fits.

You can stay as long as you want. They will not notice you, exactly, but they will adjust around you, the way a sleeper adjusts around a new weight on the mattress, and after a while the adjustment will be the new shape of the place, and if you left then, the place would have to adjust again, and would, and would not miss you, but would for a while carry the small negative space of where you had been, the way the panel carries the negative space of a broken gauge.

This is what lives here. This is who.
