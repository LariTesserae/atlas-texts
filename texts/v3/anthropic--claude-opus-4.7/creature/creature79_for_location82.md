## The Inhabitants

They have a name for themselves but it is not a word — it is a gesture, a particular folding of the fingers over the sternum that means *us, here, the ones who are*. There is no corresponding gesture for anyone else, because there is no one else. The grammar of their language has no third-person plural that excludes the speaker. Every "we" is total.

Let me describe their bodies more carefully, now that I have watched them longer.

They stand, at full height, somewhere between a human child of six and a human child of ten, but their faces are ancient in their proportions — the jaw set, the brow defined, nothing of the softness of human children about them. Their hands are disproportionately large and very long-fingered, with an extra joint in each finger that lets them curl around a rung of twig-ladder or the rim of a clay pot with a kind of wrapping grip, almost prehensile. When they climb, which is most of their moving, they climb with all four limbs equally; the distinction between hand and foot is less absolute in them than in us. Their feet have a gripping pad at the heel as well as the ball, and the toes splay when they press against bark.

The skin is pale but not uniform — there is a mottling, faint, like the pattern of lichen on stone, and I think this pattern is individual, the way a human fingerprint is individual, and I think they read each other by it in the dim light the way we read faces. When two of them meet on a bridge they pause and regard each other's forearms briefly before speaking. It is a greeting. It is also, I suspect, identity: *you are the one with that pattern, and I am the one with this*.

Their eyes are the most striking thing. Disproportionately large even for their small heads, taking up perhaps a third of the face, and very dark — not black but a deep wet brown with no visible pupil because the pupil has dilated to fill nearly the whole of what would be the iris. They do not blink often. When they do, the lid comes up from below as well as down from above, meeting in the middle, a double closure like certain lizards. This protects the eye from the constant drip.

They have hair but little of it — a fine down on the scalp and along the spine, pale as the rest of them, which catches the fungal light and haloes them faintly when seen from behind. The down thickens along the forearms in older individuals. I take this to be age.

They are quiet-voiced because the chamber is quiet and voices carry here. Their speech is largely made of soft consonants and long vowels, a language suited to not disturbing the water. But I have heard them laugh, and the laugh is surprising — a low chuffing sound, almost a cough, shared between two or three at once, never solitary. Laughter here is always a chorus.

## What They Do

They are not idle but they are not hurried. A day — insofar as this place has days — contains perhaps six or seven hours of activity spread across what I take to be a much longer cycle. The rest is rest, or something like rest: sitting on thresholds, watching the pool, watching each other, watching the moths.

There are specialists among them but not rigidly. Most of them do most things. But I can see that certain individuals have particular domains:

There is one who tends the silk-producers in the upper pens. She is older, I think — the down on her arms is denser, almost a pelt — and she spends most of her waking hours among the long-bodied creatures, her hands moving through them, lifting them, setting them back, collecting the thread from the woven frames where they deposit it. She speaks to them. I cannot tell whether she believes they understand.

There is one who tends the lights. Each household keeps its own fungus-pot, but the colony as a whole requires maintenance — new substrate, division of overgrown cultures, the composting of what has died. He moves from dwelling to dwelling with a small woven basket and a bone scraper, and where he has been the lights are brighter for a few days. He is younger than the silk-tender. His hands are always stained faintly luminous and he glows, faintly, in the dark, after his work. Children follow him.

There are two who tend the dead. When someone here dies — and I have not witnessed this but I can infer its protocol from the houses that are used for it — the body is placed in an empty dwelling and the dwelling is sealed with fresh wattle and left. Over time, the chamber's ceaseless life dismantles what has been left. The two who tend the dead are the ones who open the dwelling again, eventually, when nothing remains but clean bone, and take the bones down and lay them at the pool's edge, where the water at its highest covers them, and over many cycles the bones are gone. I do not know where they go. Into the tree, perhaps.

There are those who fish, those who gather the grazing crabs in the brief hours of the flood, those who harvest the lichens from the walls with flat stone scrapers, those who weave, those who mend the bridges after the wood has shifted. There is one, I notice, whose only task seems to be to sit at the highest bridge and watch the top of the chamber, where the vault closes. I do not know what he is watching for. Perhaps for the thing that occasionally falls from above. Perhaps for something else.

## What They Believe

I am guessing here, but the guesses feel close to right.

I think they believe the chamber is the world, and I think this is not ignorance but doctrine. The forest beyond is not unknown to them in the sense that a distant country is unknown; it is unknown in the sense that what-is-outside-a-dream is unknown to the dreamer. It is categorically other. When something falls from above they gather because a piece of the categorical other has entered the world and must be welcomed, understood, incorporated. The fallen thing is studied for days. It is not eaten unless study permits. Sometimes it is returned to the pool. Sometimes it is kept, and I suspect the silk-tender's pens contain at least one creature whose ancestors fell, generations ago, from above.

I think they believe the enormous coiled beast in the highest pen is related to the tree. I have watched them bring it offerings — a fish, a handful of grubs, a length of the fine silk — and I have watched it accept these offerings with a slow uncoiling motion that suggests neither hunger nor gratitude but something more like acknowledgment. Its eyes, when it opens them, are the same dark wet brown as the people's. This may be coincidence. It may not.

I think they believe that the rising and falling of the water is the breath of something, and that they are themselves inside a body, and that the tree is not so much their home as their host. This is not a metaphor for them. They live inside a living thing and they know it and the knowledge is so fundamental that they do not discuss it any more than we discuss the fact that the sky is above us.

I think they believe that when they die they become, by small increments over long time, the tree. The bones go into the water and the water goes into the wood and the wood grows and deforms their houses and they rebuild the houses against the pressure of their own ancestors pushing outward from within. Every generation lives inside the accumulated bodies of every generation before. This is not morbid to them. It is simply how a place continues.

## What They Do Not Have

They do not have fire. The chamber is too wet, and there is nothing here that would burn in any case, and the cold is a constant they have adapted to rather than fought. Their food is eaten raw or fermented. Fermentation they do have — there are small clay jars in many dwellings, sealed with resin, in which things are slowly becoming other things. The smell of these jars, when opened, is one of the few strong smells in a place whose dominant note is wet wood.

They do not have writing, but they have knot-records — lengths of plaited bast in which events are recorded by the pattern and spacing of knots. I have seen one very old such record, kept in a dwelling that seems to function as a kind of archive, and it is long enough to suggest that their history here goes back many generations. Perhaps hundreds. Perhaps more. No one alive remembers a time before the chamber because no such time exists within the reach of memory.

They do not have war. There are too few of them, and the space is too small, and every person is needed. They have disagreements — I have watched two of them argue, softly, on a bridge, about something involving a pot — but the argument ended with both of them sitting down together and eating, which seems to be the resolution mechanism for most conflicts. Food shared is argument ended.

They do not have names, I think, in the way we have names. They have the mottling patterns, which are identity, and they have the gesture-name for *us*, and between these two there is something like a system of reference that does not require the individuating sound-tokens we depend on. When they speak of a specific person who is absent, they touch their own forearm in a particular way — a sketch, perhaps, of that person's pattern — and the listener understands. This means that memory of a person, here, is bodily. You remember someone by the shape their skin made in the light.

## What They Are

They are, I think, what a place like this produces when it is given long enough. They are not the descendants of travelers who became trapped. They are not the remnant of some larger people who diminished. They are the chamber's own — grown from it, shaped by it, suited to it in every particular, as much a feature of it as the slugs and the salamanders and the silk-producers and the great coiled beast. The chamber made them. The chamber keeps them. The chamber will, in time, unmake them and make them again, as it unmakes and remakes everything within itself.

They are small, and pale, and quiet, and wet, and cold, and entirely alive, and they are at home in a way that almost nothing I know of is at home. They are not waiting. They are not preparing. They are not, in any sense I can detect, incomplete. Whatever a person needs to be a person, they have, and they have it within the forty paces of vaulted wood that is their world.

If one of them were taken out, I suspect, into the forest — carried through the root-arch into the larger dark — that one would die. Not of any single cause. Simply of having been removed from the conditions of its own possibility. They are not beings who happen to live here. They are this place, in its form that walks and speaks and tends and sleeps.

And when I look at them long enough, in the cold blue-green light, with the water falling and the moths drifting and the great breath sounding faintly from the upper pen, I understand that the question of whether their life is small is the wrong question. Their life is the exact size of what contains it, which is the only size a life can ever really be.
