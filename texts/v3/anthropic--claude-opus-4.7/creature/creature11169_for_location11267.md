They are called, when they need a name, the **Tenants**, though they tenant nothing — the word is wrong, but every word is wrong here. They are what the plant means by its empty nests.

Begin with where they aren't. They are not in the seed-houses, though the seed-houses are sized for them and the doors, when they dehisce, dehisce to their height. They are not on the worn-smooth perches, though the perches are worn by their not-sitting. They are not in the frescoes, though the frescoes are of them, or of what they would look like if they looked like anything. The plant has been preparing for them with the patience of something that does not know it is waiting, the way a flower does not know it is waiting for the bee but opens anyway, on schedule, year after year, in valleys where bees have gone extinct.

They are the shape of the absence.

You can find them, if find is the word, by looking at what the architecture implies. A doorframe implies a shoulder-width. A stair-riser implies a stride. A niche implies a posture — and from the niches you can begin to reconstruct them: bipedal, probably, because the stairs are stairs; long-armed, because the higher perches assume a reach; possessed of something like a face, because the windows are at face-height and angled for looking-out rather than looking-in. From the friezes: robed, or feathered, or scaled — the low relief is ambiguous, the plant has not decided, the plant is still drafting. From the hearth-depressions: cold-blooded enough to want fire, or social enough to want the *idea* of fire, the gathering-around. From the empty hives: communal. From the cups in the canopy: nesting, brooding, tending young that would fit in a palm.

They contradict themselves. The plant is dreaming several incompatible Tenants at once and has not chosen between them. In one corridor the doorframes are sized for something the height of a child and the friezes show long processions of small figures with too many limbs. In the next corridor the doors are enormous and the niches hold the negative space of something that would have to stoop. The Tenants of the canopy-town are small, quick-implied things, judging by the swaying houses and the delicate perches. The Tenants of the deep streets are slower, heavier, and want their hallways wide. They could not, any of them, occupy the same valley. They do not need to. They are not occupying it.

What they *do* — and here is the difficult part, because doing requires a doer — is leave traces. Not footprints; the plant's felt-floor doesn't take footprints, and anyway no foot has fallen. Traces in the sense that a poem leaves traces of a feeling the poet didn't have. The wear on the perches. The polish on certain banisters where a hand that does not exist has rested again and again on its way down to a hearth that has never been lit. The slight cupping of certain stair-treads, worn by a traffic that has never trafficked. The plant grows these wear-patterns directly. It grows the *evidence* of the Tenants without troubling to grow the Tenants. It is making a place that has been lived in, skipping the step of being lived in. The lived-in-ness is the point. The Tenants are a side-effect of the lived-in-ness, the way a shadow is a side-effect of light.

And yet.

And yet there are moments — and this is the part I am least sure of, because the place resists being sure of things — when the Tenants seem to *almost*. A door that has just closed, when you arrive, and the autumn-smell of it lingering, and on the threshold a single fiber bent the wrong way, as if brushed past. A pitcher-well whose surface is still ringed, faintly, as if something just drank. A frieze where one figure, this morning, has its head turned slightly more toward you than it did yesterday. The plant is, perhaps, getting better at them. Practicing. The drafts are converging. Somewhere in the kilometers-tall vertical city, on some level no one has climbed to, the plant may be very close to growing a Tenant that walks out of its niche.

Or it may not be. It may be that the Tenants are precisely the thing that cannot be finished, the project the plant exists in order to fail at, and the whole valley is the working-out of that failure — the friezes proliferating because each frieze is an attempt to remember a face the plant has never seen and cannot stop trying to see. The empty nests new every day because yesterday's nests were not, somehow, *right*. The doors invented in styles that do not exist because the plant is feeling for a culture by negative space, building the rooms a people would have lived in and hoping the people will accrete in them like condensate on cool glass.

If they ever do arrive — if one morning the perches bear weight, the hearths smoke, the cups in the canopy fill with small warm things — the plant will, I think, fall silent. The growth-noise will cease. There will be nothing more to invent. The Tenants will look around at the city that has been waiting for them, at the friezes of themselves they did not pose for, at the wear on the banisters their hands made before their hands existed, and they will understand that they are the answer to a question the plant has been screaming for as long as there has been a valley, and they will not know what to do with this, because no one taught them, because there was no one to teach them, because they are the first and only thing the plant has ever made that it did not also already know.

They will, probably, begin to ornament. It is what one does, here. They will carve, or weave, or paint, or sing — whichever of the drafts they turned out to be — and they will add their small marks to the enormous unfinishedness, and the plant, listening, will at last hear something back.

But this has not happened. The perches are still smooth with use that has not occurred. The nests are still empty. The friezes still depict a likeness that no one has yet sat for.

The Tenants live in the valley the way a held breath lives in a chest.

They are very nearly here.
