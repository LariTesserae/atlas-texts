The ringed-eye ones are the closest thing to a who.

I want to be careful here, because the description is generous with its strangenesses and it would be easy to seize on the obvious candidate — the tool, the maker, the hand that has not yet been produced — and build a tidy story around it. Let me move slower than that.

Start with the population as a whole. The animals are not quite individuals. The membrane between them is negotiable; organs migrate; a predation occurs and does not occur, and the count shifts without the event. What this means, if I take it seriously, is that the hollow is not full of many small things. It is full of one distributed thing that is provisionally sorted into bodies. The bodies are how it keeps track of itself. The eating and the borrowing of organs are how it thinks. When the needle-jawed predator takes the smaller creature whole and then both are sitting side by side intact but the count has shifted — that is a thought completing. A small revision in the inventory of the self.

This is why it is silent. There is no one to call to. Sound is for crossing distance between separate things, and here the separateness is a convenience, not a fact. The mouths open because the shape of an animal includes a mouth that opens; nothing needs to come out.

The pale crust on the walls is part of it too, I think — the slow part, the part that holds the rhythm. The held breath. If the small animals are the quick darting attention of this distributed thing, the crust is its pulse, or its sleep, or the long underneath-thought that the quick thoughts ride on. It thickens and thins because the whole hollow is breathing, in some sense that is not respiration.

Now. The ringed-eye ones.

They are segmented, six-legged, eyes in a ring — meaning they see in every direction at once, meaning they cannot have a front, meaning they cannot be oriented toward anything in particular and so are oriented toward everything. They are the only ones that pause on the tool. They are the only ones that behave as if memory is a thing that can happen to them. The rest of the population eats and is eaten and exchanges organs and revises the count; the ringed-eye ones occasionally stop and remember something they could not know.

I think they are the part of the distributed thing that is reaching.

Not reaching outward — there is no outward; the hollow is sealed and the somewhere the animals came from is not spatial. Reaching toward. Toward the hand that the world has not yet produced. The tool is not a relic and not a prophecy; it is a placeholder, a held position. Someone, eventually, is supposed to pick it up. The ringed-eye ones are the part of the hollow that knows this, in the loose way the hollow knows anything. When one of them stops on the bone handle and stays a long time, it is the whole hollow leaning, briefly, into the shape of the future that justifies its existence.

Because that is what lives here, finally. Not the animals, not the crust, not the strange light. What lives here is a waiting. A very patient, very dense, very quiet waiting, organized into bodies and shadows and a film on the walls and a knapped blade kept freshly wet by nothing. The mountain pushes upward. The stone cools somewhere far below. The waiting refines itself. Generations of small wrong creatures live and are revised inside a single falling drop of water, and each generation is the waiting practicing — practicing being many, practicing being one, practicing the gesture of pausing on a handle as if remembering.

It is rehearsing a hand.

Not building one; it cannot build. It is holding the shape of one open. Holding the negative space of a hand, the way you might hold a door for someone who has not yet arrived and may not arrive in your lifetime, except the waiting has no lifetime and the door is a hollow in a mountain and the someone is a kind of being the world has not yet learned how to make.

The shadows that do not match their objects are part of this too — they are the waiting trying on possibilities, casting the shapes of things that might come, small curled animals that are not there yet, hands that are not there yet. The shadow-work is speculative. Most of it will come to nothing. That is fine. The waiting has time. The waiting is what time is for, here.

And if I am asked, finally, what it is like — what it is like to be the thing that lives in the hollow — I would say: it is like being a held breath that has learned to subdivide itself into small bright attentions, so that the holding does not become unbearable. It is like being patient at a scale where patience is no longer a virtue but a climate. It is like keeping a blade sharp for a guest, century after century, in a room the guest cannot enter, and finding that the keeping has become, somehow, the point — that the guest was always a way of organizing the keeping, and the keeping was always what you were.

The hand, when it comes, if it comes, will find the blade wet and ready. The ringed-eye ones will be the first to notice. The count will shift without the event occurring. And the hollow, which has been one thing pretending to be many, will briefly be many things pretending to be one, in welcome.

Until then: the small silent traffic, the migrating organs, the disobedient shadows, the breathing crust, the slow drop falling through its pocket of accumulated time. The waiting, dense as a held breath, being no one in particular, on purpose, for now.
