They are not, in the strict sense, separate creatures. That is the thing one has to come to, walking the green lanes long enough.

Start with what is easy to see.

There is, for instance, a long-bodied browser the size of a large dog, pale-flanked, with a skin like fine kid leather over a layer of something denser. It has no true mouth in the way a mammal does — instead a soft puckered aperture at the front of its head that fits, with a particular intimacy, against the side of certain barrel-plants. It does not bite. It applies suction. The barrel-plant, where the browser feeds, has a place — a slightly puckered scar of its own — that has grown to receive exactly this shape of aperture. The browser drinks for perhaps a quarter of an hour and then moves on, and the place on the barrel-plant seals itself within the day. The browser, in return, has carried away in its gut the seeds of a small understorey vine that grows only in browser dung, and which the barrel-plant uses, indirectly, to bind the soil at its base.

There is no version of the browser that does not feed at these puckers. There is no version of the barrel-plant that does not grow them.

Now widen the view.

The predators — they look, at first, like something between a cat and an otter, low and long, with soft pads and a coat the color of the pale dust — kill the browsers, but not often, and not without a kind of ceremony. They have been bred quiet by the country, as everything has. A predator will follow a browser for a day, two days, sometimes longer, walking parallel through the next lane over, not hidden exactly but not announcing itself. When the take comes, it comes at a particular kind of plant-junction — a place where three or four columns meet and make a small chamber. The predator drinks the browser there, opens the body, and the body's fluids soak into the dust at the base of those particular columns. Those columns, over generations, have grown taller, fluted more deeply, made better cups for dew. The chamber, in other words, is a place the plants have made for the predators to make for them.

You begin to see it.

The soft-winged things — call them moth-things, though they are not moths — carry pollen between districts, yes, but they also carry something else: they carry, on the fine hairs of their bodies, a particular microbial film, and they deposit it on the flowering cups when they visit. Without that film the cups do not set seed. The moth-things eat from the cups; the cups eat, in a sense, from the moth-things. Neither one is whole without the other, and the film itself — when you look closely — is not really an organism of its own anymore. It has no life outside the moth-thing's hairs and the cup's inner surface. It is a tissue the two species share, the way two organs in a body share a membrane.

This is the pattern. Everywhere you look, the pattern.

The little soft-footed lizards that flow up and down the columns are eating, mostly, a kind of slow scale-insect that lives on the column's skin. The scale-insect, left alone, would smother the column. The lizard's droppings, left where they fall in the column's flutes, feed the column directly through tiny absorptive pits that have evolved precisely there. The lizard cannot digest without a particular gut-flora it picks up only by licking, at intervals, a kind of waxy bloom that certain columns secrete in certain weeks. The columns secrete the bloom only when the scale-insect load gets high. The columns, in other words, call their lizards.

The pale cattle-sized grazers — these are the strangest. They move in loose groups of six or eight through the wider lanes, and they do something no animal in our country does: they prune. They take, from each plate-plant they pass, exactly one plate, and only from plates of a certain age. They do not overgraze. They cannot overgraze; something in them refuses the wrong plate, will not even close the mouth on it. And the plate-plants, where a grazer has taken a plate, send up — within days — a new shoot from the cut, which becomes, eventually, the next tier of the building. The grazers are the country's architects. Without them the plate-plants would grow only upward, in a single stalk, and there would be no tiered structure, no shaded chambers beneath, no plant-city as such. The grazers build the city by eating it, and the city has bred them to eat it the right way.

So: what lives here?

A single distributed creature lives here. It has not noticed yet that it is a single creature. Its parts still chase and flee and feed and flower as if they were separate, and in the small local sense they are — the predator does kill the browser, the browser does die, the death is real to the browser. But step back far enough and the deaths are circulation. The browser's body becomes the chamber where the next browser will be made. The predator's patience is the country's patience. The moth-things carrying their shared membrane between districts are nerves, of a slow kind, telling one part of the country what another part is doing. The lazy winds carrying pollen and scent are a second nervous system, slower still. The whole country is one body thinking one extremely slow thought, and the thought is: *become*.

This is why it is so quiet. A body does not shout at itself. A body's organs do not call to each other across the dark; they signal in chemistry, in pressure, in the small adjustments of tissue against tissue. The rare low notes that pass between distant points in the plant-city at evening — these are not language. They are something more like a heartbeat heard from inside the chest: a confirmation, passed from one quarter to another, that the country is still here, still working, still making itself.

And the rawness of the ground beneath — the unweathered stone, the glassy fresh edges — fits, now. The country is young because the creature is young. It has only recently begun. It does not yet know what it is. The plants are its first organs, the most obvious; the animals are its later refinements, its specialists, its couriers and pruners and recyclers. In another age — a long age, an age in which the dust deepens and the stone below softens into true soil — the creature may grow further organs we cannot yet imagine. It may grow something like a brain, in some district, where the densest knots of signal-traffic meet. It may grow something like a voice. Or it may not. It may simply continue, century after century, organ added to organ, district to district, until the country has finished arriving and the creature, finally, looks around — through ten million pale eyes in ten million soft-winged moth-things — and recognizes itself.

For now it does not recognize itself. For now it grazes, and prunes, and drinks, and kills, and pollinates, and breathes warm air up into small dry clouds, and applies, somewhere below, the steady pressure of a root against a stone, and waits — without knowing it is waiting — to be born the rest of the way.

That is what lives here. One thing, still becoming one thing, in a country still becoming a country, under a warm and ordinary sun.
