# The Inhabitants

## The Marmots

There are three colonies, as you guessed from the three pitches, and they have been three colonies for longer than any human reckoning. The eastern colony lives in the talus and is the most numerous; their burrows go deep under the slabs and connect in ways no one has mapped because no one has tried. The northern colony is smaller, six or seven adults, and lives under the willow scrub where the soil is deep enough to dig but the roots make the tunnels strange and angled. The southern colony lives nearest the old wind-break and has, over generations, incorporated its lower stones into their burrow architecture; one entrance is framed by what was once part of a human wall.

They are large, these marmots — heavy-bodied, golden-brown across the back, paler at the belly, with the dark mask of their kind. In high summer they spend most of the day eating: cushion-plant flowers, willow buds, the soft tips of grasses. They sit up on rocks to watch. They are not afraid of the cirque's other residents and have worked out an arrangement with the ravens that involves the marmots' young being kept close to burrow-mouths when raven-shadows pass, and the ravens not pressing the matter.

What you should understand about the marmots is that they are the cirque's parliament. The whistling is not just alarm. The three colonies maintain, through their overlapping calls, a continuous low-level conversation about who is in the bowl and where. A pika's call does not propagate the same way; a raven's croak does not carry the same information. The marmots' whistles, in their three pitches, triangulate. When you stand by the tarn and the eastern colony begins and the others join, what is being communicated is not *danger* but *presence, located, observed*. The bowl always knows where you are.

In winter they sleep, all of them, in great breathing piles deep under the snow, and the cirque belongs for six months to the wind and the pikas and the things that move at night.

## The Pikas

Smaller, rounder, tailless, the color of weathered stone. They live in the talus on the east wall in numbers that would astonish anyone who tried to count them — perhaps two hundred, perhaps more. Each pika holds a small territory among the rocks and defends it with that sharp single note, and because the territories are densely packed the notes overlap as you noticed, into something almost continuous.

They are haymakers. Through the short summer each pika cuts vegetation — sedges, forbs, willow leaves — and carries it in its mouth to a chosen rock, where it lays the cuttings out to cure in the sun, then stores them under overhangs in the talus. By late summer every south-facing slab in the east wall has its small green offering drying on it. The pikas do not hibernate; they live all winter under the snow on these stored haystacks, moving through tunnels in the snowpack between cache and den.

They are the cirque's smallest deliberate makers. Everything else here is shaped by water and time and weather. The pikas alone *gather and arrange*, and their tiny harvests are the only agriculture this high place has ever known. The painted figure on the east wall with raised arms might, if you let yourself think loosely, be a pika-thought as easily as a human one.

## The Ravens

A pair, mated, with a nest on a ledge near the rim that they have used and rebuilt for — it is hard to say. Ravens live thirty or forty years in the wild, and nests get inherited. This nest is a great mound of sticks and bones and the occasional shred of weathered cloth from the prayer-post; it has been a nest for longer than the pair currently occupying it has been alive.

These two know the cirque better than any creature in it. They patrol it morning and evening. They know which marmot burrows have pups and which don't, where the pikas' caches are, where the blue sheep cross the talus, where a fox once denned. They know the trail down — yes — and they know the trail up, and they know which days the clouds will sit on the rim and which days they'll lift. They have an enormous, specific vocabulary of croaks and knocks and liquid notes, and a great deal of it is conversation between themselves about exactly these matters.

About human visitors they have opinions. They remember faces, or something like faces — the shape of a person, the gait, the smell of the smoke a particular person makes. Visitors who behave themselves are escorted down with what sounds like cheerful muttering. Visitors who damage things are escorted down with sharper notes and occasional low passes. Visitors who try to take the obsidian flakes from the hearth-stone find their packs investigated thoroughly the next morning, the flakes sometimes missing, sometimes simply moved. The ravens are not guardians in any mystical sense. They are simply old, and observant, and they have preferences.

## The Choughs

Different from the ravens entirely. The choughs are a flock, eight or ten, glossy black with their bright red bills and red legs, and they are not residents in the same sense — they come up from lower cliffs to feed and play in the updrafts and then go back down at night. They are the cirque's tourists, in a way. They yelp and wheel and tumble in the air for the joy of it, which is a thing choughs do that ravens generally do not. The ravens tolerate them. The marmots ignore them.

## The Larger Movement in the Willows

You were right to be uncertain. Sometimes it is a blue sheep — a young ewe most often, sometimes a small group — coming down off the higher rocks to crop the willows. Their hooves on the wet moss make a soft sucking sound that carries oddly in the bowl. They drink at the tarn and leave neat dark pellets among the cushion plants and go back up before dusk.

But sometimes, perhaps four or five times in a decade, it is the other thing. A snow leopard does pass through. Not lives here — the cirque is too small to hold one — but transits, on routes that loop across many valleys and ridges. When one comes, the marmots know first, and the whistling is different: not the standard triangulation but a high, urgent, sustained note that all three colonies hold together for as long as the cat is in the bowl. The pikas go silent. The ravens go to their nest and watch. The leopard usually drinks at the tarn, sometimes rests for an hour on a flat slab, sometimes takes a blue sheep if one is there, and moves on. It has known about this place all its life, as its mother knew, as her mother knew. The cirque is a node on a map that exists only in the heads of snow leopards and is older than any human map of anywhere.

## The Small and Numerous

In the moss live springtails and tardigrades in densities that defy intuition — millions, in the area of a single boot-print. Among the willows, bumblebees the size of grapes, fat and slow in the cold, working the cinquefoil and the gentians. A few species of beetle, dark-shelled, slow. Crane flies in late summer. A particular small moth, gray with a single eye-spot, that flies only in the brief warm hour after the cliffs catch the last light, when the air is still warm from the day but the cold is coming down — a twenty-minute moth, essentially, repeating its short urgent life across the short urgent summer.

In the tarn: very little, and what is there is small and ancient in lineage. A few copepods. Some kind of small flatworm on the under-sides of the stones. No fish — the outflow is too steep, the water too cold, the bowl too isolated. The tarn is a clear empty eye.

On the cliffs and slabs: lichens in a slow community that has been negotiating its boundaries for thousands of years. The black lichen closing over the scratched spiral is one species; the bright orange rosettes on the south-facing rocks are another; the pale gray-green crusts that cover most of the columns are a third and slowest. They are not really separate inhabitants from the cliffs themselves anymore. The rock is lichen-rock. The columns weep through lichen.

## The Ones That Are Almost Not There

Here is where it gets harder to speak plainly. The cirque has been visited for a very long time, and not all of what was brought up has gone back down. The prayer-cloths on the juniper post are mostly thread now, but each was tied with an intention, and intentions are a kind of small slow thing that lingers in places like this the way lichens linger on stone. The hearth has held many fires. The spiral was scratched by someone who was thinking about something specific, and the thinking has not entirely left the slab.

This is not to say the cirque is haunted, which is the wrong word and the wrong category. It is to say that the cirque is, among its other qualities, a place where small human attentions have accumulated without being cleaned away, and where those attentions sit alongside the marmot-parliament and the raven-memory and the leopard-map and the lichen-negotiation as one more layer of *who lives here*. The painted figure with raised arms on the east wall is, in some sense that has nothing to do with belief, still raising its arms. The count of vertical strokes is still counting. Whoever made them is long gone, but the gesture remains, fed by the same water that feeds the lichens that almost erase it.

## Together

What lives in the Hanging Cirque, then, is not a list of species but a kind of community of attentions — animal, vegetable, mineral, and something more — that has worked out a balance over so much time that the balance now feels like the place itself. The marmots watch. The pikas gather. The ravens remember. The leopards pass. The lichens negotiate. The water moves without hurry. The human marks persist quietly. And the great vertical absence holds all of it cupped, exposed, kept.

It is crowded. It is empty. It is loud. It is held. Whatever lives here lives by being part of the holding.
