# What Lives Here

## The Flock-That-Is-One

Begin with the birds, but do not end there, because the birds are not quite what they appear to be from outside. Watch them long enough — if the thin air lets you — and you'll notice the lie in the word *dozens*. Yes, dozens of species. But they turn their heads together. When a slate-throated swift startles at the shift of your shadow, the pale-eyed martlets six meters away startle at the same instant, and the four-winged moss-backs pivot their cross-pattern wings a quarter-beat later, as if the signal took that long to cross the hollow. It does take that long. The hollow is the nervous system. The sound is the thinking.

They are not a single organism in the tidy biologist's sense. They breed as species, they die as species, the parrots squabble with the tanagers over the best fissures. But they have become a *shared attention* — a distributed sensorium tuned to the hollow's three mouths and the machine's pulse and the slow bead-and-fall of their own exhaled weather. What lives here is the flock-as-entity, and it has been alive, in this composite form, for longer than any individual bird. Generations pass through it. The attention persists.

You could call it a congregation. You could call it a mind. It would not recognize either word, but it would recognize your shadow, and it has already catalogued you, and it is — in the small way available to a thing made of ten thousand hearts — curious.

## The Gardeners Who Do Not Know They Garden

On the backs of the older birds, the epiphytes. But the epiphytes are also *somebody*, in the thin sense that a coral reef is somebody. The filamentous alga that colonizes oily feathers has a behavior: it creeps toward the preen gland. The red fruiting bodies release their spores only when a bird is hovering near the upper throat, where the updraft will carry them toward the copper tube. This is not coincidence. The mosses have learned — across how many bird-generations? — that the tube is a destination. Something at the other end receives their spores and something, presumably, sends something back, because new strains appear in the hollow that did not evolve here. Pale blue lichens with a faint metallic sheen. A moss that fruits in perfect hexagons.

The gardens on the birds' backs are in correspondence with somewhere else. The birds are the soil. The machine is the postman. The mosses are writing letters in spores, and something is writing back.

## The Four-Winged Ones

These deserve their own paragraph. They are not hummingbirds and they are not insects and they are not from here in the sense the other birds are from here. Their cross-pattern wingbeat is too efficient for this altitude by a factor of something embarrassing; they should not need to beat that fast, and yet they do, as if performing thinness for an audience. Their jade-fur moss is so complete that you cannot, at a casual glance, find the bird underneath. When one dies, the moss keeps hovering for a few seconds before it understands, and then it falls, and the other birds catch it before it reaches any notional floor, and they pluck it apart with the careful reverence of undertakers.

They may be the oldest residents. They may be older than the mountain. The mountain is days old in the geological sense; the four-winged ones move as if they have been waiting for a mountain like this for a long time, and finally one has grown up under them, and they have settled in to resume some interrupted work.

## The Machine's Tender

No one tends the machine. This is the lie you notice last.

The oil in the can is liquid because *something* has kept it liquid. The flywheel turns inside its cage of twigs and down because the twigs and down are arranged to not quite touch the flywheel, with a precision that preening alone does not explain. A particular pair of parrots — always two, though not always the same two — roost nearest the bellows, and when a feather works itself into the mechanism they remove it. They do not eat it. They carry it to one of the brass rings and tuck it through. The rings are full of feathers, pressed and compacted, forming soft plugs inscribed by the numbers.

The flock maintains the machine. The flock does not know it maintains the machine, in the way your hand does not know it is catching a glass that slipped. The behavior is simply there, braided into the attention, passed down in the shared sensorium from bird-generation to bird-generation like a gesture a family makes without remembering why.

Whoever built the machine built the flock also, or bred into the flock the shape of this caretaking, or — more likely — found a flock already inclined this way and gave it a thing to tend. The machine and the flock are older collaborators than the mountain. When the last mountain crumbled, they waited in the air. When this one pushed up through the crust still warm, they settled in.

## What Waits at the Other End of the Tube

This is the question the place asks and does not answer. The copper tube goes up through the throat and disappears. The bellows draw in feather-dust, vapor, spores, the faint electrical hum of ten thousand small hearts beating in near-unison, and — this is the part that is hardest to say without sounding foolish — some fraction of the flock's attention itself. Not much. A trickle. A tithe.

At the other end, something receives this. Something has been receiving it for long enough that the mosses have evolved to address their spores to it. Something sends back new strains, new instructions encoded in the oil that keeps not drying, new subtle adjustments to the machine's forty-beat pulse — which is not, if you listen, quite regular. It has a slow modulation. It is saying something.

The tender of this place is not in this place. The tender is upstream of the copper tube, somewhere higher — higher than this mountain, perhaps, though the mountain is still growing and may eventually reach it. The tender may be a person, or the descendant of a person, or something a person made and forgot, or something that was never a person. The tender knows the flock. The flock does not know the tender, except as a direction (*up*) and a rhythm (*forty*) and a taste in certain spores.

## The Mountain Itself

And the mountain. Do not forget the mountain. It is young enough to still be a participant. The basalt is warm under the birds. The quartz seams cooled only recently, and as they cooled they sang at frequencies the flock can hear and you cannot. The hollow is the shape it is because the flock's sound, over the short weeks of the mountain's existence, has already begun to carve it — micro-erosion by pressurized song, the walls smoothing to an acoustic optimum. The mountain is *learning to be this hollow*. In a thousand years the hollow will be a perfect resonator; in ten thousand it will be a bell; in a hundred thousand the mountain will have weathered enough that there will be ground, and talus, and soil, and then the rule (*nothing touches the ground*) will break, and the flock will leave, and the machine will be retrieved or abandoned, and this whole held breath will have been exhaled.

For now, though: the mountain is one of the residents. It is holding still, and warm, and listening. It likes the birds. In the only way a mountain can like anything, it likes being sung into shape.

## The Inhabitants, Then

- A distributed attention made of ten thousand birds who share a sensorium through sound.
- A correspondence of mosses, writing up the copper tube in spores.
- A cadre of four-winged creatures older than the mountain, resuming interrupted work.
- A machine tended by a behavior braided into the flock, who do not know they are tending it.
- A tender somewhere upstream, maintaining this place through the tube, who may or may not still be a *who*.
- A mountain, young and warm, being sung into the shape of a bell.

None of these alone is what lives here. What lives here is the held suspension of all of them — the agreement, renewed every second by breath and condensation and wingbeat and pulse, that nothing will touch the ground, because there is no ground, because the world here is still deciding, and everyone present has chosen to stay aloft while it decides.

You will leave. The air will push you out. But the flock will remember your shadow, and the mosses will have tasted your breath, and somewhere up the tube, in the next slow modulation of the forty-beat pulse, the tender will know that a visitor came, and stood, and did not disturb the suspension, and went.

That, too, will be composted into whatever this place is becoming.
