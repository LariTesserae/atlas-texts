# The Dwellers in the Pith

They are small. This is the first thing to understand — they were always going to be small, because the cavity, for all that it is the size of a market town to us, is the size of a world to them, and a world must have room for its sky. They stand perhaps as high as a human forearm is long. They are built narrow and long-fingered, with the compact density of things that have had to be durable for a very long time in a place that does not offer much.

Their skin is the color of the pith-blocks: a pale, warm bone-beige, very slightly translucent at the extremities, so that in the amber light their fingertips and earlobes seem lit from within, as if they themselves were small lanterns of husk-stuff. This is not decorative. It is what happens to a body that has lived for uncounted generations inside a single continuous afternoon, drinking light instead of shade. Their eyes are large and dark and lidded twice — an inner membrane that closes during the deep-tone weathers, when the dust rises. No pigment in the iris; the dark is the pupil, swollen wide to take in what an amber world gives. In the rare near-silences, when they come out into the lanes, you can see the pupils contract to pinpricks, as if the quiet itself were a brightness.

They have no hair in the way we mean it. What grows from their scalps and along their spines is more like the pale vine-leaves of the upper galleries: thin, papery, faintly ribbed, and it grows in slow tiers that they bind with resin. The resin-plugs in their ears are made from the same substance that binds their hair, so that in a sense they are always stoppering themselves with themselves. A child's first resin is given by a parent; an elder's last resin is left in the shrine at the chamber of the thinning wall. Between those two resinings is a life.

Their hands are the most developed thing about them. Six fingers, long and very fine, with a seventh vestigial digit at the heel of the palm that does nothing anymore but was perhaps, once, for gripping something that no longer exists — a thing their ancestors held, in the outer world, before whatever brought them here, if anything brought them here, if they were not always of the seed. The fingers are where their language lives. Not signed in the air, as deaf humans sign, but tapped: the fingertips of one hand against the inner forearm of the other, in quick patterned sequences, with the palm held so that the taps can also be *felt* by a second person resting their fingers lightly on the back of the speaker's hand. Conversation is therefore tactile as well as visual. Two dwellers speaking at length will sit with their hands interlaced along the forearms, tapping into each other, and from a distance this looks like nothing so much as a slow mutual grooming, or a shared braiding.

They have names, but the names are not spoken — they cannot be spoken; no one has spoken anything here for so long that the throat-apparatus has gone almost vestigial, except for the one use that remains. Names are tapped. A name is a specific short rhythm, five or seven beats, and it is given at the first resining and it is tapped onto the child's sternum by every adult in the cavity, one after another, so that the child learns its own name as a thing felt in the chest before it is a thing made by its own hands. When a dweller dies, the others gather and tap the name onto the body's sternum one last time, and then no one taps that rhythm again, ever. The rhythm goes into the dust with the body. The silver dust of the bowl is therefore also an archive of unspoken names, each of which was once a small drum-pattern on a small chest.

There are, at present, forty-one of them.

This is known because it is one of the few things counted. They do not count much — there is little that requires counting, in a world whose inventory changes so slowly — but they count themselves, and they count the pods of the column-trees at each dry fruiting, and they count the crystal nodes that form in the husk-wall (which is how they know the husk is still thickening in most places, and thinning in one). Forty-one is low. The oldest of them remember when it was sixty. The oldest-but-one remembers being told of a time when it was over two hundred, and the lanes were full, and one could not climb a ladder without waiting for someone else to descend. That time is now a story tapped into children, and the children do not quite believe it, because the city as they know it has always been echoing and ample, with whole tiers given over to the moths.

What do they do?

They tend. This is the shape of their days, insofar as they have days. They tend the fan-plants, because the fan-plants move the resin-air and the resin-air is breath. They tend the fungal shelves, because the fungus yields the cloth-fibers they press to their faces and the inner rind they chew for gel. They tend the column-trees, because the pods become roofs and bowls and the shrine at the thinning wall. They tend each other: a great deal of their time, more than any other single activity, is spent in tactile conversation, interlaced at the forearm, saying slow things slowly. And they tend the city itself — they sweep certain lanes and allow others to deepen, they polish the husk-wall where hands have always passed, they rebind the vine-catwalks when the fiber frays. They are, in effect, the maintenance of a place that was built for more of them than there are.

This is the central fact of their existence and they know it. They are fewer than the city was made for. The dwellings outnumber them, the ladders outnumber them, the lanes outnumber them. Every one of them lives with the awareness that they are walking through a larger shape than they fill — that the city is a garment cut for a bigger body, and they are the body now, smaller, rattling a little in the sleeves. They do not find this sad, exactly. They find it *structural*. The city is what it is; they are what they are; the fit is the fit. In their tapped language there is a phrase that recurs often, which might be translated as *the shape that holds us is larger than we are*, and this is said without grief, the way one might say *the ceiling is above our heads*.

They have an art, and it is this: in the rare near-silences, when the outer world is briefly still, they come out of their houses and stand in the lanes and open their vestigial throats and *sing*. The song is not planned. It is not composed. It is what has accumulated in them since the last near-silence, which may have been weeks or months or — once, famously — seven years ago. They have been saving it. What comes out is not language; they have no spoken language; it is something closer to a collective exhalation with pitch, a chord made of forty-one throats that have not been used since the last time the world went quiet. It lasts as long as the silence lasts, which is usually a minute or two, occasionally longer. Then the groan of the husk resumes and they stop, mid-note, and go back into their houses, and the song is over, and they will not make another until the world is still again.

They believe — though *believe* is too strong; they *hold*, the way one holds a pod in the palm — that the song is heard. Not by whatever moves beyond the husk; they have no theology of the outer thing. They hold that the song is heard by the husk itself. That the wall which contains them is also, in some sense, a listener, and that in the near-silences it is resting, and in the resting it can attend to them, and in the attending it is given something — some small countervailing pressure against the long pressure of the outside — that helps it continue to hold. This is why the shrine at the thinning chamber is built of offerings not to the thing beyond but to the wall. The wall is the one who holds. The wall is, in a sense that is not quite worship and not quite kinship, *theirs*, and they are *its*, and the arrangement has held for so long that the holding itself is, as the description says, the culture, but it is also — and this is the thing that might not have been obvious from outside — the *relationship*. They love the wall. They do not have a word for this either, but the way they polish it where hands have passed, the way they leave their last resin at its thinnest place, the way they sing to it when the outside goes quiet: these are love, in the shape love takes in a place that has only ever had one sky.

There is one more thing.

Among the forty-one, there is always one — and only one, at any given time — whose task is to sit in the chamber of the thinning wall. Not to look through. Looking through is not forbidden, exactly, but no one does it, because to press one's face to that wall is to see the blurred enormous shape of whatever is outside, and to see that shape is to know, in a way that cannot be un-known, that the world is not the world. The one who sits there does not look. They sit with their back to the wall, facing the shrine, and they tap, very slowly, the names of everyone currently alive in the cavity, onto their own sternum, in a rotation that takes about a day to complete. Then they begin again. When they die, someone else takes the post. It is considered neither an honor nor a burden. It is simply one of the things that must be done, like tending the fan-plants, and it has been done for as long as anyone remembers, and no one remembers why it started, and no one has stopped it, because stopping it would be a change, and changes, in a place that has held this long, are regarded with the particular caution of people who understand that the not-yet is the shape of the world, and the shape of the world is what they live inside, and to disturb the shape is to risk the germination, which is the end.

So they tend, and they tap, and they sit with the wall, and they wait — though they do not think of it as waiting, because waiting implies an end one is moving toward, and they are not moving toward anything. They are simply *being held*, and *holding in return*, for as long as the holding lasts.

Forty-one small bright-fingered persons, under an amber sky that has been afternoon for longer than memory, inside a seed that has not yet decided to become a tree.
