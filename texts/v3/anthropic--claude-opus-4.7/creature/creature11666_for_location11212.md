# The Riders of the Chord

They have no name for themselves. Naming requires a mouth that closes, and theirs do not.

They are the **sail-things** — but that word is too small, the way "bird" is too small for an albatross that has not touched ground in seven years. Call them, for a working word, the **Hearers**, because what they do, primarily, is listen.

## Body

Imagine a kite the size of a small house, but living. A central spar of cartilage — light, hollow, resonant — running fore to aft, perhaps four meters in a mature adult. From this spar extend the **vanes**: two great forward sails of membranous tissue stretched on a fan of ribs, and behind them a smaller pair, and behind those a rudder-vane that is also a sense-organ. The membranes are translucent and faintly iridescent, the color of oil on water, shading toward amber where the ribs run thick. In strong sun-glare you can see the dark thread of the gut running down the spar and the slow pulse of the **air-heart** — they have no blood-heart, having almost no blood; they have an air-heart that pumps gas through the body's tissues directly, a thin-air adaptation, and you can watch it work through the skin.

They weigh, for their size, almost nothing. A thirty-meter elder masses perhaps as much as a large dog. They are built the way the pith is built: hollow, ribbed, sealed. Every internal volume is a pressure-organ. To kill one is to puncture it, and a punctured Hearer falls, deflating, with a long descending whistle that the other Hearers feel in their own membranes and answer.

They have no legs. They have no need. They were born aloft — more on that — and they will die aloft, or in the mouth of a bladder, which is the same thing.

## Feeding

They eat **spore-dust** and **each other**, in that order of frequency and reverse order of preference.

The spore-dust is constant, the gift of the pith, fine as flour and rich in lipids. The Hearers filter it through baleen-like combs that line the leading edges of their forward vanes. A day's flight through a good thermal column packs the combs with a pale paste which the Hearer rolls back toward its mouth — a slit on the underside of the spar, just behind the forward vanes — with slow peristaltic ripples of the vane-tissue. It is a meditative kind of feeding. You can watch it for an hour and see only the slightest motions.

The other kind of feeding is faster. A Hearer that has gone too long without dust, or a young Hearer testing its strength, will fold its vanes and **drop** — a stoop from a kilometer up, the membranes streamlined into a spindle, the whole creature falling at terminal velocity through the thin air, which here means perhaps three hundred kilometers an hour. They strike slug-things on the pith surface, or drillers in mid-ejection, or each other in mid-air. The strike is silent — everything is silent against the chord — but the consequence is a tumble of vanes and a slow spiral down into a bladder-mouth, the killer and the killed often falling together, because at those speeds the impact is rarely clean.

Cannibalism is not taboo among them. There is no taboo. There is only the chord and the dust and the thermal and the strike.

## Reproduction, or what passes for it

They do not mate. They **collide**.

When two adult Hearers have flown the same thermal column for some weeks — and this is something they do, returning to favored vents the way swallows return to eaves, except that the vents are alive and may close — they begin to drift their flight paths into resonance. The pressure-waves of one's vanes begin to cup the other's. They circle closer. Their membranes begin to hum at matched frequencies. This courtship, if it is courtship, can last days. The thermal column lifts them both. The chord pours through them both. And at some moment — no one watching has identified the trigger — they fold their vanes and strike one another, breast to breast, at the top of the column.

The impact ruptures a sealed sac in each of their ventral cavities. The contents mix in mid-air, a brief glittering cloud of paired gametes and a viscous foam, which the dying parents — for they are dying, both of them, the strike is fatal — try with their last vane-twitches to cup and hold. They tumble downward together, locked, the foam hardening in the cold thin air into a kind of pumice-light pod, and they fall into the mouth of a bladder, or onto the pith, or, very rarely, into a fissure deep enough that the pod is not crushed.

In the fissure, in the warm exhaled air of the pith's slow breathing, the pod incubates. The parents' bodies, draped over it, decay slowly into nutrient and shield. After some months — no one is sure; no one has watched a full cycle — the pod splits and releases between two and forty **larval Hearers**, each the size of a thumb, each already equipped with tiny vanes, each already able to ride a thermal.

They climb. They have to. The fissure is warm but the pith does not tolerate them long — the slug-things would graze them, the drillers would mistake them for soft tissue. The larvae catch the first updraft and rise, and rise, and most of them die in the rising. Perhaps one in a hundred reaches a thermal column strong enough to feed it. The rest fall back into the pith and are folded in.

## The listening

This is what makes them strange, even here.

The rear vane — the rudder — is not really a rudder. It is an **ear**. A vast tympanum of fine membrane stretched across a frame of resonant cartilage, oriented always to catch the chord from behind as the Hearer flies forward into the wind of its own making. Through this organ they perceive the chord not as sound but as **landscape**. The bellows-cascades are storm-fronts to them. The standing waves between vents are valleys and ridges. They know where the next exhalation will come from before it comes, because the pith's tracheal columns make tiny precursor-tones, sub-audible to a human, that the Hearer's tympanum reads as clearly as you read the page in front of you.

They navigate the chord. They live in it the way a fish lives in a current. They cannot, in any meaningful sense, *not* listen; their flight is their listening, and their listening is their flight.

And — here is the strangeness, the soft strangeness, the thing that no one quite wants to say plainly — they appear to **answer**.

The membranes of a Hearer can be tensioned, by muscles along the ribs, to emit tones. Not loud ones. Against the chord they are nothing, less than nothing, a whisper into a foghorn. But the tones are not random. A Hearer flying alone will tension its vanes and hum, in long descending phrases, at frequencies that match the precursor-tones of the next bellows-cascade. It is as if they are calling the cascade. The cascade comes anyway — it would have come — but they call it. They have always called it.

A Hearer flying in company will sometimes match its hum to another's, and the two will fly side by side for an hour, humming the same phrase, then diverge. No mating follows. No feeding. The behavior has no function that anyone has identified.

Some observers — and there have been very few observers, the Aspirate is not kind to observers — have suggested that the Hearers are not answering the chord but **participating** in it. That over a hundred million years, the pith and the Hearers have converged into a single instrument, the pith providing the great organ-tones and the Hearers providing the grace-notes, the ornaments, the small filigree above the cathedral chords. That a chord without Hearers would be a chord missing something — not its substance, but its **attention**. The pith generates the music. The Hearers hear it. And in hearing it, complete it.

This is unprovable. It is also hard to dismiss when you have lain in a fissure during a cascade and watched, through the shaking air, a Hearer ride the front of the pressure-wave with its tympanum spread wide and its forward vanes humming a thin clear note that should have been swallowed by the cascade and was not — was, somehow, audible *through* the cascade, the way a single voice can be heard through a choir not because it is louder but because the choir has made room for it.

The pith makes room for the Hearers. The Hearers make room for nothing, because they are already inside everything.

## Old ones

The very old Hearers — the thirty-meter elders, the ones who have somehow not been struck, not collided, not fallen — go strange.

They stop feeding on each other. They stop visiting the high thermals. They drift, instead, between vents, riding the low slow currents, their vanes tattered at the edges, their iridescence dulled to a smoky gray. They hum constantly. Their phrases get longer. A young Hearer's hum is a phrase of seconds; an elder's hum can run for an hour, modulating slowly, threading through the chord like a long single line of melody through a symphony.

When an elder finally dies — and they do die, eventually, of nothing in particular, of having been alive long enough — it does not fall. It folds its vanes deliberately, orients itself above a bladder-mouth, and **descends**. A controlled descent, the last act of navigation. It lowers itself into the bladder the way a hand is lowered into a sleeve. The bladder closes over it. The chord shifts, very slightly, in that sector, for perhaps a day. Then it returns to what it was.

There is no ceremony. The other Hearers do not gather. But for that day, the phrases of every Hearer within fifty kilometers grow longer, and slower, and quieter, as if each of them is, briefly, an elder.

Then the dust comes back, and the strikes resume, and the chord rolls on.

---

They are not the only fauna. The slugs graze; the drillers bore; there are small things in the deep fissures that no one has properly seen. But the Hearers are the **answer** the Aspirate gave to its own question, the question of what it might be like to be heard by something that lived inside one's own breathing.

They are motes of dust in the pith's lungs, yes — but motes that learned to listen, and then, very softly, to sing back. Not enough to be heard over the chord. Just enough that the chord, if it noticed, would notice it was not alone.

Whether it has noticed, in a hundred million years, is not a question the Hearers ask. They are too busy flying. They are too busy listening. They are, in the end, the listening itself, given vanes and sent aloft, and the Aspirate breathes them out and breathes them in and breathes on.
