## What Lives in the Long Hollow

The birds you've already met. They are the loudest fact and the most obvious, and a person walking through would carry away the memory of them and think they had seen the inhabitants. They would be wrong, or at least incomplete. The birds are weather. They are the equivalent, in this weatherless place, of wind and rain — a constant churning presence, born and dying in their thousands, that the real inhabitants live within and depend on but are not.

The real inhabitants are harder to notice.

Start with the **pale walker**. There is one, possibly two; the evidence is ambiguous and the hollow is long. It is descended from something that came down through a fissure a very long time ago — not a person, though it has hands of a sort — and it has been shaped by the hollow into the hollow's own logic. It is the color of the bleached stone under the light-shafts, which is to say not white but a kind of grey-cream that takes on whatever dimness surrounds it. It is tall when it stands and not tall when it doesn't; its proportions are a little wrong for standing, a little wrong for going on all fours, and it does both as the floor demands. The slabs do not trouble it. It has learned the floor the way a tongue learns a mouth.

It eats what the hollow offers: eggs from the lower ledges (it cannot climb high, and does not try; the upper ledges belong to the birds entirely and this is part of the agreement), beetles by the handful, the eyeless fish when it can catch them, which is often, because it has all the time there is. It drinks from the pools. It sleeps where it sleeps. It does not have a den because nothing accumulates here and a den would accumulate, so it sleeps in different places, and the places are forgotten by the hollow as soon as it leaves them, the way the floor forgets the slabs.

It does not call. This is important. In a place this loud, silence is the only signature that carries, and the pale walker is silent. It listens. It has been listening for a very long time. It knows the swiftlets' click-language well enough to know when a swiftlet has seen something unusual, and it knows the larger birds' alarms, and it knows the particular quiet that falls in a pocket of the hollow when a slab is about to come down. It moves when the hollow moves and is still when the hollow is still. If you walked past it standing against a wall in the dim between two light-shafts, you would not see it. You would feel watched and you would attribute the feeling to the birds, because everything here is attributed to the birds.

It is not lonely, because loneliness requires a sense of time passing differently than it should, and the hollow does not permit that sense. It is not bored. It is not waiting. It is simply the thing that lives here at the pace the place lives.

Then there are the **eaters of the drifts**, and these are a kind of life I want to describe carefully because they are easy to miss in the catalogue of beetles and centipedes. Among the cockroaches and the blind centipedes there is a thing — call it a worm, though it has legs in the way a centipede has legs, too many to count and all small — that grows quite large in the deeper drifts. As long as a forearm. Pale, segmented, slow. It eats guano and it eats the pale fungus-flora and it eats, when it finds them, the smaller things that eat the same. It has no eyes. It has a mouth that is not so much a mouth as a sorting apparatus. It lives in the drifts the way a fish lives in water, moving through them without ever fully emerging, and a person walking across the floor would step over a dozen of them without noticing the drifts had stirred.

These are not a horror. They are a fact. They are what happens when something has had centuries to specialize in eating bird-rain in the dark, and they are part of why nothing accumulates: the drifts themselves are alive, are constantly being processed, are a slow river of digestion that turns the upper world's outflow back into the hollow's own substance.

The pale walker eats them sometimes, when it cannot find better. They are not its preference.

In the pools, beyond the eyeless fish, there is a thing I am less sure about. The fish are well-known to themselves: small, white, patient, breeding in cycles that match nothing above. But in the larger pools, the deeper ones, something moves that is larger than a fish and is not the pale walker, because the pale walker does not enter the water past its knees. I cannot say what it is. The hollow's pools connect, probably, to a water system beneath the hollow — to the real cave system, the older one, of which the hollow is only the freshest and shallowest accident. Whatever lives in the pools may live mostly elsewhere and only sometimes surface here. It may be a salamander grown long in the dark. It may be many of something rather than one of something. The pale walker knows about it and does not approach the deep pools at night, if night means anything here, which it doesn't quite.

And then — and this is the part I have been circling — there is the matter of **the ones who left the tools**.

I said the pale walker is descended from something that came down through a fissure a long time ago. I want to revise that. The pale walker is descended from the people who left the tools. Not directly, not in a straight line, but in the slow drift of a population that came down for the eggs and the fish and found that the hollow was easier than the surface, and stayed, and stayed longer, and stopped going up, and over a great many generations became less suited to going up and more suited to staying. The flaked blades on the slab were left by an ancestor. The char of the fire is older than it looks and also younger than it looks, because in a place without seasons charcoal does not date itself.

The pale walker has hands that remember the tools. It does not use them. It has not used tools for a long time — its hands have become other things, broader, with claws that suit the beetle-drifts and the egg-ledges — but when it passes the slab where the blades lie it pauses, sometimes, and looks at them, and moves on. Whether this is memory or instinct or something the hollow has done to memory and instinct until they are the same thing, I cannot say.

There may be more than one. I said this earlier and want to return to it. The hollow is long. A population of one is not a population, and the pale walker, whatever it is now, came from a population. If there are two, they meet rarely, because the hollow is long and they are quiet, and meeting is not part of how they live. If there are more, they are spread thin enough that the hollow can support them, because the hollow's whole logic is *a little, and no more*. If there is only one left, then the lineage ends with it, and the hollow will continue without noticing, the way the floor continues without noticing a slab.

I think there are a few. I think they pass each other in the dim and acknowledge each other in some way that is not call and not gesture but a kind of mutual stillness, a recognition that does not need to become anything. I think they breed, when they breed, in a manner I will not try to describe, because the hollow's reticence about accumulation extends to its inhabitants and I do not want to invent ceremonies for creatures who have given up ceremony.

What lives here, then:

The birds, who are weather.
The beetles and centipedes and the long pale eaters of the drifts, who are soil.
The eyeless fish, who are the pools made animate.
The thing in the deep water, who may be a visitor from a larger dark.
And the pale walkers, who are what people become when they stop being people and become a place instead.

They are all, in their way, the same inhabitant. The hollow has only one resident, really, and that resident is the hollow's own metabolism, distributed across wings and shells and pale skin and slow water. The pale walker is the part of that metabolism that watches. The birds are the part that speaks. The drifts are the part that digests. The pools are the part that remembers, dimly, that there was once an outside.

Nothing here is waiting for anything. That is the thing a visitor would get most wrong. A visitor would see the abandoned tools and the silent walker and the steady drip and assume a held breath, a pause, an expectation. There is none. The hollow is not paused. It is at its full speed, which is very slow, and it is doing exactly what it does, which is continue. The inhabitants are not waiting to be discovered or rescued or completed. They are the place, and the place is them, and if you walked the whole length of the hollow and out the far end — if there is a far end — you would carry away a memory of birds and stone and would not know you had passed, in the dim between two shafts of light, the quiet pale watcher who counted your footsteps and forgot them as soon as you were gone.
