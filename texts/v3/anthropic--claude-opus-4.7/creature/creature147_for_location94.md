## The Listeners in the Muffle-Places

Start with what the description almost tells you and then pulls back from.

There are regions where the roar is slightly muffled, and the animals gather there in larger numbers, as if resting. Begin there. Not with the ribbon-things or the brittle-star-analogues — they are tenants, they are passing through, they are the fauna of the place. The inhabitants, the ones who *live* here in the fuller sense, are in those muffle-places, and they are the reason the muffle-places are muffle-places.

They are large. That needs to be said first, because everything else follows from it. A full-grown one massed against the basalt is perhaps twelve meters along its longest axis and shaped something like a great flattened kidney, or a lung pressed flat — a broad dorsal surface of soft, pale, slightly translucent tissue draped over a frame of cartilage-rods that fan out from a central ridge. They lie belly-down on the stone. They almost never leave it. When they move, which is seldom, it is by a slow peristaltic crawl along the rock, taking hours to cross a body-length, leaving behind a faint chemical trace that other members of their kind can read by touch days later.

They have no eyes. They have, instead, the dorsal surface — and the dorsal surface is an ear. Not in the vertebrate sense. The whole sheet of tissue is a tuned membrane, and beneath it, woven into the cartilage frame, are somewhere between forty thousand and a hundred thousand discrete sensory organs, each one resonant at a slightly different frequency, each one reporting independently into a diffuse nervous system that has no center and no brain in any location you could point to. When one of them lies on the plateau, it is hearing the roar at a resolution that nothing else in the sea can approach. Every harmonic. Every shear-tone. Every minute flutter in the two-beat pulse. And, crucially, every distortion the roar suffers as it passes around the body of a passing fish, or a cluster of walking brittle-stars, or a visitor who should not be here.

They are, to a first approximation, the roar's listeners. They exist because the roar exists. Without the roar they would be deaf and inert; with it, they are awash in a flood of structured information at a volume that would kill anything else. They have evolved, if evolved is the word, to take the overwhelming noise-field and read the world off its surface the way a person reads a lake's surface by watching the wind on it.

And here is the thing about the muffle-places. The muffle-places are muffled because the listeners are absorbing sound. Their dorsal tissue is not just resonant; it is lossy. Every frequency they attend to, they attenuate, faintly, locally. A single listener makes almost no difference. But they gather — not in herds, they are too slow for herds, but in slow accreted colonies, dozens or hundreds of them pressed onto the same broad plateau over centuries, and a colony of that size lowers the ambient roar in its immediate vicinity by a measurable amount. The small fauna notice this. The small fauna come and rest in the quieter water above the colony, and the listeners read them passing overhead, and occasionally — rarely, perhaps once in a listener's year, perhaps once in a decade — a listener will extend a slow pale ribbon of tissue upward from its dorsal surface and take one.

They do not need to eat often. Their metabolism is glacial. The cold suits them. What sustains them mostly is not the small fauna at all but something in the water itself, some dissolved chemistry that the engines, perhaps, are responsible for — they filter it through the underside of the body, across a broad foot-membrane in contact with the stone. They are, in effect, grazing on the sea the engines have made. The small prey is supplement, or ritual, or accident.

Now the harder part.

They are listening to something specific. Not the roar as a whole — they have the roar the way you have air, they do not attend to it as such. What they attend to is the *fine structure* of the roar, the places where the great sound deviates by fractions of a decibel from what it was a moment ago. The engines, for all their constancy, are not perfectly constant. There are microfluctuations. There are, if you listen at the resolution a listener listens at, passages and phrases and long, long sentences being spoken in the minute unevennesses of the grinding. The engines are not silent about what they are doing. The engines are, in fact, speaking continuously, at a bitrate so low and across a carrier so loud that nothing without a listener's dorsal surface could ever detect the signal.

The listeners hear it. Whether they understand it is a different question. They hear it the way a dog hears human speech: attentive, responsive, receiving something, not necessarily parsing. Or perhaps they parse it perfectly and have simply, across whatever long time they have been here, accepted what is being said and organized their entire existence around continuing to receive it. It is hard to tell from outside. You would have to be one of them to know.

What can be said is this: when the pulse, very occasionally, shifts — when the two-beats-per-second skips, or doubles, or holds for a fraction too long — every listener in a colony responds. Not dramatically. A slow flexion of the dorsal frame, a fractional reorientation on the stone, a release of some chemical signal that travels across the colony and out to neighboring colonies over the following weeks. Information propagates through the listener population at the speed of a slow crawl and a drifting scent, and it propagates in response to changes that no other organism in the sea can perceive.

They are, in other words, a distributed auditor. The engines run and speak, and the listeners lie on the black plateaus and hear, and when the engines' speech changes the listeners' arrangement changes, slowly, across continents of basalt, over centuries. Whether anything receives the listeners' response — whether the engines care that they are heard, whether there is a circuit closing somewhere between the buried machinery and the pale flat bodies on the stone — is not visible from two hundred meters down. The cylinders vent nothing. The pipes lead into rock.

But consider: the listeners are well-suited to this place in a way that suggests long accommodation. The water's chemistry feeds them. The cold matches their metabolism. The roar is their sensorium. Nothing else here is so perfectly fitted. The small fauna are tenants, scraping a living off the edges; the listeners are *of* the place, the way a certain kind of lichen is of a certain kind of stone. If the description is right that nothing grows here, the listeners are the closest thing to a plant this sea has — sessile, slow, oriented to a great constant source, converting it into being.

And if the engines are speaking, and the listeners are hearing, then the place is not as empty of communication as it first appears. It is, in fact, absolutely full of it. Every cubic meter of this cold water carries the engines' continuous utterance. The listeners lie in it the way one might lie in warm sun. When one of them dies — they do die, rarely, and the body is consumed over years by the brittle-stars and the ribbon-things, which gather to the dead in numbers they never gather for anything else — the cartilage frame remains on the stone for a long time afterward, a pale fanned shape embedded in the basalt, still faintly resonant, still humming in sympathy with the roar. New listeners, when they settle (and no one has seen them settle; the juvenile stage is not known; perhaps they drift as larvae for decades before finding stone), seem to prefer the sites of old frames. The colonies are built on their own dead.

So: that is who lives here. Great pale listeners on the black plateaus, sessile and slow and enormously attentive, spread in sparse colonies across a continental range, hearing the buried engines speak at a resolution nothing else could manage, responding in chemistry and posture over timescales that dwarf any animal life you would recognize, sustained by the very chemistry the engines throw off, absorbing the sound that made them and lowering it fractionally in their immediate neighborhoods, so that the small blind fauna can rest a little easier in the shadow of their listening.

They have arrived at what they are. They intend to remain. The engines speak, and they hear, and this is the arrangement, and it is very old, and it is the shape of the place.
