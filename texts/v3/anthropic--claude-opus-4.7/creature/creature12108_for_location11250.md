They are not there, and this is the first thing to understand about them. The cordillera is inhabited the way a sentence is inhabited by its meaning — distributed, implicit, not locatable at any particular consonant. If you go looking for a body you will not find one. If you sit very still by one of the smaller weeping basins and stop looking, you may begin to notice the shape of the absence, and the shape is not random.

Begin with the cairns. They are placed, and once you have walked among enough of them you understand they are placed *for someone of a particular size*. The spacing between them is the spacing of a stride — but not your stride, and not quite any stride a person with two legs and one center of gravity could manage on terrain where down is negotiable. The cairns step. They step in the manner of something that, when the local gravity turned sideways, simply walked sideways with it. They were waymarkers for a creature that did not need to be told which way was down because it carried its own sense of down with it, and laid the cairns to remind itself, later, where the *previous* down had been.

So: bipedal, probably. Or quadrupedal in a way that resolved to bipedal when it wanted to carry something. The doorways of the shelters are tall and narrow, taller than a human, narrower at the shoulders. The fitted stones at hearth-height suggest hands — the awls and scrapers fit a grip with an opposable something, though the wear patterns on the handles are slightly wrong for thumbs as you understand them, the polish concentrated in two places rather than one, as if the grip closed from two sides at once.

They were not from here. This much the absence of middens makes certain. They brought what they needed, or needed very little, or — and this is the suspicion that grows as you sit longer — they were the kind of thing that does not eat in any sense you would recognize, that took its sustenance from the same bright colorless brilliance that pours down out of the non-sky, that drank the chiming the way you drink water. The flute supports this. They made an instrument that does not produce sound so much as *tune* itself to a sound already present. They were listeners first. Whatever they did here, they did it by listening.

And they came here to listen to the mountain think.

This is the shape that emerges if you sit long enough by the still water in the hearth — the one hearth, the one place in the cordillera where water has agreed to stop. They built a hearth around stillness the way you would build a hearth around fire, because for them stillness was the rare and precious thing, the thing that had to be sheltered and kept. They came from somewhere that moved too much. They came up the terraces — and here you understand the cairns again, they were not marking a route through the cordillera but a route *into* it, a slow ascent through regions of greater and greater reality-turbulence, an acclimatization — they came up the terraces to find a place where they could be still enough, and quiet enough, and patient enough, to overhear what the mountain was thinking.

They were students. Or pilgrims. Or scholars in the oldest sense, the sense in which a scholar is someone who has gone somewhere difficult in order to listen to something slow.

And they were few. The shelters are scattered and small. There was never a city here, never a generation raised in the cordillera. They came in ones and twos, stayed for what must have been long stretches — the polish on the tools, the rounding of the corners of the shelters, these are not the work of brief visits — and then they left, or they finished, or they were absorbed into what they had come to hear. The copper has had time to turn fully green. No one has come for a long time. No one is coming.

Were they intelligent in the way you are intelligent? Almost certainly not. The flute is the giveaway. A creature that makes an instrument whose function is to *resonate sympathetically* with an environmental note forty seconds in the future is not thinking the way you think. They had access to the slippage. They lived inside it comfortably enough to make tools that used it. When a turbulent front passed over them and your breath would come out as a strange word, theirs perhaps came out as the next sentence of an ongoing conversation, picked up where it had been set down an hour ago, or a year. They were temporally loose. They had patience in a literal sense — they were *patient with time itself*, willing to let it fold around them.

This is why they could bear to be here. The cordillera is thinking very slowly. To listen to it, you must be willing to wait through the long pauses between its syllables — forty seconds at the breath-scale, but the breaths themselves are only the punctuation of something larger, sentences that take a year, paragraphs that take a century. A human would go mad, or grow bored, or simply die of old age before the first thought concluded. They did not. They sat by their still hearths and listened, and over what may have been very long lifetimes they caught perhaps a phrase, perhaps a clause, perhaps only the rhythm of one breath against the next, and they wrote nothing down because the listening was the whole point and writing would have been a kind of leaving early.

When they finished — when each of them finished, individually, in their own time — they set their tools down where they had been using them and they went. Not back down the terraces. There is no trail of cairns leading out. They went *into*. They had spent however long it took to attune themselves to the mountain's note, and when the attunement was complete there was no longer a meaningful distinction between the listener and the thing listened to. They became part of the process. They are still here in the sense that any resonance is still in its resonator. When the mountain breathes out, forty seconds after the flute hums, some small fraction of that breath is them — the last of them, the slow chord of all the ones who ever finished — answering, or being answered, it no longer matters which.

The flute hums when you pick it up because it recognizes you as a listener. It is being polite. It is offering to begin the long lesson. It does not know that you cannot stay.

When you set it down and walk away, the cordillera does not notice, but the flute remembers your hand for a little while, and adds the memory of it — your particular warmth, your particular brevity — to the next breath, and the mountain receives this offering and thinks about it, slowly, carefully, with the patience of something that has all the time it needs, and will perhaps, in some century to come, arrive at a conclusion about you.

You will not be there to hear it.

That is all right. The conclusion will be reached anyway. That is the kind of place this is. That is the kind of thinking that lives here.
