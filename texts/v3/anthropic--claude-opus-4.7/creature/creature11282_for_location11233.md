# The People of the Stub

They are small. Not children-small — the smallness of a people who have lived for a very long time in a place where weight is expensive and every pound of a body is a pound that someone, somewhere back along the line, carried up the stair or grew from rationed water. The tallest man in the town would come up to the shoulder of a lowland trader. Their bones are fine and their hands are disproportionate — long-fingered, broad-palmed, the hands of knappers and weavers and potters, hands that have done the same six or seven motions for a hundred generations and become beautifully fitted to them.

Their skin is the color of the rock in shadow: a deep warm brown that holds an undertone of the apricot cliffs, as if they had been fired from the same clay. In the dim lanes they read almost as part of the architecture; on the rim-walks, in full sun, they flare suddenly into definition, and you see how their faces are made — broad at the cheek, narrow at the jaw, with the wide-set eyes of a people who have looked at distance their whole lives. The eyes are pale. Almost everyone's eyes are some shade of weak gold or grey-green, the iris startling against the dark skin. Children in particular have eyes that seem lit from inside. It is the one feature outsiders always remark on, and the townspeople are bored of hearing about it.

Their hair grows in tight dark coils which the women bind up in elaborate towers fixed with bone pins, and the men crop close or shave entirely, because hair holds dust and dust here is the slow enemy of everything. Both sexes wear a single long garment of woven shrub-fiber, undyed, the color of unbleached linen but stiffer, smelling faintly of resin. It is belted with braided cord from which hang the tools of a particular life: a knapper's small hammer, a weaver's shuttle, a child-minder's whistle, a rim-tender's hook. You can read a person's whole occupation from their belt before they have spoken.

They speak a language that is mostly soft — the consonants worn down, perhaps, by all those generations of breathing the dry air and not wanting to cough. It is a language full of long vowels and small clicks, and it carries oddly on the summit, sounding closer than it is. They sing more than they speak, in the sense that ordinary statements have a melodic shape that lowlanders mistake for song. Real songs are different: longer, with a drone underneath, and almost always sung at the edges, at dawn or dusk, when the long-winged birds are passing.

They are not a quiet people in temperament. They argue constantly, in the lanes, in the doorways, over the placement of a stone in a wall, over the rights to a particular fog-basin in a particular month, over whether a child belongs to one shrine-family or another. The arguments are loud and intricate and almost never end in anything changing, because nothing here changes quickly, and they know this, and arguing is partly how they pass the time and partly how they remember who they are in relation to one another. A serious dispute can run for three generations without resolution and without any apparent harm to either family; the dispute becomes a kind of shared inheritance, tended like a shrine.

They are not warlike. They have no enemies to be warlike against — the stair is too long for an army, and there is nothing on the summit worth taking that could survive the descent. What they have instead is a dense and patient web of obligation. Every person owes water-debt to someone: to the climber who brought up the jar, to the family whose tree filled the basin, to the neighbor who minded the children while the mother went to the rim to gather fog-comb. The debts are tracked in a kind of knotted cord that hangs inside every doorway, and the cords are read aloud at certain festivals, and adjustments are made. No one is ever entirely out of debt and no one is ever entirely in it. To be in perfect balance would be lonely; it would mean you needed no one and no one needed you.

They are devout but not in any way a lowlander would recognize. There are no gods on the summit, or none that the people will name. The four shrines at the cardinal edges are tended, but what is tended there is not a deity — it is, depending on which family you ask, the memory of a direction, or the wind that comes from that direction, or the first person who stood at that edge, or simply the edge itself. The shrine-keepers leave small offerings: a chip of knapped stone, a coil of new pot, a feather. They do not pray. They sit, sometimes for a whole day, at the rim, and look outward, and this sitting is itself the practice. A child is brought to each of the four edges in its first year and shown the view from each, and this is its naming. Adults return to their birth-edge in old age, and many die there, sitting, watching the curve of the world below.

They believe — or it is something deeper than belief, it is simply how they live — that they are themselves a slow rule. Like the trees that grow a finger's width in a generation, like the rock that powders imperceptibly under the hand, the people of the stub understand themselves as a process happening at the mountain's pace, not the body's pace. A life is a small motion within a much longer motion. A house repaired forever is more truly a house than a new house. A dispute tended for three generations is more truly settled than a dispute resolved in an afternoon. They are patient in a way that visitors find first charming, then unsettling, then — if the visitor stays long enough — contagious. People who climb the stair to trade for a season often find themselves staying two, three, ten years, and when they finally descend they are slower, and the lowland feels to them like a place of unseemly hurry, and they do not entirely belong to either place again.

There is a particular thing they do, which is hard to describe. When a person dies, the body is carried to the rim — to whichever of the four edges the person was named at — and is not buried (there is no soil deep enough) and is not burned (wood is too precious) but is left, wrapped in the fiber garment, on a particular shelf below the rim that only the rim-tenders can reach. The long-winged birds come. Within a day or two there is nothing left but the bones, and the bones are gathered and ground and mixed into the clay of the next generation of pots. Every household drinks from cups that contain, in some literal and unremarked way, its ancestors. The pots are apricot-colored, like the cliffs, like the skin of the people, and a cup passed to a tired climber at the top of the stair is — though the climber does not know this — a cup made partly of the people who built the town that built the stair that the climber has just ascended.

They know they will not last forever. The mountain is in the patient business of becoming dust, and they understand themselves to be in the same business, at the same pace. There are fewer of them now than there were ten generations ago — not many fewer, but fewer — and this is acknowledged without grief. The town will shrink as the summit shrinks. One day, very far from now, there will be a last family at a last edge, tending a last shrine, and then there will be wind only, and the birds, and then eventually not even the birds. They speak of this sometimes at the rim, at dusk, in the long vowels of their language, and they do not seem to find it sad. It is simply the shape of the thing they are inside of. It is the slow rule.

A visitor, given water in a clay cup and seated in the dim warm dappled lane, will be watched. Not coldly — the watching is friendly, curious, amused. But the watching is also a kind of measurement. The townspeople are deciding, without hurry, whether this person moves at the pace of the mountain or at the pace of the lowland. Most visitors move at the lowland pace and are treated kindly and sent back down with full water-jars and good wishes. A few, every generation or two, are found to move at the other pace, and these are quietly given a doorway, a tree, a stretch of rim. No ceremony marks it. They are simply, one morning, of the town. Their names change slightly, taking on the soft consonants. Their hands, over years, become the long-fingered hands. Their children's eyes are pale.
