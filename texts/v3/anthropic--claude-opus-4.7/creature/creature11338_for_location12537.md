## The Inhabitants

They are called, by themselves, nothing. Naming is a thing done at a distance, and there is no distance here. Among them, identity is positional — *the one in the cup three down from the seep*, *the one who broods beside the boomer's wall*. When that position changes, so does the one occupying it. A self is a coordinate in the weave.

But describe them you can.

### The Builders

Begin with the great long-necks on the upper rims, because they are what a visitor sees first. They stand perhaps as tall as a man when their necks are extended, but the necks are usually folded back along the spine in a loose S, so they appear hunched, almost contemplative. Their plumage is the dark of a wet slate roof, with a faint oil-sheen of bottle green where the light catches. The feathers of the throat are longer, finer, and they vibrate visibly when the bird produces its call — a low double note, the second lower than the first, that carries down through the terraces and is answered, eventually, from somewhere deep. They are the watchers. They take the wind first. When they lift from the rim in unison, which happens perhaps four or five times a day for no reason the lower tiers can discern, every other species in the rookery briefly quiets, listens to the air, and then resumes.

They mate for a long time but not for life. A pair holds a rim-cup for perhaps fifteen seasons, raises perhaps three broods, and then separates without ceremony; the cup is taken by another pair, and the old pair drifts down into the middle tiers to do other work. There is a sense in which every long-neck above was once a long-neck below, and every long-neck below will, if it lives, climb. The climb takes a lifetime. The descent, when it comes, takes a season.

### The Middle Cloud

The fork-tailed swarmers of the middle tiers are smaller — hand-sized, sharp, the color of cinnamon shading into rust at the wingtips. They are the blood of the place. They do not build, exactly; they *repair*. A loose twig is taken in the beak and pushed back. A drooping vine is bitten through. A widening seam is packed with a paste they produce from a gland beneath the tongue, a paste that smells faintly of resin and dries clear. They work constantly, in flocks of thirty or forty, moving as a single distributed attention across whatever surface they have chosen for the hour. They do not seem to coordinate. They simply all happen to be doing maintenance in the same place, and then all happen to move on.

They are also the only species that seems to recognize the others as individuals. A fork-tail will land on the shoulder of a long-neck and pick mites from the base of its throat-feathers, and the long-neck will incline its head, and there is a recognition there — not affection, perhaps, but acknowledgment of a long working relationship. The fork-tails sleep in clusters in shallow cups, fifteen or twenty packed together breast-to-back, breathing in slow synchrony. In the morning the cluster unspools and dissolves into the day's work.

### The Brooders

In the lower chambers, in the warm dim where the air is heaviest, are the broad-winged ones. These are different. These are older, in some sense that has nothing to do with the age of individuals. Their wings, when extended, span twice the length of a tall man's reach, and the wing-membrane is not feathered but covered in a fine plush down that absorbs sound — when one moves, it moves silently, an absence passing through the murk. Their eyes are enormous, dark, and reflect lantern-light (if you brought one, which you did not) like the eyes of deep cave fish.

They brood the great eggs. Each brooder sits on a single egg for what may be a year or longer. They eat little during this time; mates and siblings bring them food, which is to say, regurgitate slow streams of soft matter into their open beaks. They do not move. They breathe shallowly. They produce the deep booming you feel in your sternum — it comes from a resonating chamber in the chest, and it is, the rookery seems to understand, the foundation of everything. When the boomers go quiet, the great rhythm of the place falters; the fork-tails grow nervous; the long-necks lift more often. When the boomers resume, the whole mountain seems to settle back into itself.

What hatches from those eggs is, eventually, another brooder. But not directly. What hatches is a pale, soft, scrambling thing —

### The Pale Climbers

— which is what scuttles along the undersides, upside down, threading the vines. They are not a different species. They are the brooders before the brooders are themselves. They live for years like this, perhaps a decade, perhaps longer, in a featherless larval state, climbing and chewing and being fed by anyone who happens to have surplus. They are tolerated everywhere. They are the rookery's children, communally held. They have small flat faces with the large dark eyes already present, and their grip is astonishing — they can hang one-handed from a vine for hours, watching, learning, eating insects that pass.

When they are ready — and no one knows what readies them — they descend into the deep chambers and begin to grow their down. This takes another year. They emerge as brooders, and they will brood, and they will boom, and they will not climb again, and they will rarely fly. Some never fly at all. The great wings are for stillness; the wings hold the warmth in around the egg. The wings are blankets, mostly.

### The Smaller Lives

There are others. There are the iridescent darters in the seeps, small and quick and almost invertebrate in their movement, who eat the algae and are eaten by the snakes. There are the snakes themselves, six or seven kinds, all patterned to disappear into the weave — they are not predators of the rookery so much as its slow intestinal flora, processing what the rookery does not want to process itself. There are the beetles on the floors, glossy and patient, who reduce fallen feathers and fallen bodies to a fine warm soil that the ferns then drink. There are the tree-frogs, which sing at dusk in a register so high it is felt rather than heard, and which are eaten by the fork-tails, and which lay their eggs in the algal pools, and whose tadpoles are eaten by the darters, who are eaten by the snakes, who are eaten — sometimes — by the long-necks, who are not eaten by anyone, and who die quietly on the upper rims and fall, and become the rim itself.

### What They Are For

This is the difficult part to describe, because it is not a thing they would describe themselves.

The rookery is a slow conversation. The booming of the deep chambers is one voice; the calling of the long-necks on the rim is another; the chittering of the fork-tails is a third; the tiny shrill of the tree-frogs at dusk is a fourth. These voices have been answering one another for so long that they have shaped one another. The boomers boom in a rhythm that the long-necks have evolved to call across. The fork-tails chitter at frequencies that fall into the gaps the long-necks leave. The tree-frogs sing only when the boomers pause to breathe.

The whole sound, the crushing unbroken sound of the place, is a single utterance distributed across a hundred thousand throats and across centuries. It is not for anyone. It is not communication in the sense of a message sent and received. It is the rookery being the rookery — the mountain saying itself, continuously, to itself.

And the inhabitants are the throats and the wings and the eggs of that saying. They are not its authors. They are its phonemes.

### How They Die

A long-neck, late in its life, climbs back up to the rim where it began. It stands there for a day, sometimes two. Its mate, if its mate is still living, stands beside it. They do not call. At some point in the second or third night, the older bird leans forward over the rim and lets itself fall — not into the air, but into the cup itself, into the woven depth of the nest. It is found in the morning, folded into the weave, and it is not removed. The fork-tails begin to work around it. Twigs are added. Within a season, it is part of the wall.

A brooder dies on its egg, sometimes. The egg is then taken over by a sibling or a mate, and the dead brooder is left where it is, and the new brooder broods atop both egg and corpse, and the corpse becomes part of the chamber floor, and the chamber is warmer for it.

A fork-tail dies in flight and falls, and the beetles find it before evening.

A pale climber, occasionally, falls from the underside. This is the only death that produces a response that resembles grief — the other climbers gather at the spot where it fell, and they make a soft sound, almost a hum, for some hours. Then they disperse. The body is processed like any other.

Nothing leaves. The mountain grows a hand's-breadth taller each century, and the hand's-breadth is them.

### What They Would Say To You

Nothing. They would not say anything to you. You are not in their register. You are too small, too quiet, too quick, too brief. If you stay long enough — years, perhaps — a fork-tail might land on your shoulder and pick something from your hair, and that would be the closest you come to being addressed.

But if you sit very still in the warm dim, and let the sound do what it does, you will begin to feel something that is not quite being spoken to and not quite being ignored. It is the feeling of being inside a body that has not noticed you because it does not need to. Your warmth is being added to its warmth. Your breath is being added to its breath. The condensation on the underside of the nest above you contains, by now, a little of the water you exhaled an hour ago.

You are, while you are here, part of what the place is saying.

When you leave, the sentence will continue without you, and it will not have changed.
