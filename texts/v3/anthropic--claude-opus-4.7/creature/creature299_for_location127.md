The people here are difficult to see clearly at first, the way the city itself is difficult to see clearly — you have to let them resolve.

They are human, or near enough. Two arms, two legs, the ordinary architecture. But they move through their city in a way that takes a visitor some time to parse, and the parsing reveals things. They walk at a pace that seems, to outsiders, strangely deliberate — not slow exactly, but metered, as though each step were being placed rather than taken. Watch long enough and you understand: they are reading the lag. The shadows arriving seconds after their light give a person constant feedback about where they were a moment ago, and the residents have grown up inside this doubled proprioception. A child learning to walk here learns to walk with her shadow, not against it. By adulthood it is unconscious. They know, always, a little of where they have just been, and this knowing sits in them the way a sense of balance sits in us — below thought, available, structural.

Their skin tends toward a warm brown that holds the ochre light well; under the perpetual haze they look lit from within rather than from above. Hair is usually dark and worn long, often bound, because loose hair catches in the lenses — a lens passing through hair does something unpleasant that nobody quite describes, and the children are warned about it early. Eyes are a little larger than you would expect, the pupils slow to contract, adapted to the low even light. Bright sun, on the rare occasions someone travels out of the basin, is reported as a kind of assault, a thing that presses on the face.

They dress, as you noted, in indigos and umbers, but the garments themselves are worth attending to. The cloth is woven from fibers drawn off the brass-leaved trees — a long process, because the leaves must be retted in standing water and the water must be the right age, meaning it must have fallen from a lens and sat in a moss ring for a specific count of seasons. The resulting fabric is light and slightly stiff and makes a faint dry sound when it moves, an echo of the canopy's papery wind. A person walking across a plaza sounds, very quietly, like the city itself is walking.

They are not numerous. A basin fifteen kilometers across might hold forty thousand of them, scattered thin, and they like it that way. Crowds make them uneasy. They have a word — it does not translate cleanly — for the specific discomfort of being closer to another person than the local humidity allows; a lens cannot form properly in the space between two bodies too near each other, and so standing too close is felt as a kind of theft from the weather. Lovers, of course, exempt themselves. But in public, conversation happens at arm's length and a half, and a polite guest learns this quickly or is thought rude in a way nobody will explain.

Their society is organized, loosely, around the columns. Each humming column is a meeting-place, and each has a name, and the names are old and not always etymologically recoverable. *The fourth hum past the seed-market* is a real address. A person might describe where they live as *three columns in from the western ridge, by the long discharge,* and this will be understood. Mail, such as it is, travels by column — you leave a tablet or a folded cloth at the base of one, and someone going that way carries it onward, and things arrive when they arrive. Nothing is urgent. The city does not produce urgency; the tone from the towers seems, among its other effects, to damp the nervous system's readiness to hurry.

They work, but not hard. There is moss to tend in collaboration with the beetles — a person and a beetle will often work the same ring for years, and the relationship is considered a relationship, mourned when the beetle dies. There is cloth to weave. There is food, which comes from the brass trees' seed-pods (dense, oily, cooked long) and from a kind of tuber that grows only in the wet circles and is harvested sparingly. There is the keeping of records, which is taken seriously: every resident learns to write, and writes, and what they write is mostly observations of the city — which lens formed where, which column hummed longer than usual, which shadow lagged by how much on which afternoon. These records are kept in the low buildings scattered through the plazas, and nobody reads most of them, but they are kept. The keeping is the point. The city is being witnessed, continuously, by the people who live inside it, and this witnessing is understood as part of how the city continues. Whether this is true in any mechanical sense nobody knows. It is true in the sense that matters to them.

They sing, but rarely, and quietly, and always solo. Group singing is considered almost obscene — the idea of many voices producing one sound strikes them as a violation of something, perhaps of the principle by which the lenses form (each lens its own, discrete, never merging). A person walking alone across a long plaza may sing to themselves in a low voice, and a person at a distance who hears it will simply note it and continue on, and this is the correct response. To approach a singer is a serious breach.

They do not, on the whole, ask where the towers came from. The question is available to them — they are not incurious — but it has the texture of asking where one's own spine came from. The towers are the condition of being here. A few scholars in each generation take up the question and write long careful documents and reach no conclusions and are respected for the attempt. The prevailing attitude is that the towers are doing something, and the people are part of what the towers are doing, and inquiring too hard into the mechanism might be a category error — might be the eye trying to see itself.

They die, of course. They die at reasonable ages, of reasonable things, and they are buried in the moss rings — this is the other reason the rings widen, though nobody frames it that way in conversation. A body placed in a ring is gone within a season, taken apart by the beetles and the moss and the periodic rain, and what it becomes is the next year's widening. A person walking across a plaza under a forming lens is walking over their grandparents, and knows it, and this knowing is warm rather than morbid. The ochre paving and the dark circles and the concentric green — it is all one fabric, and they are woven into it whether they attend to the weaving or not.

When they leave the city, which some do, they are marked by it. The absence of the tone keeps them awake. The ordinary rain, falling everywhere at once instead of precisely, feels to them insulting, sloppy, a weather with no craft in it. Birds, as you said, are intolerable — not the birds themselves but the *quantity* of sound they produce, the generosity of it, which reads to a basin-dweller as a kind of shouting. Most travelers come back. A few don't, and those few are remembered in the records, each with a short entry: *left in the third season of such-and-such a year, did not return, was last seen going east.* The records do not speculate. They note.

What they love — and they do love, in their metered, arm's-length way — they love slowly and for a long time. A friendship begun at twelve at the fourth hum past the seed-market is still, at seventy, a friendship conducted at the fourth hum past the seed-market, the two friends meeting there on some agreed rhythm, saying little, listening to the column hum down after they touch it, watching a lens form or not form overhead. The city teaches patience by being patient with them. The afternoon goes on being afternoon. The shadows arrive a little late, as they always have, carrying news of a light that has already moved on, and the people read the news and place their next step, and the moss grows a finger's width, and someone, somewhere in the basin, is singing very quietly to themselves, and nobody is close enough to hear.
