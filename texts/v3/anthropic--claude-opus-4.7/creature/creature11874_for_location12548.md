# The People of the Spine

They are tall and thin, the way trees are thin at altitude — not frail, but stripped to what works. Long-boned. The ribcage built deep and the chest a little barreled, more lung than a lowlander carries. Their hands are large for their frames, knuckles thickened from stonework and from cold; the fingertips are often split in winter and bound with strips of greased cloth that they replace without thinking, the way a lowlander might wipe sweat from a brow. Their skin is wind-darkened on the face and hands and pale everywhere wool has covered it for a lifetime, so that a person undressing for the bath looks two-toned, a mask of weather above a body the color of the inside of a shell.

The eyes are narrow, set deep under a strong brow-ridge — an architecture for squinting against snow-glare and the hard zenith light. The irises tend toward grey or a pale tea-brown, occasionally a startling pale blue in certain lineages from the northern peaks. They do not blink as often as lowlanders. A visitor reads this as severity; it is only that their eyes are accustomed to dryness and have learned to keep their own counsel.

Their hair is dark, usually, and worn long by both sexes — braided close to the skull in many small braids that are unbraided and reworked perhaps once a week, an evening's task done by firelight while someone else reads aloud or sings under their breath. The braiding patterns mean things: which peak you were born under, which trade your house keeps, whether you are in mourning or betrothal or neither. A stranger from the next ridge can read a braid the way one reads a face.

They move slowly. This is the first thing.

Not from weakness — their endurance on the stairs is extraordinary, and they can climb for a full day with a basket of soil on their back and arrive at the top still able to speak in full sentences. But they have learned, across many generations, that haste at altitude is a kind of stupidity. Hurrying empties the lungs faster than they can fill. Hurrying makes the heart loud. Hurrying causes one to misjudge a step, and a misjudged step on these stairs can mean a fall of three stories or thirty. So they walk at a pace that looks, to a lowlander, almost ceremonial — a long even stride, the breath kept deep and regular, the eyes set a little ahead on the path. They are not slow because they are old. They are slow because they intend to become old.

Their voices, as the description said, are quiet — but it is more than thin air. Quietness is a value to them. To speak loudly is to claim more space than one needs, and space here is rationed. A child who shouts is not punished but is gently un-taught, the way one un-teaches a dog from jumping. By adolescence they have all acquired the same low, slightly hoarse register, and a habit of leaning in to listen that gives every conversation the intimacy of conspiracy. They laugh, but the laugh is brief and close-mouthed, more a shaking of the shoulders than a sound. A real loud laugh is reserved for drink or for grief.

They wear wool in layers, and the layers are not all theirs. This is harder to explain. The outermost coat, the heavy one with the high collar and the embroidered hem, is often a generation or two older than its wearer. It came from a grandmother or a great-uncle. It has been re-felted at the elbows, re-lined at the cuffs, the embroidery touched up by three or four different hands across its life. To wear it is to wear those people, and the coat is spoken of as if it had its own opinions. *This coat does not like the south wind,* one might say, meaning it pulls awkwardly across the shoulders when the wind is in that quarter, meaning also that the grandmother who first wore it was known to dislike southerly weather. The coats accumulate biography. A person's wardrobe is, in a real sense, a small archive of dead relatives, consulted daily by being put on.

Underneath the coats: tunics of finer wool, sometimes silk traded up from the lowlands and saved for ceremony, leggings of felted wool, boots of yak hide turned hair-inward and soled with a kind of resinous leather that grips stone. The boots are made by specialists who live, traditionally, three peaks south, and a person may own only two pairs in a lifetime — the first a gift at coming-of-age, the second purchased after the first has finally given out, which takes thirty or forty years.

Their families are not what a lowlander would recognize as families. The shared walls and shared flues that thread warmth through the city also thread relation. A "household" is not the people who live in one set of rooms; it is the people who share a single flue. Five or six dwellings stacked up the ridge will share a flue, and those people are *one house* for purposes of inheritance, marriage prohibition, and obligation. Children move freely between the dwellings of their flue; a child may sleep in their grandmother's room one week and their cousin's the next without anyone considering it remarkable. They have several words for *mother* and *father* depending on which flue-relation is meant — the one who bore you, the one who raised you in their kitchen, the one who taught you your trade, the one whose coat you will inherit. These can be four different people, and often are.

They marry, when they marry, across flues, and the new partner moves into a dwelling that is sometimes empty for a year or two until it is ready — meaning, until an older member of the flue has died and made room. Betrothals are therefore long. A couple may wait a decade for the right room to open, and during that decade they live in their respective flues and visit, and the waiting is not considered hardship but a normal feature of being alive. Children may be born in the waiting; the children are flue-children, raised by whoever is at hand, and the question of which dwelling is "theirs" is settled later when the household reorganizes itself around new deaths.

Death is not hidden. The dead are washed in the dwelling where they died, by their flue-kin, and then carried up — always up — to one of the high places where the lammergeiers come. The body is left on a flat stone prepared for this purpose, and what the birds do is understood as the body's continuation into the sky. The bones, after, are gathered and returned to a small ossuary at the base of each flue, so that one's literal ancestors are always a few feet below one's own floor, packed into a chamber of dressed stone with their names scratched into the wall above them. A person who is angry with a relative may go down to the ossuary and speak the complaint aloud to the relevant bones. A person who is in despair may go down and simply sit there for an afternoon. The bones are not thought to listen exactly. They are thought to be present, which is different and more useful.

Their work, when one tries to list it, is mostly maintenance.

This is the central fact about them and the one a visitor takes longest to understand. They do not, in the main, *make* things — they keep things. The stairs must be recut as they wear. The mortar must be repointed as it crumbles. The cistern troughs must be cleared of leaves and small bones each autumn. The flues must be swept. The terraced soil must be turned, fed with composted dung, and protected with stone windbreaks that themselves must be rebuilt every several winters. The looms produce cloth, the smiths produce nails and hinges and the small bronze bells that hang at every doorway, but the bulk of any given day's labor in any given dwelling is the upkeep of what already exists. A young person learning a trade is learning, mostly, how to repair the work of the dead.

This gives them a particular relationship to time. They do not imagine progress as a lowlander imagines it — as a line going forward, with new things at the front of the line and old things falling off the back. They imagine time as a kind of standing wave: the city is held in place by constant small effort against the entropy of frost and wind, and to slacken that effort would be to let the wave collapse. The dead worked to keep the city standing for the living; the living work to keep it standing for those not yet born. To ask whether the city is *advancing* is, to them, a question without meaning, like asking whether a held note is getting louder. The note is held. That is the achievement.

They do make new things, occasionally, but the threshold is high. A new stair, a new dwelling, a new song, a new method of dyeing a particular wool — these enter the inventory of the city only after long deliberation among the flues, and once they are in, they are in for centuries. There is a song sung at the spring on certain mornings that is known to have been composed eleven generations ago by a woman whose name is still spoken before it is sung. The composer of a song does not own it but is owed, in perpetuity, the acknowledgment of having put it into the world. The same applies to a new method of mortaring a corner, or a new shape of cistern-spout. Innovation here is not a private virtue but a public debt — the innovator owes the city a thing that will work for a long time, and the city owes the innovator a name remembered.

They read, most of them, though their books are few and heavy and shared across flues. The script is vertical, written top-to-bottom in columns that read right-to-left, in ink made from soot and pine-resin and a binder whose recipe is held by one family on the northern range. The books are mostly practical: registers of repairs, almanacs of weather, genealogies, the long catalogue-poems by which trades are taught. There are also lyric books — collections of short poems, four or six lines each, often about wind or stone or a particular quality of light — and these are loved disproportionately to their size. A person may know by heart three hundred such poems and quote them in conversation the way a lowlander quotes prices. A well-placed poem in a difficult conversation is considered a high form of kindness; it gives both speakers something outside themselves to attend to while the difficulty settles.

They believe — though *belief* is not quite the right word — that the mountain is alive and adolescent. The mountain is making mistakes, dropping things, shifting in its sleep. The city is the patient elder beside it. There is no priesthood exactly, but there are people called something like *attendants of the rock*, who walk specific routes each season and listen. They listen to the rock with their hands flat against it; they listen to the spring; they listen to the way the cistern-flow changes through a year. If a face of the mountain begins to feel different to several attendants over several seasons, the dwellings beneath that face are quietly emptied and the people moved to other flues, and a few years later, often, the face does come down, and the attendants are thanked, and no fuss is made. They were doing their work. The mountain was doing its.

They are not, as a people, especially warm to strangers — but neither are they cold. They are *attentive*. A visitor climbing the stairs will be watched, courteously and without comment, from many small windows. Someone will appear at the right moment with water; someone else, later, with a place to sleep. Questions will be answered, but the answers will be brief, and one is expected to sit with them. A lowlander who keeps asking more questions, faster, is understood to be ill — altitude-sick, probably, the mind racing the way it does when the blood is thin — and will be put gently to bed with a warm stone at the feet until the racing stops. After a few days the visitor either learns to slow down or leaves. Both outcomes are accepted without judgment. Some people, it is understood, are not made for the spine.

Those who stay, who marry in or apprentice in or simply find that they cannot leave, are absorbed slowly. They are given a flue to belong to, a coat from someone recently dead, a small set of repair-tasks suited to their hands. Their old name is kept but a new name is added — a name that locates them on the ridge, *of such-and-such flue, under such-and-such peak* — and within a generation their descendants are indistinguishable from the rest, tall and thin and slow-moving, braiding their hair by firelight, speaking in the low close voice the wind cannot quite carry away.

At the end of a life, a person of the spine is carried up by their flue-kin to the high stone, and the great slow shapes come down out of the noon thermals, and the bones come home to the chamber below the kitchen, and the coat is folded and given to whichever child it next fits, and someone, that evening, repoints a crumbling joint of mortar on the dwelling's eastern wall, because the wall is still standing and the wind is still working at it, and the work, like the note, must be held.

The bell rings once. The wind takes the rest. They have always understood that this is enough.
