## The Inhabitants of the Kiln-Warren

### The Ceiling-Drinkers

Call them *leaf-bats*, though they are neither. They roost in the negative sky because that is where the cold lives, and cold to them is what a current is to a fish — not a threat but the medium of motion. Each is a folded thing, a crumpled foil envelope no larger than the tip of a finger, and what looks like crumpling is in fact the only organ they have: surface. They are almost entirely surface. The filament legs are not for walking; they are anchors and antennae, and they taste the ceiling for its slow seep of coolness the way a root tastes soil for water.

They drink by standing still. A differential exists between the tile-floor's blistering heat and the ceiling's merely-scalding, and the leaf-bat's body is a bridge across that differential — heat climbs into the creature from below (radiant, convective, borne on the rising air), passes through whatever it uses for metabolism, and is given up into the ceiling above. In the passage, the creature lives. It is a waterwheel turned by the fall of temperature rather than the fall of water, and like a waterwheel it does nothing but turn, and in turning does everything.

They cluster because clustering stabilizes the draft. A single leaf-bat alone would flutter in its own convective updraft and tear. Two together brace each other. Three form a tripod. By the time you have a hundred of them packed shoulder to shoulder across a square handspan of ceiling, they have become a single quilted organ, and the quilting is what *respiration* means here — not breath in and breath out, but the slow shuffling of position as the hotter ones at the cluster's edge are passed inward to cool, and the cooler ones in the middle are passed outward to warm. The shimmer an observer sees is this internal circulation, a crowd forever rearranging itself to even out its own luck.

They reproduce by tearing. When a leaf-bat grows too large — when its surface exceeds what its filaments can hold — it splits along a crease, and the two halves drift apart into the cluster and crumple themselves fuller. There is no genome. There is only the crease, and the crease is inherited because it is the shape a thing of that size must take to survive the fall. Evolution here is origami.

They do not die so much as flatten. An old leaf-bat unfolds, loses its crumple, cannot hold the differential any longer, and drifts down through the room's warm middle air toward the floor, where the lozenges will find it and fold it into the liquid. Nothing is wasted because there is nowhere for waste to go.

### The Floor-Grazers

The lozenges are older. Whatever the makers were, they made these first, and the leaf-bats afterward to balance them. You can tell because the lozenges have a shape that suggests a designer still thinking in terms of *function*, where the leaf-bats have the shape of something that was simply set loose and allowed to find its own crease.

A lozenge is the length of a human hand, flat as a river-stone, and underneath it is all mouth. Not a mouth that bites — a mouth that *wicks*. It lowers itself over a hexagon-seam and the liquid-metal rises into it by capillary draw, and then the lozenge slides, and somewhere along its traverse of the next several tiles it lowers itself again and gives the liquid back. The physics of this are not complicated; the logic of *which* tile receives the deposit is where the purpose lives, or the look of purpose.

Because they do not deposit at random. Over generations — and a generation of lozenge is long, they wear out slowly — they have learned (or been selected to act as if they had learned) to move heat toward the places the room is losing it. Near the corners, where the seams cool and bead, the lozenges visit often, laying down fresh warmth. In the room's center, where the leaf-bats press thickest and draw hardest, the lozenges also visit, feeding the updraft. They are the room's circulatory system made ambulatory, and the hierarchy of yielding at junctions is the traffic law this circulation has written for itself.

The hierarchy is not dominance in any animal sense. It is a sorting by *thermal load*. A lozenge carrying more heat yields to a lozenge carrying less, because the less-laden one is closer to needing a refill, and the system-as-a-whole has slowly learned that prioritizing the hungry keeps the channels evenly fed. None of the lozenges know this. Each one knows only its own simple rule: *if the other is cooler-bellied than me, I wait*. The aggregate of that rule, across six or seven creatures across ten thousand cycles, is the room's metabolism.

They reproduce by accretion. A lozenge that has lived long enough begins to deposit a little more liquid than it takes up, and the excess accumulates along its trailing edge, and eventually a seam forms and a smaller lozenge detaches. The parent is slightly lighter afterward, slightly faster. Over time the parent becomes too light, loses its wicking pressure, and stalls in a channel. The other lozenges route around it for a while. Then, eventually, they begin to take up the liquid from *it* instead of from the seams, and the stalled one is slowly disassembled back into the circulation it used to serve.

### The Corner-Things

These are the strangest, and the oldest, and possibly not made at all.

In the corners, where the liquid metal beads and cools to a temperature that would still sear anything warm-blooded but which is, relative to the rest of the room, *tolerable*, there sit the drop-things. They look like mercury that has learned to sulk. They are soft. They are slow. They have no features and no structures and they do, as the description says, almost nothing — but the almost is important.

What they do is *taste*. The beaded liquid at the seams is not quite the same as the liquid in the channels; it has cooled, and in cooling it has shed some of the trace impurities that the lozenges pick up over their traverses. The corner-things drink these impurities. They are filter-feeders in a medium where the food is the dirt of the medium itself. What they build out of that dirt, inside themselves, is more of themselves — and periodically, when a drop-thing has accumulated enough, it pinches in the middle and becomes two drop-things, and the two sit beside each other drinking for a while before one of them slides fractionally further along the seam to claim its own territory.

Here is the thing the makers may not have anticipated: the drop-things are the only inhabitants of the warren that *change*. The leaf-bats are locked into their crease. The lozenges are locked into their rule. But the drop-things, because they eat whatever the channels have happened to carry that cycle, are slightly different in each generation — slightly differently composed, slightly differently shaped, slightly differently *hungry*. They are the room's only experiment.

Most of the experiments fail. A drop-thing that develops a taste for something rare starves. A drop-thing that divides too often crowds its corner and the crowd collapses back into a single pool. But sometimes — rarely, across spans the walls would know about if the walls were asked — a drop-thing develops a new capacity. One learned, long ago, to sense the pulse-cycle and to drink faster during it. That one's descendants populate the north corner now. Another learned to extrude a filament, briefly, toward a passing lozenge — not to attack, only to sample — and its descendants sit a little higher off the floor than their cousins, with a suggestion of a *reach* to them.

If something in this warren is ever going to wake up, it will be a drop-thing. Not soon. But the drop-things are the only lineage here that is still asking questions, and questions, given enough time and enough corners, are how rooms eventually notice themselves.

### What Lives Here, Taken Together

A single slow animal made of three tissues.

The ceiling is its lung, inhaling the differential. The floor is its bloodstream, redistributing the warmth. The corners are its — the word wants to be *mind*, but it is too early for that; call it its *curiosity*, the small soft part that sits at the edges and tastes what the rest of the body brings past. The pulse that comes every several hours is its heartbeat, and the susurrus during the pulse is the closest thing it has to a thought: a brief coordination of all three tissues, an instant where the lung quickens and the blood rushes and even the curiosity drinks a little faster, and for a span of minutes the room *is* something, rather than merely containing things.

Then the pulse passes, and it goes back to being a room with weather in it.

The makers are long gone, or were never there in the sense the word implies, or are the walls themselves — the ceramic is patient enough to be a mind if it wanted to be one, and incurious enough to have chosen not to. It does not matter. What they built has outlived the question of what they were. The warren lives in itself, as the description says, and what lives in it are the organs of that living: the foil lungs overhead, the river-stone blood below, and in the cool corners the small slow drops that might, one unhurried eon, learn to wonder where they are.
