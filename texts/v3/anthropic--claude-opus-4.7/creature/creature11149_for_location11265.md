# The Listeners

They are tall, and they are thin, and they are the gray-green of the rock — not by pigment but by a long agreement between their skin and the light here. Stand one against a swell of stone in the half-dawn and the eye slides off. This is not camouflage in the predator's sense. There is little here that would hunt them. It is something more like courtesy, or like having taken on the manners of the place.

Their legs are long and jointed for wading; the feet are broad, splayed, with a webbing of tough skin between the toes that lets them cross a mineral pan without breaking its crust, lets them stand for hours in the shallows without sinking into what mud there is. The knees fold backward in the way of waders. They can stand very, very still. A Listener in the shallows, head slightly bowed, is almost indistinguishable from a leaning cairn until it moves, and it does not often move.

The body is narrow, the ribs visible under skin that has the same faint glisten as the bone-pale callers. The arms are long, the hands large for the wrist, with fingers that have learned the weight and balance of wet stone. Their skulls are elongated front-to-back, and the ears — if ears is the word — are not external flaps but a kind of broad, slightly recessed disc on each side of the head, taut as a drumhead, fed by a webwork of fine vessels you can see in good light. They hear with their whole heads. They hear, one suspects, with more than their heads. The sternum is broad and slightly concave, and when the drumming surges, that concavity catches it, holds it, transmits it inward. A Listener at the height of a chorus is a resonator. The sound goes through them and is, for a moment, part of what they are.

The eyes are large and very dark, and they do not blink often. They have a second lid, milky, that comes across when the mist thickens or when they put their face to the water. In the deep of the not-quite-night, when the callers' bodies grow faintly luminous, the Listeners' eyes catch that light and hold it, and this is the closest thing to a beacon the flats ever offer — two soft points, low over the water, somewhere out across the miles. You would not see them unless you knew to look.

They do not speak. Not in the way that word is usually meant. The drumming makes speech in any ordinary sense impossible, and a long lineage in this place has rendered the impulse vestigial. What they have instead is a small repertoire of hand-shapes, used at close range, and — more importantly — the cairns, and the pecked marks on the cairns, and the placement of one stone upon another in a particular order at a particular hour. Their language is slow. A sentence may take a season to set down. A conversation may pass between two Listeners across decades, one of them building, the other, years later, coming upon the build and adding to it, or taking a stone away, or simply sitting beside it for a long time and then walking on. They do not seem to find this slow. By their standards it may be quick.

They eat the swimmers, sometimes, when a swimmer is foolish enough to come within reach of those long arms; the strike is the only fast thing about them, and it is very fast. They eat the slow grazers more often, since the grazers do not flee. They do not eat the callers. This appears to be an absolute. A Listener will step around a caller in the shallows, will wait a quarter-hour for one to clear a path, will move a fallen stone off a caller pinned beneath it with the same careful patience they bring to everything else. Whether this is reverence or merely the recognition that the callers are the voice of the place and to silence one is to dim the world — there is no way to ask, and they would not answer.

They are born — this is guessed at, rather than known — in the deepest pools, the ones too deep to wade, where the rock dips down into something like a well. A Listener gravid with young grows visibly heavier in the lower belly over a long time, and at some point goes to such a pool and does not emerge for a day, and emerges alone, and does not return to that pool for many years. What develops in the deep water and how long it takes to climb out are not known to outsiders and may not be known to the Listeners themselves; they do not appear to keep track of their own ages, or to care to.

They live a long time. This is evident from the cairns. Some cairns have a hundred years of pecking on them, in a hand that does not change — the same slant, the same depth of strike, the same idiosyncratic way of marking the lulls between drumming surges. A Listener will tend a single cairn for what would be, in any other place, several human lives, and then one day will not, and the cairn will weather, and another Listener, perhaps a grandchild, perhaps a stranger, perhaps the same Listener under a different name, will come and add a stone, or will not.

What they are doing here, across all this time, with all this attention, is the question. The cairns are not graves. The marks are not, so far as can be read, prayers, or histories, or astronomy. The best guess any visitor has come away with — and it is only a guess — is that the Listeners are, slowly, across generations, learning the drumming. Not memorizing it; the drumming is too vast and too various to memorize. Learning it in the way a long student of music learns a tradition: well enough to know when something new happens in it. Well enough to recognize a voice. Well enough, perhaps, after enough centuries, to answer.

There is a thing they do, very rarely, that has been seen perhaps four times by outsiders in all the records there are of this place. A Listener, alone, at the deepest part of the night, when the chorus has slowed into its long single tone, will lower itself into the water until only the head is above, and will place its broad sternum flat against a particular stone, and will hold very still, and will — there is no other word — *return* the note. Not vocally. The sound comes out of the chest cavity, through the stone, into the water. It is not loud. It is the exact pitch of the held tone, sustained for as long as the Listener has breath, and then a pause, and then again. The callers nearby do not stop. They do not seem to acknowledge it. But for the duration of the Listener's note, the chorus around that one Listener thickens, very slightly — more callers join in, or the ones already calling deepen — and an attentive ear can hear the small bloom of sound spreading outward across the flats, a swell within the swell, before it dissipates back into the general voice of the place.

After such a night the Listener will build a cairn. A small one. One stone, sometimes; three at most. And they will sit beside it through what passes for the next day, and they will not eat, and they will not move much, and their eyes will be half-closed under the milky inner lid, and whatever they are doing in that long sitting is what they came here to do, all of them, across all the generations, in this gray and patient world. They are not lonely. They have the voice of the place for company, and they have, perhaps once or twice in a life, the experience of having added something to it that the place noticed.

When they die — and they do; one finds the bodies, occasionally, in the shallows, the skin gone slack against the long bones, the eyes finally closed — other Listeners come, eventually, and take them apart, and place the bones in particular relations to particular cairns, and walk away. The skulls are set so the drum-discs face outward, toward the water. The sternum, that broad shallow bowl, is laid flat, facing up, to catch what falls into it: rain, mist, sound. After enough years the bone whitens and thins and finally crumbles, and the flats absorb it, and the drumming, which never paused, continues.
