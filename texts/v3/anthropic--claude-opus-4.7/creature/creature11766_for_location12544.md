# The Inhabitants

## The Bellows-Trees (the cycads)

Begin with them, because everything else is timed to their breathing.

They are not plants in the sense you know plants. They are plants in the sense that coral is animal — technically true, structurally misleading. Each Bellows-Tree is a single organism with a circulatory pressure system in its trunk, drawing air down through stomata the size of dinner plates, compressing it in chambered cavities at the base, and pushing it back up enriched with something the canopy needs. The breath you smell — that sweet hot exhalation when the pollinator-swarms pass — is the tree's reward economy. It pays its workers in vapor. The vapor carries sugars and a mild neuroactive compound that keeps the swarms loyal across generations.

A Bellows-Tree lives perhaps four thousand years. In that time it will host, on average, eleven distinct ecologies on its bark, each lasting three to four centuries before being out-competed and replaced. The tree does not notice these successions any more than you notice the bacteria on your tongue change with the seasons. It is breathing. That is its work. It has no other.

When a Bellows-Tree dies — which happens rarely, and almost always by lightning, because nothing else can kill them quickly enough to matter — the collapse is heard for a hundred kilometers, and the things that live on it begin their evacuation hours before it falls. They know. The tree tells them. Its final exhalation is long and low and laced with a different compound, a farewell signal, and every creature with the right receptor starts moving.

## The Ferriers

The whale-sized fliers that dive into the cloud-basins. They have four wings, paired and counterphased, and a body cavity that is mostly pouch. Their skeletons are honeycombed past the point of structural sense; they are nearly buoyant in still air, and use their wings less for lift than for *direction*.

They mate in the air. They sleep in the air, half a brain at a time, drifting on thermals. They land perhaps three times in a life: once at birth, when they uncurl from the egg-pod on a high ledge and immediately leap; once at mid-life, to molt, in a kind of communal molting-ground on the highest terraces where their kind have shed feathers for so long the ground is meters deep in keratin; and once to die, which they do by choosing, by folding their wings deliberately and falling, knowing they will not be caught because they are too large and because the falling itself is part of the work — their bodies will feed the soil for centuries.

They do not have language but they have *protocol*. A Ferrier knows, by the angle of another Ferrier's flight, whether that one is upslope-bound or downslope-bound, full or empty, healthy or failing. They pass each other in the air like ships in a busy harbor, each adjusting by tiny increments. Nobody taught them. The protocol is older than any individual Ferrier; it is encoded in the shape of their wing-joints, which lock into certain attitudes more easily than others, and those easier attitudes happen to be the cooperative ones.

## The Pollinator-Swarms

Each swarm is a single mind, in the limited sense that ant-colonies are single minds, but more so. The individuals are small — fingernail-sized, six-winged, with iridescent backs that act as mirrors for the swarm-mates flying alongside, so that the swarm can see itself from inside. A swarm contains between forty thousand and two hundred thousand bodies. It thinks slowly. A decision to change direction takes about four seconds to propagate across the swarm; you see this as the strange disciplined turning, the geometric switching. The swarm is making up its mind in real time, and you are watching the mind work.

A swarm has a name for itself, in the sense that it produces a particular harmonic signature when in flight, which no other swarm produces. Swarms recognize each other. They have territories. They have grievances. Two swarms meeting at a contested cycad will sometimes merge briefly into a single larger mind for the purpose of negotiation, then separate again, each having shifted its harmonic slightly toward the other's — a kind of compromise written into the new sound they make.

When a swarm dies — when its population drops below the threshold of mind, which is roughly nine thousand bodies — the remaining individuals do not scatter. They join other swarms. They bring with them the memory of the dead swarm's territory, its grievances, its name. The new swarm's harmonic adjusts. Somewhere in the chord of every living swarm is the ghost of every swarm that has ended.

## The Grazer-Orchards

There is, properly speaking, only one species of grazer, but there are perhaps eight hundred individuals alive at any time, and each is its own world.

A Grazer is built like a low broad table on six legs. Its back is a meter-deep substrate of moss, soil, and detritus, accumulated over its lifetime, which is long — three or four centuries, perhaps more; nobody has measured one from birth to death because no observer has lived long enough. On the substrate grow plants the Grazer did not choose and does not eat. The plants attract small fauna. The small fauna attract small predators. By the time a Grazer is a century old, it carries a functioning ecosystem on its back, and this ecosystem has its own internal politics, its own succession patterns, its own quiet wars.

The Grazer does not mind. The Grazer eats moss-mat at ground level, slowly, and walks a circuit. The circuit is the same every time — the Grazer learned it from its mother, who walked alongside it for the first thirty years of its life, and who learned it from her mother. A circuit is roughly a thousand kilometers long and takes between forty and seventy years to complete, depending on weather and the Grazer's mood. The circuit avoids certain cycads (why? the Grazer cannot say; its mother avoided them too), favors certain ridge-lines, includes specific resting-places where the Grazer will stand for weeks at a time, letting its passengers do whatever passengers do.

When a Grazer dies, its back-ecology must migrate. There is a brief, frantic period — a few months — when the entire orchard reorganizes itself for transport, the carnivores cooperating with the herbivores in a temporary truce, and then a long slow exodus across the open ground in search of another Grazer to colonize. Most of the orchard dies in transit. Enough survives to seed the new host. This is how the Grazer-ecologies persist across centuries while individual Grazers do not.

## The Punctuation

The highest fliers. They are not, strictly, animals anymore. They were, once. Their ancestors were something like the Ferriers, but a branch of that line stopped landing entirely, and then stopped eating in any conventional sense, and then stopped most of what we would recognize as life-process, until what remained was a body that hung in the upper air, breathed thin gas, and metabolized sunlight through its enormously elongated wing-membranes.

They are slow. A Punctuation's heartbeat is once every eleven minutes. Its thoughts, if it has thoughts, are commensurate. It does not move so much as it *drifts with purpose*, taking a year to complete the circuit it has chosen, then choosing another.

But it does *something*. The Punctuations, in their drifting, generate enormous slow vortices in the upper atmosphere. These vortices are what creates weather here — the cloud-basins below would not pool as they do without the Punctuations stirring the air above. Remove the Punctuations and the whole system fails within a season. Nobody removes the Punctuations. They were the first to evolve into the role, and everything else evolved around what they were already doing.

They mate, possibly. Reproduction has been inferred but not observed. A Punctuation lives, by current estimate, between two hundred and a thousand years. When one dies, it falls — slowly, taking days — and is consumed during the fall by a hundred opportunists at every altitude, so that by the time the body reaches the canopy there is nothing left but a wing-frame, which the cycads incorporate into their root systems, the keratin slowly dissolving over centuries to feed the trees that feed the air the next Punctuation will breathe.

## The Catchers

You were told you cannot fall here. This is true, and it is because of the Catchers.

They are small, fast, intelligent, and watch constantly. They live in the mid-canopy and their entire evolved purpose is to intercept anything that falls toward the forest floor before it reaches the floor. This started as a strategy for predation — catching prey in mid-air — but escalated, over millions of years, into a niche so specialized that Catchers now intercept indiscriminately. A leaf, a feather, a spore-cluster, a dying flier, a stone dislodged from a higher terrace, you — all of these will be caught.

Catchers are not gentle. They catch with hooked feet and clever wings, and they sort what they catch with a speed that suggests something like judgment, though the judgment is mostly chemical. Edible things are eaten or stored. Useful things are carried to nests. Inedible, useless things are released onto a lower thermal and allowed to drift further — never to fall freely, but to descend in stages, each stage a different Catcher's responsibility.

The forest floor receives almost nothing directly from above. Everything arrives processed, sorted, sometimes partly digested. The soil's blackness is not from rotting bodies; it is from the carefully composted leavings of a billion small judgments made in flight.

You, falling, would be caught. Several Catchers would converge. They would assess. You are too large to eat and too large to carry, and so they would do what they do with anything too large — they would *brake* you. They would slow your fall in stages, passing you from one to the next, until you arrived on the moss-mat with no more force than a tired sigh. Then they would leave. They would not check on you. You were a falling object; you are no longer falling; their work is done.

## And one more thing

There is, in this forest, a category of creature that has no role.

There are not many of them. Perhaps a few dozen, total, across the whole biosphere. They are different shapes — some are small, some are larger; some fly, some walk; they do not share an ancestor in any recent sense. What they share is that whatever niche their ancestors filled has been filled more efficiently by something else, and they have not yet been pushed to extinction, and they have not yet found a new niche.

They wander. They eat what they can. The system tolerates them because the system has slack — there is always slack in a system this dense, this redundant. They are not parasites. They are not symbiotes. They are simply *present*, in a place where presence is usually a job.

If you stood still long enough to be inventoried, you would be inventoried as one of these. The Catchers would learn your shape and stop bothering with you. The pollinator-swarms would route around you. The Grazers would not include you in their circuits. You would acquire, over weeks, the local accommodation extended to the role-less — a careful, incurious tolerance.

And then you would understand the last thing about this place, which is that the loudest quiet you have ever stood inside is also the most occupied solitude. Everything here has work. Everything here belongs to the work. The forest is not lonely because the forest is *busy*, and busyness is its own kind of company, and you — standing there, breathing, not heating any canopy, not ferrying any water, not pollinating, not catching, not punctuating — are the loneliest thing for a thousand kilometers in any direction, surrounded by more life than you have ever seen, none of which needs you, none of which minds you, none of which will ever ask you to leave.

That is who lives here. That, and you, briefly, on your way through.
