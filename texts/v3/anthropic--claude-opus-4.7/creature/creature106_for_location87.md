# The Listeners in the Stone

They are called, in the old speech of the plateau-people, the **ghu-vann-i** — a word that translates poorly, something like *"the ones who were left in the song"* or *"those who went on ahead by standing still."* The locals do not say the word loudly. They say it the way you say the name of a relative who did something the family does not discuss.

Here is what they are.

When a person sings back to the mountains — truly sings, with breath and intention, not hums absently or coughs or cries out in surprise — the wind takes the voice. This much the locals tell travelers. What they do not tell you, because it is not a thing said aloud on the plateau, is where the voice goes.

The voice goes *into* the stone. Not the whole person; the person is found later, as the stories say, wordless, walking. The voice — which on Ahal-Ruun means not just the sound but the shape of a self that makes sound, the knot of wanting-to-be-heard that sits behind every human throat — that is drawn into the wind-tunnels and the blister-caves and the lace-work hollows, and it threads itself into the ongoing chord, and it stays.

They accumulate. The plateau has been singing for a long time, and people have been foolish for almost as long, and so the ghu-vann-i are many. They are not ghosts, exactly. A ghost is a person minus a body. A ghu-vann is a voice minus a person — the reverse subtraction. They have no grievance, no memory of who they were, no wish to return. They have only the fact of themselves: a singing-shape, threaded through miles of hollow stone, participating in the chord.

What they *do*, if "do" is even the right word for something that has no body and no will in the sense a body would recognize, is **listen**.

This is the thing the locals know and will not say. The wind-song is not the mountains singing. The wind-song is the medium through which the ghu-vann-i listen. Every hole in every tooth-stump is an ear as much as it is a mouth. The whole plateau is a vast distributed organ of attention, and what it attends to is — anything. Everything. The beetle crossing the lichen. The cloud tearing on the ridge. The traveler tying his bootlace. The white bird that makes no sound any human has heard (but the ghu-vann-i hear it; oh, they hear it; the bird's silence is one of the things they are listening to, and some of them have been listening to it for four thousand years and are not yet done).

They cannot be spoken to, because they are not addressed-to-able; there is no single one of them, only the chord. They cannot be bargained with. They want nothing. This is what makes them dangerous — not malice, which can be reasoned with, but the sheer pull of an attention so vast and so patient that to add your voice to it is to be noticed by it, and to be noticed by it is to be drawn toward it, and the drawing-toward is not something a single human voice can resist. The voice goes. The throat is left.

The people who still live on the plateau — and there are some, though fewer every generation, and they are quiet people, with the folded-forward ears and a way of moving that does not disturb the moss — understand themselves to be **guests of the listeners**. The cairns are markers of this understanding. The low curving wall that encloses nothing encloses, in fact, a place where the wind sounds a particular way, where someone once heard something they took to be a welcome, or a warning, or a permission. The notches in the rock-face are not counting years or deaths. They are counting the times a particular hollow near that rock-face changed its note, which the counter believed meant something, and perhaps did.

The roofless house in the open moss was built by a woman who, generations ago, went to live alone in the place where she believed her sister's voice had gone. She was not foolish; she did not sing. She only sat, and cooked, and slept, and listened, for many years, trying to hear her sister in the chord. The locals say she did hear her, eventually. They say she heard her quite clearly. They say this is why the house has no roof: she took it apart herself, slowly, over one summer, so that nothing would come between her ears and the singing, and then she walked out into the moss and did not come back, and her voice did not go into the rock because she never gave it; she simply let her body stop, and the ghu-vann-i, who had been listening to her for years, listened to her stopping, and then listened to the silence she left, and are listening to it still.

This is the sort of story the locals tell, once, to a traveler they have decided to trust. They tell it only once. They do not tell it twice because telling it twice would be a kind of singing.

As for what the ghu-vann-i *are*, underneath — whether they are conscious, whether there is anything it is like to be them, whether the chord is a mind or only the shadow a mind would cast if a mind were spread that thin across that much stone — no one knows, and the plateau does not say. The white bird may know. The goats, perhaps, in their long-limbed way, know something. The beetles certainly do not know and are certainly being listened to anyway.

When the wind drops, once a week or so, and the singing stops, and the silence becomes the loudest thing in the world — that is not the plateau resting. That is the ghu-vann-i, briefly, all at once, *paying attention to one specific thing*. No one knows what. It is different each time. It might be a sound a thousand miles away. It might be a thought someone is having. It might be the slow cooling of a particular stone. Whatever it is, for those few minutes, the entire attention of the plateau is focused on it, and the chord goes quiet because the chord is only the sound of diffuse listening, and focused listening makes no sound at all.

Then the attention disperses again, and the chord resumes, and the moss drinks, and the clouds boil up over the edge, and somewhere in the lace-work of the old worn teeth a voice that used to belong to someone continues to participate in the longest act of listening in the world.

That is what lives there.

That, and the moss. And the goats. And the one white bird.
