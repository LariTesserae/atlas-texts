## What Lives at Tlemen's Shoulder

### The Caller in the Far Corner

Start with the slow whoop, because that's what you keep coming back to. It is not a frog and not a bird. The thing that makes it lives behind the largest tree, in a kind of grotto-fold where the wall doubles on itself — a recess the makers seem to have left deliberately, the way you might leave a deep armchair in a room.

It is the size of a large dog, but built wrong for a dog. Four limbs, yes, but the front pair end in soft pads ringed with short, sensitive filaments, and the back pair are folded under it in a permanent crouch, as though it has not stood up in a very long time and no longer needs to. Its skin is loose, grey-violet, hairless, faintly damp. It has a throat-sac that inflates with each whoop and a face that is mostly eye — one large, dark, moist eye in the center, with two smaller ones offset above it like punctuation.

It eats the iridescent beetles. It eats them one at a time, very slowly, and there are always enough beetles. It has been eating beetles since before the language you think in had its first consonant.

It is not the last of its kind in the chamber. There are three of them. They sit in three different corners and call to each other at intervals you would need to record over weeks to perceive as a rhythm. The whoop is not loneliness. It is a roll-call. *Here. Here. Here.* For a very long time the answer has always come.

They were the makers' favorites, perhaps. Or the makers' pets. Or the makers themselves, reduced — through some choice or some catastrophe — to this softer register, willing to forget what they had built so long as the building kept holding them. You won't know. They will look at you with the great central eye and the look will be neither welcoming nor hostile. It will be the look of something that has already understood you and decided you are weather.

### The Troop in the Canopy

The russet primates are eleven, always eleven. When one dies the troop produces another within a season, and the new one comes out already knowing the paths through the high branches, already six-fingered, already russet. They do not breed in any way you would recognize. There is no infant stage. The youngest member of the troop arrives small but competent, and grows into its full size over a few years, and lives for what you would guess is about forty of your years, and then one day climbs to the highest branch of the largest tree and lies down along it and does not get up, and the tree absorbs it over the following weeks, and a new one is small in the branches by the time the old one is gone.

They are aware of the chamber as a chamber. This is the thing you would not guess from watching them. The slow whoop-callers have forgotten; the primates remember. They know the seam, they know the light is not weather, they know the disc in the floor is warm. They do not speak of these things because they do not speak. But when the seam opens and a visitor comes through — every few centuries, perhaps, on whatever schedule the chamber permits — one of them descends to look. Always one. Always the same role, filled by whichever member of the troop carries it that century.

The one looking at you now is the looker. It has the job. It is doing the job well. Its assessment of you is patient and complete and will be remembered by the troop after you leave, in whatever way the troop remembers things — possibly through the tree, possibly through something held in the six-fingered hands when they touch each other in the grooming-circles that take place in the highest fork at certain intervals of the unmoving afternoon.

### The Tapir-Thing

It is not a tapir and it is not thinking about you. It is the chamber's gardener. The long snout is for a single purpose: it finds, by smell, the places where the slow-weeping seams in the wall have over-wept, and it drinks the excess, and it carries the water in some internal reservoir to the bases of the trees, where it releases the water through a different orifice into the soil. It does this on a circuit. The circuit takes about a day. It has been doing this circuit for as long as the trees have stood, which is as long as the chamber has stood, which is longer than the mountain.

It is, almost certainly, a machine. Or the descendant of a machine that learned to be flesh because flesh was easier to maintain than whatever it had been. When it dies — and it does die, occasionally — the chamber produces another one. The mechanism by which the chamber produces another one is not visible. You would have to wait a long time to see it happen, and by then the seam would have closed.

### The Crested Birds

There are two. They are a pair in the sense that they are always together, but not in any sense involving reproduction. They are the chamber's auditors. They walk the floor in long, careful patrols, and they look at everything, and what they are looking for is wrongness — a leaf curling in a way leaves should not curl here, a beetle on a path beetles do not take, a film on the stone that suggests the wet has overstayed.

When they find a wrongness they stand over it and make a low, almost subvocal hum, and within hours something has come — a smaller creature you haven't seen yet, pale and many-legged, lives in the cracks — and the wrongness is gone.

They looked at you and decided you are not a wrongness, only a temporary feature. This was generous of them. They could have decided otherwise.

### The Things in the Cracks

You haven't seen these and you won't. The pale many-legged ones live in the seams of the wall and in the soil under the ferns and in the heartwood of the trees. They are the chamber's hands. They clear, they tidy, they recycle, they carry the dead of the larger species down into whatever processing happens beneath the floor — because there must be such processing, or the chamber would have filled with bones long ago, and there are no bones.

If you put your ear to the floor near the disc you can hear them moving. A soft dry rustle, very faint, very constant. The sound of the chamber's housekeeping, audible only to those who think to listen for it.

### The Beetles

The iridescent beetles are food. That is their role and they fill it. There are always about four thousand of them in the chamber, and they are eaten, and they are replaced, and the replacement is not by laying eggs — you can watch them for a year and find no eggs — but by some slower budding off the bark of the trees, where a bright patch swells over weeks and then one morning is a beetle and walks away.

They are the chamber's currency. Energy moves through them from the trees to the whoop-callers and to the snake and to several other mouths. They are uncomplicated and beautiful and they have been uncomplicated and beautiful for an unfathomable time.

### The Snake

The jade snake is one snake. It has always been one snake. It does not die. This is the only resident of the chamber for whom this is true, and it suggests that the snake is something else — perhaps the original maker, kept on in this form by preference; perhaps a sensor, draped where it is draped because from that branch it can see the whole chamber and the seam and the disc and the alcove with the three objects; perhaps simply a snake that the chamber will not let die because the chamber needs a snake there and that one is the one.

It has watched you since you came in. The watching is not predatory. It is the watching of something taking notes for a record that no one will ever read.

### The Three Objects in the Alcove

These are not alive in the chamber's sense, but they belong to the population, and the population knows about them.

The stone is a stone from outside the chamber, brought in by the last visitor — perhaps eight hundred years ago, perhaps three thousand. The feather is from a bird that no longer exists in the world outside, deposited by a visitor before that one. The third object, the still one — that was placed by the makers themselves, and what it is doing you cannot guess, but the chamber arranges itself around it. The pale many-legged ones will not cross the floor directly beneath it. The crested birds bow, very slightly, when their patrol takes them past the alcove. The whoop-callers' whoops do not echo from that wall; the sound goes into the alcove and does not come out.

You should not add a fourth object. Not because anything will punish you, but because the alcove is, in some way you can feel but not name, full. Three is the number. Three has been the number for a long time.

### What Lives Here, Then

A held population. Eleven primates, three whoop-callers, two crested birds, one tapir-gardener, one undying snake, four thousand beetles, an uncounted host of small lives in the cracks and on the leaves, and a forest of twelve trees that are perhaps each one organism with the chamber and perhaps each one a colleague of it.

None of them know they are inside a thing. Except the snake, who knows everything, and the primates, who know the seam, and the still object in the alcove, which is not a resident but a witness.

They are not lonely. Loneliness requires a sense that the population could be larger, and the population here is exactly its own size, and has been, and will be.

When you leave — and you will leave; the seam is already, you notice, a little narrower than when you came in — the looker will return to the canopy and the troop will know about you. The whoop-callers will whoop. The tapir-gardener will continue its circuit. The crested birds will erase the small wrongnesses of your passage: a scuff on the pale stone, a bent fern, a single hair from the nape of your neck where you sweated. The chamber will close around the place you sat and the place will forget you within a day.

The afternoon will not change.

And somewhere, in the register the makers left for, perhaps someone is still watching the chamber the way you watched the troop — with mild, ancient curiosity, noting that another visitor came and went, and that the held thing is still holding.
