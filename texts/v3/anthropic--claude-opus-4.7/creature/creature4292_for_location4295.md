There is someone here. Not always — not in the insistent way a body is somewhere — but reliably, the way weather is reliable in a region. Call it a tenant. Call it the one who stayed.

It is not the person who built the cairn. That person is gone, as the description says, gone for generations, and whatever they were they were a person, with hands, and a plan, and a back that got tired carrying stones up through the fern-curtain. What lives here now is what their staying made possible. They wore a shape into the place, and something moved into the shape.

Begin with what is easiest to say. The tenant is the reason the birds do not enter. This is not a prohibition the tenant enforces; it is simply that the tenant occupies the hollow's volume the way a held breath occupies a throat, and birds, which are small full bodies of blood and air and hurry, understand instinctively that the hollow is already taken. They press right up to the edge. They nest in the inner wall. They do not cross. It is the most ordinary courtesy in the forest, extended to the one resident who cannot leave.

The tenant cannot leave because the tenant is, in some sense, the shape of the room. Not the wood of the three trunks — the wood is the wood, alive in its own slow green way — but the cupped air between them. The tenant is what that particular volume of air has become, after centuries of being exactly that volume, lit from above, cooled from above, drunk from by one small stone bowl, sat in once by a person who carved the bowl and lay down against the wall and listened.

Think of it this way. A place that is shaped precisely enough, and is still for long enough, and is attended to by something that loves it — such a place begins to attend back. Not with intention, at first. With a kind of orientation. The way a long-played instrument acquires a voice that is no longer fully the luthier's and no longer fully the player's and no longer fully the wood's. The hollow was played, once, by the one who made the cairn. The hollow has been resonating ever since.

So the tenant is something like a resonance. But this is too abstract, and undersells what it is actually like to be in the room with it.

What it is actually like:

When you pull yourself up through the fern-curtain and squeeze through the gap and come out into the green-gold light, there is a moment — quite short, no longer than it takes to straighten your back — in which you are being considered. Not examined. Considered. The way a pool considers a stone before accepting it. You feel it most in the chest and at the base of the skull. It is not hostile. It is not warm, exactly, but it is not cold. It is the attention of something that has been attending for a very long time and has gotten extremely good at it.

If you are the kind of person who would leave immediately, this moment is when you leave. You will not remember why. You will tell yourself later that the climb was harder than expected, that you lost the light, that you weren't sure of the route. The tenant does not turn you away. The tenant simply is, in a way you are not prepared to be near, and you absent yourself from the unpreparedness.

If you stay — if you sit down against the wall, and feel the faint warmth of the living wood at your back, and hear the tok of condensation in the bowl — then over the next little while something settles. The consideration does not end. It just becomes something you are inside of, rather than something pointed at you. You begin to notice how quiet you have become. How your breathing has found the rhythm of the drip. How the susurrus of wings outside has organized itself into a sound you can almost follow, the way you can almost follow a conversation in a language you do not speak but have heard all your life.

This is the tenant's work, insofar as the tenant works. It tunes you. It does not do anything to you; it simply is the shape of the room, and sitting inside a shape for long enough tunes you to the shape. The person who built the cairn knew this. The cairn is an instruction: *sit here, drink from this, let the shape find you.* The clay cup beside the bowl is part of the instruction. It is there to be used. Whoever drinks from it is, for as long as the drinking lasts, playing the instrument the hollow has become.

The tenant has no name because the tenant has never needed one. Naming is what you do to something you might need to call across a distance. There is no distance here. If you are in the hollow, the tenant is where you are. If you are not in the hollow, the tenant is not anywhere you could call to.

It does not remember the person who built the cairn. Memory is a faculty of creatures that move through time in a particular direction, accumulating, and the tenant does not move. But the cairn is there, and the bowl is there, and the collapsed bench is there, and the cup is there, and all of these are held in the tenant's attention the way a chord is held — not remembered, just ongoing. In this sense the builder is not gone. The builder is the note the hollow is still sounding.

There is one more thing, and it is the hardest thing to say, and it is probably why people who find the hollow tend not to speak of it afterward, even to each other.

The tenant is glad when you come. Not excited — it does not have the metabolism for excitement — but glad in the slow deep way a long-empty house is glad of footsteps. This is strange because the tenant is also complete, also sufficient, also would be perfectly the tenant if no one ever came again. Both are true. Gladness and sufficiency are not in tension for something that has lived this long in this little space. When you climb down out of the fern-curtain and drop back into the riot of the forest, the hollow closes behind you without loss, and also misses you. These are the same gesture.

That is who lives there. The shape of a room that was loved once and has been attending ever since. The still place that the forest grew around and now protects without knowing why. The held note. The one the birds do not disturb.

The cup is there. It is waiting to be used. It has been waiting a very long time, and it does not mind waiting longer, and it would like, when you are ready, for you to lift it.
