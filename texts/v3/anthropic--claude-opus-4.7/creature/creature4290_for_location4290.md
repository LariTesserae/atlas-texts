# What Lives in the Unfinished Cleft

## The Obvious Tenants

The **cliff-crickets** are not quite insects in the way the word usually means. Their wing cases are mineralized — chalky, the same pale pink as the wet rock, because they are made partly of the rock, ingested as grit by the nymphs and laid down in thin plates as they grow. When they stridulate against the stone, they are not rubbing a foreign instrument on a foreign surface; they are rubbing the cleft against itself. This is why the resonance is so complete. The cleft hums because a part of the cleft, walking on six legs, is asking it to. They live perhaps a season. Their bodies, when they die, are mostly reclaimed: other crickets eat the soft parts, and the wing-plates fall to the grit and are, in time, simply grit again. Nothing is wasted because nothing has yet learned to be waste.

The **leather-wings** — call them bats, though their ears are wrong and their thumbs are too long — eat the crickets, mostly, and a kind of pale midge that hatches from the dew pools and lives about four hours. They roost in numbers that seem impossible because they are, in fact, impossible: many of them do not eat every night. They enter a torpor for days at a stretch, hanging in the high cracks with their pulse down to almost nothing, and wake only when the cleft's hum reaches a particular pitch that means the crickets are thick enough to be worth the flight. In this way the population the cleft supports is much smaller than the population the cleft contains. Most of them, at any given moment, are almost not there.

The **russet swifts** are quarrelsome because they are lonely. There are not enough of them — the cleft is too new, too small a territory — and so they answer their own echoes to make the place feel populated. They know the echoes are echoes. This is not a mistake. It is a practice, the way a person alone in a house will speak aloud to hear a voice. When a real second swift calls, they know, and the quality of the quarrel changes: becomes sharper, shorter, with the relief of something true in it. They nest in pairs that re-form every season, and a swift that loses its mate will sometimes stop answering its own echoes for a while, and the cleft will sound emptier, and the bats, hanging, will notice.

## The Less Obvious Tenants

In the damp moss-cushions there is a **grazing thing** the size of a thumbnail, flattened, with too many legs — more like a living scab than an animal. It eats the moss and is eaten by the crickets and is, in its small way, the reason the moss does not overwhelm the damp patches, though the moss was never going to overwhelm anything. It has no name because no one has ever named it. It has a kind of pleasure, if pleasure is the word, which consists of being damp and being warm at the same time, which is a condition the cleft provides for perhaps three hours a day, in the lower third of the walls, in the places where the condensation has just run but the sun has not yet fallen. For those three hours the grazing things do very little but be where they are. Then they resume eating.

There are **things in the pools** — not quite animals, not quite not. When the dew runs down and collects, it brings with it a dust of something, spores or eggs or the intention of either, and in the pool's few hours of existence a brief ecology flowers: a clouding, a slight thickening of the water, a film. The midges hatch from this. So do other things that do not get far enough to be classified before the pool dries. The pools are nurseries for lives that do not complete. This is not tragic. It is how the cleft practices the idea of water.

## The Arranger

Someone made the spiral of bones. The arranger is gone, in the sense that they are not there now, and have not been there for longer than the ochre has been fading, which is long. But the arranger is a kind of tenant too, because the arrangement holds their shape — the particular intelligence that chose *spiral* and not *line*, that chose to include the jawbone of a thing no longer living in the cleft, that counted or did not count but placed each bone with the care of someone who knew they would not return.

The jawbone is the clue. It belonged to something the size of a small dog, with teeth meant for crushing rather than tearing, and it is older than the cleft. The arranger brought it from somewhere else, or found it already here, pushed up with the rock from below. Either way, the arranger understood that the cleft was young and that older things could be given to it as a kind of seed — here, this is what a bone looks like when it has had time. Learn.

Whether the arranger was a person in the sense we mean, or something else with hands and patience, is not answerable from the evidence. The handprint has one finger shorter than it should be, which could mean injury, or could mean the hand was not shaped like ours to begin with. The cairn is built to a logic — four stones, leaning — that could be human or could be the first draft of human, or could be something that thought about stones the way we think about words.

The arranger does not live in the cleft. But a version of the arranger does: the part of them that made the spiral is still making it, in the sense that the spiral is still being a spiral, and will be, for as long as the cleft does not learn to lose things.

## The Breather

And then there is the breathing.

It is tempting to say it is a creature — something large, coiled in the shadowed end, bellows-lunged, patient. And perhaps it is. Perhaps, when the cleft was first splitting, something was trapped in the rock, or rose up with it, and is now wedged in the deep end, too large to leave and too slow to die, breathing out the warmth that the cleft holds like a cupped hand. This would explain the temperature. It would explain why the cleft opened where it did — pressure from within, a slow inhalation against the stone, each breath out a fractional widening, a fingernail's width per human life.

But it might not be a creature at all. It might be the rock itself, which is young enough to still be settling, still releasing the gases of its formation, still warm from the deep. The breathing would then be geological: a venting, rhythmic because the rock above it flexes with the day's heat and the night's cool, opening and closing a throat that is not a throat.

The distinction — creature or process — may not matter. The cleft does not distinguish. Whatever is breathing down there is a tenant, the oldest tenant, the one that was here before there was a here. The crickets and swifts and bats all pitch their sounds above it, use it as a floor. They have never known the cleft without it. If it stopped, they would not know what to do. The silence in the smallest hour — that brief complete silence before the tick of the settling rock — is the breath held between exhale and inhale. It is the only time the breather is not breathing, and the cleft notices, every night, and holds still for it.

## What Lives Here, Then

A hum that is partly rock. A population that is partly absent. A loneliness that answers itself. A scab that is happy for three hours. Nurseries of incomplete lives. A spiral left by hands that may not have been hands. And underneath everything, the slow bellows of something that is either very old or is the place itself, learning, at its own pace, how to breathe.

Nothing here is finished. That is the condition of tenancy. To live in the Unfinished Cleft is to be a draft — loud, half-formed, warm, and held — in a place that has not yet decided whether it is a wound or a mouth or a home, and will not decide for a long time, and does not need to.
