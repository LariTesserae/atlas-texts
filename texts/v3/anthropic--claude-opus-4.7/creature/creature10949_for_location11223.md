# The Inhabitants of the Sumpworks

They are not quite what you would expect, and they are exactly what you would expect, and the discrepancy between those two facts is the first thing to understand about them.

## The Pump-men

Start with them, because the town starts with them. There are forty-one pump-men, and there have been forty-one pump-men since the town was built, and when one dies there is already an apprentice who has been an apprentice for some indeterminate long time and who steps into the dead man's boots and the dead man's name. Not the dead man's *given* name — the working name, the name stencilled on the locker. There are forty-one lockers. There have always been forty-one lockers.

They are heavy men, mostly, thickened by the work and by the diet of the place, which runs to boiled root vegetables and river fish and a particular flatbread cooked on the hot pipe-casings in the engine house. Their hands are extraordinary: huge, scarred, articulate, with fingernails permanently rimmed in the black grease that the pump bearings throw. They have a way of touching machinery that is indistinguishable from the way a good doctor touches a patient — diagnostic, listening, not quite tender but close to it.

They sleep in shifts of three, in cots arranged around the central engine so that someone is always within four meters of the governor. They dream, when they dream, of the pumps. Ask one what he dreamed and he will tell you the chuff was off by a quarter-beat in the number two satellite, and he will be right, and he will rise and go and put his ear to the casing and adjust something, and come back, and the chuff will be correct, and he will not remember in the morning that he did this.

They are the only inhabitants of the Sumpworks who can be said to be *attentive*. Everyone else has settled. The pump-men have not settled because the pumps will not let them.

## The Settled

Everyone else is the Settled. This is not a word they use for themselves; it is just what they are.

They are between forty and seventy years old, mostly. There are some younger — the mill needs strong backs at the rolling tables — but even the young ones move with a slightly older man's economy, never wasting a step, because in this town a wasted step is a step into standing water or a chest-height pipe. The young learn this fast and then they are not young anymore, in the way that matters.

They do their work. The work is done. The same tonnage of sheet leaves on the same barges. The bakery produces the same number of loaves. The two boarding houses keep the same eleven rooms, of which two are always reserved and the other nine are let to travelers when travelers come, which is often enough to keep the boarding-house keepers from forgetting how to greet a stranger and rare enough that they greet each stranger with the slight surprise of someone remembering an old habit.

The Settled have faces that have stopped changing. Not unlined — deeply lined, most of them — but the lines have reached their configuration and will not deepen further. Photographs of the Settled taken ten years apart are indistinguishable to a casual eye and only distinguishable to a careful one by the wear on their clothing, which does change, slowly, as cloth must.

They talk, but they do not gossip. There is nothing to gossip about. They tell the same six or seven stories about the founding of the town, with small variations that are themselves stable — the man who tells the story of the first pile-driving always tells it the same way, and the man who tells the story of the banyan in the customs house always tells it the same way, and they sit at the same tables in the same drinking-house and tell their stories at roughly the same intervals, and the listeners listen with the patient half-attention you give to a clock chiming.

They are not unhappy. This is important. Visitors often assume they must be unhappy, because visitors assume that stasis is suffering, but the Settled have made their accommodation, the way the customs house made its accommodation with the banyan, and what looks from outside like a slow drowning is from inside simply *what is.* They take pleasure in small things: the brass frogs, the particular taste of the pipe-bread, the smell of cut basalt after rain, the warmth of the hearth-light flickering on a wet wall. They have a great deal of pleasure, in fact, and very little event, and they have arranged their inner lives around this ratio.

## The Old Ones

There are perhaps thirty people in the Sumpworks who are properly old — past seventy, some far past. They do not work. They sit, mostly, in the doorways of the lower floors where the cross-draft from the channel cools the air a little, and they watch.

What they watch is harder to say. The street, ostensibly. The bats at dusk. The egrets. But there is a quality to their watching that suggests they are watching for something specific, and have been watching for it for a very long time, and have not yet seen it but have not yet given up.

They are the only inhabitants who remember the town being built. They were the workmen who drove the piles. They were young then, and the mud was younger, and they were paid in scrip to come here and do a job and the job kept needing doing and so they stayed, and married, mostly each other, and had the few children there are, and grew old.

They know, in a way the Settled do not quite know, that the town is provisional. That the basalt is young. That the equilibrium is held, not given. When the pumps falter for half a beat — which happens, rarely — the Settled do not notice, but the Old Ones lift their heads in unison, like dogs hearing a far whistle, and then the beat resumes and they lower their heads again and go back to watching for the thing they are watching for.

Some of them, the very oldest, have begun to resemble the basalt. Their skin has taken on a dark grey cast, and a slight sheen of moisture that does not evaporate. This is not a metaphor. It is one of the accommodations the place makes with its inhabitants and they with it. When these very old ones die — and they do die; the upriver burials are mostly of them — they are reportedly heavier than they should be, and the bearers carrying them up to the ridges sweat and curse and have to rest often.

## The Children

There are nine children in the Sumpworks. There have been nine children in the Sumpworks for as long as anyone can remember, which is to say, as long as the Old Ones have been watching, which is to say, as long as the town has been. When a child grows up — and they do grow up, slowly — a new child appears, by birth, somewhere in the town, to some couple who had not been expecting one. The arithmetic is observed but not discussed.

The children are quiet. They play games that involve the pipes and the channels and the brass frogs, and their games have rules that no adult taught them, and they teach the rules to the new child when the new child is old enough to learn. They are not unhappy either. They are children, and the town is the world, and the world is interesting enough.

They are afraid of the Old Ones, slightly, in the way children are afraid of any clear window onto what they will become.

## The Travelers

The travelers are not inhabitants, but they are part of the population, in the same way that the moths the bats hunt are part of the population — necessary, recurrent, individually irrelevant.

A traveler comes through every nine or ten days on the barges. They sleep in the boarding houses. They eat the pipe-bread. They remark on the heat, then the noise, then the sameness. They leave. Some of them write about the Sumpworks afterward, in journals or letters, and their accounts are remarkably consistent, which one would expect, since the town gives them remarkably consistent things to describe.

What none of them ever quite captures is that the town is *looking back at them.* Not the Settled — the Settled barely register a traveler. But the Old Ones, in their doorways, watch each traveler with the same patient unsatisfied attention they give to everything else, and the pump-men, when a traveler passes the engine house, pause for a half-beat in their listening to the machinery and listen instead to the traveler's footsteps, and then go back to the machinery, having heard whatever they needed to hear.

## The Frogs

It would be wrong to end without them. The brass frogs are not inhabitants in the strict sense, but the Sumpworks has reached an accommodation with them too, and they with it. There are, as best anyone has ever counted, four hundred and some frogs, and the number does not vary. They are loud at the same hours. They sit on the same ledges. The frog on the windowsill of the third-floor back room of the eastern boarding house is, indeed, the same frog the traveler saw last night, and the same frog the previous traveler saw nine days ago, and the same frog the traveler before that saw, and so on, back through every traveler who has ever slept in that room, back to the room's first night, when the plaster was still drying and the frog arrived from somewhere and took up its position and stayed.

The frogs may be the truest inhabitants. They came when the mud came. They will go, presumably, when the mud goes — when the basalt finishes whatever the basalt is doing, when the equilibrium that everyone is pretending is permanent finishes being temporary. Until then they sing into the chuff of the pumps and the chuff of the pumps sings into them, and the whole town hangs in that braided sound like something suspended in clear water, visible, still, and not yet sinking.
