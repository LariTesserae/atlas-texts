# The Weavers

They have no name for themselves. Names require a second party to distinguish from, and there has not been a second party in living memory — not in anyone's memory, not in the memory of anyone's grandmother's grandmother. The word they would use for *person*, if they still had words, would also be the word for *here*, and also the word for *us*, and also, probably, the word for the stalk itself. These distinctions do not carry weight in a world this small.

They are, as the description says, small and pale and deaf. But this undersells them.

They are small in a particular way — not stunted, not childlike, but *fitted*. Generation after generation of climbing the ribbed wall has bred a body that matches the wall: a short torso, long strong arms, legs slightly bowed outward so the knees clear the belly when squatting on a narrow ledge, and feet that are almost a second pair of hands. The toes are long and splayed and partially prehensile, with the outer two toes set a little apart from the inner three, like a tree-frog's. A weaver standing on a node does not grip with their feet; they *settle* onto it, the way a hand settles onto a familiar tool. They can sleep standing on a ledge no wider than a thumb. Many do. Beds are a luxury of the lower floors, where the pith-floss piles thick enough to nest in.

Their skin is the color of the inside of a new potato — not white, but a pale yellow-green that in the submarine light of the chamber reads as simply *the color of the air here*. Capillaries show through. The veins at the wrists and temples are visibly blue-green. Under the lamps they flush faintly pink when they work hard, and this is the closest thing to weather their bodies know.

They are deaf, yes, but *deaf* is the wrong word for what they are, because deafness implies the absence of a thing they ought to have. They have no absence. They have instead an extraordinarily developed sense of vibration through bone and fingertip and sole. A weaver standing on a platform can tell you — not in words, they have no words — which of the seventeen great looms is slightly off-beat this hour, which belt is going to need retensioning by next shift, whether the hydraulic shriek is a quarter-tone higher than yesterday (it usually isn't; sometimes it is; this matters). Their skulls are resonators. Their teeth are tuning forks. When two of them press foreheads together to communicate, they are not shouting through bone as a workaround — they are using the primary channel. Speech, for them, is a thing that happens *inside* two heads at once, a shared vibration in a shared cavity, the way a single note might be sung by two strings of the same instrument. It is intimate in a way that outsider speech is not. You cannot lie forehead-to-forehead. The skull gives you away.

Their eyes are large and dark and slightly protuberant, adapted to the green gloom, with pupils that do not contract much because there is nothing bright to contract against. The whites are faintly green-tinged from the ambient light reflecting off the walls of the eye itself. They see in the dimness the way a fish sees underwater: perfectly well, for their purposes, and they would be blinded by anything we would call adequate illumination. A weaver brought up to the outer skin of the stalk, where the translucence admits real daylight, would weep and shield her face and have to be led back down.

Their hands are the remarkable thing. The hands are where a weaver *lives*. The palms are thick and pale and callused to a texture like soft leather, but the fingertips — the fingertips are hypertrophied, each pad broadened and ridged with whorls deep enough to see from across a room, and each finger is wrapped, usually, in a long spiral of the thread they themselves produce. The bandaging is not for injury. It is continuous. A weaver's hand, unwrapped, looks nude and startled and wrong. The wrappings are renewed constantly, unspooled from one finger and respun onto another as the weaver works, so that the thread passes *through* the hand on its way from floss to skein — and this is not metaphor. The thread of the warren actually travels across the skin of every weaver in the warren at some point in its making. They are a stage in their own textile. The cloth remembers their hands because their hands were briefly the loom.

They dress in their own cloth, which means they dress in themselves. The garments are loose wraps of the pale green-to-bone thread, layered and overlapped and pinned with small splinters of hardened resin. Over time a weaver's wraps become indistinguishable from the weaver: the cloth absorbs the faint oils of the skin, the skin absorbs the faint dyes of the cloth, and a weaver of middle age presents as a single continuous pale-green surface from scalp to sole, interrupted only by the dark eyes and the working hands. Faces are wrapped against fibre-dust except when eating or sleeping or communicating forehead-to-forehead. Many weavers go whole days with their faces wrapped. Some prefer it. The wrap is not a veil in any social sense; it is simply the natural state of a face that lives in this air.

They eat the pith-floss. This is perhaps the thing an outsider would find hardest to absorb. The same substance that becomes the thread also becomes the food — the younger, softer floss from the highest-growth walls is gathered separately, steeped in the sweet condensation-water until it swells, and eaten as a kind of pale warm porridge that tastes faintly of grass and faintly of pepper and faintly, always, of the resin-lamps burning overhead. They supplement with crickets, ground fine, for the fats and proteins that pure cellulose cannot provide; and with the moth-larvae from the wall galleries, which are rich and buttery and considered a delicacy. They drink the condensation. Their bodies are, chemically speaking, the plant rearranged. A weaver's corpse, left alone, would compost back into the floor of the chamber within a season and be drawn up into the next generation of pith-floss, and be spun, and be worn, and be eaten. This is not a belief they hold. It is simply what happens. The cycle is so short and so total that it does not require theology.

They do have — not religion exactly, but a practice, centered on the ceremonial skeins worked with moth-wing iridescence. These skeins are not used. They are woven and stacked and left. There is a particular alcove, high up near where the stalk's pith narrows into the next chamber above (a boundary none of them have ever crossed, though they know it is there from the change in the hydraulic tone), where the ceremonial skeins accumulate. The alcove is perhaps four centuries deep in skeins now. The outermost skeins are pale bone; the inner ones, if anyone dug down, would still be faintly green. No one digs. The act of adding to the pile is the practice. What the pile *means*, if it means anything, is not a question they have the language to ask — and remember, their language is the pressing of skull to skull, a language that cannot hold abstractions well. You cannot vibrate a metaphysics through a forehead. You can only vibrate a fact: *the loom is off-beat. the floss is ready. i am here. you are here. the thread continues.*

There are perhaps sixty of them. This number has not changed meaningfully in a very long time. A birth is noted by the whole warren through a change in the rhythm of work — one pair of hands briefly withdrawn, one loom briefly slowed — and a death the same way, and the balance is kept without anyone keeping it. Children are not exactly *raised*; they are set down near the smaller carders and allowed to watch, and their hands find the work the way a vine finds a trellis. By the time a child is tall enough to reach the low looms, the child is already weaving. There is no instruction because there is nothing to instruct. The work is the world and the world is the work and a child learns the world by being in it, the way a child anywhere learns to breathe.

They are not unhappy. This needs saying, because an outside description of their lives sounds like a description of something terrible — sealed in, deaf, pale, eating themselves, unable to speak. But happiness and unhappiness are comparative states, and there is nothing here to compare to. A weaver at her loom, fingers wrapped in the thread she is making, feet settled on the warm node of a living wall, the great bass groan of the turbines vibrating steady in her sternum, the quarter-tone chord of the whistles steady in her teeth, her sister two platforms over felt rather than seen, the green dim light steady, the cool damp steady, the work steady, the skein growing under her hands at exactly the rate it has always grown — this weaver is not suffering. This weaver is, in a sense that does not translate out of the warren, *tuned*. She is a string in an instrument that has been playing one long chord for longer than anyone can remember, and she is playing her note correctly, and the chord is still sounding, and this is enough.

It has always been enough.

It is difficult to imagine what would not be enough. It is difficult, inside the warren, to imagine *imagining*. The faculty has atrophied like a muscle unused. What would they imagine? Another chamber? There is no other chamber. A different life? There are no different lives. The stalk is the world and the work is the life and the thread is the thread and the loom comes down, and the loom comes down, and the loom comes down, and somewhere among the slamming and shrieking and the green unchanging light, sixty small pale patient people who do not know they are remarkable continue being exactly what they are, which is the plant, weaving itself.
