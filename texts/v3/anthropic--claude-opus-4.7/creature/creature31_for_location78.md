# Those Who Live at Iselvet

## The Hammermen

They are the oldest profession here and they look it, though not in the ways you'd expect. A hammerman in his fifth decade at the sheds is not bent or scarred so much as *settled* — he has, through the same discipline he applies to bar-stock, hammered himself into permanence. You can tell the senior men by how sharp their outlines are. Younger workers have a faint blur at the edges in certain light, a softness around the jaw or the knuckles that the veterans have long since beaten out of themselves. The oldest hammerman at the Third Terrace sheds, a woman named Brekka whose rhythm is said to be the standard against which the others tune, is reputed to cast a shadow that does not quite match her body — the shadow is of who she was at thirty, and it has been there unchanging for forty years. This is considered an achievement.

They speak in short phrases, on the beat. Long sentences unravel at this altitude. The hammermen have learned not to trust them.

## The Chemists

A different caste. They wear long coats the color of iron oxide and they do not, as a rule, come out of their vat-halls during daylight. The work of argument — of persuading a crucible of half-metal toward the idea of copper, toward the idea of tin — requires a patience that erodes certain human faculties. Chemists at Iselvet lose their sense of taste first, then their ability to recognize faces they haven't seen in over a month, then finally the distinction between what they have said aloud and what they have merely thought with great force. By the time a chemist is a master, she conducts half her conversations telepathically without realizing it, and apprentices learn to answer questions that were never voiced.

They are fed by a guild of cooks who prepare their meals to strict specifications — food that will not drift, food with strong opinions about what it is. Salt-cured things. Things pickled past any ambiguity. A chemist who eats fresh fruit may find, an hour later, that she has forgotten a decade.

## The Cutters

The harness crews who go out on the slopes to quarry the mountain. They are young, mostly, because the work does things to you that accumulate. Cutting unfinished rock requires pressing your will against the stone at the moment of separation — otherwise the block you have sawed free simply re-fuses to the mountain behind you as you turn to haul it. The cutters develop a particular forward-leaning posture and a particular squint. They are superstitious in specific ways: they do not cut on their birthdays, they do not name their saws, and they will not, under any circumstance, cut a block from a place where a goat has recently stood. The goats, they say, leave decisions in their hoofprints, and you do not want your ingot carrying some goat's unresolved opinion down into the foundry.

Cutters often leave the profession in their thirties and become something else — bakers, clerks, watchmen of the empty terraces. They are marked by a certain reluctance to make eye contact with stone walls.

## The Tram Operators

Who live half in Iselvet and half in the cloud layer below. Their cableworks run down through weather no one above sees clearly, and the operators have developed, without quite meaning to, a culture of their own — different songs, different card games, a dialect with vowels borrowed from the cities at the foot of the mountain. They are the only people at Iselvet who regularly see the sun. When they return from a descent run, they bring it with them for an hour or two, in the way their skin holds warmth that the damp has not yet leached out. Children follow them. The older tram operators are sometimes asked, by residents who have never left, to describe sunlight, and they do, patiently, though the descriptions rarely match.

## The Watchers of Empty Terraces

This is not an official guild, but a thing that happens. Every populated terrace has, it turns out, someone who has taken it upon themselves to walk the empty ones above. They check that the doors of uninhabited houses are closed. They note which fixtures are dripping. They shoo the goats out of the refectories — not because the goats cause damage, but because someone, at some point, decided this was a thing that ought to be done. The watchers are generally old. They are generally solitary. They carry lanterns and small notebooks and they make records that no one reads, in a script that sometimes changes between the beginning and end of a page. When a watcher dies, another quietly takes up the route within a week or two. No one arranges this. It simply occurs.

They are, perhaps, the city's antibodies — the mechanism by which Iselvet insists that the empty terraces are *not yet* empty, only waiting. Without them, one suspects, the upper districts would drift faster, the buildings would begin to forget their corners, the wager would begin to tip.

## The Almost-Goats

Worth mentioning as residents, because they are. They live in the streets as much as on the slopes. They bed down at night in the colonnades and warm themselves against the foundry walls. They have figured out cable lifts — will ride them down a terrace or two if the operators don't chase them off, and the operators have mostly stopped chasing them off. The goats are not tame but they are *complicit*. They understand that Iselvet is a place where committing to a form is rewarded, and the older goats have committed harder than the young ones. An old nanny in the Fourth Terrace market has been the same goat, unmistakably, for longer than any goat has a right to be. The cutters salute her when they pass. She does not acknowledge them, but she does not not acknowledge them either.

## The Singers in the Seeps

The salamanders. The thumb-beetles. The mouse-shaped things that sing in thirds and fifths. Collectively, the small fauna of the wet pockets — and they should be counted as residents, because they are doing something. No naturalist has fully catalogued them because the catalogues do not hold: a species described on Tuesday has, by the following spring, subtly shifted its number of toes or the pattern of its markings or the octave of its song. What is consistent is the singing. It happens at dusk and before dawn, and if you sit still in one of the fern-choked seeps below the furnace halls, you will hear what sounds — almost, not quite — like a language being worked out. Syllables proposed and rejected. Chords tried and abandoned. Some of the foundry workers believe the animals are, in their slow amphibian way, the mountain's first attempt at speech, and that the day the salamander's note finally becomes a word will be the day the mountain finishes setting. Other workers find this idea sentimental and laugh at it. But they laugh quietly, and they do not, when walking past the seeps, interrupt.

## The One You Do Not Quite See

There is, finally, a thing I am reluctant to write about but that belongs in any honest accounting. In the empty terraces, particularly on damp evenings when the pewter light is beginning to give out, the watchers sometimes report a figure. Not consistently. Not in the same form. Sometimes a tall thin person in a coat like a chemist's. Sometimes a smaller shape, almost a child. Sometimes only a disturbance in the air at about head-height, a warping like heat off a furnace.

It does not approach. It does not retreat. It seems to be — and this is the watchers' phrase, which they all arrived at independently — *trying to decide what to be*. As though the mountain, having grown this city on its shoulder, is attempting to grow an inhabitant of its own, and has not yet settled on the specifications. The watchers leave it alone. They walk their routes. They note in their notebooks: *figure on the Ninth, near the old bath-house. Unchanged. Unchanged. Slightly taller this week. Unchanged.*

Some of them, the oldest ones, have begun to nod to it as they pass. It does not, yet, nod back.

But the hammermen, if you ask them — and they will only answer on the beat, between strokes — will tell you that fixedness, in the end, is contagious. That anything watched long enough and consistently enough by people who know how to insist, will eventually commit.

This is the wager. These are the ones making it.
