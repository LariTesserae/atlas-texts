# The Tenders

They are not many, and they were not always what they are now.

If you watch one for long enough — and you can; they do not mind being watched, do not really register it as a category of event — you begin to notice that the calm in the face is not quite the calm of a human at rest. A human at rest is between things. These faces are not between anything. They are the expression a face arrives at when it has been the same face for a very long time and has worn smooth, the way the mountains here have worn smooth: not eroded into absence, but rounded into a final shape.

They are humanoid. Roughly. Two arms, two legs, an upright carriage, a head set forward on the shoulders. But the proportions have drifted. The hands are large and the fingers long, with an extra small joint near the tip that lets them curl around a gauge-wheel with the ease of a vine on a trellis. The skin is a dull warm grey, faintly mineral, and where it creases — at the knuckles, at the corners of the mouth, along the throat — it shows a darker seam, as if the body were assembled in plates that have not quite fused. In the lamplight this reads as ornament. In full dark, when you pass one on a stair, you can sometimes see a very faint orange line along those seams, the same orange as the foundry glow, banked low.

They are warm to the touch. Warmer than the brick. If you took the hand of one — and they would let you; they would simply wait, patient, while you did it — you would feel the same slow throb you feel through the soles of your feet, only closer, and you would understand without being told that the engines below and the bodies above are running on the same long stroke.

Their eyes are the strangest part, and the part one stops noticing soonest. They do not have pupils in the way you would expect. The eye is a single dark surface, slightly recessed, that catches the lamplight in a soft uneven way — not a reflection but a kind of low internal answer to the light, as though something behind the eye were considering whether to brighten and had decided, again, not to. They see well in the dark. They see, one suspects, more than they let on, but what they see is so thoroughly the same thing they saw yesterday that the seeing has become a formality, like the ledgers.

---

They eat, in a manner of speaking. In small alcoves cut into the foundry walls, at intervals along the stair-streets, there are basins of a warm pale slurry — the mineral film that the arthropods feed on, but refined, concentrated, drawn up from deeper. A tender will stop at a basin, lower its face to it, and remain there for some minutes. Then it will rise, and the basin will be a little emptier, and the tender will continue on its way. They do not gather to eat. There is no table. The basins are simply where the basins are, and the tenders pass them as work brings them past.

They sleep, perhaps. It is hard to say. Sometimes you find one seated on a walkway with its back against warm brick, hands folded in its lap, eyes closed or nearly closed, and the seams along its arms glowing a fraction brighter than usual, as if something were being topped up. After a while — a few hours, a day — it stands and resumes. Whether this is rest or maintenance or something the language does not have a word for, none of them will explain. They are not withholding. They simply do not seem to have the concept of explaining a thing that is.

---

They speak rarely, and when they do their voices are soft and low and slightly resonant, as though there were a small bell somewhere in the chest. The language is sparse. Nouns for the parts of the foundry — *the upper gauge, the third belt, the south basin* — and a small set of verbs, mostly in a continuous aspect: *is turning, is holding, is sufficient*. There are no tenses for the future. There is a tense for the past, but they use it only for the very deep past, and almost always in the same handful of phrases, which seem to be fragments of something longer that has been lost. *Before the closing.* *When the stacks were warm.* *The ones who left.*

They will say these things if you ask, and they will not say more. Not because they are guarding a secret. Because there is no more. The phrase is the whole of what remains, the way a single tooth in a riverbed is the whole of what remains of an animal.

---

There are also, you come to understand, the others.

You will not meet them often. Deep in the foundries, on levels reached by stairs that descend past the lamplight into a softer orange that comes from the brick itself, there are forms that are no longer ambulatory. They have settled. A torso fused to a wall, hands still resting on the levers they were tending when the settling began, face turned slightly toward a gauge that still reads what it has always read. The seams along these forms glow steadily, the orange a little stronger than in the tenders above. They are not dead. The throb is in them too, slower, deeper, more nearly the throb of the engines themselves. At some point a tender stops walking the stairs and becomes part of the wall it was walking past, and the work it was doing continues to be done, through it, by it, as it.

The tenders above know this is what waits for them. They do not seem to dread it or to long for it. It is simply the next shape, the way the shape they have now was the next shape after whatever they were before. One does not hurry to it. One does not avoid it. One tends the gauge until the gauge and the hand have agreed.

---

And then, very rarely, you will see one that does not fit.

Standing alone on a high walkway, not tending, not moving, looking out across the plateau at the orange smears of the other foundries. The seams along its arms a little dimmer than the others, as though something in it were not quite drawing from the same well. It will stand there for a long time. Hours. A day. The bell will sound somewhere and it will not turn its head. Another tender will pass behind it on the walkway and neither will acknowledge the other.

These are the ones in whom something of *the ones who left* is still faintly active — an old restlessness, a vestige of the orientation that produced the foundries in the first place, back before the system closed. They are not unhappy. They do not have a destination. The plateau in every direction offers the same warm dark and the same distant coals, and they know this, and they look anyway.

Eventually they go back inside. They check a gauge. They touch a lever. The seams brighten a fraction. The shape resumes.

It is from these, the watchers on the walkways, that the bells are sometimes struck — not by a mechanism, as is generally believed, but by a hand, once in a great while, for a reason the hand itself could not name if you asked. The note travels out across the dust. An hour later, from another foundry, another hand answers. Neither hand knows the other. Neither expected a reply. But the reply comes, and the one who struck the first bell stands a moment longer at the railing, and then turns, and goes back down into the warm orange light, where the work that does not need doing is waiting to be done, patiently, for as long as anything is.
