## What Lives There

Begin with the grazers, because they are the easiest to see, and because seeing them properly will teach you how to see the rest.

They move in loose herds of twelve or twenty, and the herds drift along the warm pipelines the way a finger traces a vein. Six legs, yes — but the legs are not arranged as you would expect. The front pair is shorter and set wider, splayed for balance on uneven basalt; the middle pair carries most of the weight; the rear pair is long and slightly backward-jointed, for the climb out of basins. Their feet are pads of horn and soft tissue, broad as dinner plates, and the pads are warm even when the ground is cold, because beneath the horn is a mesh of blood vessels they can flush or shutter at will. They sleep standing, with all six pads pressed flat to a heated pipe, drawing warmth into themselves the way a lizard takes sun.

Their hide is the color of wet slate and shimmers faintly, oily, repelling the iron-smelling rain. The hair, where there is hair, grows only along the spine and in tufts at the joints, and it is fine and black and conducts heat poorly — insulation against the cold basins, where they sometimes have to cross. They have no horns. They have no need of horns. What they have instead are the eyes: enormous, low-set, with horizontal pupils that close to slits in the brighter glow-fields and open to full discs in the dark walks between. The tapetum behind the retina is what gives them their famous orange coins, but in truth the color is not orange — it only reflects the ember-light of the engines and would shine green or blue or nothing at all under a different sky.

They eat the black succulents, mostly, and the bark-flake of the grove-trees when they can reach it, and they drink the condensation that beads on the cooler housings by lipping it directly off the metal. Their tongues are long and dark and slightly rough, and over a lifetime they wear shallow grooves in the housings they favor — so that the oldest grazing-routes are marked, if you know how to look, by faint tongue-polish on certain stones. They live perhaps forty years. They calve once every three or four. The calves are born during the long pause between piston-falls in whatever sheltered seam the herd has chosen, and they can stand within an hour, walk within a day, and find their own warm pad to sleep against within a week. They are called nothing. No one has ever named them.

The pack hunters are smaller than the grazers but not by much — shoulder-high to a person who isn't there. They are also six-legged, but their build is wholly different: narrow, deep-chested, with legs that fold close and a spine that flexes through the run. Their coats are the color of cold basalt, and in the dark they are nearly invisible until they move, and when they move they are committed. A pack will be six to ten. They hunt by timing.

This is the thing that is most uncanny about them, and the thing that has shaped them most. They know the engines. Not as we know things — they cannot tell you a piston is a piston — but they know, in the way a tide-pool crab knows the moon, that certain shapes in the rock will produce a deep concussion at certain intervals, and that an animal whose ears are still ringing from that concussion cannot hear a hunter's rush. So they wait. A pack will lie up against the lee side of a great housing for hours, watching a herd graze in the basin below, doing nothing, until the piston falls — and in that long underground heartbeat, while every grazer in the basin has its head jerked momentarily up by the body-felt thud, the pack is already halfway across the open ground, silent, because their footfalls are inside the fall. By the time the grazers' senses settle, one of them is down.

The packs have a kind of culture, if culture is the word. Different packs prefer different engines. A young hunter, dispersing from its birth-group, will spend a season wandering and listening before it chooses an engine to attach itself to, and the engine it chooses will shape its hunting for the rest of its life — a long-cycle piston gives you patient, fasting hunters; a fast-cycle valve releaser, every twenty minutes or so, gives you opportunists who eat smaller prey more often. The pack inherits the rhythm of its engine, and when its engine stops, as engines sometimes do, the pack starves or scatters or, very rarely, finds another engine and learns it, and the learning takes years.

In the gear-teeth and on the slow-turning flywheels live the climbers. These are smaller — cat-sized, some of them, others no larger than a fist. They have hooked claws and prehensile tails and a habit of going utterly still when the wheel they are clinging to begins its slow revolution, riding it up and over and down again, foraging for the moths that lay eggs in the warm seams. The largest of them, a long-bodied thing with rust-colored fur on its underside and ash-gray above, makes its nest deep in the spaces between gear-teeth, where the meshing is so slow that a generation of climbers may be born and weaned and dispersed in the time it takes for the teeth to fully engage and disengage once. They mate, raise young, and leave — and behind them, the gears continue to close, and the nest, abandoned, is crushed slowly to felt over the course of a century.

The moths are everything's food, and they are also, in their way, the plateau's clock. The pale moth lays only on metal above a certain temperature; cooling engines lose their moths first, and the loss of moths is the first sign, to the things that watch for such signs, that a region is dying. A grazer herd that notices the moths thinning in a basin will drift out of it within a season. The pack hunters, who eat moths themselves between large kills, will follow. The climbers may stay longer, because their needs are smaller and a residual warmth lingers in dead engines for years, but eventually they too will go. By the time a basin is fully cold, it holds only the burrowers, who do not need the warmth, and the lichens, which die where the metal cools below their tolerance and live where it does not, mapping out the slow retreat in pale crusts.

The burrowers I have left for last because they are the strangest. They tunnel in the tailings around the vents, yes, and they look at first glance like any blind digging thing — pale, low-slung, six small legs, snout like a wet pad of leather. But they are not eating roots, because there are no roots to eat. What they are doing in their tunnels is less clear. They seem to be eating the tailings themselves: the fine grit that the engines extrude from certain vents, a powder of metal and stone and something else, something that smells faintly sweet. They process it through long winding guts and leave it behind them as a finer, paler grit, and over generations the burrowers of a vent-field will sift the tailings down to a particular consistency, and the consistency seems to matter to the engines, which extrude more freely into well-sifted ground. Whether the engines need the burrowers, or merely tolerate them, or have somehow grown to expect them across some span of time we cannot guess at — this cannot be answered. The burrowers do not know. The engines do not say.

And the night-bird with the flute-voice — there is only one kind of bird here, and it is not really a bird, but it flies and it sings, and that is enough for the word. It is small, no bigger than a thrush, with a body covered in dark scales rather than feathers and four wings arranged in two pairs, fore and aft, that beat out of phase. It does not flock. Each one holds a territory of several square kilometers, and at dusk — though dusk here is only a deepening of the always-dark, signaled by a softening of certain glows on a long slow cycle no one has measured — each bird sings from a high perch, a single descending phrase of three or four notes, very pure, very far-carrying. The phrase differs between individuals. Some have been heard singing the same phrase for what must be decades. They eat moths in the air, taking them on the wing with a small audible click, and they nest once a lifetime, in the cooled seam of a long-dead valve, and they raise one chick, and the chick inherits the territory but not the song.

All of these things live together without knowing they live together. The grazer does not know that its tongue-polish on a housing is a sign read by the hunter. The hunter does not know that the piston which masks its rush also dries the basin which produces the succulents which feed the grazer. The burrower does not know that the moths above it are eaten by a bird that will never descend into its tunnel. The lichen does not know anything at all.

And yet the whole thing holds. Each creature has draped itself across the engines' purpose as the engines drape themselves across the rock, weathered in, fitted, and the fit is so old now that no part of it can be lifted out without tearing. When an engine dies and a basin cools, what leaves is not just the warmth; it is an entire knit of lives, each of which has spent a thousand generations becoming exactly suited to that warmth and no other. They move on if they can. Many cannot. The pale crust of dying lichen, the empty tongue-grooves on the cold housings, the abandoned gear-nest crushing slowly to nothing — these are the plateau's only memorials, and they are sufficient, because there is no one to read them.

Except, perhaps, you. If you stayed long enough. If you learned to walk in the warm dark by the pulse of the ground, and to know the basins by their breath, and to recognize, by the second night, the particular three-note phrase of the bird that holds the territory you have wandered into — you would begin to be readable, too. The grazers would learn your shape and not bolt. The hunters would learn your rhythm and decide, one way or the other, what you were. The moths would land on you, because you would be warm. And in time you would become another small fitted thing in the long warm working dark, and the engines would turn beneath you, and you would no longer ask what they were for, because the question would have stopped meaning anything, the way it has stopped meaning anything to everything else that lives here.
