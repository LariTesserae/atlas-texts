## The Inhabitants

Begin with the circler. You watched it a long time and it did not descend, and this is the first thing to understand: it does not descend because it cannot. Not won't — *cannot*. It has forgotten how, or its lineage has, across a span of time long enough that the forgetting is now structural. Its bones, if you could call them bones, are hollow in a way that goes past hollow into something like *intentionally absent* — a scaffolding of mineralized air. To land would be to break. To break would be to end. So it rides.

It is wider than you guessed. From the ground it read as hawk-sized; in truth its wingspan would shadow a small house, but the wings are so thin, so nearly transparent, that distance erases them. The body proper is no bigger than a cat. The rest is sail. The sail is grown, not feathered — a single continuous membrane stretched on cartilage that branches like the veins of a leaf, and the membrane itself does what the fronds do, drinking light, condensing the high cold air's reluctant moisture against its inner surface. The flier is a frond that learned to leave the ground. Or the fronds are fliers that learned to stop. The kinship is real; you would see it if you could examine the tissues. Same translucent walls. Same held inner sea.

It eats — when it eats — by descending in long shallow spirals to skim just above the rosettes and draw, through a proboscis it unfurls only at the moment of feeding, a single bead of fluid from the tip of a chosen spire. The spire, afterward, is not damaged. The frond seems to *offer* the droplet, the way certain flowers offer nectar, and what passes between them is older than any contract you know how to read. The flier carries pollen, or something pollen-like, between distant companies of fronds that would otherwise never meet across the sterile pale stone. One flier. Ten thousand square kilometers. A reproductive system stretched so thin it should not work, and yet has worked, for however long this place has been itself.

It does not sleep. It cannot. To sleep it would have to land. Instead, one hemisphere of its small bright mind dims while the other steers, and the steering in the dim phase is mostly the thermal doing the work — the plateau breathes warm columns upward all day, and at night the residual heat of the stone keeps a few last columns alive until dawn. In the worst hours, just before sunrise, the flier sinks lower than it likes. You can sometimes, if you are out before light, see it pass close enough overhead to make out the slow patient blink of an eye the size of a coin.

There is no mate for it. There has not been a mate for it in a long time. Whether this is the end of something or whether its kind reproduces by means you have no framework for — a budding, a fissioning, a slow division across decades — you cannot tell. The circler is alone in a way that does not seem to trouble it, because trouble is a thing for creatures with the option of arriving somewhere.

The thumb-sized grazers in the shade are a different proposition. They are many. Hundreds beneath every large rosette, thousands across the plateau, and they are not, when you finally see one clearly, quite what you thought. The six legs are right. The translucent body is right. But there is a structure inside the body, visible through the walls, that resembles nothing so much as a tiny version of the frond above them — a rosette of internal blades arrayed around a central spire. They are walking fronds. Or they are the seed-stage of fronds, the mobile phase, the part of the life cycle that gets to choose its ground before settling. Some of the shed husks you find are not death but metamorphosis: a grazer that has found a patch of stone it approves of, climbed down off its legs, and begun the slow business of becoming stationary. The husks are not corpses. They are *molts*. Somewhere nearby, if you knew where to look, a new young frond would be unfurling its first blade out of what used to be a creature.

This rewrites the plateau. The fronds are not strangers scattered across the stone; they are settled grazers, each one having chosen, once, where to spend its centuries. The bare ground between companies is not empty — it is where the current generation of mobile young is moving, slowly, invisibly, evaluating. The whole landscape is in extremely slow migration. Over ten thousand years, the companies drift. The dead-glass spires mark where the ancestors stopped.

And then there is the third thing.

You have not seen it. You will not, unless you stay much longer than you intend, and even then probably not. But it is here, and the evidence is in the negative space — in the fact that the grazers move only when unwatched, in the fact that the husks are sometimes found in arrangements that are *almost* too regular, in the way the high flier occasionally breaks its circle for no thermal reason and dips, briefly, toward a particular patch of stone before resuming.

There is a watcher.

Not many. Perhaps a handful across the whole plateau. Perhaps one. It is built of the same materials as everything else here — quartz-pale, glass-veined, water-hoarding — but it is large, and it is patient past the point where patience is a virtue and into the point where it is a metabolism. It moves so slowly that its motion is invisible on any human timescale; you could sit beside one for a day and not know. Its body is low and broad and lobed, fitting into the rounded shoulders of the stone the way a hand fits a held thing, and from above, from the flier's vantage, it would look like just another swell of pale rock with a vein of glass through it. From the ground, the same. It is the plateau's own shape, refined into a creature.

It does not hunt. There is nothing to hunt that it would want; the grazers are small and the flier is unreachable and the fronds are kin. What it does, as best as can be guessed from the slight arrangements of husks and the small adjustments in the spacing of companies over centuries, is *tend*. It is a gardener whose garden is the entire highland, and its gardening is so slow that the garden cannot tell it is being gardened. A husk nudged a hand's-breadth east, once a decade. A young mobile frond steered, by some pheromonal suggestion, toward a more favorable patch of stone. The dead-glass spires positioned, over millennia, into the long low gleaming veins that catch the sun and throw it sideways — those veins are not random. Sit on a high swell at the right hour and you will see that the gleams form lines, and the lines converge, very gently, on points where new companies of fronds are densest. The plateau is being lensed. The light is being farmed back into itself. Someone is doing this, and has been doing it long enough that the doing has become indistinguishable from geology.

The watcher drinks light too, of course. Everything here does. But it drinks slowly, and it has, across its long life, accumulated a surplus — a deep internal sea, pressurized, jewel-bright, that it will never need to spend on anything as urgent as growth or reproduction or movement. It is full. It has been full for a long time. What does a creature do, when it is full and slow and alone on a plateau the size of a continent under a sky the color of held breath?

It listens. The rare clean tones the wind draws from the spires — minutes apart, never gathering into music — are not, to the watcher, rare. On the watcher's timescale they arrive in dense succession, a constant fine chiming, a music the plateau makes for the one ear slow enough to hear it as music. The watcher is the audience this place has grown for itself.

You will leave. You were never the right kind of creature, and the change the light would work on you would take longer than you have. But before you go, sit still on a high swell for an hour, and watch the slow pulse of the fronds dimming and brightening under the cloud-shadows, and listen for the next clean tone, and understand that you are inside a held composition that has three performers and one listener, and that you are, briefly, a fourth — uninvited, unobjected to, and about to be forgotten the moment you descend.

The flier completes another circle.

The watcher, somewhere, has not yet finished the gesture it began at your arrival, and will not finish it for some time after you are gone.
