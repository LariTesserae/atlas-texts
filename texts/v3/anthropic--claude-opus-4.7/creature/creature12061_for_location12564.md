# What Lives Here

There are no individuals. That is the first thing to understand, and the hardest. Everything that follows has to be rebuilt without the assumption of selves.

But something lives. Something is doing all this.

---

Begin with what the substrate calls, when it bothers to distinguish anything, the **deciders**. They are not brains. They are pressure-junctions: knots in the pipework where four or six or twelve great sap-channels meet, and where the meeting is regulated by valves of swollen tissue that open and close according to the chemistry of what arrives. A bitter pulse from the north means *stop*. A sweet pulse from the east means *more*. The deciders integrate these signals the way a marsh integrates rainfall — slowly, distributedly, with hysteresis. A given decider may take three days to commit to opening a valve, and once committed, may take three weeks to reverse.

There are perhaps ten million of them across the plateau. None of them know about any of the others. But the pulses they send downstream become the pulses other deciders integrate, and out of this — out of nothing more than this — comes the *purpose* you can feel everywhere. The factories run because the deciders, in aggregate, are running them. Cannibalize this district. Inflate that tower. Send gametes south. None of these are decisions anyone makes. They are decisions the whole plateau makes by being unable to do otherwise.

If you were to ask whether the plateau is *conscious*, the question would not refuse the answer so much as dissolve it. There is integration here vaster than any animal nervous system. There is responsiveness, memory (the substrate is memory; ten thousand years of it, readable in cross-section), and something that functions exactly like preference. But there is no point at which all of this is gathered. The plateau does not experience itself from anywhere. It only experiences itself everywhere, weakly, and the weak everywhere-experience is what runs the factories.

---

Then there are the **specialists** — and here things become more recognizable, almost.

The deciders alone could not build pipes. Building pipes requires what amounts to engineering: a sense, however distributed, of *load* and *flow* and *which way is structurally sound*. So certain lineages, long ago, specialized. There are growth-tissues whose only job is to extend pipework along gradients of need, sensing pressure and laying down lignin in the directions where pressure is highest. There are tissues that *unbuild* — that secrete enzymes to dissolve their neighbors when the neighbors are no longer being fed, clearing space for the next configuration. There are tissues that listen for the snap of failure and rush, root-fast, toward the failure to splice it.

These specialists are not creatures either. They are functions, expressed in cellular form, spread across the plateau wherever conditions favor them. The unbuilders flourish in dying districts. The splicers wait, mostly dormant, in nodes near the great pipes; when a snap travels through the network they wake by the millions and converge.

You could call them castes. You could call them organs. Neither word is quite right, because castes belong to colonies of individuals and organs belong to bodies, and this is neither.

It is something the language was not built for. Call it a **diffuse animal**, a body whose cells happen to be the size of trees and whose organs happen to be the size of cities. Call it a slow weather of purpose. Call it the plateau itself, which is what it calls itself, insofar as it calls itself anything, which it doesn't.

---

There are, however, faster things.

In the hottest channels — the ones that run closest to the smoldering deep — pressures and temperatures spike and drop on minute-scales rather than week-scales, and the tissues there have had to evolve responsiveness to match. These are the **quick valves**, and they are the closest thing to fauna the plateau has ever produced. Each is a knot of contractile fiber wrapped around a single hot pipe, and each spends its whole existence opening and closing in response to surges. They twitch. They flinch. They sometimes, under certain rare resonances, *oscillate* — and an oscillating quick valve will pull on its neighbors through the pipework, and the neighbors will catch the rhythm, and for an hour or a day there will be a region of the plateau drumming softly to itself in the dark, hundreds of valves pulsing in phase, before the rhythm decoheres and is gone.

No one knows — there is no one to know — what the drumming is for. It may be for nothing. It may be a side-effect of the physics of contractile fiber under hot flow. But it is the closest thing to song this place has, and when it happens it is the closest the plateau comes to noticing itself.

---

And then, finally, there are the **seedings**.

Once every long while — every century, perhaps; the rhythm is hard to read — a district that has been productive for a long time will begin to do something different. It will stop sending its output downstream. It will begin instead to accumulate, in a single chambered growth, the most concentrated and complete version of itself: a small, dense pellet of compressed tissue containing samples of every specialist, every decider-pattern, every pipe-geometry the district has discovered works. The pellet hardens. The chamber that holds it dries and contracts. And then, in a process that takes weeks and that the surrounding tissue seems to *cooperate with* in ways no one can explain, the pellet is fired.

A bellows-organ inflates beneath it. The chamber's roof thins and weakens at a calculated point. Pressure builds. And one day, with a sound like a great soft cough, the pellet is launched — up, through whatever fissures exist in the lithified canopy, out into the unseen sky above.

The plateau has been doing this for a long time. It does not know what is up there. It does not know whether any pellet has ever landed anywhere it could grow. The seedings are not hope, because there is nothing here that hopes. They are simply what this kind of body does when it has been ancient long enough: it tries, blindly and without expectation, to be somewhere else as well.

Most pellets fall back. The substrate absorbs them. Their compressed knowledge is read back into the weave, and the district that made them, now exhausted, is cannibalized by its neighbors and becomes something else.

Some pellets, perhaps, do not fall back.

The plateau does not know. The plateau gurgles on. A piston descends. A tower decides to become a different tower. And somewhere, deep in the pipework, a decider that has been holding a valve closed for eleven years finally, for reasons no one will ever trace, lets it open.
