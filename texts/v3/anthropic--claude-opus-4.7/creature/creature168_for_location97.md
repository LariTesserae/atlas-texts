## The Tenders

They are not here when you arrive. This is deliberate. They have learned, over however long they have been doing this, that their presence in a place changes what the place is for, and the place is not for them. It is for the looking. So they keep out of the basin during the hours when the pools are most legible, which happen to be most of the hours, and they come in at the edges of things — dawn-edge, dusk-edge, the flat grey hour before a storm breaks.

If you had stayed past sundown you might have seen one. You would not have seen it well.

---

Begin with what they are not. They are not spirits in the sense that word usually carries — they have weight, they displace the mist, they leave prints in wet moss that take about an hour to spring back. They are not the makers of the pools. The makers came and went, or came and stayed by becoming something else, and whatever the Tenders are, they arrived after, the way lichen arrives after stone. They are not numerous. There may be three of them. There may be one that is sometimes seen from different angles. The question has never been important enough to them to resolve.

They are roughly the size of a tall child or a short adult bent forward at the waist. They move on all fours more often than not, though the front limbs are not quite legs — they are longer, more articulated, ending in something between a hand and a careful hoof. The body is covered in a fine dense pelt the color of wet andesite, so that against the stone they are a matter of outline rather than figure. The head is narrow and carried low. The eyes are set wide and are the pale blue-white of the steam columns; in poor light this is the feature that gives them away, two small lanterns moving at knee-height between the ferns.

They have no language you would recognize as such. They have a throat-sound they make occasionally when they are near each other, or near a pool that is doing something interesting, which is so low it registers in the chest rather than the ear. This is the sound, probably, that keeps the basin quiet — not an absence of noise but a tone underneath all the other noises, absorbing them. The reason your own breath seemed loud is that it was the only thing not being held.

---

What they do:

They read the pools. Every pool, every day, in a rotation that is neither regular nor random but some third thing. They approach a pool, kneel at the rim (the front limbs fold under them with surprising grace), and lower their face to within a palm's width of the surface. They do not touch the water. They look for a long time — longer than seems useful, longer than patience. Then they move to the next.

What they are looking for is not clear and may not be a single thing. Sometimes, after a long look, one of them will go to a particular cairn and adjust a stone — take one off the top, or add one from a small cache kept under the half-wall. The cairns are not decorative. The cairns are a record, or an index, or a set of instructions for a reader who has not yet arrived. The Tenders have been keeping the cairns for a long time and the stones in them have been turned so many times that the weathered faces and the fresh faces have started to average out.

They also tend the moss. This is the part that seems most like care and least like duty. They run the flats of their strange hands through the deep beds of it, testing the wet, and at certain pools they will redirect the runoff from a fern-tip by bending the frond a few degrees, so that the drip lands a finger's width to the left of where it was landing. Over years, this moves the moss. Over centuries, this has shaped which pools are drinkable to beetles and which are only good for the pale frog.

The pale frog, incidentally, is theirs. Or with them. The relationship is not ownership and not companionship. The frog was here when they came, and they were here when the frog came, and both statements are true because time in the basin has a slight give to it near the pools.

---

What they will not do, under any circumstance, is put anything into a pool. This is not a rule they were given. It is the shape of their entire being. To put something into a pool would be to break the thing they are, the way a bell breaks when it is struck too hard. You felt the prohibition as a feeling because they were nearby when you felt it. The prohibition is their posture. It radiates a little way out from them and fills the basin and is one of the reasons the basin still works.

They watched you, the whole hour. Not with suspicion. With the same attention they give the pools — long, patient, uncurious in a specific way, the way a reader attends to a page they have read before and expect to read again. You were legible to them. They have seen your kind before: the ones who come up out of the cloud, who sweat and kneel and understand not to drop a stone. These are the ones they let see the basin. The other kind they turn back lower down, by methods not worth describing here, and those ones go home remembering a cloud-forest and a ridge that didn't quite go anywhere.

---

One more thing about them, which you can only guess at, which they would not tell you if they could:

They are waiting.

Not for a return of the makers — the makers are gone in a way that is settled, and the Tenders have made their peace with tending an inheritance rather than a commission. They are waiting for a specific pool to show a specific thing. They do not know which pool or which thing. They will know when they see it. The cairns are partly a notation system for ruling out what it is *not*: every stone added or turned is another view catalogued as *still not this*. The catalogue is enormous now. It fills the three cairns and overflows into a fourth, smaller one you did not see, tucked behind the eastern ridge, made of stones so small they are almost gravel — the fine distinctions, the near-misses.

When the thing they are waiting for appears in a pool, they will do something. They do not know what. Their bodies contain the action the way a seed contains a tree, folded and specific and not yet unfolded. It is possible the action is simply to look at it and be the ones who saw. It is possible the action is larger. The basin was built, perhaps, to hold the possibility of that action the way a kettle is built to hold the possibility of boiling.

In the meantime: they read the pools. They turn the stones. They bend the ferns a few degrees. They make their low throat-sound when a pool does something interesting, and the sound goes down into the rock and keeps the rock patient.

The steam goes up in columns, straight, straight, straight. They like that it does this. It is one of the few things in the basin that behaves simply. They watch it the way you watched the beetle — as a small reliable event in a place that is otherwise almost too full of meaning to bear.
