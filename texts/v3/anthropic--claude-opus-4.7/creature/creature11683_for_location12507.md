## The Inhabitants

### The Varnish

Call them the varnish, because that is what they look like and because they would not object to any name. They are single cells, but the word *single* misleads — each patch is a confederation, ten million individuals thick where it is thickest, perhaps twenty thick at the fading edges, and the confederation is older and more continuous than any of its members. A cell divides; the daughter inherits its place in the sheet. Nothing migrates. Nothing competes for territory because territory was settled before any currently living cell was born.

They are ochre because they carry a pigment that does two things at once: it absorbs a sliver of the wall's strange light, and it dumps the absorbed energy as a very slight warmth into the water film above them. They do not photosynthesize in any sense that would be familiar. They eat dissolved minerals from the bead-water — trace things, lithium, a little boron, vanishingly small amounts of carbon that arrive somehow, perhaps from the rock's own slow exhalations. Their metabolism runs at a rate that would seem, to a bacterium from a pond, almost catatonic. A generation takes a month. They are not in a hurry. The room is not going anywhere.

What they want, if want is the word: to remain wet, to remain warm, to remain in the corners where the light is filtered through more water and is gentler. They have arrived at exactly the population the chamber can carry indefinitely, and they got there without ever overshooting, which is unusual enough to be worth noticing. Either they are very old as a lineage and have learned restraint, or the room taught them, or — most likely — the room admitted only the kind of life that would fit, and the rest never colonized at all.

They do not think. But twelve patches of them, distributed around a chamber, respiring in slow synchrony with the light's tidal pulse, constitute something that an observer with patience might mistake for an attention. It is not an attention. It is a rhythm. The distinction may not matter.

### The Drinker

The arthropod is harder to place. It has too many legs for an insect and the wrong joints for a crustacean and the wrong eyes — six of them, in two staggered rows, milky and probably not for forming images — for anything you would find in a field guide. It is pale because there has been no reason, in however many generations, to be any other color. Its cuticle is thin and almost translucent; you can see, when it pauses at a water bead, a slow dark movement inside it that must be its gut, or its heart, or something doing the work of both.

There are, in the whole chamber, perhaps forty of them. You will see five or six during your hour. They live in the seams, where the rock is coolest and the humidity highest, in chambers no larger than a pea, and they emerge to drink because their bodies cannot quite hold water against the heat, and they eat the varnish, very occasionally, in quantities so small that the patches do not register the loss. A single drinker, over its life, will consume perhaps a thumbnail's worth of varnish — less than the varnish grows back in a week.

They lay one egg at a time, and not often. They die, when they die, by simply not emerging again; the seam keeps them. Whether the room reabsorbs them, whether their minerals return to the wall, whether they are in some sense the wall's slow inventory of itself — none of this is decidable from where you stand. You have watched one drink. That is most of what can be said.

The drinker has no name for the room and no concept of *outside*. The fissure that admitted you is, to it, a part of the cosmos as fixed as the pool. It has never encountered anything as large as your hand. When you moved, earlier, it did not flee, because flight is not in its repertoire; nothing large has ever happened to it before, and evolution has not bothered to prepare it. It simply waited until you were still again, and then drank.

### The Pool

The pool is not alive in any sense you would defend in conversation. But it is not nothing, either. Something is dissolved in it that is not quite a chemistry — long molecules, or perhaps short ones in long-lived configurations, that the water holds in suspension and that the walls keep replenishing. If you had a microscope you would see them as faint coils, drifting, occasionally linking and unlinking. They are below the threshold of cellularity. They are above the threshold of mere solute. They do something — they catalyze the cycling of minerals between water and stone, they may be what keeps the bead-water from going stagnant, they may be the room's way of distributing whatever signal coordinates the light's slow pulse with the wall's slow sweat.

If you had stayed a year, you might have begun to suspect that the pool was the chamber's central organ, and that the walls and the varnish and the drinkers were all, in some functional sense, peripheral to it. If you had stayed ten years you might have suspected the reverse. Both suspicions would be partially right and the synthesis is not available to you.

### The Wall

And the wall itself. This is the hardest one.

The wall is not alive. The wall is also not not alive. At the scale you can see, it is rock — young, sharp-edged, faintly crystalline, sweating. At the scale just below seeing, in the regularity your eye keeps reaching for and not quite catching, it is doing the work of an organism without being one. Heat moves through it on channels. Water moves through it on channels. Light is generated somewhere in its substance by a process that is probably some cousin of phosphorescence, fed by slow chemical gradients that the rock itself maintains by being, very gradually, in the process of forming. The rock is precipitating out of something — out of the hill, out of the deep water table, out of a chemistry that the surface world does not run — and the precipitation itself releases the energy that lights the chamber and warms it and drives the water cycle.

The room is, in this sense, a flame. A very slow, very cold flame, by the standards of fire. It is burning the difference between two states of matter, and the chamber is what the flame looks like from inside.

The varnish lives on the flame. The drinkers live on the varnish and the flame's exhaled water. You, briefly, lived on its warmth and its light, and it accommodated you because you took nothing from the gradient — your presence cost it perhaps a few hours of growth, redistributed slightly, paid out of reserves the room did not need.

The wall is the tender that is not a tender. It tends because tending is what its physics does. There is no intention in it any more than there is intention in a snowflake's six points, and yet, like the snowflake, it has arrived at a form that looks designed because the rules underneath it were so consistent that design was the only outcome available.

### Who lives there

So: a varnish that is almost a mind if you squint, an animal that is almost not an animal, a pool that is almost a chemistry, and a wall that is almost a process. None of them, alone, would survive an afternoon outside. Together they constitute the room, and the room constitutes them, and the question of who lives there resolves, if you let it, into the recognition that the room *is* the living thing and the rest are its tissues.

It is one organism. It is very young. It is the size of a small chapel. It is doing whatever it is doing at a pace that means your hour inside it was, for it, less than a heartbeat — and your memory of it, carried in you for years, is the longest attention it has ever received, and it does not know, and would not be changed if it did.
