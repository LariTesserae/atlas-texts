# Those Who Keep the Bellworks

You will not meet them on your first visit. Possibly not on your second. But they are here, and the evidence of them is everywhere you look, once you know how to look.

---

There are, at any given time, somewhere between four and nine of them. The number drifts. It has drifted in this range for as long as anyone outside the basin has bothered to ask, which is not often. They do not call themselves anything in particular when speaking among themselves, because there is no one else nearby to need distinguishing from. When they must give a name to outsiders — the rare bell-buyer, the rarer tax assessor — they say *the founders*, using the word in its old sense, the people who work a foundry. It is also true in the other sense. Their predecessors founded this place. They have not left it.

They are small people, most of them, and they get smaller with age rather than stooped — a compaction rather than a collapse, as though the damp has been pressing them gently downward for decades and they have accepted the pressure the way the brick has accepted the fig. Their skin takes on, over a lifetime in this basin, a faint greenish cast at the temples and the backs of the hands, which is not pigment but a very thin persistent film of algae that grows on anyone who stays long enough and washes rarely enough. Newcomers — and there have been newcomers, though not in this generation — lose the film in a week of dry travel. The founders never go anywhere dry.

Their hair, when they have it, is the color of the bog oak tailstock: a black so saturated with age and moisture that it reads as brown only in direct light. They wear it long and bound up out of the way of the belts. One of them, the oldest, has hair to her knees and keeps it in a single thick braid wrapped twice around her head and pinned with a bronze bell-clapper whose bell was lost so long ago that no one remembers what note it rang.

Their clothes are leather and waxed canvas and a coarse linen they weave themselves from flax grown in a clearing upstream. Everything they wear is some shade of brown or green, not by choice but by inevitability — the iron silt stains anything pale within a week, and they gave up fighting this around the time their great-grandparents did. The leather is oiled with the same oil that goes on the journal bearings, which is rendered from something they will not discuss with visitors. It smells faintly sweet and faintly wrong and keeps the wet out beautifully.

They move slowly. This is the first thing a visitor would notice, if a visitor ever saw them, and it is the thing that most distinguishes them from people elsewhere. They have calibrated their pace to the pace of the place. A founder crossing the covered walkway from the mill house to the drying-shed takes perhaps four minutes to do it, where you or I would take thirty seconds. They are not dawdling. They are simply moving at the rate at which things here need to be moved at. If you watched one of them pour molten bronze into a sand mold you would see that the pour itself happens at ordinary speed — bronze does not wait — but everything on either side of the pour, the walking-to and the walking-from, the setting-down of the crucible, the wiping of the brow, happens in this other tempo, this fig-root tempo, and the pour itself is the only fast thing in their day.

They speak rarely and in a register so low that the mill-thrum nearly covers it. They have developed, over generations, a vocabulary of small gestures for passing information at a distance without raising the voice: a particular tilt of the wrist means *the belt on the number three lathe is slipping*, a particular fold of the fingers means *I will be at the foundry for the next hour*, a hand laid flat against a brick wall means *listen*. Listen to what, the outsider could not tell you. They can.

They tune the bells by ear, and their ears are extraordinary — not because they were born with extraordinary ears but because they have spent their entire lives inside a continuous chord of seasoning bronze and have learned to hear individual bells within it the way a shepherd knows individual sheep in a flock. A founder tuning a new bell on the bog-oak lathe will pause, strike the bell with a small hammer, listen — and not to the bell alone, but to the bell *against* the chord of the thousands hanging in the eaves. The new bell must take its place in the chord. If it does not, she files a little more bronze from the inside of the rim and listens again. A bell may take a full day to tune. There is no hurry. There has never been a hurry.

They eat what the basin gives them and a little of what comes up the stream from the south in flat-bottomed boats twice a year: salt, flour, iron stock for the foundry, small tools. The watercress, the mint, the fish from the braided streams, the eggs of a particular duck that nests in the reed-beds and which they do not hunt but whose clutches they partially harvest on a rotation so old it is no longer thought of as a rotation but simply as what one does in the sixth week after the spring floods. They keep no livestock because livestock are loud and disturb the chord. The cat you heard is not theirs; the cat belongs to itself and has for many cat-generations. They feed it scraps. It tolerates them.

They do not have children often. When they do, the children are raised collectively in the warden's quarters above the covered walkway, and they learn to walk at founder-pace before they learn to walk at any other, and some of them, as adolescents, leave down the stream in the flat-bottomed boats and are not heard from again, and some stay, and the ones who stay become founders in their turn. There is no ceremony for this. One day a person is a young person who helps with the bells, and one day, years later, someone notices that this person is now the one teaching a still-younger person how to hold the tuning hammer, and that is the transition. It is not marked. Nothing here is marked. Marking implies a before and an after, and the basin prefers duration.

They are not unhappy. This should be said, because the description might suggest otherwise. They are, in fact, among the most deeply settled people you could ever fail to meet — settled in the sense that a stone in a streambed is settled, having found after long tumbling the exact hollow that fits it. They laugh, quietly. They have small jokes among themselves, most of which turn on the behavior of particular bells or particular fig roots or particular recurring leaks in particular roofs. Their humor is almost entirely about things that have been going on for longer than any of them has been alive and will go on after all of them are gone. This is, if you think about it, a deeply consoling form of humor. It implies that the joke will outlast the teller, which means the teller is part of something that outlasts her, which is the only kind of comfort this place offers, and it offers it abundantly.

They know they are the last, probably. There are fewer of them than there were a century ago, and fewer a century ago than the century before. The bells still sell — there is a narrow continuous demand from temples and ships and certain old houses downriver — but the demand is not growing, and the founders are not, on the whole, reproducing at replacement rate, and they are aware of this without being troubled by it in any way a visitor would recognize as being troubled. They have made, collectively and without ever discussing it, a kind of peace with the idea that the Bellworks will one day stop. They have also made peace with the idea that it might not — that one of the young ones who left down the stream might come back, or that a stranger might arrive with the right sort of patience and be slowly, over decades, absorbed. Both outcomes are acceptable. Neither is pursued.

In the meantime, they oil the bearings. They replace the belts. They drive the small wedges into the new cracks. They pour the bronze, and tune the bells, and hang them in the eaves to season, and the chord grows by a bell or two each week and loses a bell or two to sale, and the net change across a year is close enough to zero that zero is a reasonable approximation.

If you came back in a decade and looked carefully, you might notice that the oldest founder's braid is now white rather than bog-oak black, and that there is a new founder, small and green at the temples, who was not here before. You would not be able to tell where she came from. She would not tell you. She would be busy listening to a bell.
