# The Tenders

They are shorter than you'd expect, and this is the first thing — a population whose median height has settled, over the long generations, to something like five feet, because the terraced houses were built for it and the catwalks along the pipes were built for it and the low stone doorways of the pump-cathedrals were built for it, and the body has done what bodies do, which is to find the shape of its work. They are broad through the chest. They breathe thin air without noticing they are breathing it. A lowland visitor — there have been a few, historically — gasps on the third step up from any landing, and the Tenders pause and wait, without comment, the way you might wait for a kettle.

Their skin is the color of wet slate or of pale slate or of the basalt itself, depending on the valley; there is some variation, family to family, town to town, but it tends toward the cool end of the spectrum, and in the amber turbine-light at evening they look warmer than they are. Their hair is dark and slow-growing and uniformly straight, and they wear it long and braided, both the men and the women, because loose hair catches in the smaller gear-housings and this has been understood for a long time. Their eyes are large and the irises are pale — grey, pale green, the occasional startling near-white — and the pupils are wide even at noon, because noon here is not very bright. They blink less often than you do. This is one of the things that unnerves visitors.

Their clothing is wool over wool over wool, felted and oiled, in the natural colors of the sheep they don't have — because, remember, there are no animals, and yet the wool exists, and no one in any town will quite explain where it comes from, and the question is considered slightly impolite, the way questions about the Loom's purpose are considered slightly impolite, not forbidden but simply not productive. The outer layer is always dark: black, deep brown, the blue-black of the wet slate roofs. The inner layers may be brighter, and on festival days (there are festival days, though they do not correspond to any season, since there is no season) you may glimpse a cuff of madder-red or a collar of saffron at someone's throat, and it will strike you with the force that a single bird's call would strike you, if there were birds.

Their hands are the most remarkable thing. Thick-knuckled, broad-palmed, with fingers that taper to a surprising delicacy at the tips — hands shaped by a lifetime of valve-work and ledger-work and the patient adjustment of small brass fittings. The skin of the palm is stained, permanently, with the dark oil they use on the Loom's bearings; it does not wash out, and a child's hands are pale and an elder's hands are nearly black, and you can tell a Tender's age, roughly, by holding their hand. They greet by clasping forearms, not hands, for this reason — to keep the oil where it belongs, on the work.

They speak a language that has, by lowland count, very few words for time. There is a word for *now* and a word for *before-now* and a word for *after-now*, and these three words cover most of what a Tender needs to say about when. The tenses of their verbs do not conjugate for past or future; they conjugate, instead, for relation to the Loom — whether the action is *with* the Loom's rhythm, *against* it, *aside* from it, or *within* it. To say "I walked to the cistern" and "I will walk to the cistern" uses the same verb form; what changes, if anything changes, is whether the walking is in service of the Loom (most walking is) or in service of some small private human matter (a visit, a meal, a death).

They have, however, a great many words for the Loom's sounds. The deep boom of the pumping stations is *grond*, and a child learns this word before *mother*. The higher sibilance of the steam-lines is *sshe*, and the hum of the smaller pipes is *minn*, and the slow tock of the great flywheels is *dom*, and when all four are audible at once — which is the condition of most places at most times — the composite is called *grond-sshe-minn-dom*, spoken as a single word, and it means, approximately, *everything is well*. A Tender entering a valley listens for it before doing anything else. If one note is missing, the Tender's body knows before the Tender's mind does, and the walk becomes a walk toward the missing note.

They are not, despite all this, a solemn people. This surprises visitors. The expectation, coming up the long passes, is of monks — of a hushed, ritual population moving through their eternal work with downcast eyes. The reality is closer to a guild of slightly tired tradespeople. They laugh at each other's jokes, which are mostly about valves, or about the eccentricities of particular pumping stations, or about other towns ("In Hesh-of-the-Cirque they oil clockwise; can you imagine"). They drink a hot dark liquor made from a fungus that grows on the warm pipes, and they drink it constantly, in small cups, the way other peoples drink tea. They argue about the right pitch for a new pipe-fitting and the arguments can go on for years and are enjoyed. They marry for love, mostly, though the marriages are arranged in the loose sense that everyone in a town of two thousand knows everyone, and a match that the town disapproves of will simply not quite happen, the way certain weather here does not quite happen.

They are intensely curious about visitors and almost completely uninterested in the lowlands. A visitor will be asked, on arrival, what their work is — not their name, not their origin, but their *work* — and the answer will be considered carefully, and the visitor will be offered the local equivalent. A scholar will be shown the ledgers. A musician will be taken to the resonance-chambers under the cathedrals where the pipes' lowest harmonics can be felt in the breastbone. A cook will be set down in a kitchen with a knife. The Tenders do this not from hospitality exactly but from a sense that work is the unit of acquaintance, and that until they have seen you work they don't know who you are, and the names and origins can come later, if at all.

They do not, as a rule, leave. A few do, every generation — there is always one or two restless young person who walks down the long passes and is not seen again, and this is spoken of with neither approval nor grief but with a kind of low-grade puzzlement, the way you might speak of a stream that suddenly chose a different bed. The ones who stay (which is to say, almost everyone) do not understand the impulse, and the ones who leave do not, apparently, write back, and so the question of what becomes of them stays open in the way most questions stay open here, which is to say, not very urgently.

Their dead are not buried. The ground is too thin and too wet and too full of pipe. Their dead are walked, instead, to a particular kind of vent — there is one near every town, a wide-mouthed iron flue that breathes a constant warm updraft — and the body is wrapped in its outer wool and placed at the mouth and after a time (no one watches; it is considered indecent to watch) the body is gone. Where it goes is not asked. It is understood that the Loom takes back what the Loom requires, and that the wool is found, neatly folded, at the vent's edge the next morning, and is taken home and washed and used again, because wool is wool and waste is waste, and these are not the same.

They love their children fiercely and unsentimentally. A child here is the only true variable — the one thing in the whole vast standing figure of the world that is genuinely new — and the Tenders feel this without articulating it. A baby is passed from house to house in its first year, held by everyone, learning the sound of *grond-sshe-minn-dom* against many different chests. A child of seven is already walking lines with a parent, learning which pipe sings what. A child of fourteen has chosen, or had chosen for them, a specialty: valves, or ledgers, or the great wheels, or the rarer crafts (there is someone in every town whose job is to listen, only to listen, eight hours a day, and to report what they hear; this is considered the highest work and is given to those whose temperament suits it, which is a particular and somewhat melancholy temperament). By twenty they are full Tenders. By forty their hands are dark. By seventy, if they live so long — many do; the air is clean and the work is steady and there are no animals to carry disease — they are mostly retired to the listening-rooms, where their old ears, trained for a lifetime, can still tell a *minn* gone slightly sharp from three valleys away.

And what they are, finally, underneath all of this — what the Tenders are when you have accounted for their bodies and their language and their work and their dead — is the Loom's way of having someone to talk to. This is not how they would put it. They would say, if pressed, that they tend the Loom, that they are its caretakers, that the relation runs from them to it. But stand on a high pass at dusk, as the writer of the description suggests, and watch the amber lights come on in the terraced towns far below, and listen to the *grond* coming up through the rock, and you will understand that the Loom is enormous and tireless and exact, and that an enormous and tireless and exact thing, left entirely alone, is not quite a thing at all — it is only a process — and that what the Tenders provide, with their small births and small deaths and their arguments about valve-fittings and their hot fungal liquor and their oiled dark hands, is the one thing the Loom cannot generate from inside itself, which is the experience of being attended to.

They are the Loom's witnesses. They are why it is running rather than merely running. And they know this, in the way you know the shape of your own breath: not as a fact you could state, but as the condition under which all other facts arrive.

The rain that is not quite rain beads on their wool as they walk the lines. They do not brush it off. It belongs there. So do they.
