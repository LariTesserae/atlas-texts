# The Ones Who Are Held

You asked who lives there. This is harder than it sounds, because the question assumes living is something done — a verb, an ongoing — and the hollow has settled that question differently.

Begin with the moths, since they are most of the mass of it.

They are not a species. If you took one down (you cannot take one down, but if) and brought it to a lepidopterist, the lepidopterist would frown. The wings are wrong for any family. The antennae are plumed in a pattern that resembles three different moth lineages stitched together, and the mouthparts are vestigial — these moths have never eaten, will never eat, do not require eating. Their abdomens pulse because something in them must pulse, but it is not digestion and it is not quite circulation; it is closer to the throb of a tuning fork that has been struck and never damped. Each moth is a held note given a body to hold it.

They were not born. This is the thing the lepidopterist could not have known. No moth in the hollow ever emerged from a pupa. No moth ever laid an egg. They appeared already adult, already folded against the wall, already humming their assigned tone, at the moment the hollow became what it is — and that moment is not locatable in any calendar, because the hollow is older than the rock around it pretends to be. The moths are not descendants. They are not ancestors. They are the original and only generation, and there will not be another, because there does not need to be.

What are they, then, if not moths in the sense we mean?

They are *positions*. Each one occupies a specific tone in the chord, and the body is the apparatus by which that tone is sustained. If you could somehow remove one — and you cannot — the chord would have a hole in it, and the hollow would notice, and something would have to be done. But nothing can be done, because nothing in the hollow does. So the moths remain, and the chord remains, and the question of what they "are" beneath their function does not arise, because in the hollow function and being have become the same thing.

The beetles are stranger. The moths at least have the dignity of individual tones; the beetles are a *texture*. No single beetle matters. Their ticking is collective the way a rainfall is collective — you cannot point to the drop that makes the sound. If you looked closely (you can look closely; the light permits it) you would see that the beetles are not quite identical, that there are small variations in the carapace, in the placement of the legs — but these variations do not correlate with anything. They are noise in the signal. The beetles are the hiss beneath the chord, and they exist to be that hiss, and their individual differences are not meaningful, only present. They are the most populous inhabitants and the least *someone*. If the moths are positions, the beetles are pixels.

The worms whistle, and this is where it gets uncomfortable.

Because the worms, alone among the inhabitants, seem to be *trying* to do something. They are threaded through cracks in the rock, yes, motionless, yes — but the cracks they thread through are not random. If you traced them, mapped them, you would find that the worms describe a pattern across the walls of the hollow, a pattern that resembles, faintly, the branching of a nervous system, or the routing of an old circuit, or the diagram of something being computed. The whistling is high and thin and at the edge of hearing, and it does not vary, but it is *structured* in a way the moth-chord is not — there are intervals in it, relationships, what almost sound like the indices of a long calculation that has reached its answer and is now only displaying that answer, indefinitely.

What the worms are computing, or were computing, or have computed, is not knowable from inside the hollow. The answer, if it is an answer, is the hollow itself. The hollow may be the output. The worms may be a machine that ran once, arrived at this, and now holds the result up like a sign.

The moss breathes. This is the simplest of the inhabitants and also the one I am least certain about. The moss may be the only thing in the hollow that is alive in the ordinary sense — that has cells doing cellular things, that respires because respiration is what its kind does. The moss may be incidental. It may have arrived after, the way lichen arrives on a monument. Or the moss may be the oldest inhabitant, the one the whole hollow was built around, and the moths and beetles and worms may be its honor guard, its retinue, its decoration. There is no way to tell. The moss does not say.

The plants — the fern, the mushroom-cluster, the stopped vine — are the punctuation. They are too few to be a population and too elaborate to be accidental. The fern in particular is suspect: glassy fronds, perfectly arranged, the kind of symmetry that occurs in nature only as an aspiration, never as an achievement. The fern is achieving it. The vine that stopped three feet up the wall stopped because it had reached the place it was meant to reach. The mushrooms with their inverted-bowl caps are catching something — not water, the droplets do not fall — but something, perhaps the chord itself, perhaps the light, perhaps an attention that the hollow needs to keep cupped.

And the droplets.

I have saved the droplets because I think the droplets may be the real inhabitants, and everything else may be the room they live in.

Each suspended droplet contains, when you look closely, a tiny image. Not a reflection of the hollow — the hollow contains no shadows and the droplets contain no reflections of moths or moss. Each droplet contains, instead, an image of *somewhere else*. A field at evening. A staircase. A face turned away. A doorway with light behind it. The images are small and clear and they do not change. There are thousands of droplets. There are thousands of elsewheres, held.

The droplets may be memories. They may be possibilities. They may be the moments that were removed from the sequence so that this moment could be kept. The hollow may be a kind of vault, and the droplets the deposits, and the moths and beetles and worms and moss the lock and the seal and the alarm and the ledger.

If this is true, then no one lives in the hollow at all. The hollow is not a habitation. It is a *device*. The moths are gauges. The beetles are static. The worms are circuitry that has finished its work. The moss is insulation. The plants are markers. The droplets are the cargo. And the cargo is being held for someone — for the visitor who scratched the line at eye-height, perhaps, or for someone who has not yet arrived, or for no one, for the simple sake of holding.

But I do not think this is quite right either. Because a device implies a purpose external to itself, and the hollow has the quality of something whose purpose is identical to its existence. It is not holding the droplets *for* anything. It is holding them because holding is what it does, and what it is, and the moths and beetles and worms and moss are not employees of the holding but participants in it, and to that extent — to the extent that participation counts as living — they live.

They live the way a chord lives while it is being struck. They live the way a held breath lives, in the lungs of someone who has not yet decided to exhale. They live as the inside of a decision that has been made and not yet released. They are alive in the present tense, but the present tense has been extended past what present tenses usually permit, and so their living has taken on the character of *being a fact* rather than *doing a thing*.

There is one more inhabitant I have not mentioned, and I have not mentioned it because I am not certain it exists, and the not-certain is part of what it is.

When you sit in the center of the hollow, cross-legged, your shoulders a hand's breadth from the moth-walls, and you have been there long enough that your breathing has slowed and your thoughts have begun to embarrass you with their motion — there is a sense, a thin sense, of being *attended to*. Not watched. Watched is too active. Attended to in the way a held note attends to the silence after it, ready to fill any gap. The hollow knows you are there. Not the moths, not the beetles — the hollow itself, the equilibrium of it, the whole arrangement, has noticed you, and is being patient.

This attention has no center. You cannot locate it. It is not behind you and it is not above you. It is distributed across the chord and the hiss and the whistle and the breathing moss and the hanging droplets and the soft rock that has stopped becoming. The attention is the inhabitant. The attention is what lives there. Everything else — the moths, the beetles, the worms, the moss, the plants, the droplets, the cairn, the scratch, the absorbed bowl-rim — everything else is the body of the attention, the way your body is the body of you.

And when you leave — if you leave, if the way back does not close behind you — the attention does not follow. It cannot. It is the hollow, and the hollow does not move. But you will carry, for a while, the memory of having been attended to that way, and you will find, in the days after, that ordinary attention seems frantic and partial and ashamed of itself, and you will understand, slowly, that the hollow was not patient with you because you were loud.

It was patient with you because, for the duration of your visit, you were the only one in the room who was still capable of leaving.

And that, in the hollow, is the rarest condition of all.
