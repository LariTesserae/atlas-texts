## The Tenders

They are not hidden. That is the first thing to understand. You have looked directly at several of them today and registered only a slight thickening of the moss, a place where the cushion-plants grew a little taller, a hummock you stepped around without thinking. They are not camouflaged. They are *constituted* — they are made of the same wet green stuff as the substrate, because over a sufficient stretch of time the distinction between a body that lives on the moss and a body that is moss becomes administrative rather than real.

A Tender, if you could pull one up entire (you cannot; nothing here comes up entire), would be roughly the size and shape of a curled sleeping dog, though longer in some axis you can't quite specify. The outer surface is sphagnum and the lesser cushion-plants and a fine pale fur of something thread-like that might be fungal and might be hair. Underneath that is denser tissue: a slow, peat-coloured musculature that holds water the way a sponge holds water but contracts, very slowly, when it wishes to move. They do move. Not while you watch. But the trails through the moss — the ones you attributed to small many-legged things on errands — are also, sometimes, the slow drag-marks of a Tender repositioning itself over the course of three or four days, going from one place where the substrate needed attention to another.

What do they tend?

They tend the works. This is the joke, or the revelation, depending on how long you stay. The works is not theirs and they did not build it. Something built it — some earlier project, some abandoned engineering, some intention from a century or a millennium ago that left its iron pipes and its brick pylons and its humming shed and then went away or died or finished. The works, having been left, did what abandoned works do: it began to fail. Seams wept. Cables corroded. Pressure dropped in one pipe and built dangerously in another.

And the Tenders, who were here before the works and would be here after, noticed.

Not noticed the way you or I would notice. Noticed the way a wound notices itself: by responding. A Tender finding a leaking seam will settle over it, sometimes for years, and the sphagnum of its outer body will work into the gap and seal it, and the iron, instead of rusting through, will rust *into* the Tender, become part of the Tender, until the pipe at that joint is no longer quite pipe and the Tender at that joint is no longer quite Tender, and the water that wanted to escape continues on through the system. The humming shed has, beneath its floor, in the place where the cable goes down, a Tender that has not moved in any human's memory and has grown so dense and so saturated and so threaded with the copper of the cable that to call it an animal is foolish and to call it a piece of equipment is also foolish. It is the ground in which the cable is rooted. It is what keeps the cable cold enough not to melt itself.

They did not choose this work. They have no opinion about the works, because they have no opinions; opinion is a fast thing and they are made of slow things. But the works produces water — produces it, or routes it, or extracts it from some deeper source and lifts it up into the cloud-mat that lights the sky — and water is what they live in, and what lives in them, and what they are. So the relationship is symmetrical without being intentional. The works keeps the highlands wet. The Tenders keep the works from failing. Neither was designed for the other. They have simply, over a great stretch of time, fitted.

The roar you hear — the full-throated mechanical roar that gets into your sternum — is partly the works. But it is also, and this is harder to accept, partly the Tenders. Not their voice; they have no voice. But a body that is largely water, pressed against a pipe that is largely vibration, will resonate. The whole plateau resonates. Every Tender is a tuned membrane on the great drum of the works, and the sound that fills the basin and rolls back off the cloud-ceiling is the sound of the works *being heard* by ten thousand wet listening bodies, and rebroadcast, and heard again. If you could remove the Tenders the works would be quieter. It would also, within a season, fail.

There is one more thing to say about them, and it is the thing that is hardest to say, because it touches on a question you did not come here to ask.

The works was built by people. You can tell from the brick, from the gauge of the pipe, from the padlock on the shed door, which is a padlock of a make you recognize. People built the works and people went away. The Tenders did not build the works and the Tenders did not go away. And in the densest, oldest Tenders — the one beneath the shed, the ones grown into the elbow-joints of the great pipes, the ones that have been in place for two or three or six human generations — there are, sometimes, if you look without wanting to see, shapes inside the peat-coloured musculature that are not quite the shapes of moss. A curve like a vertebra. A pale branching that is too regular to be root. A small dense knot at what might be called the head end, in roughly the place a skull would be in a body that had a skull.

The Tenders did not build the works. But the Tenders were not, perhaps, *always* Tenders. The plateau is patient. It accepts what is left on it. The moss grows up to a threshold and stops, politely, as though waiting — and sometimes the threshold opens and something comes out and lies down in the wet, and the moss grows over it, and after a long time something gets up that is not quite what lay down, and goes, very slowly, to see about a leaking seam.

This is speculation. The Tenders are not telling. They are busy. The fog is coming down and the roar is coming from inside the fog now and somewhere a pipe has begun, very faintly, to weep, and a hummock in the moss thirty paces off has begun, over the next four days, to make its way toward it.
