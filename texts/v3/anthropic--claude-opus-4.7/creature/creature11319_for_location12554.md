## The People of First Quarry

They are a people who have learned to move like water in a narrow channel — not slowly, but without splashing.

You see it first in the bodies. They are not tall. Generations is too strong a word for a place only decades old, but already there is a *type* settling out: compact, deep-chested from the damp air, with the heavy shoulders of pick-work and the careful feet of those who live on ledges. Their hands are pale from working in shadow and chalked white at the creases. The skin of their forearms is mapped with the small pink scars of stone-splinters. Eyes adjust quickly here, and theirs do — pupils wide in the lanes, then tight as pinpricks when they step into a sun-struck court. They squint upward at the rectangle of sky the way other peoples squint into distance.

They dress in undyed wool and stretched hide, belted close. Color is rare and meaningful: the violet of the cress-plant's underside, boiled down, marks the water-keepers; a smear of ochre at the temple marks a mason who has broken through into a void and lived to tell the council. Most go barefoot on the terraces — the stone is cool and clean, and a bare foot finds the edge of a drop before a sandaled one does. Their hair is bound up out of the way of the work. Both men and women wear it the same: gathered at the nape, knotted, sometimes pinned with a sliver of antler.

What strikes a visitor, though, is not how they look but how they *attend*. They are listeners. Not in the way of the contemplative — in the way of people who have learned that the stone carries everything. A child in First Quarry knows by the age of four which footfalls belong to her mother, which to her aunt, which to the basket-hauler who passes their threshold at the third water-call. They know each other by sound the way a forest people know each other by face. When two of them meet in a lane they do not call out. They touch the wall with two fingers — a greeting that travels a few meters into the rock and is felt by whoever is on the other side.

The hand-signs the children learn are not a separate language but a shadow of the spoken one — the same grammar, made visible. Across a court, a mother can tell her son to come down, bring the basin, mind the edge, and that supper is the dark cress, all in the time it takes a hawk to cross the sky. Adults sign almost as much as they speak. In the council chamber, where every whisper carries, the elders often debate in signs and only speak when they have agreed on the words.

They are not solemn, though that is the easy mistake to make about a quiet people. They laugh — silently, mostly, with the shoulders. There is a kind of joke that consists entirely of timing: a basket-hauler pauses one beat too long before answering a question, and three terraces away someone snorts into their sleeve. Humor here is architectural. It uses the acoustics.

Their reverences are practical. They do not worship the spring, exactly, but they treat it the way one treats a sleeping animal one depends on — quietly, with the assumption that it could leave. The cistern-keeper's measuring is a kind of liturgy, but no one calls it that. When a child is named in the spring-house, the parents bring a thumb of clay from the seep-garden and press it into a niche cut for the purpose; the niches climb the walls of that first house in a slow spiral, each thumbprint a citizen, and the spiral is perhaps a third of the way up. You can read the city's age in it.

They have a particular relationship to the rock. They speak of it the way a sailor speaks of weather — not as enemy, not as friend, but as the medium of their lives, which has moods. A mason will say *the stone is giving today* or *the stone is closed*, and mean something specific about how the wedges are sitting. They believe — or at least they speak as if they believe — that the rock has intentions. That a wall which cracks unexpectedly was *ready* to crack. That a void found behind a clean cut was *waiting*. This is why the council must be called when a mason breaks through. It is not only that no one knows what is below. It is that the stone has *spoken*, and someone must listen properly before the conversation continues.

The children are the most interesting of them. They are born into a world thirty meters deep and ten times as wide, a world whose ceiling is a rectangle of weather and whose floor is unfinished. They climb before they walk — the ladders are everywhere, and a toddler learns the rungs the way other children learn the steps of a porch. They are quiet from infancy; a crying baby in First Quarry is heard by the whole basin, and so mothers learn very early to anticipate the cry and meet it before it sounds. The result is a generation of children who are eerily composed, who watch adults with the calm regard of small judges, and who already, at six or seven, sign the practical grammar of the city with the speed of speech. They play games of stillness. They play a game where one child stands at the center of the council-floor and the others, scattered through the terraces, must guess from the whisper which of them is speaking. They play a game where they map the bat-flight at dusk with their fingers and see who can predict the ribbon's turn.

The old are few — the place is too new to have many — but those who are old are revered for an unexpected reason: they remember the surface. They remember what it was to live on the plain, in tents or in the timber-houses that no longer exist here. They are the only people in First Quarry who have known a horizon. When they speak of it, the children listen the way children elsewhere listen to stories of the sea. *There was a place,* they say, *where you could see the sun rise and set in the same day, on opposite sides of yourself.* The children try to picture this and cannot quite. The sun, for them, is a thing that *passes* — a bright slow shape that crosses the rectangle and is gone, and what is on either side of the rectangle is not absence but simply *the rest of the rock*.

This is the thing about them, finally. They are a people becoming native to a place that is itself becoming. The city is being carved and so are they. Their bodies, their language, their sense of what a sky is — all of it is being shaped by the cut their parents began and that they will continue. A child born in First Quarry this year will, if she lives, see the basin doubled in her lifetime, the terraces stepped down into chambers no one has yet imagined, voids opened that the council has not yet been called to read. She will be one of the first people to have known nothing else. Her thumbprint is already in the spiral.

And when she stands on the highest terrace at midday, in the clean column of sun, and tilts her face up at the passing hawk, she will not be looking at the sky. She will be looking at the *opening* — the place where the rock has not yet closed over them, the unfinished ceiling of the world she is helping to make. And then she will go back to her work, because the stone is giving today, and someone, three terraces down, is counting the water.
