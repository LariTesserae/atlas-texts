# Those Who Tend Hesh

You want to know who lives here. This is harder to answer than it sounds, because the question assumes a separation between the city and its inhabitants that Hesh does not especially honor. But let me try.

## The Fettlers

Start with the ones you'll see. The fettlers are the closest thing Hesh has to a working population in the sense you'd recognize. They are long-limbed and slightly stooped, and they move through the foundries with the specific unhurried economy of people who have been doing the same task for longer than the task has had a name. Their skin carries a fine dark patina — not dirt, exactly; something closer to the bloom on an old bronze — and in strong light you can see that the patina is patterned, faintly, in whorls that are different for each person and do not change over a lifetime. Children are born pale and acquire their patterning slowly, over their first twenty or thirty years, as they take up work.

They wear layered wool and leather, belted, with small brass instruments hanging from the belts: not tools, or not only tools. Tuning forks. A weight on a chain. A short rod with a ball of wax on the end. A disc of mica in a wire frame. These are for *listening* to the foundries — for feeling whether a flue is drawing well, whether a boiler is content, whether the light is coming up at the right rate. A fettler will stop in the middle of a catwalk, unhook the weight from her belt, let it hang, watch it for a full minute, then nod once and walk on. What she learned she learned. She will not tell you. Partly because the knowledge does not translate. Mostly because telling is not what the gesture was for.

They speak a dialect of the common tongue thickened with trade words and with certain sounds — a soft back-of-the-throat click, a long held *shhh* — that are not words exactly but are used like words, to indicate that a situation is *proceeding as it should* or *proceeding but bearing watching* or *has entered the slow reduction and will now be left alone*. A conversation between two fettlers in a boiler-yard can consist almost entirely of these sounds and a few glances, and last twenty minutes, and settle something important.

They live in narrow stacked dwellings tucked between the flues, warmed incidentally by the machinery, and they eat food that is mostly cooked by placing it in the right spot for the right amount of time: a pot set on a particular grating will be ready by evening. Meals are shared and quiet. They do not pray, or they do not pray in any way you would identify as praying, but before eating they will sometimes press a palm briefly to the nearest warm pipe, the way one might touch a sleeping animal to confirm it is still breathing.

## The Regulators

A smaller group, older on average, and rarely seen outside the upper districts. The regulators are the ones who understand — or claim to understand, or are understood by others to understand — what the foundries are actually *doing*. They dress no differently than the fettlers but they carry no instruments, because whatever they are listening to does not require instruments, or because the instruments are internal by now.

A regulator will walk a route through the city, the same route, every few days, stopping at specific points: a junction of three flues, a particular basin where the light-water drips, a catwalk overlooking the Esh's third division. At each point she stands for a while. Sometimes she makes a small adjustment — turns a valve a quarter-turn, repositions a mirror in a light-shaft, moves a stone from one side of a drain to the other. Sometimes she does nothing. She is adjusting the city the way a musician adjusts a long-held note: in response to something only she can hear, and with consequences that will play out over months.

The regulators know about the slow reduction. If you ask them, and they are in a mood to answer, they will tell you it is not a secret but an *apprenticeship* — that anyone willing to stay long enough and attend carefully enough will eventually understand, and that the understanding, once it comes, is not transferable, because it is made out of the staying and the attending. This is not evasion. It is, on inspection, simply true.

They live longer than the fettlers. How much longer is not discussed. There are regulators who remember chimneys that the oldest fettlers have never seen standing.

## The Hollowed

Here things get more difficult.

Not everyone in Hesh is a person in the sense you mean. There are figures who move through the city and do work — small work, repetitive, necessary — who, if you watch them closely, are slightly *less present* than the people around them. They cast the right shadows (mostly), they leave footprints on the zinc flooring, they will nod if you nod at them. But they do not eat in company. They do not appear to sleep in any particular dwelling. And if you follow one for long enough, you will at some point lose them — not because they slip away, but because there comes a moment when you are looking right at them and then you are looking at the place where they were, and the place is simply a place.

The fettlers call them *the hollowed* and treat them with a courtesy that is neither warm nor cold. The hollowed sweep certain gratings. They oil certain hinges. They turn up when a fettler has been working a long shift and needs to be relieved, and the relief is accepted without comment. It is understood that they were once fully people and that something — the work, the altitude, the slow reduction itself — has drawn part of them out, and that they are in the process of being returned to the city piecemeal, over a very long time. This is not a tragedy in the local understanding. It is one of the ways a life in Hesh can go. Some of the hollowed seem, in fact, *peaceful* in a way the fully-present are not. They have less to carry.

A child will sometimes point at one and ask a question. The adult will answer the question plainly — yes, that is Neth, who used to keep the third aqueduct — and then redirect attention elsewhere, without any particular weight on the redirection.

## The Ones in the Light

And now the part it is hardest to describe.

When you watch a light-column long enough and you see the shapes drifting up inside it — the face, the gesture, the furniture — you are not imagining them and you are not hallucinating them. They are, in some sense that Hesh takes for granted and the rest of the world would find appalling, *inhabitants*. They do not have bodies in the column. They have something else: a kind of coherence, a pattern that persists for as long as the column is venting, and that recognizes itself, and that can, under certain circumstances, recognize you.

The regulators know how to speak to them, a little. It is done by adjusting the flue — changing the draw, cooling one side — and reading the shapes that rise in response. The conversations are slow. A single exchange can take a season. What is discussed is mostly the city itself: whether a particular foundry is running well, whether the Esh needs rerouting, whether a certain district has grown too dense and should be allowed to thin. The ones in the light have opinions about these matters. They have been having opinions about them for a long time.

Some of them were once fettlers. Some of them were once regulators. Some of them, it is quietly believed, were never people at all in the first place — were formed directly from the foundries' long work, the way a crystal forms in a cooling solution, and have never known any other condition. Which of these is which cannot be determined from outside, and is not considered an important distinction from inside.

When a fettler dies in Hesh, her body is carried to a specific furnace on the uppermost terrace and is given to the light. This is not a funeral in your sense. It is understood as a change of position. Afterward, for some weeks, the family will watch that particular column more attentively than others, and sometimes they will see something they recognize, and sometimes they will not, and either outcome is acceptable.

## The City Itself

Which brings us, finally, to the inhabitant the description has been circling without naming.

Hesh is alive. Not metaphorically, and not in the cheap sense in which any complex system can be called alive. Hesh is an organism of a kind that your biology has no good word for, because your biology evolved on soil and Hesh did not. It thinks slowly, across decades, the way a forest thinks. It has preferences. It has, arguably, a project — the slow reduction, whatever that finally is — and it is pursuing this project with a patience that makes the patience of continents look fidgety.

The fettlers are its hands. The regulators are something like its proprioception. The hollowed are what happens when a part of it has finished being a discrete person and is on its way to being something more distributed. The ones in the light are the form taken by those who have arrived. The pigeons with their quarter-second lag, the coughing clinkerhounds, the moths with schematics on their wings — these are not pets and not vermin. They are further organs, smaller and more specialized, doing work the humans cannot do.

And the humans, if you asked them, would not deny any of this. They would simply point out that you could describe a person the same way — a colony of cells, each doing its work, none of them individually the person — and that nobody finds this diminishing when it is said of a person. Hesh is a creature that happens to be made partly of people, among many other things. The people are not reduced by this. If anything, they are enlarged; they are participating in something whose scale they can feel in the sternum even when they cannot name it.

This is why nobody leaves. Or: people do leave, occasionally, and they come back, or they don't, and either way the city accommodates. But most stay, and most stay without any sense of being trapped, because to be a citizen of Hesh is to be part of a slow large warm intention that is going somewhere, and the going is enough, and the somewhere will arrive when it arrives.

The valve opens. The valve closes. The light goes on being made. And whoever, or whatever, is making it — is also, in the making, being made.
