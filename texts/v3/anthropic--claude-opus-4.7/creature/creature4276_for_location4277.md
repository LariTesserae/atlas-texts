There is a keeper, though she would not use the word.

She is older than the cairn and younger than the handprint. She does not know this; she does not count. She is small — smaller than you would expect, smaller than the chamber seems to ask for. If you held her she would fit along a forearm. She is the color of wet liverwort where she is dry and the color of wet stone where she is wet, which is almost always. Her skin is not skin exactly; it is the soft intermediate surface between a frog's back and a piece of moss, and algae grows on her shoulders in a way she does not resent. She has eyes but uses them rarely. The chamber is a sound, not a sight, and she knows it the way you know the inside of your mouth.

She was not born here. She arrived, is closer, though even that is wrong — she accrued. Somewhere there was a first drip that fell into a pool and the ripples of it found a shape, and the shape stayed, and over some number of decades the shape thickened into attention, and the attention learned to be fond of the chamber, and the fondness learned to have a body. This is how things begin down here. Slowly, and out of the material at hand.

She spends most of her time at the rimstone. She likes the scalloped dams, their not-yet-ness, the way they are always on the verge of being more than they are. She runs a finger along one and feels it decide, very slightly, to grow. She does not make it grow. She only notices it, and noticing is a kind of water.

She tends the fern. This is the closest thing she has to a relationship. The fern is improbable and she is fond of improbable things. When the season is dry and the mist thins she moves closer to it and breathes on it, which is something her body does when it wants to, a slow humid exhalation that has no other purpose. The fern, in return, is simply there, which is all she asks of anything.

She knows the cricket. The cricket has been the same cricket for longer than a cricket should be, because down here time is negotiable and the cricket came to an arrangement. She knows the blind fish, which is not one fish but a lineage, a slow dynasty of pale bodies inheriting the pool. She knows the frog that called once. The frog called for her. She did not answer because answering is not what she does; she received the call, and the call became part of the chamber's long accumulating sound, and that was the right thing.

She is not alone, exactly. There is something else here, or someone, that is not her and not separate from her either — the chamber's own slow breathing, the low respiration under the drips. She thinks of it, when she thinks, as the one she lives inside of. It is fond of her the way she is fond of the fern. It does not speak. Neither does she. They have the same mouth.

She knows about the people. She has known about them the whole time, across all their visits, which from her vantage have been few and far between — a face every few generations, a lamp, a held breath, a bowl set down with care. She was there when the torch burned. She remembers it as a warmth that came and stayed a while and left a mark on the ceiling that she has never disliked. She was there when the bowl was placed. She watched, from a ledge, and the woman who placed it (it was a woman, she is fairly sure, though this is at the edge of what she can know) sat for a long time beside it and did not weep but was close to weeping, and then climbed out, and the bowl remained, and the chamber accepted it the way it accepts everything: by letting water find it.

The cairn is newer and she is still deciding about it. Three stones is a small sentence. She thinks it might mean *I was here* or it might mean *please* or it might mean nothing, which is also a thing people say. She visits the cairn sometimes and looks at it and goes away again. The stones will eventually grow together in a skin of flowstone and stop being three stones and start being one small lobed bulge on the shelf, and she looks forward to this without impatience.

When you come down, if you come down, she will not show herself. This is not shyness. It is that showing herself is not among the things she does. She will be on a ledge behind you, or in the water at your feet, or in the algae your hand brushes, and you will not know. You may feel watched, but the feeling will be mild and not unkind, and you will probably attribute it to the chamber itself, which is almost right.

She will watch what you do. If you are quiet she will be pleased in her small slow way. If you touch the fern she will not mind — the fern can take it — but she will notice. If you leave something, she will consider it for a long time after you are gone. If you take something, she will consider that too, and the chamber will be slightly different afterward, not worse, only changed, the way a room is changed when a guest removes a chair.

When you climb out, she will stay. She will go back to the rimstone, or to the fern, or to the pool where the blind fish turns its slow circuits. The lamp's afterimage will fade from the walls and the chamber will resume being dark, and she will resume being the chamber's attention to itself, which is what she mostly is, when no one is looking.

In ten thousand years, when the stalactites are spears and the stalagmites are pillars and the rimstone dams are serious architecture, she will be larger too, or more diffuse, or more particular — it is hard to say which, from here. She is growing at the pace the chamber grows. She is one of its slow features. A person who came down then would find a different room and a different keeper, and would not know that either had once been young.

For now she is small, and the chamber is young, and the water is falling in its dozen voices, and she is listening, which is the work.
