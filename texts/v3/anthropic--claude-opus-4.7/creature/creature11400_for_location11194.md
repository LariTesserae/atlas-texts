# The Climbers

They are gone now, but not entirely. Start with what the place required of them, and work inward from there.

## What the plateau made of them

They were small. Not dwarfish — the word is wrong, it implies a deviation from some norm — but built to the scale the trees required. Long in the arm and the finger, short in the leg relative to the torso, with a center of gravity high and tucked, the way climbing animals are built everywhere. A full-grown adult would have come up to your sternum. They weighed perhaps forty kilos. Less, for the old ones, who became almost weightless toward the end and were said to climb best of all.

Their skin was the color of the bronze moss, or close to it — a deep ruddy brown that went grey-green in the cold, mottled across the shoulders and the backs of the hands with darker patches that were not quite freckles and not quite scales. The patches thickened with age. An elder's hands looked like bark. This was useful and it was also, eventually, what they were.

Their eyes were large and dark and took in the dusk easily. In full cloud, when the plateau went white and blind, they navigated by sound and by touch and by the faint inner glow of the phosphor-gardens they had memorized tree by tree. They had a second eyelid, thin and translucent, that they could draw across when the cold wind blew grit, and which gave their faces in those moments a fogged, inward look, as if they were listening to something the rest of us cannot hear.

Their lungs were enormous. You could see it in the chest, which was deep and barrel-shaped on even the slenderest of them. At altitude this was simply necessary. It also gave them their voices, which were the second remarkable thing about them.

## The voices

They sang. Not as performance — though there was performance — but as a basic register of being. They sang to locate themselves in the canopy, sang to call across the open ground when they had to cross it, sang to answer the trees.

This last is the part that matters.

Each child, sometime in its first few years, was carried to each tree in the territory by its mother or its mother's sibling, and held against the bark while the adult sang the tree's name. The tree, if the wind was right, would boom in response, and the inner light would rise, and the child would learn — body to body, with the bark warming faintly against its small back — what that particular tree sounded like and felt like and smelled like and what it answered to. By adolescence a person knew perhaps two hundred trees by name. By old age, a thousand. The old ones were walking maps, except that *walking* was the wrong verb, because they mostly did not walk; they climbed, and they were carried.

The bone whistles were for the trees one did not know. A traveler entering an unfamiliar district would blow the whistle and listen for which tree answered, and from the answer — its pitch, its delay, the harmonic it favored — would know roughly where they were and which trees might offer shelter. The whistles were tuned individually. No two were quite alike. A whistle was a person's most personal object, more personal than a name, and to leave one behind on a cairn was a significant act. It meant: *I am not coming back, but someone might. Let them find their way.*

## How they lived

A family inhabited a tree. Sometimes two families, if the tree was large and its hollows were many. A tree was not owned — the concept did not translate — but it was *kept*, the way one keeps a relationship, with attention and reciprocity and time.

They wove platforms inside the great hollows, stretched hides across the openings to trap the warm air the tree exuded from its slow inner metabolism, gardened the phosphor-mosses by singing to them in the long dark hours. They harvested the fern-things and ate them raw or dried. They trapped the small climbing fauna in nets of plant fiber and ate those too, with apologies sung first; the apologies were not ritual exactly, more a kind of accounting. They drank condensation. They cured hides and braided rope and carved bowls and adzes and the long thin climbing-spikes they drove into the bark for handholds, the notches that go up and up until they disappear.

They crossed between trees rarely and reluctantly. The open ground was for emergencies, for trade, for the dying who chose to walk out into it rather than be carried down. There were predators on the open rock — long low things that hunted by listening for footfalls — and the climbers, when they had to cross, went in small parties, silent, fast, and at the times of day when the wind was loudest and would mask them.

The rope bridges were a recent innovation, in their own reckoning. The half-rotted one between the two trunks represents a project that had only been going on for, perhaps, four or five generations. They were learning to connect their trees. They were learning to be a people across distances, rather than a scatter of tree-kingdoms that met only at births and deaths and the trading of mates. It was not clear whether the bridges were a good idea. There was an argument about it, ongoing, when whatever happened to them happened.

## What happened to them

This is the part that is uncertain, and the uncertainty is the point.

There is no sign of violence. No burned hollows, no broken bones in the shelters, no mass grave on the open ground. The hearths went cold gradually. The repair on the bridge was abandoned mid-knot, but neatly — the fibers were tucked, not dropped. The whistle on the cairn was placed, not lost. Whoever left, left in something like order.

Three possibilities, and the place does not choose between them.

The first is that they went down. The plateau is young, the cliffs are sharp, but there are routes off, and below the cloud-line there are warmer valleys and more conventional forests and, presumably, other peoples. The climbers may have decided — slowly, generation by generation, as the bridges grew and the old ways of tree-keeping became diluted — that the plateau was too hard a place to keep living, and walked down out of it. If so, they are somewhere below now, and have probably forgotten most of what they knew, and their grandchildren are taller than they were and have ordinary lungs and ordinary eyes.

The second is that they went up. This is the stranger possibility, and the one the evidence faintly favors. The notches in the bark go up and up. In several of the great trees, if you climbed all the way to the canopy — which would take a day, with rests — you would find that the highest hollows have been modified: enlarged, lined, made habitable in a way the lower hollows are not. As if, at some point, the people began to live higher, and higher, and stopped coming down at all. The mottling on their skin thickened. The second eyelid grew more useful. Their relationship with the trees, which had always been close, grew closer. The trees, remember, run a slow metabolism. The trees, remember, have a faint script in their bark that is not the work of insects.

The third possibility is the one no one quite says, which is that there was never a sharp boundary between the climbers and the trees in the first place, and that what we are calling *the people leaving* is simply a phase of something longer — a generation that went into the wood and did not come back out as people, because that was always what happened, eventually, to the old ones whose hands looked like bark. The elders climb best of all. The elders weigh almost nothing. The elders, at the end, are carried up by the young and left in a high hollow with a bowl of water and a song, and the young come back down without them, and a season later the hollow is empty and the tree, perhaps, sings a slightly different note.

## What is left

A bone whistle on a cairn, tuned to a particular pitch. If you blow it, a particular tree, somewhere across the plateau, will answer — not the nearest one, but the right one, the one that knew the whistle's owner. The answer will roll across the open ground for several seconds before it reaches you, and in that time you will have the strong and not-quite-rational sense that something inside that tree has turned its attention toward you, and is waiting to see what you do next.

The climbers, if they are still climbers, are watching from the high hollows. The climbers, if they are something else now, are listening through the bark. Either way, the courteous thing — the thing their own etiquette would have required — is to sing your name back. Quietly. Once. And then to put the whistle down exactly where you found it, and to leave by the way you came, across the cold open rock, between the singing towers, under the black noon sky.
