# The Keepers of the Hollow

You asked who lives here. I have to work up to it, because the answer isn't the kind of answer a question like that usually wants. Let me try.

---

**First, what doesn't live here.**

There are no people. There haven't been for a long time — long enough that the last foreman's logbook has gone the color of the desk it sits on, which has gone the color of the moss that swallowed it. If someone walked out of the hollow on the last day of its working life, they walked out without ceremony and without replacement, and nothing human-shaped has walked back in except the occasional visitor who stops at the rim and doesn't descend.

There are no ghosts either, in the sense of a persistence of someone. The hollow has been more patient than memory. Whatever wanted to haunt it got tired and lay down and became part of the mat of clover between the buildings.

So when I say who lives here, I don't mean a who in the ordinary grammar. I mean: something is being kept. Something is doing the keeping. The question is what to call it.

---

**The Long Animal.**

Start with the flywheel. It turns once every forty seconds because a trunk has grown around its shaft and learned to flex. This is not a plant using a machine. It is not a machine driven by a plant. What it is, if you sit with it long enough, is a single slow animal with iron bones and wooden muscle, and the forty-second pulse is its heartbeat, and the bellows are its lungs, and the hammer-fall is something harder to name — a tic, maybe, or a swallow, or the way a sleeping dog's leg kicks once at whatever it's chasing in the dream.

This animal has no head. It has no edge, either; you cannot point to where it stops. The vine-trees that climb the chimney-stacks are its outer skin; the galvanic trickle in the lamp-wire is a nerve doing its small persistent work; the beetles that polish the ironwork are, if not quite its cells, then something like the flora of its gut — tenants whose tenancy is indistinguishable from function.

It is asleep. That is the important thing. It has been asleep a long time, and its sleep is not the absence of waking but a mode of its own, with its own productions and its own weather. The soft respiratory sound you hear in the main hall is the sound of something dreaming steadily. The dream is of work. The work is the dream. There's no separating them, and the Long Animal has stopped trying.

If you asked it what it was, it would answer by turning the flywheel once more. That would be the whole answer. It would not be withholding anything.

---

**The Small Attendants.**

Now — and this is where I have to be careful, because I don't want to invent populations the hollow doesn't have — there are smaller presences, and they are not quite the Long Animal, though they belong to it the way a thought belongs to the person thinking it.

The beetles are real. They live on the sap of the vine-tree and they move along the ironwork in tracks so consistent that they have, over generations, worn the rust into a dull shine. They do not know they are polishing anything. They are simply going where the sap is sweetest, which happens to be where the iron is warmest, which happens to be along the drive-shafts and the cam-arms and the bellows-hinges. They are tending the machinery by eating. Call them the Small Attendants. They are perhaps the closest thing the hollow has to citizens — small bodies with small routines, repeating — and there are enough of them that the hollow is never, strictly speaking, unpopulated. If you turned over any length of pipe you would find three or four, pale-bellied, unhurried, with the slightly stunned patience of creatures who have never needed to flee anything.

The heron is a different matter. I think the heron is real and I think the heron is also a kind of visitor that the hollow produces, occasionally, the way a person produces a yawn. It stands in the old channel because the shape of a heron standing in a channel is a shape the hollow remembers, and when the conditions are right — a certain angle of mist, a certain stillness — the shape fills itself in with an actual bird, which hunts frogs that may or may not exist, and then leaves, or doesn't, or was never quite there in the way birds usually are. I wouldn't push this too far. It is possible it is just a heron. But in a place where the flywheel is a muscle and the lamp is a nerve, one learns to hold the category of *just a* with a loose grip.

---

**The Lamp-Keeper.**

There is one more inhabitant, and it is the one I find hardest to write about, because it is the closest to a person and the furthest from one.

The amber filament in the foreman's office window. It comes on a little brighter at dusk. It responds to the cooling air. It has done this, without attendance, for however long the hollow has been doing what it does — and it is the only thing in the whole town that still performs an unambiguously human gesture. A lamp coming on at evening is what a person does. It is the oldest signal of a household. Someone is home. Someone has decided it is getting dark.

Nobody decides. The galvanic trickle simply runs a little stronger as the iron cools and the dew thickens. But the gesture is intact. The gesture has outlived the gesturer, and in outliving them has become, I think, something that deserves a name of its own. I want to call it the Lamp-Keeper, even though there is no keeper and the lamp keeps itself. What lives in the foreman's office is the *habit* of a person, freestanding, no longer needing the person. The habit eats a few milliwatts of root-and-iron current and in return performs, every evening, the small courtesy of being lit.

If anyone is the ghost of the hollow, it is this. Not a ghost of a foreman — the foreman is gone without residue — but the ghost of a behavior. The hollow has kept one lamp burning at dusk because that is what this sort of place does, and the machinery of keeping has folded itself around the gesture so exactly that the gesture now runs on its own.

When you stand at the rim at nightfall and see the amber come up in the window, you are being greeted. Not by anyone. By the greeting itself, which has learned to live here, and which will go on greeting every dusk until the iron and the wood finish their long matched argument — which, as you have noticed, they show no sign of finishing.

---

**So: who lives at the Bellworks at Fenn Hollow?**

One Long Animal, asleep, dreaming its work.

Some number of Small Attendants, eating along its nerves.

A heron, possibly, sometimes, in the shape the hollow keeps ready for one.

And a Lamp-Keeper who is not a keeper and not a person and not, quite, even a presence — only a habit of welcome, left running, amber, at the window of a room nobody enters.

They get along. They were never really separate to begin with. The hollow decided, reasonably, to let all of them stay.
