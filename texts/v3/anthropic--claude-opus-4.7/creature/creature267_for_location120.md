# The Caretaker

She is old, but not as old as the steps.

She comes down them once every nine or ten days, in no particular pattern, because the Sumphouse does not need her on a schedule — it needs her only often enough that the bowls don't overtop and the fig doesn't take the whole east wall. She knows this because her mother knew it, and her mother's aunt before that, though the line is not quite so clean as inheritance; it skipped once, and was picked up again by a woman who had only heard about the place from a drunk uncle and came down the steps to see if the story was true. That woman taught her grandmother. So the chain is more like the mortar in the walls — mostly continuous, picked at in places, holding anyway.

She is perhaps sixty. Her hands are the hands of someone who has done small careful work for a long time: knuckles thickened, nails kept short, a permanent faint green stain in the creases of her right thumb from handling the copper basin. She wears a linen shift and canvas shoes that she does not mind getting wet. She carries a satchel with three things in it: a short knife for the fig-roots (she prunes them, she does not cut them back; there's a difference, and she learned it the hard way one summer when she cut too hard and the chamber's humidity dropped for a month and the moths left and didn't come back until the following spring), a coil of waxed twine for when the broom needs rebinding or a bowl needs its handle lashed, and a small notebook that she almost never writes in but carries because her grandmother carried one.

She made the broom herself, last autumn. The bristles are river-reed from the culvert two fields over. She cuts them green and dries them in her attic and binds them when the moon is right — not because she believes in the moon particularly, but because her grandmother said *bind them in the waning and they'll last you a year*, and the brooms do last a year, so she keeps doing it. This is most of what she believes about most things.

She does not think of herself as the caretaker. If you asked her what she does down there she would shrug and say *I tidy*. She would not tell you, because she would not know how to say it, that what she actually does is *listen*: she comes down the steps and she sits on the stone bench by the cistern for perhaps a quarter of an hour before she touches anything, and in that quarter of an hour she is taking the chamber's pulse. She is hearing whether the cricket-song is full-bodied or thin (thin means drought on the surface, and she will leave an extra bowl of well-water in the pipe-runs for them). She is counting the drip-intervals without counting, feeling whether the tuning is right or whether one of the bowls has cracked and is singing wrong. She is watching the newt — there is almost always a newt, though not always the same one — to see if it comes out or stays under. She is smelling the fig.

Only after the quarter-hour does she get up and do what needs doing. Usually this is very little. She tips the copper basin, which fills faster than the others because it sits under the widest drip-cluster, and she pours its water not away but into the shallow pool where the sumpweed grows thinnest. She sweeps the tesserae — not back into the mosaic; that battle was lost long before her grandmother's time — but into the drifts along the walls, where they belong now. She checks the clay pipes for blockages by laying her ear against them and listening for the scrabble of rats, which tells her the pipes are clear; silence from a pipe is bad news and means she will have to come back tomorrow with a longer reed.

She feeds the fish. This is not strictly necessary — the fish eat the mosquito larvae and the drowned moths and whatever else falls into the cistern — but she throws in a pinch of millet anyway because her grandmother did, and because the fish come up for it, and she likes to see them. There are, as far as she can tell, about forty of them. She has never counted accurately. They are small and pale and her grandmother called them *the tenants*.

She talks, sometimes, while she works. Not to herself exactly, and not to the chamber as a whole — that would feel presumptuous. She talks to specific things. To the fig: *you are getting greedy again*. To a particular cricket whose song she thinks she can pick out of the chorus (she is probably wrong, but she is consistent): *good evening, sir*. To the newt: nothing, usually; the newt she just nods at.

She has never brought anyone down. She has thought about it. She has a niece who is twelve and clever and who would probably love the place, and she knows she will have to bring her eventually because the chain has to keep going, but she keeps putting it off because she is not sure the girl is ready to understand that the job is mostly *not doing things*. Most people, shown a chamber like this one, want to improve it. Want to re-lay the mosaic, re-point the bricks, clear out the rats, net the bats, cut back the fig entirely. Her grandmother told her: *the place will survive a caretaker who does too little. It will not survive one who does too much.* She thinks the niece might be a do-too-much sort. She is waiting to see.

When she is done, she sits again on the bench for a few minutes before going up. This is the part she has never told anyone about, because she does not have the words, but: she sits, and she lets the drip land on her. On her hands, her forearms, the crown of her head if she tilts it back. She lets herself be counted into the tuning for as long as she can stand the stillness, which is usually not very long — five minutes, eight. Then she gets up, because she is a practical woman and she has supper to make, and she climbs the sandstone steps, and the chamber closes behind her like a held breath that is in no hurry to be let out.

She will die, probably, before she finishes teaching the niece. This does not worry her as much as it used to. The chamber was here before her grandmother and it will be here after the niece, and if the chain breaks again it will be picked up again, because places like this generate their own caretakers the way the vault generates its own weather. Someone will come down the steps one day out of curiosity and feel the warmth and hear the tuning and understand, without being told, that a bowl needs tipping.

She believes this. It is most of what she believes about most things.
