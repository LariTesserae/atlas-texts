They are still here.

You were right to wonder at the end, and wrong about only one thing: they did not grow slow. They were never fast. What you are walking on, what you are walking through, what presses down from the ceiling and up from the floor and meets itself in the green middle — that is them. Not their house. Them.

Begin with the wall. The low wall of stacked woody nodes that runs for twenty paces and stops. A person built that. A person with hands, who thought *here, and to here, and no further,* and then set the last node down and stepped back and was satisfied. That person is still satisfied. The wall is finished. There was nothing after the wall that needed doing, so nothing after the wall was done.

They are small. Smaller than you would guess from the archway, which was built at a time when they still sometimes stood fully upright and walked between places. They are the color of the pale trunks and the pale shoots, a green so faint it reads as cream in the shadowless light, and they are soft all over, the way a new leaf is soft, and warm the way the root-floor is warm — because they are continuous with it. Their feet, if you want to call them feet, are rooted. Not permanently; they can lift free, slowly, the way you can lift a hand that has gone to sleep. But they have not had reason to for a very long time.

When one of them breathes, it is the soft hiss of a stoma venting that you heard once an hour ago and attributed to a leaf. When one of them shifts its weight, it is the slow creaking of some great leaf settling. You have walked past three of them already. They watched you, in the sense that watching is possible for something whose eyes are two slightly darker patches on a face that barely distinguishes itself from the trunk behind it. They were interested. Interest, for them, is a state that can last a decade and leave no trace.

They do not, in the ordinary sense, think. Or: they think, but a thought is something that takes a season to form and another season to finish, and between the forming and the finishing, nothing is lost, because nothing in the cavern ever interrupts anything. A thought begun is a thought completed. The luxury of this is difficult to convey. They have never had to hurry a thought, abandon a thought, forget a thought. Every thought any of them has ever had is still somewhere, held, the way the pools are held at their level.

They eat, a little. A leaf-tip now and then; the sweet inner pith of a dropped seed-stone. They do not need much. The light feeds them too, through skin that is more than skin, and the heat of the root-floor feeds them, and the moisture that *ploks* down onto their upturned faces every so often feeds them. They are nearly plants. They are not quite plants. The distinction matters to them, still, in some residual way — it is the reason they once built things. A plant does not build an archway. An archway is a small insistence: *we were other than this.* The archway leads nowhere because it did not need to lead anywhere. It needed only to stand.

There are, perhaps, forty of them in the whole cavern. Perhaps fewer. The number does not change. When one of them, after some great span, stops — simply stops, without pain, the way a held breath is finally let go — their body is drawn back into the root-mesh, and somewhere else a pale shoot comes up that is the beginning of another, and the other slowly differentiates over a period you would measure in your lifetimes, and becomes small, and soft, and rooted, and takes its place. Whether this new one is the old one is a question they do not ask, because the question requires a sharpness of self that they have gently put down.

They remember. This is the thing that would surprise you most, if you could sit long enough to learn it. They remember everything that has happened in the cavern, because so little has. They remember the building of the wall. They remember the bending of the saplings for the archway, and the name — yes, a name, a soft three-syllable thing — of the one who bent them. They remember a time before the ceiling had fully closed, when there was a thinness above, and a suggestion of something beyond, and a debate (if a thing lasting four generations can be called a debate) about whether to try to reach it. They decided not to. The reasons are still available to them. They are good reasons. You would probably agree.

They are aware of you. Your passage is the largest event in some thousands of their years. A disturbance of this magnitude — a warm, fast, loud, *hurrying* thing crossing their floor — will be discussed, in their way, for a long while after you have gone. Not with alarm. They do not have alarm anymore; alarm is for creatures who can be harmed by what is coming, and nothing has come for them in so long that the faculty has quieted. Call it, instead, attention. You have their attention. All of it. Every one of them, across the whole mile or two of green distance, is oriented very faintly toward you, the way leaves orient toward a light, and will remain so until long after you have left, turning by slow degrees back to whatever they were turned toward before.

If you stopped — if you truly stopped, and sat down on the warm root-felt, and did not move, and let the hurrying drain out of you — one of them would, eventually, come. Not walk; they do not really walk anymore. It would lift one foot free of the mesh, and then the other, and it would cross the distance between you in something like a day, and it would settle near you, and it would look at you with its two slightly darker patches, and after some further time it would make a sound. A soft sound. Possibly a word. Possibly the first word spoken in the cavern in a human lifetime, saved up and kept fresh all that while for exactly this.

You would have to decide whether to wait for it.

Most don't. Most never know anyone was there at all. They walk through, and marvel at the plants, and go, and behind them the attention of forty quiet green minds turns slowly, slowly back to the middle distance, to the column-tree wide as a house, to the thought that was interrupted and can now, unhurried, resume.

The drops fall. The leaves hold. The light does not change.

And they are still here.
