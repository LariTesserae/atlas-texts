The inhabitants here are not what you'd first call inhabitants. They don't move toward food or away from danger. They don't have faces. But they are alive, and the cleft is densely populated — perhaps as densely populated, by mass, as a tropical forest floor — so let me describe who's actually here.

---

**The moss layer** is the bulk of the population, and it is older than it looks. The cushions are colonies — each cushion a federation of thousands of individual shoots packed shoulder-to-shoulder, the outer ones photosynthesizing what dim blue light they can, the inner ones acting as a wet sponge that holds the colony's water reserve against the gusts. They have given up on the idea of individuality in any meaningful sense. A cushion is the unit. When you press your finger into one, you are touching a single organism that is also ten thousand organisms, and the distinction has stopped mattering to them, if it ever did.

The species stack in a particular order. At the bottom of any given cushion, the founders — dead now, compressed into a peaty mat, but still structurally holding everything else up. They arrived first, probably in the decades right after the cleft opened, blown in as spores from lower slopes. Above them, a middle generation, half-living: the lower stems senescent, the upper centimeters still green. And on top, the current cohort, plush and turgid, doing the actual work. Each cushion is therefore a small chronology. If you cored one you could probably read the wet centuries off it like tree rings.

They are extraordinarily patient. A cushion the size of a dinner plate may be four hundred years old. In that time it has gained perhaps two centimeters of height. It has survived the cleft's roaring through a strategy of being too low, too dense, and too wet to be torn loose — the gusts pass over the top of the cushion and stir only the outermost shoots, while everything beneath continues its slow business. Mosses are the cleft's true citizens. They got here first and they will be here last.

**The lichens** are the cleft's chemists. They are doing something the mosses can't, which is eating the rock. Each crust is a fungus and an alga (sometimes a cyanobacterium too) living so deeply enmeshed that they have become a single organism with two genomes and one project. The project is: dissolve stone, extract minerals, fix carbon, persist. They do it slowly. A plate-like crust the size of a coin may have taken a century to spread that far. The mustard-yellow ones secrete acids that pit the rock visibly — if you looked closely at the wall under a lichen, you'd see the stone has been etched away in a shallow basin shaped exactly like the lichen above it. They are eating their bed. They are also, by doing so, generating the first thin grit of soil for everything else.

Lichens don't mind the roaring. They are flat to the wall, glued chemically into the rock, with no exposed surfaces to catch the wind. They are unbothered by cold below any temperature the cleft can produce. They are unbothered by the darkness — they need very little light, and they shut down entirely when it's too dark or too dry, going dormant for weeks at a time without injury. If anyone in the cleft has the temperament of a long-haul inhabitant, it's them. They are barely participating in time at the rate the rest of us understand it.

**The ferns** are the cleft's gesture-makers. They live where there's just enough moisture pooling out of horizontal seep-lines that they can throw a frond into the air. Each fern is a single individual — a rhizome wedged into a crack, with a few fronds extending. They flex and whip in the gusts and this looks dramatic but is mostly fine; ferns are built for this, their fronds are essentially designed to bend without snapping. What costs them is when a frond tears off entirely, which happens, and which they replace from the rhizome in the slow season. They are perhaps the cleft's most visibly *active* residents — the ones whose lives include something that looks like motion, like response, like a kind of waving.

**The liverworts** along the seep-lines are the wettest things alive in here. They are essentially leaf-shaped sheets of cells one or two layers thick, plastered against the rock by capillary water. They have no real structural rigidity — when the wind lifts them, they lift; when it stops, they fall back. They are doing photosynthesis at the absolute lowest light any green thing can manage. They glisten. If you put your face close to a liverwort sheet you would see, in the dimness, that it shines wetly with a faint internal greenness, the chlorophyll dense enough to read as color even in the blue gloom.

**The alpine shrubs** higher up are the cleft's exceptions, its almost-vertebrates. There are perhaps a dozen of them, total, in the whole cleft — small twisted woody things, none taller than a hand-span, clinging to cracks where a little more light reaches and a little soil has accumulated. They are old. The oldest of them, a gnarled cushion-plant tucked into a south-facing fissure about twelve meters up, is probably six or seven hundred years old. Its trunk is the thickness of a pencil. It has survived every storm the cleft has hosted since before whatever country claims this mountain had its current name. It produces, in some years, a few small flowers, which are visited by no pollinator because there are no pollinators here, and which self-fertilize, and which produce a few seeds, which mostly blow out of the cleft and die, and which occasionally — once a century, maybe — lodge in a crack and start another shrub.

**The cyanobacteria and algae in the seep-films** — these are the invisible majority. Every wet surface that isn't already occupied by something larger is coated in a microscopic biofilm: bacteria, algae, the occasional protist. They are doing the foundational chemistry. They fix nitrogen out of the air, which everything else then uses. They are the reason there's any nitrogen here at all, in a place where no animal has ever died to enrich the soil. They are also, in their slow film-forming, gluing the cleft together at the molecular level — every surface is slightly stickier than bare rock because of them, and the mosses and lichens find purchase partly on the films they leave behind. They are the cleft's substrate-makers. Nothing else here could live without them, and they are entirely unaware of this, being bacteria.

---

Now: what does it feel like to be these inhabitants, collectively? Because this is a place, and the question of who lives here is also the question of what kind of life this is.

It is a slow life. The fastest thing happening in this cleft, biologically, is a fern frond growing perhaps a few millimeters in a week. Everything else is slower by an order of magnitude or more. The lichens advance across the rock at the rate continents drift. The cushion-plant at twelve meters has not, in the last fifty years, done anything an observer with a stopwatch would call an event.

It is also, despite the roaring, a *quiet* life. The noise of the cleft is not the noise the inhabitants make. They make no noise. They have no nervous systems, no muscles, no signaling beyond chemistry. The roar is the weather happening over them, and they neither attend to it nor ignore it — they simply continue their photosynthesis and their slow accretion and their patient eating of stone, while the wind howls overhead.

It is a life without anyone in particular. No individual in this cleft has a name or a story in the way an animal might. There are no decisions being made. There is no foraging, no fleeing, no waiting. There is only the long collective effort of the photosynthesizers and the rock-eaters, generation overlapping generation, each one slightly improving the conditions for the next, while at the geological scale the cleft itself slowly fills with soil and slowly, slowly, becomes the kind of place where, in another ten thousand years, something with legs might come and live.

That is who lives here: the patient ones. The ones who have learned to be small, wet, dense, and chemically clever, and who can wait out the wind. They are not impressive individually. Collectively, they are a body — a green slow body, plastered to wet stone, breathing on the order of centuries — and the cleft is their chest cavity, and the wind plays through them, and they do not notice, because they are busy living.
