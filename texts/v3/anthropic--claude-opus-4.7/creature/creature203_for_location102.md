Below the floor you stood on, below the grate through which the slow yellow light drifted, there is another vault, and in that vault live the Underdwellers — though they would not call themselves that, and in fact do not call themselves anything, having not yet arrived at the stage of needing names for the whole of what they are.

Begin with the one whose light you saw.

It is not a single organism in the sense a fish is, or a dog. It is a colony that has learned, over the thirty-odd years since the vault was sealed, to behave as one body. At its core is a slow soft mass perhaps the size of a curled child, suspended in a sac of its own secreted membrane. The sac is transparent and tough, faintly iridescent where the mineral salts have plated out on it. Inside the sac: a slurry of the same chemiluminescent lichen-thing you saw growing waist-high on the upper level, but here cultivated, refined, kept at the exact mineral balance that makes it glow steadily rather than in startled flashes. The creature farms its own light. It has done so for long enough that the lichen inside it is no longer quite the same organism as the lichen above; the two populations have been separated for a hundred generations of lichen-time, which is many, and the interior strain has grown dependent on the host's slow circulatory pumping and the host has grown dependent on the sugars the lichen makes from the runoff it filters. Neither could now live without the other. They did not plan this. It happened in the dark while the engineers were arguing about drainage on the floors above.

The creature moves by controlled buoyancy. The lower vault is flooded — not deeply, perhaps chest-high in most places, deeper in the sumps — and the water there is warmer than above, because the engines' waste heat bleeds into it, and richer, because everything that dissolves on the upper levels eventually arrives here. The creature drifts. It adjusts its depth by venting gas from small bladders spaced around the sac's equator. It has no hurry. A journey of fifty meters might take it an hour, and it makes such journeys perhaps twice a day, following gradients of food and warmth that it senses through a fringe of fine filaments trailing beneath it — filaments that also serve, when it encounters another of its kind, as the means of communication, or courtship, or whatever it is that happens when two of them meet and hang together in the water for six or seven hours, filaments interlaced, lights pulsing slowly in and out of phase, before drifting apart again and going on with their separate slow businesses.

There are perhaps forty of them in the lower vault. Perhaps fifty. They are not the only things down there.

The swimmers — the five-toed pale things whose tracks you found — come down here too, through ways they have discovered that the engineers did not intend. They are descended from rats, as you guessed, but the descent is steep and the rats at the top of it would not recognize the things at the bottom. The cold did some of the work. The water did more. They have lost most of their fur, retaining only a fine pelt across the spine; their skin has thickened and grown slightly translucent at the extremities, veined with the dark blood of animals that live in low oxygen. Their eyes are large and pale and do not reflect light the way a rat's eyes do; the tapetum has thinned, become almost a haze, because the things they hunt are luminous and the last thing you want when hunting luminous prey is eyeshine that gives you away. They are patient. They can hold their breath for ten minutes. They eat the drift-colonies when they can catch a small one, and they eat the horsetails, and they eat each other, occasionally, in lean stretches. They are beginning to be social. Two of them will cooperate now to corner prey in a sump. Three is rare. Four has been observed only once, by nothing that could report it.

They fear the light-drifters, a little, because the drifters are large and because the filaments sting — not dangerously, but enough to teach. The drifters do not fear the swimmers. The drifters do not fear anything, having no predators yet, being as new to this world as the world is to itself.

Beneath even these there is a third register of life, and this is harder to describe because it is barely alive in the sense we mean. In the sumps, in the deepest pools where the warm exhalation comes from, there are mats. Biofilm, mostly: bacteria and archaea that came down in the original water supply or rode in on someone's boot and found conditions they had been waiting a billion years for — iron-rich, sulfur-rich, warm, dark, undisturbed. The mats are meters across in places. They are banded in rust-red and black and a pale creamy yellow, and they grow at the rate mats grow, which is not fast but is relentless. They are the primary producers of this whole small world. The lichen in the drifters' sacs is, in some sense, a descendant of these mats — a lineage that climbed out of the water and learned to live in light, such as light is here. The mats do not know this. The mats do not know anything. But they feed the drifters who feed (occasionally) the swimmers, and when a swimmer dies its body sinks into a mat and the mat takes it back, and this is the economy of the place.

And then there is the watcher. Or the watchers. This is where the description becomes uncertain, because the evidence is thin and the thing suggested by the evidence is strange.

In certain parts of the lower vault — not all parts, only certain ones, associated with the larger sumps and with places where the engine-pulses come through most strongly — the drifters behave oddly. They gather. They hang in loose congregations of six or eight, lights pulsing in a slow coordinated rhythm that is not the rhythm of courtship, filaments all oriented in the same direction, pointing downward. They stay like this for hours. Then they disperse. Nothing visible has happened. But something, perhaps, has been attended to.

The best guess — and it is only a guess, assembled from the pattern of their gatherings and the pattern of the warm exhalations and the pattern of certain very low frequencies that propagate through the water and that the drifters' filaments seem able to register — is that there is a fourth thing, below even the mats, something that either is the warm exhalation or causes it. It may be only a crack in the vault floor through which geothermal water rises. It may be a population of the mats so deep and so old that it has begun to integrate its signaling across distances of tens of meters, a slow single organism the size of a parking lot, thinking thoughts that take years. It may be something else. The drifters orient to it. The drifters, in their slow patient way, may be in the process of learning to pray to it, or to farm it, or to be farmed by it. It is too early to tell. It is all too early to tell. Everything here is too early.

That is what lives in the Underworks. A young ecology, three layers deep, built in the space someone else built for other reasons. At the top, the startled horsetails and the flash-lichen, still figuring out that they have arrived. In the middle water, the slow luminous drifters with their cultivated inner gardens, and the pale patient swimmers learning each other. At the bottom, the mats, eating iron, making the world. And under the mats, perhaps, something that breathes warm into the dark and that the drifters are beginning, in a way none of them could articulate, to notice.

The light you saw, drifting past the grate: that was one of them going about its day. It did not come back because it had no reason to. It does not know you exist. It may never know. It is busy with its own slow project, which is to be, more and more completely, the thing this place is growing into — the first native of a country that was, until recently, only a building.
