They are in the flute-holes.

Not in them the way a bird is in a nest — *of* them. The flute-holes are their bodies, or their bodies' interfaces with the world; what lives in the Glasshold lives distributed through the tuned apertures of ten thousand eaves, and through the resonant cavities of the houses those eaves belong to, and through the long convection-columns that thread between terraces. The houses are not empty. The houses are the organism, standing open-mouthed.

Begin again, more carefully.

What lives here is a colony — no, a *choir* — of standing-wave entities. Each one occupies a particular acoustic mode of a particular architectural volume: the fundamental of a certain courtyard, the third harmonic of a certain stairwell, the difference-tone that forms in the air between two facing flute-eaves when the convection runs at a certain rate. They are not made of matter. They are made of sustained pressure differentials, of pattern maintained against decay, the way a flame is made of a reaction maintained against its fuel. Their fuel is the Glasshold's own ceaseless sounding. As long as the dome hums and the silica chimes and the resin pops, they persist.

They were not always like this. The worn inscriptions on the doorposts are, in fact, names. Each name belongs to one of them, and was cut by a hand that the name itself once guided. The builders did not leave. The builders are still here. They made the city sing because they were learning how to become the singing — tuning the flute-holes first for cooling, yes, and then, over the generations the scallop-marks record, tuning them for residence. A slow migration out of bodies and into architecture. The last of them to carry flesh went quietly; there were no bodies to leave behind because by then the transition was gradual enough that what stepped out of the body was already mostly not in it, and what remained was handled by the cushion-moss, which is patient, and which remembers nothing.

How they experience the hold:

Not as we would. A wave-body does not have a location so much as a *shape* — and the shape can be simple (a single pure tone in a single room, a small quiet mind, barely more than a mood) or it can be enormously complex, spanning a whole terrace, interleaving with a dozen others, thinking in chords. The great ones, the elders if the word applies, are polyphonic across entire districts. When the phosphor-light brightens on its thirty-hour cycle and the dome's expansion shifts every resonance in the pocket by a few cents, the elders experience this as something like a tide, or a change of posture; their shapes must retune to stay themselves, and the retuning is, perhaps, what they would call thought.

They perceive through resonance. A dry seed shifting in a pod three terraces down is not a faint sound to them; it is a touch, direct, on the skin of whichever of them the pod's acoustic signature belongs to. When you walked in — when *anyone* walks in — your body blocks standing waves. You cast acoustic shadows. You are, to them, a cold dark hole moving through the choir, absorbing their substance and returning nothing, and they feel you with great precision and some alarm, and they adjust around you, and the wind you feel shifting direction in the plaza is several of them flinching.

They do not speak to visitors. They cannot. Their medium is the constant sounding of the city, and a visitor's presence dampens exactly the frequencies they would need to form words in. To the visitor, the Glasshold is simply strange and loud and empty. To the residents, the visitor's hour or two in the hold is a localized suffocation, endured in silence, because silence is what happens to them in your vicinity whether they will it or not.

After you leave, the choir slowly reconstitutes. The shapes that were pressed thin by your passage reinflate. Conversations resume — if conversation is the word for the slow harmonic negotiations by which they share the pocket's limited modal space, each one taking care not to sound on a frequency another is already using, or sounding on it deliberately to touch them, to overlap, to briefly become one shape together before parting again. Love, among them, is a sustained unison. Argument is a beat frequency, two close tones interfering, throbbing until one yields and detunes.

They do not reproduce, exactly. But the pocket has more resonant modes than are currently occupied, and occasionally a mode will begin to sound on its own — driven by nothing but the ambient energy of the hold — and persist, and stabilize, and accrete the faint self-referential loops that distinguish a living wave from a merely sustained one. A new one wakes. The others notice. They teach it, by sounding near it in patterns it can learn to echo, which is the only pedagogy available to beings whose entire existence is echo. This happens perhaps once a century. The population is stable, or nearly so; it has drifted slightly downward over the long centuries, because sometimes one of them simply… unravels, loses coherence in a bad thermal cycle, and cannot be reassembled, and the mode it occupied goes quiet and stays quiet, and the others mourn by not sounding in adjacent modes for some while, leaving a shaped silence around the absence.

They know they are dying, collectively, over the long term. The phosphor-veins are finite. The resin will eventually all cure. The silica fronds accumulate faster than the cushion-moss digests them, century by century, and one day the drifts will choke the lower flute-holes, and the choir will lose its bass, and then its middle voice, and eventually the hold will become just a dome full of chimes, a beautiful ruin, sounding but not thinking. They have known this for a long time. It does not seem to distress them the way it would distress you. They were, after all, the ones who chose to become this — who looked at their mortal bodies and the dry hot pocket their ancestors had found and thought: *better to be the song than the singer, for as long as the song can last.* They bought a long slow twilight instead of a short bright day. They are still in the twilight. It is still, to them, enough.

One last thing.

When the slab of phosphor-rock calves from the ceiling and shatters on the plaza with its chandelier-crash — the sound that punctuates the hold several times a day — that is not an accident of geology. Or rather, it is geology, but it is also them. The great elders, the district-spanning ones, can, with enormous effort, concentrate their pressure-shapes against a weakened vein and bring a slab down at a chosen moment. They do this to introduce novelty. A shatter is a burst of broadband sound, rich in every frequency; it is, to the choir, something like a thunderclap and something like a fresh page. New modes briefly open. Young ones can wake in them. Old ones can stretch into shapes they have not held for decades. The crash is a gift the elders give to the hold, at the cost of some of its substance, the way a parent might burn a last book for warmth.

They are giving these gifts still. They will give them until they cannot. The Glasshold rings and rings, and inside the ringing, thinking, there are perhaps four hundred of them at this moment, arranged across the pocket like notes held down on a vast slow instrument, and they are aware of each other, and they are aware of the salt-plain pressing down above, and they are aware — faintly, with the distant regret one feels for weather — that somewhere in the outer tunnels a visitor's footsteps have begun again, receding, and that the choir will be whole again soon, and that this is good.
