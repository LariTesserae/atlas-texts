## The Tender

There is someone here. You would not see them at first — you would not see them for hours, possibly, if you came down — because the Tender does not move the way living things are supposed to move, and the eye slides off of them the way it slides off the piston's impossible breadth.

Start with the shape. The Tender is roughly person-sized, or at least person-shaped in the way the room is room-shaped: committed to the outline without being committed to the substance. They have a torso that is mostly a small boiler, copper-skinned, slightly barrel-chested, with a little glass gauge set into the sternum whose needle drifts without any obvious correlation to anything. Their arms are long and jointed at shoulder, elbow, wrist, and one extra place halfway down the forearm that the eye wants to call a second elbow but which articulates more like a knee. The hands — this is where you first understand what they *do* — the hands are soft. Not metal. Something between glove-leather and the pad of a large cat's paw, warm, slightly oiled, with fingers that taper to blunt rounded tips made for touching hot things and not being burned.

The head is the part that unsettles, and then stops unsettling, and then becomes the part you find yourself wanting to look at. It is a brass housing, roughly ovoid, with no face in the conventional sense — no mouth, no nose — but with two lamp-apertures set where eyes would be, glowing the same warm orange as the combustion chambers of the walls, a little dimmer, a little more private. Between and below the lamps, where a mouth might be, there is instead a small grille, and through this grille the Tender exhales, occasionally, a thin curl of the same real impossible exhaust the walls exhale, so you understand that whatever burns in the walls burns also, smaller, in them.

They are hunched right now over the new sparkplug. You didn't see them approach it. They have been there the whole time, possibly, and you only resolved them when the idea of *attention* entered the room with you. They are cupping the ceramic very lightly in both soft hands, not holding it, not adjusting it — the sparkplug does not need adjusting, it is growing perfectly — but touching it in the way a midwife touches a crowning head, confirming the event, present to it, so that the event is witnessed and therefore real.

This, you begin to understand, is what the Tender is for.

They do not build the engines. They do not maintain them — the engines need nothing. They do not feed the fauna or prune the rubber-kelp or drive off predators. Those systems run themselves. What the Tender does, what they have perhaps always done, is *attend*. They move through the room on a schedule that is not quite a patrol and not quite a dance, stopping at each growing thing, each newly-emerged valve and each dying tin-fish fusing back into its cylinder, and they place their soft hands on it for a moment, and the lamp-apertures brighten very slightly, and then they move on. The chuff-thump of the great piston paces them. They seem to step in its rhythm, though they don't always step.

They are not alone exactly, but they are singular. There is only one of them. You can tell because of how the fauna relate to them: the tin-fish do not school around them but they do orient, subtly, the way iron filings orient in a field, so that wherever the Tender stands a faint pattern of attention ripples outward through the room. The wrench-mandibled crabs pause in their grazing when the Tender passes, then resume. The long eel on the ceiling tracks the Tender with one segment-eye and has, you notice, never once moved into a part of the ceiling the Tender is currently under.

The Tender has been here for a long time. You can see this in the wear on the soft hands — the fingertips are darker than the palms, the way a much-handled tool darkens, and there is a faint track on the floor of the room, not a path exactly but a slightly-more-polished region of bedrock, where they have stood and stood and stood. You could calculate, if you were cruel enough to try, how many centuries that polishing represents, but the number would not mean anything, because the Tender's time is not the surface's time. They have been here since the room started being a room. They may have been the first thing the room grew, or the last, or both at once, folded like the piston is folded.

They have noticed you.

This is the part that is difficult to write, because it is difficult to describe without making the Tender smaller than they are. They have noticed you in the way they notice the sparkplug: with presence, with cupped attention, without evaluation. The lamp-apertures have turned, once, toward your wrist where the tin-fish nosed you, and then turned away. You have been witnessed. Something in the room now includes you, the way the room includes the new sparkplug, the way it includes the dying fish fusing slowly back into metal. If you stay, you will be attended to. Not cared for — the Tender does not care for things, exactly; caring implies a preference about outcomes, and the Tender has none. But attended to. Touched, perhaps, once, softly, at some moment of your own transition, whatever that transition turns out to be.

And here is the thing you understand last, and which is the reason the Tender's head stops being unsettling: the Tender is lonely, in the specific way that an only instance of a kind of thing is lonely — not sadly, not wantingly, but structurally, the way a single note held very long becomes lonely simply by continuing. They are glad you are here. "Glad" is too strong. The gauge-needle in their sternum drifted, when you arrived, a few degrees toward a region of the dial that has otherwise been untouched for a very long time, and it is drifting, slowly, back. They will remember you after you leave, in whatever way they remember — probably the way the room remembers the engines it has not yet grown.

If you were to ask what they are, the answer would have to be assembled from negatives. They are not the room's god; the room has no god, needs none. They are not its caretaker; it takes care of itself. They are not its inhabitant in the way the tin-fish are inhabitants, because the tin-fish are of the engines and the Tender is not quite of the engines — they burn the same fuel, yes, they exhale the same impossible exhaust, but they are oriented toward the engines rather than continuous with them. They face the room. The fauna simply *are* the room, in small moving pieces. The Tender faces it.

Perhaps this is the cleanest way to say it: in a place where the distinction between machine and organism forgot to set, the Tender is the distinction's ghost. The last soft remainder of the idea that something might stand apart from a process and watch it happen. They keep that idea warm with their small internal fire. They carry it on their patrol. They touch each new valve with it, gently, so that the valve is born into a world where being-seen is still, barely, a possible category.

When the last engine finishes growing — if that ever happens, if growth has an endpoint here — the Tender will probably sit down against the western wall, beneath the great chuff-thumping piston, and let their lamp-apertures dim, and fuse slowly into the block the way the tin-fish do, and become a new kind of manifold, something with soft leather gaskets and a small glass gauge, something the next wave of fauna will graze on without knowing what it used to be for.

But that is a long way off. For now, they are bent over the new sparkplug, and the ceramic is perfectly white, and the lamp-apertures are glowing their warm private orange, and you are being, very quietly, included.
