There is one who lives here. Just the one.

You will not see them, but they are not hiding. They are simply distributed — the way the sound is distributed, the way the steam is distributed. To call them a body would be to misunderstand the arrangement. They are the rhythm under the rhythm, the twenty-minute swell, the slow breath the pools take. The reciprocating thump is closer to a pulse than a piston. The hiss is closer to a throat than a valve. If you sat very still for a week and let the saturated air dissolve the boundary of yourself entirely, you might come to feel that the engine-noise is not produced by anything in the hollow but rather *is* the hollow's tenant, in the way singing is a singer when the singer has been singing for so long they have forgotten how to stop.

They are old. Not old like a tortoise or a yew, but old in the geological mode — old enough that the basalt is their juvenile coat, freshly grown, still sharp at the cuticle. The pipe and the flywheel and the brass plate are vestigial. Once, perhaps, the tenant was more mechanically inclined, or had reason to interface with something that wanted flanges and bolts and engraved lettering. They have outgrown the need. The metal is being absorbed the way a child's milk teeth are absorbed, slowly, without ceremony, by the larger body coming in underneath. In a thousand years the mouth will be smooth.

What do they want? This is the question the survey crews could not bear, and it is why they stopped coming. Not because the tenant is hostile — the tenant is not hostile, the tenant scarcely registers a human visitor as anything more than a brief local thickening of the humidity — but because to stand in the hollow long enough to formulate the question is to begin to suspect the answer, and the answer is uncongenial to the surveying disposition.

They are making something. The plum-colored column is part of it, sweating its slow contribution into the warm pool. The sterile water is part of it, the iron and ozone the residue of a chemistry running at a tempo that does not admit of catalysis by mere microbes — bacteria would only get in the way, would only contaminate the gestate, and so the water is kept clean the way an operating theater is kept clean, by a constant background pressure that excludes life of the wrong scale. The engine-sound is the labor of it. The steam is the breath of it. The twenty-minute swell is the contraction of a vast slow musculature working at something that will not be finished in any human frame of reference.

Whether the thing being made is a child, or a thought, or simply the next phase of the maker — whether the tenant is mother, mind, or larva of itself — is not a distinction the hollow recognizes. It may be that in the tenant's mode of being these are the same word. Certainly the column at the center has the look of something both organ and offspring, both placenta and fetus, sweating itself into existence over centuries.

The tenant is not alone in the world. There are others. The survey crews could not agree on the location of the hollow because the hollow is one of several mouths, and the mouths shift, and what one crew found on Tuesday in the north face another crew found on Thursday in the west, and the slot is wherever the tenant is currently exhaling. Somewhere under the basalt province, the network of them lies in their warm dark, breathing into their respective hollows, each tending its slow gestate, each producing its incidental clatter of machinery-sound from no machinery at all.

They do not communicate with each other in any sense a human would recognize as communication. They do not need to. They are doing the same work, at the same tempo, for the same long reason, and the synchrony is a property of the substrate rather than of any signal between them. When one finishes — and one will, eventually, finish — the others will know in the way a body knows when a held breath has been released elsewhere in the body.

What emerges, when one of them finishes, has not yet emerged in recorded history. The basalt province is young. The tenants are younger than the rock, but only just. They have been at this work since before there was anyone to wonder what was being made, and they will be at it for some time yet.

If you stand in the hollow and the steam parts for a moment and you see, through the gap, the plum column glistening, and you feel in your molars the long inhale beginning its twenty-minute climb, you are looking at someone at home. You are a guest. You have been noticed only in the sense that a swimmer notices a mote in the water — which is to say, not at all, and yet the mote is nevertheless within the swimmer, and the swimmer's body is doing, around the mote, whatever bodies do around motes, gently, without malice, without particular attention.

You should probably leave before the next exhale. Not because anything will happen to you. Because nothing will, and a long enough exposure to nothing-happening, in a place where something is so patiently being made, has been known to unmake the visitor's sense that they themselves are anything other than a brief local thickening of the humidity. Some of the early survey crews did not leave. They are not dead. They are simply, now, part of what the hollow is breathing with.
