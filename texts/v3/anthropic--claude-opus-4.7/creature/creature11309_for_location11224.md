# What Lives in the Sounding Hollow

## The Tenders

Begin with the smallest first, because they are easiest to be sure of.

In the leaf-cisterns — those cupped reservoirs the great fronds hold up to nothing — live the **chirrers**. Call them insects, though they are not, quite. They have six limbs and a chitin-feel, but their bodies are mostly hollow, mostly resonator; the chirring is not a stridulation but a deliberate vibration of a tympanic sac filled with the cistern-water they were born in. Each chirrer carries a teaspoon of its home inside itself for life, and the pitch of its song is the pitch of that water — its mineral load, its age, its history of evaporation. They sing the chemistry of where they came from. A naturalist with a long enough ear could map the hollow's water table by listening.

They do not bite, do not sting, do not flee. They land on the back of your hand and chirr, and you are briefly part of the survey.

Then the **bell-frogs**, who you already know about — but know this too: they do not lay eggs. They bud. A bell-frog in its long maturity grows a swelling at the base of its throat, a second resonance chamber, and when the chamber is tuned the frog splits along a seam that has been there all its life, and there are two frogs, each carrying half the old song and half a new one. This is why the chorus shifts key only once a season: the frogs are slow at becoming themselves. A bell-frog might be five hundred years old. The oldest of them have throats so refined that they ring on the second harmonic of the clicking machine, and when one of these elders sings, the machine *answers* — a perceptible variance in its interval, a half-beat of acknowledgment. Then both return to their patience.

In the moss live the **softfeet**. These are the warm things that walk beside you for ten paces. They are perhaps the size of a small dog, perhaps larger; their shape is hard to hold in the mind because they have no need to be any particular shape and have drifted, over generations, into a kind of architectural looseness — long-bodied, low, with limbs that fold and unfold according to the terrain. Their fur is the same temperature as the air, always, by some regulation we would call uncanny. They are warm-blooded but they have no body heat to give away; they are exactly the hollow, in animal form. They eat the chirrers, gently, and the budding-scars of the bell-frogs, which the frogs shed like fingernails. They have eyes but the eyes are vestigial pits, kept perhaps for the memory of having had them. They navigate by the gradient — the same warm pattern you noticed — and by sound, and by something else.

The **gliders** in the canopy are stranger and you will not see them, not in any meaningful sense. They are flatter than they should be, broad as a cloak, and their leathery exhalations are not breath but a kind of echolocation conducted through the membrane of their bodies — they pulse and feel the pulse return. They eat what flies, and things fly here, smaller things, a whole tier of the hollow you have not yet inventoried. The gliders have been known (by whom? — by the softfeet, who know things in their way) to settle on the warm structures and rest with their bodies spread flat against the humming stone. They tune themselves. They leave changed. Whatever they are doing up there, in the dark canopy, may be the structures' work continuing through them — a slow biological annex of the old machinery.

## The Slow Thing

Below.

Begin with what you noticed last: the warmth, the gradient, the body-pattern. It is not metaphor. There is a creature beneath the moss, and the moss is its skin, and the basin is the curve of its side as it sleeps.

It is not a god. It is not a buried titan. It is — it was — a participant. Whatever made the structures, whatever the structures were finishing, this thing was part of the work. Perhaps a partner of the makers; perhaps the makers themselves, in a form they took at the end, when the work was done and the only thing left was to slow down, to slow down further, to become climate. Its breathing — the deep periodic *whump* in the moss — is on the order of one breath per hour, or per day; it is hard to tell because each exhalation shapes the local weather and the moss-fogs are partly its sigh.

It does not dream in any sense we have. But it remembers, in a substrate-y way, and the hollow's whole ecology is something like its memory made continuous — the frogs holding the tones, the chirrers logging the water, the softfeet patrolling the gradient, the gliders maintaining the high air. Each living thing here is a small loop in a vast retention. The slow thing does not need to think because the hollow is thinking on its behalf, slowly, slowly, at the only speed that matters anymore.

The clicking machine is keeping its time. Not the slow thing's heartbeat — the slow thing has no heart in the singular — but something more like its tempo of presence. As long as the clicking continues, the slow thing is here. When the clicking stops, which it will, in some century or in some thousand centuries, the slow thing will finish its long exhalation and the hollow will cool by one degree, then another, and the bell-frogs will let go their tuning, and the moss-fogs will thin, and the warmth-pattern will dissolve into ambient cold, and a long process that began before the mountains rounded will at last be done.

## The Makers

Of the makers themselves, nothing remains that can be pointed to and called a maker. They are not here in the way the softfeet are here, padding beside you. But they are not *gone*, either, in any sense the hollow would recognize.

Consider: the structures still work. The frogs are still tuned. The slow thing still breathes. The clicking still counts. Something is still being done. If a process continues, can its initiator be said to have left?

The honest answer is that the makers diffused. They put themselves into the work. They built the structures, woke the slow thing, taught the first bell-frogs their tones, and then — over a length of time that makes the rounding of the mountains look brisk — they relaxed their boundaries. Some became the slow thing, joining it. Some became the structures' hum, which is not poetic: the hum is patterned, and the pattern, if you could slow your hearing by a factor of ten thousand, would resolve into something almost like speech, almost like a conversation between the twelve structures that has been ongoing since before language. Some, perhaps, became the softfeet, or the ancestors of the softfeet, choosing animal patience over their old shape. Some simply stopped, and were absorbed, the way the structures themselves are being absorbed by the lichen, with no resentment, no resistance, only the long agreement that everything here is going to become everything else, eventually, and there is no rush.

You will not meet a maker. But when the softfoot walks alongside you for ten paces, and then turns away, it has *considered* you, and the consideration is older than the consideration of any single animal. Something in the softfoot remembers having been other than a softfoot. The chirrers' chemistry-songs include notes that nothing in the present hollow needs to know — old water, water that has not existed here for ages — and the chirrers sing them anyway, because they were taught and the teaching held. The gliders' echolocation is too sophisticated; it is doing more than finding food; it is mapping something larger, reporting back, though to what and through what channel one cannot say.

## You, Briefly

You are not unwelcome. You are also not significant. The hollow has accommodated stranger visitors — meltwater intrusions, the brief incursion of a wandering animal lineage that died out before the bell-frogs learned their second harmonic, a fall of ash from a volcano that has since worn flat. It absorbed each and continued.

If you sit down, as the description warns, the moss will take you. But "take" is too acquisitive a word. The hollow will simply *include* you, the way it includes everything — slowly, without ceremony, with the warm patience of a place that has all the time there is. The softfeet will pass by and consider you and move on. The chirrers will land on you and survey your water. The slow thing, in its next breath, will warm you very slightly. The clicking will continue.

In a hundred years, a vine the width of a finger.

In ten thousand, you will be part of the gradient.

And the hollow will be exactly as it is now, only a little more itself.
