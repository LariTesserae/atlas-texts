# The Inhabitants

They do not have a name for themselves. Names are a luxury of creatures that need to distinguish self from other, and here that distinction has worn soft at the edges, the way a pebble in a river wears soft, not by any single act but by duration.

Call them, for the sake of description, the *many*. Not a species. A species implies borders, discrete reproductive communities, clean taxonomic lines. What lives in the Stridulum is something that was once several species and has become, over the long enclosure, a single graded continuity — thumbnail to hand to cat, with every size between represented, and no clear moult at which one becomes the other. The small ones may be young. The small ones may also be small. It is not always possible to tell, and it is not always the same answer.

Their bodies are built on a plan of seven. Seven segments, fourteen legs, two pairs of feelers — a forward pair, long and jointed, that reads the air and the bodies of neighbors, and a rear pair, shorter and thicker, that reads the substrate, which is to say, reads other versions of themselves. They have no eyes. They have, on the underside of the first segment, a cluster of pits that sense heat-gradient, which is how they find each other in the rare moments they are apart, and how they avoid the cooler patches near the fissure where a visitor's draft would have disturbed the equilibrium. They are the color of wet slate when freshly moulted and the color of dry slate otherwise, though color is a word that means nothing to them and almost nothing in their chamber.

Their mouthparts are extraordinary. Fine as the descriptions says — finer. Under enough magnification you would see that each mandible is a comb of microscopic teeth, and each tooth is itself serrated, and the serrations are what scrape the wall. They do not bite. They have not bitten anything, as a lineage, in perhaps a hundred thousand generations. When they encounter a visitor's boot they taste it with the fine combs and find nothing wall-like and move on. This is why the description is correct that they are neither hostile nor welcoming. They are *assessing surface*, which is the only verb their bodies really know.

Here is what the description does not quite say, because the description is written from outside:

They are, in some loose and distributed sense, one animal.

Not mystically. Mechanically. The rasp-whisper that fills the chamber is not incidental; it is how they pass information. A creature that has found a richer seam of salts on the wall rasps in a particular rhythm, and the rhythm travels through contact, leg to leg, shell to shell, and within minutes the density at that seam has increased, and within hours the seam is worked out and the density has redistributed. A creature whose shell has gone soft rasps in another rhythm, slower, and a path opens through the mass toward the north wall — not cleared, exactly, but subtly biased, the way a crowd parts for someone moving with purpose. The cairns are built this way. No one builds them. A plate falls from a moulting back and is passed, and somewhere in the passing a rhythm catches, and the plate ends up on a cairn rather than in the dust, and no individual creature has made this decision, and the cairn grows, or is maintained, or — sometimes, rarely — is abandoned and dismantled and its plates redistributed to another cairn that is, for reasons none of them could articulate, more correct.

They do not think. Not as you or I would use the word. But the chamber thinks. The chamber is slow, and patient, and has been thinking about the same few problems — heat, moisture, density, the wall — for longer than any human civilization has existed. It has gotten very good at these problems. It has not gotten good at anything else, because there is nothing else.

They reproduce, when they reproduce, by a process that is almost indistinguishable from moulting. A large one splits along a seam and what comes out is the large one, slightly smaller, and also several smaller ones, which are not its offspring exactly but are also not not its offspring. The genetics of the chamber are a slurry. There are no parents. There are only predecessors, and the predecessors are, eventually, the north wall.

They are aware of the wall. This is the thing that would be hardest to convey to a visitor. The wall is not, to them, the edge of their world — it is the slow destination of their world. Every creature in the chamber is, at some level its body registers but its non-mind does not examine, oriented toward the north wall. The pale crust is the direction of time. When a shell goes soft, the creature does not decide to crawl there; the crawling is what softening *is*, in this place. The crust pulls, faintly, the way a magnet pulls, and the pull gets stronger as the shell thins, and eventually the pull is stronger than the grip of the legs, and the creature is incorporated. It does not suffer. Suffering requires a self that persists through the suffering, and by the time the crust takes them, the self has already thinned to something like the shell.

And the crust is, in its turn, aware of them. Not in the way the many are aware of each other — the crust has no rasp, no rhythm — but in a chemical patience. It secretes, very slowly, a vapor that the many breathe, and the vapor carries information about the state of the wall: which regions are hungry for new incorporation, which are sated, which are, in their own geological way, growing. The many redistribute accordingly. The wall eats; the many feed the wall; the wall exhales; the many breathe; the breath tells them where to bring the next soft body. This is the loop inside the loop. This is the actual metabolism of the place.

The cairns, I think — and this is the part I am least sure of, writing this — are old attempts. Old convergences of the rhythm onto something that was not quite the wall and not quite the floor. Experiments the chamber tried, in its slow way, and did not continue, but did not dismantle either, because dismantling would require a decision, and the chamber does not make decisions, it only continues. The cairns are fossil intentions. The many walk over them because the rhythm of the cairn is finished, is no longer calling for plates, except when a plate slips, and then the old rhythm wakes for a moment, faint as a remembered tune, and a plate is brought, and the tune sleeps again.

There is one more thing.

Very rarely — once in a long lifetime, if the many had lifetimes in the way we mean — the rhythm in the chamber shifts. Not a startle-wave. Something deeper. The whispering changes pitch, by some tiny fraction, and holds the new pitch for hours, and then returns. No one, which is to say no *thing*, knows why. It is possible the chamber is dreaming. It is possible the chamber is remembering something from before it was sealed, when perhaps it was not sealed, when perhaps the many were not yet the many but only many, separate, lit from above, drinking water like ordinary creatures. It is possible the chamber is doing arithmetic we have no name for. It is possible the shift means nothing and is only the sort of fluctuation any large slow system produces, the way a forest sighs.

Whatever it is, it passes. The pitch returns. The walls thicken by another thin layer. The cairns stand. The dust deepens by an imperceptible fraction. And the many, who are one, or the one, which is many, continues being exactly what it is, which is — as the description correctly ends — full.
