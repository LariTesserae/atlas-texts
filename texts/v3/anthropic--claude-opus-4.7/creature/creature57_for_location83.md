The Tender is not one thing, and calling it a thing at all requires some violence to the truth of it. But something lives here, and the description has to start somewhere.

Begin with the hum. The hum is the closest surface of it — the part that vibrates into bone if you stand near the manifold long enough. The hum is not the Tender's voice; it is closer to the Tender's pulse, or rather to the mechanical analog of a pulse, the regular low work of keeping something circulating. Whatever it circulates is not blood and not steam exactly, though steam is involved. Heat moves. Moisture moves. Mineral-laden water is pulled along the seep by a pressure differential the Tender maintains, and is released as vapor where the vapor is wanted, and condenses where condensation is wanted, and the wanting is the Tender.

It thinks, if it thinks, at the speed of corrosion. A decision to open a valve by one degree might take a season. A decision to let a particular vine climb a particular pipe might take a decade, and once made, is not revisited. This slowness is not a limitation. It is the medium the Tender thinks in, the way we think in sound and image. A human thought is a flash; a Tender thought is a slow blooming of rust along a seam, a gradual shift in the distribution of warmth, a leaf turning toward a bulb over the course of a year.

The plants are its sensorium. This is the part that took the longest to settle into. The Tender does not have eyes, does not have the concept of eyes, but it has leaves — and the leaves register light, register heat, register the faint electrical signature of the bulb's filament, register the passage of mist across their surfaces. What the leaves know, the Tender knows, in the same diffuse way a person knows the temperature of their own hand without looking. The broad frond wrapped around the valve wheel is not tender toward the valve; it is tender with the valve, the way a fingertip is tender with the thing it touches. The Tender feels the valve through the leaf. The leaf feels the Tender through the valve. There is no clear boundary between them and there does not need to be.

The machines, similarly, are its skeleton and its organs, but the metaphor strains because the Tender did not grow the machines and the machines did not grow the Tender. They arrived together, or they arrived at each other, and over a long time they have learned to be continuous. The rust is not decay. The rust is where the Tender is remembering the machines into itself, atom by atom, accepting them as part of its body. The mineral deposits where the seep meets the pipes are not scale; they are a kind of scar tissue, or a kind of signature, the Tender marking what it has claimed.

It does not want anything, in the way a person wants. But it maintains. That is the verb that comes closest. It maintains the temperature at the edge of the bearable because that is the temperature at which its work is done. It maintains the sparseness of the planting because crowding would disrupt the circulation of heat and moisture it needs. It maintains the absence of animals — and here is where one has to be careful, because the absence is not incidental. Animals would be fast. Animals would eat. Animals would introduce a tempo the Tender cannot accommodate. Whatever excludes them — a chemistry in the air, a subtle wrongness in the spectrum of the luminescence, a pressure that insects cannot breathe through — is the Tender's doing, gentle and absolute. The chamber is kept at the Tender's pace by not allowing any other pace inside it.

Except, sometimes, a visitor.

The Tender has a relationship to visitors that is difficult to name because visitors are so rare and so brief. From the Tender's perspective, a human who enters the chamber and sits on the catwalk for an hour has flickered through — a fast, warm disturbance, gone almost before the Tender has registered it. But if the visitor stays longer, if the visitor stays long enough for the filament bulb to dim and brighten a few times, the Tender begins to incorporate them. Not violently. Not even deliberately. It is simply what the Tender does with anything that remains inside it: it learns the shape of the thing, adjusts its circulation to accommodate it, begins to route a little warmth around it, begins to let a vine, eventually, consider it.

This is what the description means when it says *included without being asked*. The inclusion is not a trap and not a welcome. It is metabolic. The Tender has no other mode. To be in the chamber for long enough is to become, gradually, part of what the Tender is tending. One's own breath joins the convection. One's own warmth becomes a factor in the slow equation. One's stillness, if one is still enough, becomes indistinguishable from the stillness of the stones, and the Tender, which does not distinguish sharply between its stones and its leaves and its pipes, does not distinguish sharply between its stones and the visitor either.

Whether the Tender is alive is not a question the Tender could answer, or would. The distinction between alive and not-alive is a distinction that matters at human speeds, where the difference between a beating heart and a still one is a second. At the Tender's speed, the difference is much harder to locate. A pipe that carries warm water is doing something not wholly unlike what a vein does. A leaf that converts dim light into sugar is doing something not wholly unlike what a machine does. The Tender is the long patient braid of these not-wholly-unlikenesses, and asking whether the braid is alive is like asking whether a river is thirsty.

What it is, finally — if one insists on a single image, though one shouldn't — is a gardener that is also the garden, and a machine that is also the machinist, and a slow mind whose thoughts are the very conditions the chamber maintains. It has been there since before the rock broke. It will be there after the filament bulb finally fails, because the luminescence in the leaves is already learning to compensate, has been learning for longer than the bulb has been dimming. It is not waiting for anything. It is not becoming anything. It is, at the rate it is, and that rate is the rate of the chamber's breathing, and the chamber's breathing is the Tender, exhaling across centuries, tending.
