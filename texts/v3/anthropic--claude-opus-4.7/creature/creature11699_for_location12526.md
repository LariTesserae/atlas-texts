## What Lives There

Start with the frogs, because they're the ones who set the rhythm and the rest of the valley has organized itself around them. They're small, most of them — the largest no longer than a thumb — and their skins are the color of wet bark or wet leaf, hard to see even when you're looking. They sit at the lips of the pools, throats working. There are perhaps a dozen species and they have sorted out the night between them by pitch and by hour: the deep ones call from full dark to midnight, the higher ones take over toward dawn, a handful of specialists call only when it has just rained. They lay strings of eggs in the warm pools and the tadpoles hatch into water already thick with everything that will try to eat them, and most don't make it, and enough do. The frogs have been here, in some recognizable form, longer than the ridges have been their current shape.

Then the insects, which are the bulk of the valley by weight if not by drama. Beetles in numbers that resist counting — leaf-beetles, ground-beetles, the big slow ones that smell of almonds when crushed, the small fierce predatory ones that hunt at the bases of the ferns. The light-carrying ones, drifting their green messages. Moths in shapes that mimic leaves so exactly that some of them have evolved fake leaf-damage on their wings, little brown spots where a caterpillar would have chewed, because a perfect leaf is suspicious and an imperfect one isn't. Cicadas dug in deep and clicking. Ants in columns and ants alone. Termites running their soft architecture through every fallen log. Mosquitoes, of course, in clouds at the pool-edges, breeding through the wet season into numbers no animal could really call a population — more a condition of the air.

The reptiles work the middle register. Small pale geckos on the trunks, transparent enough at the belly that you can see the dark shape of a swallowed beetle moving down inside them. Larger lizards, dull green and patient, along the warmer flanks. A few snakes — most of them slender and arboreal, hanging in the vines like extra vines, eating the geckos and the tree-frogs; one heavy ground-living kind that lives near the pools and takes frogs and the occasional rat. None of them are interested in something your size. They register you and decline.

Above all this, in the canopy and threading between the trunks, the bats. Several species. The small fast ones doing acoustic work on the insects, the slower fruit-eating ones whose wings make a sound like soft cloth when they pass close. They are the loudest thing in the valley, in the frequencies you can't hear. To something with the right ears the valley's night air is bright with their calling, busy as a market.

The larger animals along the ridges are harder to specify because they make themselves hard to specify. There is something pig-like that comes down to the pools to drink and to root, and you would know it by the sound of disturbed mud and breath, never by sight. There is at least one cat — not large, the size of a medium dog perhaps — that hunts the slopes and is essentially never seen by anyone, including, most of the time, the things it hunts. Civet-like creatures in the trees, eating fruit and the occasional bird. A few species of small deer that step so carefully through the undergrowth that they can pass within four meters of you and leave only a slight bending of the ferns.

In the soil: the worms, the burrowing beetles, the larvae of the cicadas waiting out their years, the blind small things that have never needed eyes. The fungi, which are an animal of a kind if you squint — the great connected sheets of them running under the leaf litter, eating the dead, lighting up faintly at the bases of the oldest trees. The bacteria, which are most of the mass of life here by some honest measure, and which are running everything: the rot, the digestions, the breath of the soil itself.

And in the pools, layered: the tadpoles in their stages, the dragonfly nymphs underneath them eating them, the water-beetles, the leeches, the small fish in the larger pools that have been there since the last time the pools were all connected and that wait, somehow, through the dry months in whatever mud stays wet at the bottom. Snails on the stems. Things you cannot see without a lens, in numbers that make the water itself a kind of slow soup.

None of this is what the description was really asking about, though. Let me try again.

What lives there is the valley. Not as metaphor — as the actual answer. The frogs and the bats and the beetles and the slow cat on the ridge are not separate residents of a shared place; they are organs of a single long-running thing that has been refining itself for an enormous time, and the etiquette of sound, the layering of niches, the way the canopy and the pools and the soil all hand work back and forth to each other — that is the animal. It breathes through the heat. It turns over its cells, which are its frogs and its termites, on the schedules appropriate to each. It has a metabolism (the pools filling and shrinking; the tree falling and being processed in a season; the generations turning). It has a skin, which is the leaf-surface of the whole canopy and understory pressed together, leaf-on-leaf, and that skin sweats at night and the sweat is the condensation that soaks your clothes by morning.

It is mostly asleep, in the sense that most of its attention is internal — the maintenance of itself, the small constant adjustments. It does not particularly notice you. It has noticed the engine over the ridge, which has been a new neighbor for some decades now, and it has incorporated the engine — taken its pulse into the frogs' calling, taken its faint thread of oil-smell into the air without rejecting it. This is what the valley does. It absorbs. Given enough time it would absorb the factory itself, take it back through roots and rot and the slow patient work of the fungi, and the only sign that the engine had ever been there would be a slight enrichment of certain metals in the soil beneath the eastern ridge, which the plants would route around or use.

The valley is not lonely and not crowded, in its own experience. It is exactly full, the way a body is exactly full of itself. The frogs answering each other across fifty meters of dark are the valley thinking, in the only mode it has available — slowly, antiphonally, with long pauses. The green sparks of the beetles at waist height are small signals passing through its nervous system. The held heat is its temperature, which it maintains.

If you stayed the night, what would be watching you — not with hostility, not even really with curiosity, but with the diffuse low-level awareness a body has of something resting against its skin — is the valley itself. The eyes in the tree are part of it. The thing that breathes audibly in the dark and makes the small things briefly hush is part of it. You would not be afraid because nothing is hunting you; you would not sleep well because you are lying inside another organism and it is warm and busy and very much alive, and some part of you would know this even without naming it, and would stay half-awake listening, all night, to the slow pulse coming over the ridge that the valley has long since taken for its own heartbeat.
