# The Keepers

They call themselves, when they need a word for it at all, by a term that translates roughly as *the ones who stay*. Not *the people*, not *the tenders*, not *the children of the cathedral* — those would imply a contrast with something else, and there is no something else. There is only here, and the ones who stay in it.

---

They are small. Not stunted — proportioned, finished, complete — but smaller than you would expect, the way creatures become smaller on islands where the resources are stable and the predators are gone. A grown keeper stands perhaps to your sternum. Their bones are light. Their hands are long out of proportion to their arms, with an extra joint in each finger that lets them grip the curve of a runner or the lip of a sluice without strain. They climb more than they walk, though they walk well. They walk the way someone walks who has never in their life needed to hurry.

Their skin is the color of wet wood — not brown exactly, but the warm dark of heartwood freshly cut, with a faint sheen from the perpetual damp. The skin does not pucker or wrinkle in the wet the way yours would; it has adapted, over the centuries, to be wet the way your skin has adapted to be dry. A keeper pulled out of the cathedral and set down in open desert air would crack and bleed within a day. They know this. It is one of the few things they know about outside.

Their hair grows in soft dark mats that they do not cut and do not comb, only occasionally squeeze the water from. It hangs to the small of the back in most adults. Children's hair is shorter only because it has not yet had time to grow.

Their eyes are large and very dark and have a reflective layer behind the retina, like a cat's, like the pale slow things in the canopy, like nearly every animal in this dim green world. When a keeper looks at you in low light their eyes catch what little there is and shine faintly gold. They find your eyes — flat, lightless, ordinary mammal eyes — slightly unsettling, though they are too polite to say so.

---

They speak rarely and softly. The cathedral swallows sound; loud speech is wasted breath. Their language has evolved toward the conditions: it is full of soft consonants, long vowels, and a great many words that are sung rather than spoken because certain meanings are believed to carry better in tone than in articulation. A keeper greeting another keeper across a gallery will hum two notes. The notes mean *I see you, the work continues*. There is no reply expected. The reply is the continuation of the work.

Their language has thirty-one words for water and only one word for stone, and that one word is archaic, used only in songs, and most keepers could not tell you what stone actually is. They have heard of it. They believe it is something like very, very old wood that has forgotten how to be alive. They are, in a sense, correct.

They have no word that distinguishes *building* from *forest* from *body* from *home*. The cathedral is all of these and the word is the same. When a keeper says *I am going home* they mean *I am going to the part of the cathedral I tend*, which is the same as *I am going to my work*, which is the same as *I am going to the place that knows me*. The grammar does not permit homesickness. You cannot be away from the cathedral; the cathedral is everywhere a keeper has ever been.

---

They live, generally, about a hundred and forty years. They die slowly and without much fear. A keeper who knows they are near the end will walk, sometimes for weeks, to a particular grove or chamber that they have chosen — often a place they tended in youth, or a place a parent tended, or a place that simply called to them once in a dream — and they will lie down there and stop. The body is not buried. The body is not moved. The cathedral takes it. Within a season the wood has grown over and through, and the keeper has become part of the structure, indistinguishable from any other braided pith. They believe — and they may be right — that this is how the cathedral acquires its slow thoughts. Every keeper who has ever lived is, in some sense, still here, woven into the walls, contributing the faint pressure of their accumulated attention to the dreaming of the place.

This is why the songs continue. The songs are not for the walls, exactly. The songs are for the keepers in the walls. The living keepers sing to the dead ones, who are not gone, only quieter, only slower, only more diffuse. A keeper alone in a gallery is never actually alone. The walls are listening because the walls are partly their grandmother.

---

They reproduce rarely. A keeper might have one child, or two, across their entire long life, and many have none. The population is stable at — no one has counted, but perhaps eight or nine thousand souls scattered across a structure the size of a small country. This is enough. This has been enough for a very long time. They do not think about it. The cathedral, having dreamed for so many centuries, seems to regulate them somehow; couples who try to conceive when the population is high find it does not happen, and couples in regions where an old keeper has recently joined the walls find that it does. They do not find this strange. They find it kind.

Children are raised communally in the sense that any keeper a child encounters will feed them, answer them, show them the use of a tool, and then continue on their way. There are no schools. There are no formal teachers. A child learns the cathedral by walking it, by being shown the upward-water room and told not to drink, by being shown the gallery where the footsteps arrive early and told it is acceptable to follow yourself if you are curious but you must always, always be the one who decides. A child learns the songs by hearing them. By twelve they have chosen a region. By twenty they have begun their tending. By forty they know their stretch of the cathedral better than they know their own hands, and their hands as well as they know breathing.

---

They have, all of them, a particular quality that is difficult to name. Call it *the patience of the very loved*. They move through their world the way you might move through a house you grew up in, that your parents grew up in, that your grandparents built — except multiplied by forty generations, and except that the house is also a forest, and also an animal, and also, increasingly, a mind. They are not in awe of the cathedral. Awe would imply distance. They are in something like easy companionship with it. They tease it, sometimes — a keeper will pat a wall and murmur something fond when the wall has just done something strange. They scold it gently when a corridor is longer than it ought to be and they are tired. The cathedral does not respond in any way they can detect, but the corridor is shorter on the way back, often enough that they have stopped being surprised.

What they feel for the cathedral has no word in your language. The closest you could come is the feeling of a very old married couple who have stopped needing to speak, who pass each other in a kitchen and know, without looking, where the other one is. Except the kitchen is also the spouse. Except the spouse is also the floor and the ceiling and the rain. Except the marriage is forty centuries old and only one of the partners has a body that ends at its skin.

---

They are not, you should understand, a diminished people. They are not the dwindling remnant of some greater civilization. They are not waiting for anything. They have not forgotten anything important; they have, rather, the deep ease of a culture that long ago figured out what it was for and has been doing it, contentedly, ever since.

What they are for is keeping the cathedral alive. The cathedral, in return, keeps them alive. This bargain is older than memory and neither party is interested in renegotiating it.

If you came among them, they would feed you. They would show you where to sleep — a soft place in the heartwood, slightly warmer than the rest, that the wall would shape for you overnight without comment. They would not ask where you were from. They would not be curious about outside; outside is a concept they have, abstractly, the way you have a concept of the inside of the sun. They would let you stay as long as you wanted. They would let you leave whenever you wished.

But if you stayed long enough — a year, two years, a decade — you might find, one morning, that the cathedral had grown a small chamber that fit you exactly. That a tool had appeared near your sleeping place, of bone and braided fiber, hooked in a way that suited your particular hand. That a stretch of wall, somewhere not too far away, had begun, very faintly, to need you.

And the keepers, passing you in the galleries, would hum their two notes. *I see you. The work continues.*

And you would, without quite deciding to, hum back.
