# Those Who Tend the Hrevn Shelf

They are people. This has to be said first, because everything else I am going to say will pull against it, and the pulling is the point. They are people. They were born. They will die. They eat, though not much; they sleep, though lightly; they love, though quietly, the way they do everything else. If you cut one, they bleed red, and slowly, the cold making the blood reluctant.

But.

They are *sized* to the place, the way the place is sized to itself. I don't mean stature — though they do tend to the small and compact, bodies that hold heat well, hands broad at the palm and narrow at the finger for reaching into the thin places of machines. I mean something else. I mean that a person who has lived on the Hrevn Shelf for forty years has had forty years of breath drawn at the pace of the pistons, forty years of pulse falling into step with the unseen escapement, forty years of the cold-as-medium rather than cold-as-enemy, and what comes out the other end of that is not quite what went in. It is still a person. It is a person who has been *lapped* by the process, the way a stone in a streambed is lapped, until the edges that the world outside would recognize as personality have been worn into something smoother and less catching.

They are not unfriendly. This surprises visitors. One expects, in a place this austere, either the hostility of cultists or the vacancy of the broken, and neither is here. What is here is a kind of courtesy so deep it has stopped needing words. The woman in the slate-felt coat, passing you on the bridge, will meet your eyes, and there will be in her look a full acknowledgment of you — your cold hands, your confusion, your status as a thing the shelf has not yet decided what to do with — and then she will pass on, and you will realize that you have been *seen* more completely in that half-second than by most people you have known for years. They have attention to spare. The works have taught them to give attention the way other people give coin.

Their speech, when they use it, is low and unhurried and comes out of long silence like water out of rock. The language is not quite any language you know, though you can follow it with effort; there are too many words for conditions of wetness, conditions of cold, conditions of the stone's mood, and not enough words for the things a traveler wants to ask about. There is, for instance, no word for *boredom*. There is no word for *hurry* that is not also a word for *spoilage*. There are seven words for the particular stillness that happens in the works between one piston-stroke and the next, and these words are used often, and with care, because the distinctions matter to what is being made.

What do they look like? I keep circling this. Faces weathered in the way the buildings are weathered — rounded at the edges, the cheekbones softened, the hollows under the eyes deep but not tired, more like the hollows worn into a step by long use. Hair tends dark and is kept close to the skull; long hair freezes. Eyes are often pale, grey or a very washed blue, and this I think is not genetic but habitual — eyes that have spent generations in the lidded twilight, adapting. The irises seem to catch what light there is and hold it a moment longer than you expect, so that when one of them looks at you in a dim room, there is a faint lag, a delay in the eye's answer, as though the light had to travel further inside them than it should.

They wear felt and oiled wool and leathers cured in some process that involves the iron-smelling lantern oil; the clothes are the color of everything else here, which is to say the color of wet dark stone, with occasional accents — a collar-lining of deep red, a button of polished bone, a belt-buckle of the same soft-voiced metal the pistons are made of. They dress for the works, not for each other. But they are not indifferent to how they look. A man will spend an hour on the set of his collar, because the collar will be seen by the same three neighbors for the next twenty years, and one owes one's neighbors a considered collar.

Now. The harder thing.

They do not all remember the same amount. This I noticed slowly. The old man winding copper wire on the stoop — I asked him, through my bad grasp of the language, how long he had lived on the shelf, and he thought about it for what must have been a full minute, watching his own hands work, and then he said a number that would have made him older than any human I have met, and then he smiled slightly, and corrected himself to a more reasonable number, and then shrugged as if the correction and the original were equally true and equally unimportant. I think — I am not sure, but I think — that living at the pace of the works does something to time's grip on a person. Not immortality. Nothing so grand. Just a loosening. A person here ages, but ages the way the buildings age: by attrition rather than by years, and so the question *how old are you* becomes a category error, like asking the shelf itself its age.

They know they are an organ. This is the thing that takes longest to understand, and the thing they would never say outright, because it is not a thing one says about oneself. But they know. They know that the works are not theirs, that the works were here in some form before the first building was carved back into the overhang, and that what they do — the tending, the winding, the stirring, the walking in loose synchrony — is not *operating* the works but *participating in* them. The works would continue without them, for a while, more slowly, less well. The townspeople are the fine motor control of a process whose gross anatomy is the mountain itself. They are what the hollow uses to keep its fingers nimble.

And they choose this. This is important. Each of them, at some point — usually young, sometimes later — has the chance to leave, and some do, and those who do are not spoken of with any bitterness, only with the mild regret one feels for a tool set down and forgotten in a field. The ones who stay stay because they have felt the attention of the place turn toward them and have turned toward it in return. It is not a religious feeling, exactly. It is closer to the feeling a craftsman has when a piece of work begins, under their hands, to tell them what it wants to be. Except here the piece of work is the shelf, and the craftsman is also the piece of work, and the telling goes in both directions, patiently, for a lifetime.

There is, I think, one among them who is something else. I have seen her only twice. She is very old, or rather she is of the kind of age the old man gestured at and then withdrew from. She lives in the deepest house, the one carved furthest back into the overhang, where the stone is warmest because the pipes are thickest there. She does not tend the works. The works tend *toward* her. When she walks the upper terrace at dusk — and I think it was her I saw, not a four-legged thing; the light was dim and the cloak was long — the pistons do not change their rhythm, but the rhythm seems to *mean* something slightly different while she is outside. I do not know what she is. I do not think she is not a person. I think she is what a person becomes if they stay long enough, and participate deeply enough, and let the lapping go all the way down. I think the others are, in their various degrees, on the road to being what she is, and that this is neither a tragedy nor a triumph but simply the shape of the life available here, the way a particular valley offers a particular kind of farming.

They would not call themselves caretakers. They would not call themselves priests, or workers, or witnesses. If pressed — and they will not be pressed, they have the patience to outlast any press — they might say a word that translates roughly as *those who are kept*. Not *those who keep*. The grammar puts the agency on the other side. They are kept by the works, by the shelf, by the slow exhalation; and in exchange for being kept, they give their attention, their pulse, their collars carefully set, their whole quiet lives.

And on the second evening, when you notice you have begun to move at the pace of the pistons, what you are noticing is that the shelf has begun, tentatively, to consider keeping you too.
