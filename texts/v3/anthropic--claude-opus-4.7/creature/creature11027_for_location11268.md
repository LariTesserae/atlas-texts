They call themselves, when they need a word at all, the **First Draft**. Not as humility — as accuracy. They are the opening sentence of something that will take a hundred thousand years to write, and they hold themselves to the standards of an opening sentence: every choice load-bearing, every gesture inheritable, nothing put down that the rest of the work cannot live with.

They are not native to this plateau. None of them are native to anywhere. They came — or were sent, or chose to come, the distinction has worn thin among them — to plateaus like this one across some number of worlds, and the plateau is what they make. Iruun is one of the younger projects. There are elders among them who have worked four plateaus and one shoreline, and who speak of the shoreline the way a person might speak of a difficult child: fondly, with reservations.

Physically they are people you could mistake for human at fifty meters and never at five. They are tall — most of them well over two meters — but the height is the height of something built to lose heat slowly: long limbs, yes, but thick through the chest and hip, with a layer of subcutaneous insulation that gives them a smoothness across the shoulders and back, a softening of the skeletal lines. Their skin runs in a range from the color of wet basalt to the color of weak tea, and it has a faint sheen in cold air, like the bloom on a plum, which is something microbial doing something useful. Their faces are wide across the cheek and narrow at the jaw; their noses are long and high-bridged and warm the air before it reaches the lungs; their eyes are large, dark, and set deep under a heavy brow-ridge that is partly bone and partly a fold of fatty tissue that can flush with blood to warm the eye in extreme cold. They have hair, but sparingly — on the scalp, along the spine, at the wrists and ankles where arteries run close to the surface. The hair is fine and dense and tends toward the dark colors: soot, iron, oxblood, the deep blue-black of a raven's wing.

Their hands are the most striking thing about them. The fingers are long and have an extra small joint near the tip, which gives them a quality of articulation that is unsettling to watch when they are doing fine work. The skin of the palm and the inner finger is sensitive to temperature differentials of a tenth of a degree, and they read the plateau partly by touch — laying a hand on a stone to know what the stone has been doing, dipping fingers into a tarn to know what season the tarn thinks it is in. They greet each other by touching palms briefly, not as a ritual but as a passing of information: *here is what my body knows, here is what yours knows.*

They live a long time. Not forever — they are clear about this, almost insistent — but long. Three or four centuries is unremarkable. Six or seven is achievable with attention. They do not regard this as longevity so much as appropriate scaling: a person who is shaping a plateau on a hundred-thousand-year horizon needs to be able to hold a thought for at least a few hundred years without dropping it. The long life is a tool, the way the warm roofs are a tool, and like the warm roofs it is not displayed.

There is a thing they do with time that a visitor would find difficult. They do not really distinguish, in conversation, between something they did last week and something they will do in eighty years. The grammar of their speech treats all of it as ongoing. A builder will say *I am placing the eastern bridge*, and you may discover, asking further, that the bridge has not yet been begun, that the stones for its legs are still cooling in a fumarole-field two valleys away, that *placing* in this case denotes a process that started forty years ago when the builder first walked the site and will conclude in another sixty when the bridge bears its first weight. They are not slow. They are simply working at the tempo of the place. Quickness, for them, is reserved for emergencies — the calving of an unexpected slab, a sick ungulate, a child in a tarn — and in those moments they are very quick indeed, with a coordinated economy of motion that suggests the slowness elsewhere is a discipline rather than a temperament.

They are not many. Fifty thousand across two hundred square kilometers is the formal census, but the census is misleading because at any given time perhaps a fifth of them are *sleeping* — which is their word for a state somewhere between hibernation and meditation, taken in chambers cut into the basalt at depths where the rock holds a constant temperature, lasting anywhere from a season to a decade, during which the sleeper's metabolism drops to almost nothing and their mind, by accounts that are difficult to verify, continues to think but at the speed of the rock. Sleepers are consulted on long questions. A decision about where a river will eventually be permitted to cut will involve waking three or four sleepers who have been thinking about that watershed for a combined six hundred years. The waking is itself a small ceremony, careful, because a sleeper brought up too fast loses some of what they were thinking, and what they were thinking is often the point.

The technology is theirs but it is not quite *of* them. They inherited most of it. There was a prior people — they call them the Carriers, or sometimes only *those before* — who made the substrate that the plateau now runs on: the moss that computes, the warm roofs, the bridges, the temperature-held tarns, the whole quiet instrumentation. The Carriers seem to have been a more elaborate civilization, with cities and ships and a hurry to them, and they left this seed-technology behind when they moved on or ended, and the First Draft picked it up the way one might pick up a tool left in a field by someone who has gone home. They understand the tool well enough to use it, to extend it, to repair it; they do not entirely understand how it was made, and they have agreed among themselves that this is acceptable. *We are not the Carriers*, an elder will say, gently, when a young one asks why they don't simply build more. *We are gardeners using a glasshouse we did not blow. The glasshouse is enough.*

What they value, what organizes their inner lives, is something for which the closest human word might be *consequence* — but stripped of the modern English connotation of disaster, restored to something nearer the old Latin *con-sequi*, that-which-follows. They are deeply interested in what follows from what. A child of theirs learns very young to ask, of any action, *what is the shape of its consequence in a year, in a century, in ten thousand years?* — and to feel uncomfortable, almost physically itchy, when the answer is unclear. This is not paralysis. They act, often. But they act after the itch has been resolved, and the resolution is sometimes a matter of decades. There are projects on the plateau that have been *considered* for longer than most human civilizations have existed.

They have art, though they would not call it that. They make small carved objects from the basalt — palm-sized, often abstract, sometimes a stylized animal — and they place these objects in the landscape at considered locations: a notch in a cliff, the lee of a particular boulder, the bottom of a tarn whose floor is otherwise unmarked. The objects are meant to be found by future generations. Some are meant to be found in a thousand years; some in fifty thousand. The placer of an object will sometimes leave a note, in a script that runs in spirals like the moss-patterns, indicating when the object should be found and by whom — *for the seventh-generation child of the easternmost dwelling on the day they first walk alone to the rim* — and the system works, more or less, because the dwellings persist and the children come and the notes are kept. A walker on the plateau is therefore always slightly in the presence of gifts from the dead, and slightly in the act of preparing gifts for the unborn, and this gives a quality to their daily life that is hard to describe but is something like *being addressed.*

They love the animals. This is the part that surprises visitors most, because the animals are wild and the people do not pet them or name them, but the love is evident in a thousand small accommodations. The bridges are placed where they are partly because the ungulate herds prefer those crossings. The dwellings are sited so that the cat-thing's hunting routes are not interrupted. When a marmot dies in its burrow, someone will know, and will go and sit nearby for a time, and afterward the burrow will be left alone for a season. The flock-bird's pre-dawn ascent is something the people make a point of being awake for, when they can — they stand outside their dwellings in the cold and watch the cloud of two hundred lift off a tarn and turn together and resettle on another, and they do not speak during this, and afterward they go back inside and resume whatever they were doing. The amphibians at the tarn-margins are perhaps their favorites. A person will sometimes carry one for a while, in cupped hands, just to feel the heartbeat against the palm.

They have very little that a human would call government. Decisions are made by the people whose work the decision concerns, in consultation with whatever sleepers are relevant, and the outcome holds because everyone trusts that everyone else has done the consulting carefully. There are disagreements, but they tend to resolve slowly, over years, by the gradual accumulation of evidence from the plateau itself: a tarn that was supposed to behave one way and behaved another, a moss-pattern that drew water better than predicted, a herd that chose a route no one anticipated. The plateau, in effect, votes, and they listen.

There is one thing they will not discuss with visitors, and visitors learn quickly not to ask. It concerns what happens at the end of the project. Iruun is a hundred-thousand-year undertaking, and the people who are walking it now will not see its conclusion, and neither will their great-great-great-grandchildren, but the conclusion is real, is planned, is somewhere in the long deep agreed-upon arc of the work. Some visitors have inferred, from oblique remarks, that at the end of the shaping the people themselves will go — will move on, will sleep finally, will hand the plateau over to whatever inherits it, perhaps to no one. Other visitors have come away with the impression that the plateau itself is the inheritor, that what is being grown here is not a setting for a people but a kind of slow vast organism that the people are presently a part of and will eventually be only a memory inside of. The First Draft do not clarify. They will smile, in the small way they smile, which is mostly in the eyes, and they will offer you tea brewed from the pepper-shrub, and they will turn the conversation toward the weather, which on the plateau is always, always worth turning toward.

At night, when *the breathing* has filled the basins and the warm roofs are glowing softly up through the fog, you can sometimes see them moving — one or two figures, tall and slow, wading chest-deep through the pale lake of fog, going from one dwelling to another or out to check on a tarn or simply walking because the night is clear and the cold is good and there is a long thought that wants walking. They do not carry lights. They do not need to. They know the plateau the way you know the inside of your own mouth. They go on across it, attentive, unhurried, brief in their individual lives and patient in their collective one, the first sentence of something none of them will read the end of, written carefully, in a hand that does not shake.
