They are small. That is the first thing to say, because everything else follows from it. Whoever lives here is not tall — could not be tall, because the cell will not admit a tall person, and the wall was stacked by someone whose reach is short, whose stones are the size a child could lift but fitted with an adult's deliberation. Call them a handspan and a half shorter than you. Call them compact. Call them made for this.

Their skin is the color of the wet rock when the mist is on it: pale, a little blue beneath, as though the cold had gotten into the pigment and stayed. It is not human skin exactly — it is thicker where thickness is needed, at the knees and the heels and the outer edges of the hands, and thinner where thinness is useful, at the temples and the throat, where the blood shows through in a slow mapwork. They do not have much hair. What they have is on the scalp and is long and fine, and it is the hair that was braided into the cord around the reeds — one's own hair, given to hold one's own bundle. This tells you something. They are not wasteful. They are not sentimental in the way that keeps things; they are sentimental in the way that uses things.

Their hands are the most particular part of them. The fingers are long for the body, and there are — you would not notice at first — small webs at the base of each finger, low, vestigial, of no real use for swimming but of great use for holding water briefly, for cupping the mist and drinking from the palm. The nails are flat and broad and tough. These are the hands that fitted the wall. These are the hands that scratched the marks. These are the hands that, once, caught a crab and held it to the light and watched the blue thread inside it and set it back down, because what the crab eats is also what they eat, and one does not eat what one is trying to understand.

They eat the moss. They eat, sparingly, the crabs, but only the ones that have stopped moving on their own. They drink from the mist, never from the basin — the basin is not for drinking; the basin is what they came to be beside. They do not eat the reeds. The reeds are for listening to.

Their ears are not like yours. They are small and set flat to the skull and can close, the way a seal's can close, with a fold of skin that draws shut against pressure. They learned long ago — or someone before them learned, and it was passed down in the shape of the body itself — that to live near the column you must be able to un-hear it. When they sit in the cell, their ears are open. When they walk the rim, their ears are shut, and they hear the water not as sound but as the drum-pressure in the chest, which is what the water is anyway, which is what the water has always been trying to tell them.

They came here — and here the account gets speculative, because they have not said, and the marks on the wall are not quite a saying — they came here because the mountain is new. This is important. They do not live on old mountains. Old mountains have finished their argument with the sky and settled into an answer, and the answer is boring. This mountain is still asking. The water comes down and the rock goes somewhere and the peak rises and nothing is decided yet, and to sit beside an undecided thing is, for them, the whole purpose of a life. They are a species, or a people, or a single lineage — it is not clear which, and may not be a meaningful distinction for them — who attend. Who witness the becoming of places. Who find a young mountain and build a cell on its dry flank and stay until the mountain is old enough to be uninteresting, at which point they leave, or die, and another of them finds another young mountain somewhere else.

There are not many of them. There cannot be. Young mountains are rare, and each one gets only one attendant, because the attending is a solitude. If two came, they would talk, and talking is the opposite of what the cell is for.

They do talk, though — to themselves, and to the bird. When the black bird comes down through the column and surfaces in the boiling basin, the attendant walks to the rim (ears closed, chest thrumming) and watches. The bird does not look at them. The bird has no eyes visible, or its eyes are closed, or it simply does not need to look. But the attendant believes — and this is a belief, not a knowledge — that the bird knows they are there, and that the bird's coming-down is a kind of report from whatever is above the cloud, delivered to whoever is willing to stand at the rim and receive it. The attendant adds a mark to the wall after each bird. Not a tally of birds. A tally of receipts. The marks are not the same shape each time; they vary with something the attendant perceives and we do not.

The bell that is not a bell, the flat stone on the bone tripod — that is for the day the attending ends. They will strike it once, with the copper hook, when they are ready to leave or when they know they are dying. It will not be heard by anyone. That is the point. It is a sound made for the mountain, which by then will be old enough to hear a small sound, having spent a life being deafened by a large one. It is a gift of contrast. It is the last lesson, delivered to the student that is the rock itself.

The bones of the tripod are from the previous attendant. This is how it is done. You arrive, you find the tripod already built from the one before you, and you know that one day your own long bones will be the poles of the next one's bell, and the cord of your own hair will tie the next one's reeds, and the marks you added to the wall will be read or not read by eyes that are a little like yours and a little not. There is no ceremony around this. It is simply the shape of the work.

They are, in their quiet way, happy. Or the word for what they are is close enough to happy that happy will do. They sit cross-legged in the cell in the mornings, when the diffuse light is brightest, and they hold the clay bowl in both hands, empty, and they look at the inside of the bowl and they listen to the reeds sing faintly through the rock wall behind them, and the mountain rises under them by the width of a hair, and somewhere above the cloud a bird is folding itself for descent, and the column comes down and down and down into the basin and through the basin and into the mountain and into the making of the mountain, and the attendant is there for it, small and blue-pale and patient, doing the one thing they came to do, which is to be the still point beside which a place becomes itself.
