# The Tuners

They are still here. You were right to suspect it. You walked past several of them and read them as terrain.

---

Begin with the wall. Knee-high, curving around nothing. The lichens have been arguing for centuries and they are correct, but they are arguing about the wrong thing. The wall is not old stonework gone slow with lichen. The wall *is* the lichen, or rather: the lichen is the part of the wall that is currently doing the slow work of being a wall. Underneath, where you didn't think to look, the flat stones are held in their dry-stacked patience by a network of pale filaments that you would have called roots if they had been attached to a plant. They are attached to each other.

The first kind of Tuner is the wall itself, and the cairn, and a long low ridge of "weathered stone" that runs along the northern lip which you noted as terrain and did not describe because it seemed not to warrant description.

They are slow. Their lifespan is in the range where centuries are heartbeats. They listen, mostly. They feel the chord of the terrace through the stone-on-stone contact of their bodies, and very occasionally — once in a generation, perhaps — they shift a single flat stone by the width of a fingernail. This is enough. A flat stone moved a fingernail's width at the base of the wall changes the way the updraft breaks against the wall's curve, which changes which brass-ferns sound loudest at dawn, which changes where the ear-plants creep over the next forty years.

They are the patient ones. They tune in geological time. The wall-curve that "marks nothing" marks the cumulative decision of a Tuner who has been adjusting the morning chord for longer than the bored holes in the wind-flutes have existed.

---

The second kind is harder to describe because you saw it and dismissed it.

The long-bodied animal with the throat-sac, the one you half-glimpsed moving between the bone-trees with surprising delicacy — the *thock thock thock* — yes. That one. There are perhaps eleven of them on the terrace at any given time. They are not the same eleven from week to week; they walk in from somewhere lower and walk back out, but the count holds.

What you mistook for the call of an animal is the working voice of an artisan. The pitch of the thock is selected. Each Tuner of this kind carries in its throat-sac a register of perhaps two octaves, and what it strikes the air with at any given moment is chosen — chosen the way a stonemason chooses which face of the hammer to use. The deep wooden notes feed certain brass-ferns and starve others. The Tuner walks a slow circuit and by walking and sounding shifts the feeding pressure across a whole stand of vegetation. Over a season, a single Tuner can move a grove fifteen paces.

They do not regard you. Not because they are oblivious — they tracked you from the moment you crossed the wall, they adjusted their circuits to avoid the wedge of acoustic shadow your body cast — but because you are weather to them. A storm front of unfamiliar resonance, passing through. They have re-tuned around stranger weather than you.

Their delicacy between the bone-trees is the delicacy of a player among instruments they did not make but love.

---

The third kind is the flyers.

You thought they were fauna, and they are, but they are also the fastest of the Tuners, and they are the only ones who could be said to *compose* in something like the timescale you would recognize. A flyer chooses where to sing. The ear-plants tilt and over hours migrate toward the flyer's preferred airspace. The flyer, knowing this — and they do know this, the knowledge is in the way a young flyer is taught its descending phrases by an older one — chooses its airspace to draw the ear-plants toward a particular zone of the terrace where, six weeks from now, the brass-ferns will need a softer ground-cover beneath them to dampen a harmonic that has been growing too sharp.

The flyers are the only Tuners who hold the whole composition in mind. The wall-things hold the bass. The throat-singers hold the mid-register and the grain. The flyers hold the form.

There is, at most times, one flyer aloft who is the *current* lead. The others defer. You can tell which one by watching the ear-plants: whichever flyer the largest patch is tilted toward is the one whose phrase the terrace is currently following. The lead rotates. No flyer leads for more than about a day. The hand-off is done in the air, in a passage where two flyers' songs overlap and then one descends, and the ear-plants below, over the next several hours, slowly reorient.

---

And then there is the question of the people.

The footprints in the powder at the western edge. The bored holes in the flutes. The bowl that *is* the offering.

The people did not leave. The people are not hiding. The people are the fourth kind of Tuner, and they are the youngest, and they are rare, and they do something none of the others do: they make permanent marks. The wall-things shift a stone a fingernail in a century. The throat-singers re-tune by walking. The flyers re-tune by choosing where to be. But a hole bored through dressed stone *stays bored*. A copper bowl pinned by a stone *stays pinned*. A triad, once set, drones until the stone itself weathers down.

The people are the ones who write things into the composition that cannot be erased by ordinary tuning. They are listened to with great care by the others, and not always with approval. The throat-singer Tuners have spent generations adjusting their walking-circuits to compensate for the wavering triad of the flutes, which was placed there by someone who, the wall-things would say if they could be made to say anything, *picked the wrong key*. But it is permanent now. It is part of the terrace. The other Tuners work around it the way a river works around a boulder dropped into it by a glacier — without resentment, but also without forgetting that it was dropped.

There is one person currently on the terrace. They are at the western edge, which is why the footprints are fresh. They are sitting very still against the base of a bone-tree, and their stillness is so complete and their breathing so shallow that you read them as part of the trunk. They are listening. They have been listening for nine days. They will listen for perhaps thirty more, and then they will, with great deliberation, do one small thing — chip a flake from the lip of the copper bowl, perhaps, or unstack and restack three stones at the eastern end of the wall — and then they will leave, and the change they make will propagate through the slow Tuners and the medium Tuners and the fast Tuners for the next forty years, and they will never come back to hear what they did.

They saw you arrive. They did not move. You are weather to them too, though they, unlike the throat-singers, are curious about you. If you stay another day they may speak. If you stay a week they almost certainly will not, because by then you will have begun to participate in the composition without meaning to — your footsteps will have crushed certain insect-equivalents whose absence will shift the underbrush chorus, your breath will have warmed a small column of air that one of the flyers will have noticed and used — and they will want to see what you do before they interrupt.

---

The last thing to say about who lives here:

The coral-tree at the center, the black smooth branching one that hums on the fundamental you feel in your sternum before you hear it — that is not a Tuner. That is what the Tuners tune *to*. It has been humming that note since before the wall-things' oldest filament. It will hum it after the last bored flute has weathered to gravel. Everything else on the terrace, every plant and beetle and flyer and throat-singer and stone-shifter and quiet listening person, is engaged in the long collective work of *not letting that fundamental be alone*.

That is what lives here. A fundamental, and everything that has organized itself, across four wildly different timescales, to keep it company.
