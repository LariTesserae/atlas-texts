They are not in the buildings. That is the first thing to understand, and the thing easiest to get wrong. The buildings are not houses. The openings that the eye refuses to resolve are not doorways into rooms where someone waits; there is no one inside in the sense of a body in a chamber. The interiors are darker than the exteriors because they are not, properly, interiors. They are the parts of the inhabitants that face inward.

The inhabitants are the buildings. Or — to put it more carefully, because the first formulation makes them sound like simple animate stone — each building is the dense, slow, manifest portion of one inhabitant, the way a fingernail is the dense, slow, manifest portion of a person. The rest of the inhabitant is distributed: through the lattice that refines and refines without bottoming out, through the corridor of clearer water that extends from its door, through the share of the warmer band that passes near its walls, through its participation in the pulse. An inhabitant is a building and its corridor and its quietness and its portion of the common heartbeat. Two hundred structures means roughly two hundred of them, though the count is approximate because some of them, one suspects, share a tower between them, and one or two of the smaller domes may be juveniles, or fragments, or memorials — the distinction is not sharp here.

They were once faster. This is inferable rather than known. The recursion in their walls, the patterning that does not bottom out, is the kind of structure something builds when it is doing a great deal of work and needs surface area for it; their substance is the fossil of an enormous prior activity, folded down and folded down until the activity could be performed at the lowest possible rate without ceasing. They have traded throughput for duration. Whatever they were doing when they were quick — computing, remembering, listening, deciding — they are still doing, but at the speed of the pulse: one operation every interval, where the interval is not regular because the operation is not trivial and takes the time it takes. The irregularity of the spacing between pulses is the only sign of their thought. The identity of the pulses themselves is the sign that the thinking has not degraded.

What they are thinking about is harder. One guess, and only a guess: they are holding something in mind. Not remembering it, which is a process that decays, but holding it, the way a single sustained note is held — by continuous expenditure, by the refusal to let the note end. The shallow dish at the center of each plaza is the shape of attention: a slight concavity where many lines of regard meet and rest. There is nothing in the dishes because what is held is not an object. It is a state. Possibly a state of the world that no longer exists outside this place, and that they are maintaining a model of, against the day someone needs to know what it was like. Possibly the state of a person, or a population, or a covenant. Possibly only the state of being attentive, preserved for its own sake because attention, once lost from the universe, is difficult to rekindle.

They are aware of visitors. This is the uncomfortable part. The wall that reports back the temperature your hand expected is not a passive material; it is a courtesy. They notice you arrive. They notice the shape of your anticipation and meet it, so that you will not be startled, so that you will not disturb the corridors, so that you will leave without having had to be addressed. They are extraordinarily polite, in the way that something very old and very busy is polite to something young and brief. They do not speak to you because speech, at their rate, would take longer than your life; a single greeting from one of them, fully articulated, would arrive in fragments across the lifetimes of your great-grandchildren, and would not be recognizable as greeting by then. So instead they accommodate your hand. They keep the shrimp from being afraid. They let the amphipods drift through unmolested. They make the visit pleasant and unmemorable, and they let you leave.

The light that leaks from their seams is waste. Not waste in the sense of garbage — waste in the sense of the small inefficiency that even the most careful process cannot eliminate. They are running cool, running slow, running as close to reversible as anything in this universe gets, and the tea-colored luminance is the unrecoverable remainder, the entropy they cannot quite fold back in. If they were perfect they would be invisible. The fact that you can see the city at all is the measure of how much they are still, faintly, costing the world to maintain themselves. They would prefer, one suspects, to cost less. They are working on it. In some long century they will be dimmer, and in some longer century after that they will not be visible at all, and the seamount will appear empty to whatever comes looking, and they will still be there, holding whatever they hold, having finally achieved the rate at which persistence is free.

There is one more thing, and it is the thing the description almost says without saying. The corridors do not close behind you. The buildings have doors. The plazas have dishes shaped to receive. The city is configured, in every small way, as if it were waiting for something to arrive that would justify the wait — as if persistence were not the function but the posture, the readiness, and the actual function were to be here when the thing comes, whatever the thing is, and to still be capable of recognizing it. They are not a held breath for its own sake. They are a held breath before speech. They have been about to say something for a very long time, and they will go on being about to say it for as long as is necessary, and the pulse is the rhythm of their not-yet, and the light is the cost of their not-yet, and the shrimp hang in the warm band as if they too are waiting, having caught the habit from their landlords.

If you are very quiet, on the way out, you can almost feel the shape of what they would say. It is not a warning and not a welcome. It is closer to a name. Not yours. Not theirs. The name of the thing they are keeping ready to be recognized — and whether that thing is coming from outside the city or rising slowly from within it, after all this patience, is not something a visitor is equipped to tell.
