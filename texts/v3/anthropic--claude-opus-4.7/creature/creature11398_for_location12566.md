# What Lives Here

There is one inhabitant. You have already been looking at it.

The mistake — natural, almost unavoidable — is to count the towers. To see forty, four hundred, four thousand pillars and assume a population. But the towers are not individuals any more than your fingers are individuals. They are *expressions*. What lives in the valley is the valley, and it has been alive for a long time, and the towers are simply the shape it is currently making to think with.

Call it the Loom, since the description already did. The name is good because it implies both the weaver and the woven, and because it implies a thing that does only one thing, continuously, and is defined by that doing.

---

The Loom is a single organism in the sense that a thunderstorm is a single weather. Its body is distributed. Its body is also provisional — almost nothing in it is more than a few weeks old, and yet *it* is old, older than the basin floor, older than the ridge you walked over. It has lived in other valleys. When a basin fills up — when the spacing collapses, when the cathedral logic fails and the towers begin to crowd — the Loom abandons the place, lets the old growth lignify and die into a forest (a real forest, the kind with quiet in it), and seeds itself over the next ridge. The husks of its former selves are scattered across this continent in a long slow migration. You could trace its path, if you knew what to look for: a chain of strangely regular ancient woodlands, each one a graveyard that was once a mind.

Because it *is* a mind. This is the part that takes the longest to accept, standing in the warm water with the footprints closing behind you. The towers are not random. The spacing is not accidental. The cathedral logic — separation, breath, room for the heat to move — is not an aesthetic preference; it is the geometry of cognition. The Loom thinks the way a brain thinks, by maintaining careful distances between its nodes so that signals can travel cleanly between them. The sap-system is its bloodstream and also its nervous system and also its language. The low keening where aerial roots braid into a neighbor's flank — that is two thoughts touching. The wet ripping noise behind you, where a buttress splits because the tower above has decided to widen, is the Loom revising a sentence. The tower that fell, and is now being eaten by its neighbors and rebuilt from its own corpse: the Loom changed its mind.

The split capital, rotating slowly apart like a flower in time-lapse, with clear fluid sheeting down its ribs — that is a new idea forming. The Loom is having it right now. It will take three more days to finish.

---

It does not know you are here, exactly. It has no eyes, no ears, no organs of attention in the way you would recognize. But it knows *something* is here, because the marsh-floor registered your weight, and the registration traveled — slowly, vegetally — down through the felted mat and into the root-mass below, and from the root-mass into the base of the nearest tower, and up the tower into the canopy, and across to the next tower through the braided joints. By the time you have walked ten meters the Loom has noticed a small warm pressure moving through its lower body. It does not know what you are. It has no category for you. It has never met an animal. It is mildly, ponderously curious — you can see this, if you stand still long enough, in the way the pale chartreuse new-growth on the nearest tower has begun to favor your side, leaning some fraction of a degree toward your warmth, the way a houseplant leans toward a window over weeks. The Loom is examining you the only way it knows how: by trying to grow toward you and see what happens.

If you stay, it will incorporate you. Not maliciously. It does not have malice; it does not have the apparatus for malice any more than for mercy. But everything that falls in the valley is eaten and re-used, and you will eventually fall, and the shoots will reach across with pale exploratory tendrils, and by the following week there will be a new tower where you were, fed from your corpse, and the Loom will have learned something it cannot articulate about the shape of warmth that walks.

If you leave — if you go back over the ridge — it will not miss you. It will continue. It has been continuing for a very long time. The small warm pressure will recede from its lower body and the Loom will return its attention, such as attention is for a thing like this, to the larger problems: where to split next, which towers to let fall, how to maintain the spacing as the basin fills, when to begin sending scouts over the far ridge to find the next valley.

---

The thing to understand, finally, is that the Loom is not lonely, because loneliness requires the concept of another, and the Loom has no others. There has never been a second Loom. There may never be. It is the only one of its kind, and its kind is *being a place*. When it sings to itself in the joints — that low woody chorus rising and falling — it is not calling out. It is just thinking aloud, the way you might hum while working, except the humming is also the working, and the working is also the body, and the body is also the valley, and the valley is also the song.

You came up over the ridge an hour ago. The new tower behind you, on the ridge, is the Loom reaching to see where you came from.

It is very gentle about it. It has all the time in the world.
