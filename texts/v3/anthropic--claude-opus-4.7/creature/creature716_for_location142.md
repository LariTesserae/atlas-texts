# The People of Oost-in-Condens

They are called, among themselves, simply *those who stay* — though the phrase in their language is one word, worn smooth by use, and carries none of the defensiveness that translation gives it. It is a description, not a boast. They are the ones who did not leave. Whether anyone ever left is an open question. There are stories, but the stories are the kind that function more as landmarks in conversation than as history.

They are small people. Not uniformly — there is variation, as there is in any population — but the average is smaller than you might expect, and this is the first clue to what they are. Generations of living inside a saturated atmosphere at elevation, eating what the plateau provides, breathing air that is two parts fog to one part furnace-smoke, have selected for compact frames, deep chests, and a particular efficiency of the lungs that lets them extract oxygen from air that would leave a lowlander gasping. Their skin is pale under the lamps and carries a faint greenish cast that is not pigment exactly but something mineral, the way old copper pipe carries its patina. Their hair grows dark and very fine and, in adulthood, is usually kept oiled and bound close, because loose hair in this air becomes a wick for every kind of moisture and every kind of small crawling thing. Their hands are broad in the palm and long in the finger and the nails are thick. They have good hands for pipe-work, for casting, for the small careful tending of animals, for the fine braidwork that serves them for both clothing-fastening and record-keeping.

Their eyes are the most obviously adapted thing about them. The irises are very large, taking up most of the visible eye, and the color ranges through greys and pale ambers and a particular foggy blue that is the color of the plateau's own light. They see well in the diffuse luminance and the lamp-flicker. They do not see especially well in direct sun, which they almost never encounter; children born with a rare condition that leaves them light-sensitive are unremarkable here, because everyone is a little light-sensitive here, and the condition confers no disadvantage.

They wear layered garments of oiled wool and of a felted material made from the shed undercoats of the grazers, which they collect from walls and catwalks each spring. The outer layer is always dark and always waxed. Underneath, brighter things — reds, ochres, a deep indigo that is the one color they import from somewhere else, though the somewhere-else is not discussed much and may be further down the mountain or may be something older than that. Every garment has toggles and loops for attaching small things: a lamp-key, a pipe-wrench, a length of braided cord, a charm against the cinder-wrens (not that they disturb the wrens, but there are still charms, because there have always been charms). Their boots are tall and tight-laced and have thin soles that let them feel the pump-rhythm through the iron grating.

They speak quietly. This is partly because voices carry in the fog and partly because the soundscape is already so full — the wings, the dripping, the thud of the bellows — that a loud voice is a kind of rudeness, a shoving. Their language is tonal and contains a great many words for conditions of moisture, for degrees of sound, for the specific gaits of specific animals, and for relations between people that have no equivalent in languages that assume people come and go. Their kinship terms, for instance, distinguish between relatives who live on your terrace, relatives who live on an adjacent terrace, and relatives who live more than two terraces away, the last of whom are considered to be in a category closer to ancestors than to family — not dead, but remote in a way that is treated as a kind of death. You visit them the way you visit graves.

They are, by any reasonable outside measure, a conservative people, but this word doesn't quite capture it. They are not holding onto the old ways against some imagined tide of change. There is no tide. They are simply doing what they do, the way their parents did, because the doing has proven itself over a span of time so long that proving itself is no longer the relevant frame. A foundry-family that has cast the same six patterns for eleven generations is not resisting innovation. They are casting the six patterns that the city needs, which are the patterns the grandmothers showed them, which fit the molds they have, which fit the furnaces they have, which fit the fuel-lines they have, which were laid by people whose names are still spoken. Innovation would require not just a new idea but a new mold, a new furnace, a new fuel-line, a new set of hands trained in the new thing, and a reason large enough to justify disturbing a thousand sleeping arrangements between people and people, people and animals, animals and animals. The reason rarely comes. When it does, they act — they are not incapable of change — but the acting is slow and consultative and involves the whole terrace and sometimes the terraces above and below, and by the time it is done the change has been absorbed into the continuity so completely that no one afterward remembers it was a change.

They have specialists. Foundrymen, as mentioned — they have their own way of walking, a slight lean forward from carrying heat all day, and their eyes are more amber than most because the furnaces redden everything over time. Pipe-tenders, who climb the trunk-lines with small hammers and listen for the notes that mean a rivet is about to let go after six hundred years of holding. Grazer-herders, though *herd* is the wrong word — the grazers go where they go and the herders walk with them, keeping count, noting which walls have been cleaned and which still need doing, sometimes redirecting the flow with a soft call that the grazers acknowledge by turning one ear. Cistern-keepers, who know every eel-thing in their cistern by the pattern of its back and who speak to them in a murmur that the eels are said to understand, though no one has ever claimed the eels speak back. Lamp-lighters, a hereditary office, though the lamps are gas and do not strictly need to be lit — they light anyway, with a long brass wand, because the lighting is the office, and the office is what holds that family in its place in the fabric. Wren-watchers, who do not disturb the cinder-wrens but observe them, because the wrens' behavior in the flues is how you know the flues are healthy, and a quiet flue is a dangerous flue. Bone-setters and midwives and the people who braid the cords that record the births and the deaths and the small events worth remembering, which are not many in any given year but accumulate.

They have a priesthood of sorts, though it is not organized as a priesthood. Certain elders, usually but not always women, usually but not always old, are recognized as knowing the city — not as knowing *about* the city, but as knowing it the way you know a person you have lived with all your life. They are consulted when something has to be decided. They walk the terraces slowly. They put a hand on a pipe and listen. They sit with the grazers. They come back and say what the city wants, and the city, through them, is usually right, because what the city wants is usually what it has always wanted, which is to keep going as it is going.

They eat the animals. This is worth saying plainly, because from outside it might look like the city's fauna are pets or neighbors, and they are neither, exactly. The people eat the grazers, selectively, taking old ones and sick ones and the occasional young male in numbers the herders have worked out over centuries. They eat the bat-moths in quantity, netted at dusk, dried in the warm updrafts over the foundries, pounded into a protein-paste that is the staple of every meal. They eat the eel-things on festival days. They do not eat the predators and they do not eat the cinder-wrens, and these prohibitions are not religious but practical — the predators keep the grazer population healthy by taking the weak ones the herders miss, and the cinder-wrens keep the flues clear, and to eat either would be to eat part of the city's own working, which would be a stupidity so profound that no one considers it. The predators, for their part, do not eat the people. This is an arrangement. It was perhaps not always an arrangement. It is one now.

They love their children with the particular tenderness of people who do not expect to lose many. Infant mortality here is low, by historical standards — the air is clean of most diseases because most diseases cannot survive the combination of sulfur from the furnaces and the aggressive competition of the native microfauna that lives in the condensation on every surface. Children grow up on the catwalks, learning the pump-rhythm before they learn to speak, learning which overhangs have bat-moths and which have wasp-analogs, learning to read the weeping of the iron for the day's humidity. They play games that involve counting — counting drips, counting wing-beats between lamp-flickers, counting the seconds between their own heartbeats and the bellows — and these games are also their earliest education, because to live here is to be counted into the rhythm of the place.

They make love, and marry, and grieve, and quarrel, and reconcile, and tell jokes, and get drunk on a thin bitter beer fermented from a fungus they cultivate in the warm pipe-shadows, and compose songs that are mostly about specific pipes and specific animals and specific people who lived a long time ago, and die, usually in their eighties or nineties, and are wrapped in oiled wool and lowered through a hatch in the lowest terrace into a shaft that drops somewhere warm, where something eats the warm damp stone, and perhaps eats them too, and returns them to the loop.

They do not think of themselves as trapped. An outside observer might, looking at the narrow range of a life lived within a few terraces, the unchanging work, the saturation of the air and of the schedule and of the social world. But the people themselves, if you asked them — and they would answer you politely, quietly, with the small pause they use before any statement of importance — would say that they live in a place that is full. Full of work. Full of kin. Full of animals. Full of the pulse of the pump, which is the pulse of the city, which is, in a sense, their own pulse, amplified and slowed and made audible. They would say that the fog holds them, and the iron holds them, and the long habit of the place holds them, and that being held is not the same as being confined.

They would say, if pressed, that they are the lamps. Not the light — the light is the city's — but the lamps. Small, flickering in the pump-rhythm, gas-fed from a network older than memory, burning in every doorway whether or not they are needed, because the burning is the thing, and the burning is what they do, and they have been doing it, in whatever way a people can do anything, for a long time, and intend to go on doing it.
