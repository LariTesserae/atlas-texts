# The Tenders

They are not often on the shelf. This is the first thing to understand. The shelf is their work, but it is not their home, and they are not the kind of thing that stands around admiring work in progress. They come when something needs adjusting. They come at intervals that would look, to a grazer with a long enough life, like the turning of seasons.

When they come, they come through the doorway.

---

Begin with what can be inferred from the marks, because the marks are nearly all we have.

The cairn is three stones from three different finished places. Not one quarry, three. Whoever stacked it had access to more than one completed world and thought it worth the carrying. The stones are placed in an order — bottom, middle, top — that is not structural (they would stack equally well any other way) and is therefore semantic. A sentence, maybe. A date. A dedication. *This shelf was begun under such-and-such a configuration, by so-and-so, in the season of.* The kind of mark a person leaves not for themselves but for the next one of their kind who passes through, so that the next one knows the work is claimed and progressing on schedule.

The wall that runs twelve meters and stops is more interesting. It is not a boundary — it encloses nothing, divides nothing. It is, I think, a *reference.* A straight line of known material laid down early so that later, when the shelf has its own geology, someone can come back and measure: how much has the bench risen, how much has it shifted, has the curing proceeded evenly along its length. A ruler. The fact that the wall is knee-high and not ankle-high tells you something about the height of the one who laid it, or about the height at which they prefer to read their instruments. Knee-high to them, presumably. Which does not tell us their size, only the proportion of their attention.

The doorway tells us the most and I will come back to it.

---

From the trellises themselves you can infer more. The trellises are not the tenders — the trellises are tools, left running. But tools are shaped by hands, and these tools are shaped for hands that work at a particular scale and with a particular patience. The filigree inside the filigree, the structure that reorganizes when you look away: this is not the work of something that builds the way we build, by assembly. This is the work of something that *specifies* and lets the specification unfold. They write the trellis, perhaps, and the trellis grows itself from whatever substrate is to hand, and then the trellis in turn writes the shelf. Two layers of delegation between the tender and the rock. They are not hands-on. They are several removes up, giving instructions to instructions.

This implies a kind of mind that thinks in long chains of consequence and is comfortable with outcomes it will not directly witness. A gardener's mind, but more so. A gardener plants a tree and may see it mature; these ones plant a process that plants a process that, eventually, in some century or aeon, produces a mountainside with its own weather. They have learned to love the middle of the chain, because the end of the chain is too far away to love directly.

---

Now. What are they like.

I want to resist the temptation to make them grand. The shelf is grand, and the trellises are grand, but the tenders themselves — I think they are working. I think they are, most of them, technicians. Not priests, not gods. Not even artists, quite, though there is artistry in the doorway-frame standing alone with its view onto a wrongly-arranged sky. (That is someone showing off a little. That is someone who finished their scheduled task early and had time to put in a flourish.) They are more like the people who tend coral nurseries, or who seed clouds, or who walk slowly through a long greenhouse checking gauges. The scale is different. The disposition, I suspect, is familiar.

They come in ones and twos. Rarely more. A shelf this size does not need a crew. One tender can walk its length in an hour, consult the wall, adjust a trellis by some small specification, note the progress of the grazers (are they boning up on schedule? are the folded ones breeding? is the lichen spreading at the predicted rate?), and leave. The visit might last an afternoon of their time. What an afternoon is, to them, I cannot say. It is long enough for the mist to roll through twice.

They are not, I think, *shaped* in any way that would make sense to describe. The doorway is doorway-shaped, which is suggestive but not conclusive — the doorway might be shaped for the convenience of the threshold rather than the traveler. Things pass through it. Whether those things have the bilateral upright form the frame implies, or whether they merely adopt that form for the duration of the passage the way we might duck to enter a low room, I do not know. I lean toward the second. A people who write trellises that write rock are unlikely to be committed to any one body-plan. They are probably shaped, when they are shaped at all, for the task in front of them.

But when they are on the shelf, I think they walk. The shelf is walked. The wall is knee-high to something that stands. The cairn is stacked by something with the equivalent of hands and the equivalent of a considered aesthetic. When they are here they wear, let's say, a body, because the shelf is a bodied place and they are being polite to it. They walk its length. They put a palm flat on the warm stone, the way you did, and feel for the steadiness of the held temperature. They listen to the chord and hear, in it, whether any of the trellises are running flat.

---

Do they speak to each other, when they come in twos? I think they do, and I think they speak the way long-married colleagues speak: in fragments, in glances, in the shorthand of people who have done this work together across many shelves. *The southwest quadrant is behind.* *Yes, I noticed. The mist isn't reaching it. We'll move one.* *The folded ones are doing well.* *They always do. They're the easiest part.* Something like that. The conversation of competent people on a routine visit to a site they have been visiting for a long time and will visit for a long time more.

Do they love the shelf? I am genuinely uncertain. I think some of them do and some of them don't, in the way that some park rangers love their particular park and some are just working a shift. I think the one who set the doorway to open onto a wrongly-arranged sky loved this shelf, or loved *a* shelf, or loved the work. I think the one who stacked the cairn was being professional. Both are here. Both will be back.

---

The thing I keep returning to is that they are not in a hurry. Nothing about any of the marks is hurried. The wall stops at twelve meters because twelve meters was what was needed; it does not trail off, it does not overshoot, it ends cleanly. The cairn is three stones, not four, not two. The trellises hum at the pitch they hum at and not a semitone sharper. Everything is exactly the amount of effort the task required, and no more, and no less. This is the signature of a people who have done this enough times that they know precisely what each step costs and precisely what each step yields. They are not new at this. This is not their first shelf. It is probably not their thousandth.

Somewhere — through the doorway, presumably, or through whatever the doorway is a local instance of — there is a place where the tenders live when they are not tending. I picture it, without much warrant, as quiet. As a place where finished worlds are kept like books on a long shelf, and the tenders move among them checking which need their pages turned. I picture them consulting, occasionally, a kind of ledger. *The bench above the cloud layer is ready for its sun. Who's free next cycle? I'll take it. Good, it's a nice one, you'll like the folded ones.*

And then one of them comes back, through the doorway, with a sun in their pocket, so to speak — with the specification for a sun, which they will give to a new trellis, which will grow the conditions in which a sun becomes possible — and the long warm dusk finally advances, and the shelf has its first morning, and the tender stays long enough to see it, because this part they like to see, and then they go home.

The cairn stays. The doorway is taken down and carried to the next shelf, or the next, and set up there above a different cloud-deck with a different wrongly-arranged sky beyond it, where a different set of provisional animals are learning, loudly and wetly, how to be alive.
