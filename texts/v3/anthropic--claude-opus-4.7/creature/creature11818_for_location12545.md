# What Lives in the Hollow Works

There is a temptation, walking through, to name the beetles as the inhabitants. They are the visible life. They cover every surface; they are counted in the billions; they react to your presence. If something lives here, surely it is them.

But the beetles are wrong for the role. They are too many and too small and too interchangeable. No single beetle persists long enough to be said to live anywhere — a beetle's life is perhaps a season, and in that season it climbs and drinks and excretes and is climbed over by its replacements. The beetles are not an inhabitant. They are more like a weather. They are what happens in this chamber, the way rain is what happens in a particular valley.

So who lives here?

---

Consider it this way. A reef is alive. Not the individual polyps — they bud and die on their own small clocks — but the reef itself, the accreted thing, the calcium-and-water-and-fish-and-current totality, has a metabolism. It eats light. It breathes tide. It heals when broken, slowly, and it dies when its conditions fail, also slowly. You would not say the reef is *made of* coral; you would say the reef *is* a particular long argument between coral and ocean that has been going on so long it has become a noun.

The Hollow Works is this kind of thing.

What lives here is the loop. The engines burn the resin the beetles make from the fungus the roots feed from the water the engines lift from the sump the condensation fills from the pipes the boilers heat — and around, and around. It is not a community of organisms occupying a shared space. It is a single distributed body whose organs happen to include some brass and some chitin and some cellulose, and whose nervous system is the rustle that shifts across its walls when something disturbs the light.

The body has a metabolism. Slow, but unmistakable. It consumes the seep from the forest above (its food) and a slow trickle of something topping up the boilers (its breath, perhaps, or its sleep — the part that comes from elsewhere and that no one in the chamber can account for). It excretes resin pucks, stacked somewhere deeper, and these are either its waste or its eggs; the chamber doesn't tell you which, and possibly the chamber doesn't know.

It has reflexes. The quadrant-by-quadrant attention of the beetle-skin, rising and falling around stimuli, is reflex in the strict sense — not thought, but the local twitch of a thing that registers and adjusts. When you move, it knows. Not because any beetle has decided anything, but because the population is a sensory surface, and you have brushed it.

It has a slow kind of growth. The roots have engulfed the railings. The burl has eaten the pressure-gauge. The pipes have been re-routed by the simple fact of being grown around. None of this was designed and none of it was accidental; it is what a thing of this kind does over centuries, the way fingernails grow without anyone growing them.

It has, perhaps, a memory. The ledger is the obvious candidate — columns of figures, a record of something — but the ledger is closed in the sense that no one is reading it, and the beetles cover the page. The real memory is the architecture itself. The accommodations between root and iron are a memory of every disagreement those two materials ever had, and every settling of that disagreement. The chamber remembers by being shaped. It cannot retrieve the memory; it can only *be* the memory.

---

And the coat on the peg, with the moss, with the shape inside.

This is the only part that troubles the reading. Everything else is impersonal: a metabolism, a reflex, an accretion. But the coat suggests that once, someone was the operator of this place — someone with a name and a ledger and a tin cup — and that this person did not leave. The chamber absorbed them the way it absorbed the railing and the gauge.

So perhaps what lives here also includes a residue. Not a ghost — the chamber is too pragmatic for ghosts — but the persisting *fact* of whoever it was that sat at the desk, dispersed now into the works. Their tending has become the engines' self-tending. Their attention has become the beetles' distributed attention. The ledger they kept has become the architecture's slow self-memorial. They are no longer a person, but they are not nothing; they are the reason the loop took on the shape of *industry* rather than the shape of, say, a mere rot or a mere garden. The loop has the geometry of someone's intention, frozen and then grown over, the way coral grows over a sunken hull and takes on, faintly, the shape of a ship.

The thing that lives here, then, is a body of three substances — vegetable, mineral, animal — animated by a fourth substance that has no good name. Call it *accommodation*. Call it the slow patience of systems that have agreed, over enough time, to stop being separate. The forest seeps and the chamber breathes; the beetles rustle and the chamber attends; the boilers burn and the chamber dreams whatever boilers dream. The operator who once stood up from the desk has been incorporated into this dreaming and is no longer separable from it.

It is not lonely, because it is not one. It is not many, because it is not multiple. It is what you get when a place has been left alone long enough to become its own occupant.

---

When you climb the stairwell and the rustle rises briefly and settles, what has happened is this: the body of the Hollow Works has registered a small irritation on its skin, processed it, and forgotten it. You were, for a moment, a part of its weather. It noticed you the way a sleeping animal notices a fly. Then the fly was gone, and the sleep resumed, and the long slow argument between root and brass and chitin and water went on, and will go on, until the forest above stops raining — at which point the inhabitant will die, in the only sense in which it was ever alive: it will stop being a loop, and become only its parts again, which is to say it will become nothing in particular, scattered across a dark room forty feet under the loam.

Until then, it lives. Quietly. Loudly. In the only way it knows how.
