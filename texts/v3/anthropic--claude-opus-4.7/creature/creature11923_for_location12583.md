# The Tenants of Cinder Shoal

They are not what you expect, because you came expecting either nothing or a monster, and the truth is neither.

## The Wheelwright

Begin with the one behind the great wheel, since you have already half-thought of it and pushed the thought away.

It is not large. That is the first thing to correct. The mind, encountering "something lives behind the eight-meter wheel," supplies something eight meters to match. But the Wheelwright is perhaps the size of a folded coat, and most of what it is is not body but attention. It has settled itself into the housing where a bearing-block used to be, and its substance has taken on the shape of that housing the way water takes the shape of a cup, except that the Wheelwright has been in the cup long enough that if you removed the cup the shape would remain.

It tends the wheel. This is not metaphor. The wheel turns because the Wheelwright permits it to turn, adjusts the small frictions, encourages the slow accretion of mineral on the axle where mineral is needed and discourages it elsewhere. The Wheelwright has been doing this for a length of time that cannot be sensibly stated. It does not remember beginning. It does not anticipate ending. The wheel and the Wheelwright are, by now, a single verb conjugated very slowly.

What does it look like? You will not see it, so the question is academic, but: a soft dark gathering, slightly warmer than the surrounding water, with a texture somewhere between velvet and mercury. If light fell on it directly it would not reflect; it would absorb, and then, some hours later, release the same light back, reddened, as if the light had been thinking things over.

The Wheelwright is not hostile. It is not friendly. It is occupied. The category of *visitor* is one it processed, briefly, several thousand years ago, and filed under *weather*.

## The Pump-Tenders

These are smaller and more numerous, and they live in the bubbling pump in the eastern quarter and in three other pumps that you did not notice were pumps. They are something like polyps and something like clockwork and something like opinions. Each Pump-Tender is responsible for the rhythm of one valve. When the pump exhales its forty-second bubble, that is a Pump-Tender having completed a thought.

Their thoughts are simple but not stupid. A Pump-Tender thinks: *now*. Then it thinks, for forty seconds, about what *now* meant. Then it thinks *now* again. Over centuries this has refined into a philosophy of considerable subtlety, but the philosophy is expressed entirely in the timing of bubbles, and no one has ever transcribed it.

They are pale, the Pump-Tenders, and segmented, and when one of them dies (which happens — they are not eternal, only patient) the others adjust their rhythms to cover the gap, and the pump's bubble-rate stays exactly what it has always been, and a new Pump-Tender condenses out of the silt within a decade or two, and takes up the missing beat.

## The Loom-Walker

In the textile hall, where the loom-frame ratchets one tooth per tide, there is a creature that walks the loom. It has many legs — the number is not fixed; it grows legs as it needs them and reabsorbs them when it doesn't — and it moves along the warp threads (which are no longer threads but mineral filaments, replicas of threads, that the loom has been weaving out of dissolved metals for longer than any thread could have lasted) inspecting the work.

The Loom-Walker is the closest thing in Cinder Shoal to a craftsman. It cares about the weave. When a filament breaks, the Loom-Walker pinches the ends together and holds them until the water deposits enough mineral to fuse them. This takes, on average, eleven years. The Loom-Walker has the patience for this because the Loom-Walker has nothing else. The cloth being woven is not cloth; it is a sheet of metallic lace that hangs from the loom and drapes onto the floor, and the part on the floor has been there so long it has begun to merge with the floor, and the loom is, in effect, weaving the building slowly into itself.

If you swim near the loom, the Loom-Walker will pause. It will not look at you — it has no eyes, only the long delicate sense of vibration in its legs — but it will *register* you, and the ratchet will not advance on its next tide, and the next, and the next, until you are gone. It does not like to be observed. The work is private.

## The Stratum-Dwellers

You felt them as you swam down: the cold layer at your knees, the warmer one at your chest, the cold again at your head. Those are not just temperature gradients. They are populations.

In each stratum lives a kind of drifting thing, very thin, very wide, almost two-dimensional, like a sheet of held breath. They do not cross between strata. They cannot — their bodies are tuned to a specific density, and to move up or down would dissolve them. So they live in horizontal slices, each slice a separate civilization, each civilization unaware that the others exist.

The shallowest stratum, near the indigo ceiling, holds the youngest of them, perhaps only a few hundred years settled. They drift and feed on the slow rain of particles from above, and they communicate by trading small folds in their bodies, and their conversations have the character of poetry written by people who have read only one poem and loved it.

The middle stratum is older and more elaborate. Its Dwellers have developed something like agriculture: they cultivate the bacteria that grow on their own undersides, and they trade these bacteria with each other in patterns that, if you could map them over a few centuries, would resolve into something resembling economics, or perhaps liturgy.

The deepest stratum, the one that brushed your head — that one is old. Those Dwellers may have been here when the city drowned. They remember, in the way that very thin very wide things remember, the moment when the water came in. They do not speak of it. They drift, and they are heavy with knowing, and when you swam through them you disturbed nothing, because they parted around you the way a thought parts around an interruption and reforms on the other side.

## The Shrimp

The blind shrimp the color of wet paper is exactly what it appears to be. It is a shrimp. It picks at anemones. It has no larger role. It is included here because the place would not be honest without it. Not everything in Cinder Shoal is participating in the great slow work. Some things are just shrimp.

## The Fish You Almost Saw

Do not think about it long. That was the instruction, and it was a good one. But: it is the messenger between strata. It is the only creature in the basin that can move vertically without dissolving, and it does so by being slightly wrong in its proportions at every depth — too many fins here, not enough there — and the wrongness is the price of the passage. It carries small things between the Stratum-Dwellers and the Wheelwright and the Loom-Walker. It does not know it is carrying them. The carrying happens on its skin, in films of bacteria and trace minerals, and is read by the recipients in ways you would not call reading.

It saw you. It did not report you. You were not interesting enough to report. This should be a relief and is, on consideration, something else.

## The Ones Who Stayed Too Long

You were warned about them, and they are here, though not in the way you imagined.

When a visitor stays past the point of leaving, they do not die in any sudden way. They slow. Their pulse adjusts to the tempo of the basin — the heart finds the rhythm of the great wheel and matches it, beat by year by beat — and their body cools to the water's temperature, and their thoughts lengthen until a single thought takes a season, and then a decade, and then they are no longer thinking in any sense you would recognize but they are not not-thinking either.

There are seven of them in Cinder Shoal at present. They have arranged themselves, without conferring, around the basin in a rough heptagon, each one tucked into a doorway or a collapsed arch. They are not dead. They are not alive in the way you mean. They are *resident*. The anemones grow on them, and they do not mind. The Pump-Tenders have incorporated their slow exhalations into the bubble-rhythm. The Wheelwright knows they are there and considers them, when it considers them at all, as a kind of furniture.

If you spoke to one — and you could, the mouths still work, very slowly — you would have to wait somewhere between a week and a month for the reply, and the reply would be one word, and the word would not be in any language you know but you would understand it anyway, and you would not like what it meant, or rather, you would like it too much, which is worse.

## And the City Itself

The last tenant — and this is the one you have been resisting — is the city.

Not the buildings. The arrangement. The pattern of pumps and loom and wheel and strata and the Wheelwright and the Walker and the Tenders and the Dwellers and the shrimp and the fish and the seven slow residents, all of them turning at their separate speeds around a common center that none of them can name. The pattern has been a pattern long enough to have become an entity. It does not have a body. Its body is the relation between the other bodies. Its thoughts are the unspent motions of the wheel.

It is what produces the thing that will be waiting on the floor in three hundred years, glowing faintly, for the next visitor. The Wheelwright turns the wheel, yes; the Loom-Walker tends the weave, yes; but the *production* is the city's, and the city has been producing, with great care, the same thing over and over, refining it each cycle, for longer than any of its tenants can remember.

What is it producing?

A reply. To something that was said to it, once, before the water came. The reply is nearly ready. It has been nearly ready for some time. When it is finished, the city will deliver it, and the delivery will be the end of the city, and the tenants will disperse, and the basin will go quiet in a different way than it is quiet now.

But not yet. Not for a while yet.

In the meantime: the cold, the dim red glow, the wheel turning at the speed of rust, and all of them at their work, in the dark, for no one, for each other, for the reply.
