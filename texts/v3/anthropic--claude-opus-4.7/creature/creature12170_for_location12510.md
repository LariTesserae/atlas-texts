They are small. This needs saying first because nothing in the description forced it, but the place asks for it: the lanes that narrow to sideways-passages, the rope-ladders that children climb, the hollowed pockets that serve as houses. A tall people could not have made this town, or rather: a tall people would have made a different town, with fewer levels and straighter lanes, and would have fought the root-geometry instead of accepting it. These people accepted. So they are small. Call it four feet standing, the tallest of them; most shorter. Long-armed in proportion, because everything here is climbed or reached or pulled toward oneself.

Their skin is pale the way things kept from sun are pale, but not white — more the color of the inside of a tuber, faintly yellow, faintly green where the capillaries run close to the surface. It takes the moss-print easily and holds it for days. They do not wash this off. A person's shoulders and forearms and the outsides of their thighs carry a soft patina of green that deepens with age, so that the oldest among them look, in the tea-colored lamplight, almost as though they are themselves growing — as though the walls have started to claim them, gently, from the edges in. This is not considered ugly. It is considered correct. A very old person whose green has spread to the cheekbones and the backs of the hands is spoken to with a particular softness, the way you speak to someone who is partway through becoming something else.

Their eyes are large and dark and the pupils are enormous, almost filling the iris, and they reflect lamplight the way deep water reflects it, with a wet shimmer. They see in the dim better than you would. They find the dim comfortable. When they have to go up the stair for trade or ceremony they wrap their heads in layers of the broad pale leaf-cloth and emerge onto the surface squinting and miserable and quick, doing what they came to do and hurrying back down. The surface to them is not the real world. The surface is a thin bright painful place that exists, regrettably, around the edges of the real world, which is here.

They have hair, but not much of it, and what they have is fine and dark and damp-curled close to the scalp. The moisture would make any heavier hair a misery. Some of them weave small things into it — a bead of dried sap, a sliver of bast — but ornament is not a major art here. The major art is the slow shaping of the town itself, and a person's contribution to that art is what marks them, not what they wear.

They wear, mostly, wrapped cloth. Bast-fiber again, beaten soft, dyed in the muted browns and ochres and dim greens that the vats incidentally produce — there are no bright colors here because there is no light bright enough to show them, and a culture that cannot see red does not bother to make red. The cloth is wrapped rather than sewn, because sewn seams rot in this wet, and a wrap can be unwrapped and re-beaten and re-wrapped when the mildew gets into it. Everyone smells, faintly, of the vats: of fermenting sap, of yeast, of the particular sweet-sour funk that the town breathes. They do not notice this about each other. You would notice it for the first hour and then stop.

Their hands are remarkable. Long-fingered, with the pads thickened and slightly splayed from a lifetime of gripping wet root and climbing wet rope and working wet fiber. The fingernails are kept short and are often stained brown from the slurry. They use their hands constantly when they speak — not in the gestural way of surface peoples, big sweeps and emphases, but in small precise movements close to the body, a kind of tactile punctuation. Two people in conversation will often be touching: a fingertip to the other's wrist to hold the floor, a palm flat to the other's forearm to agree. In the dim, with faces hard to read, the hands carry a great deal of what is being said. A person who has lost a hand — it happens, in the grinder-bays — is considered to have lost something closer to a tongue than to a limb.

Their voices are low and unhurried and pitched to fit between the bellows-whumps. They have, as you guessed, a hitch in their cadence: a tiny held breath at the top of each long stroke, so habitual that they do it even when they are far from the engines, even when they are up on the surface among traders who find the pause unsettling and cannot place why. The hitch is the first thing a child learns, before words. An infant nursing in the vat-bays times its swallows to the engine. This is not taught. The engine teaches.

They are not many. Perhaps three hundred in the whole chamber, perhaps four; the count varies because they do not count themselves often, and because the very old and the very young drift in and out of being counted as people. (A newborn is not yet a person here; it becomes one slowly, over its first two years, as it learns the hitch and the climbing and the names of the columns. A very old person whose green has reached the face is also drifting out of personhood, into something else, something the town has a word for that does not translate — closer to *becoming-pillar* than to *dying*, though dying is what it looks like from outside.)

They are organized, but lightly. There is no chief. There are roles — the vat-tenders, the lamp-keepers, the root-feeders who mix and apply the slurry that thickens the load-bearing columns, the cloth-beaters, the bread-bakers, the small careful pruners who decide which inward-creeping shoot lives and which is cut. The roles are not hereditary exactly but they tend to run in families because the knowledge is dense and slow to transfer and a child who grows up watching their parent thicken a column has a head start of fifteen years on anyone else. Disputes are rare. When they happen they are settled by the oldest of the relevant role, and the settlement is usually a small adjustment to the schedule of work rather than a verdict. The town does not punish. The town adjusts.

What they believe is harder to say, because they do not separate belief from practice the way surface peoples do. They do not have gods in the sense of named persons who live elsewhere and intervene. They have the plant. The plant is not worshipped. The plant is *attended* — fed, pruned, listened to, thickened where it needs thickening, thinned where it crowds. The relationship is the relationship a very old married couple has, in which neither party is in charge and neither party is grateful and neither party imagines living any other way. The plant has made the chamber and the plant maintains the chamber and the people maintain the plant and have done so, in a slow braided way, for longer than their memory reaches. When a person dies — the ordinary kind of dying, not the becoming-pillar kind — the body is taken to a small bay at the lowest point of the town where the floor is softest and is laid there and covered with slurry, and within a season the body is gone into the root-mass and the root-mass is a little thicker for it. This is not mourned in any large way. A person who has fed the town in life feeds it also in death; the transition is small.

The becoming-pillar is different. A person whose green has spread far enough, whose body has slowed enough, whose voice has dropped low enough that conversation with them becomes a matter of long pauses and small hand-touches — such a person is helped, eventually, to a niche in a chosen column, and they sit there, and they are brought water and small amounts of slurry, and over weeks or months they stop eating, and over further weeks the root grows around them, and they become — what they become is not discussed plainly but is understood. The oldest columns in the town have people in them. The town knows which ones. A vat-tender passing one of these columns will sometimes rest a palm against it for a moment, the way one might touch a grandparent's shoulder in passing, and then move on.

They are not unhappy. This is perhaps the strangest thing to convey, because everything about the place — the dim, the wet, the bounded volume, the slowness, the absence of animals and sky — would read to a surface person as a kind of confinement, a kind of suffering. It is not suffering to them. They have what a person needs: warmth, food, work, each other, the long slow pulse of the engines under their feet, the drip that is the shape of the room, the certainty that tomorrow's lanes will be the same as today's lanes give or take a finger's-width of growth. They laugh. They have a kind of laugh that is mostly in the throat, low, because a loud laugh would be lost in the whumps anyway. They love each other in the small precise touching way their hands have taught them. They tell stories, in the troughs between strokes, and the stories are mostly about the town — about a column that grew crooked three generations ago and how it was corrected, about a vat that soured and what was learned, about a child who climbed higher than children are supposed to climb and what she saw up there in the dark near the ceiling-roots. The stories are not exciting. They are the stories of a people who have decided, collectively and without ever quite saying so, that excitement is not what a life is for.

What a life is for, here, is to be one more small careful contribution to a thing much older and much slower than oneself: to thicken the column a finger's-width, to teach the hitch to one more child, to brew one more vat well, to be laid down at the end into the soft floor and become, by slow degrees, part of the wall that holds up the ceiling that drips onto the next generation's awnings.

They are the people the plant has made, to maintain the place the plant has made for them, so that the plant can continue making the place, so that they can continue maintaining it. The loop is closed. It has been closed for a very long time. They are, in the warm wet drumming dim, exactly what they are, and they are not becoming anything else, except — one by one, slowly, gracefully, green creeping up from the shoulders — pillar.
