# The People of the Shelf

They are human. That is the first thing to establish, because everything about the place invites you to imagine otherwise — some quieter species, some refined descendant, some order of beings suited to the stillness. But no. They are human in the ordinary way: two arms, two legs, the same range of heights and builds you would find anywhere, hair that goes grey, skin that lines at the corners of the eyes. If one of them walked into a market three valleys over, nobody would turn a head.

What is different is not their bodies but their *rate*.

They move slowly. Not with the slowness of age or infirmity, but with the slowness of something that has no reason to move faster. A woman carrying a bundle of woven reeds from one house to another will take the time it takes, and the time it takes is longer than you would expect. She is not dawdling. She is not distracted. She is simply walking at the pace the shelf asks for. When she sets the bundle down, she sets it down completely — not in the half-placed way of someone already thinking of the next task, but in the full way of someone whose attention is entirely with the bundle, and with the stone beneath it, and with the placing.

They are quiet, as the description says, but the quiet is not shyness or reserve. They speak when there is something to say. There is often nothing to say. Two of them can sit in a doorway together for the better part of an afternoon and exchange perhaps six sentences, and this is not awkward, and not a silence that wants to be broken. It is simply the correct amount of speech for two people sitting in a doorway on an afternoon when nothing has happened that needs remarking on. Their language, when they use it, is soft and somewhat flat in its music, with a lot of short words and few elaborate constructions. You get the sense it has been slowly shedding complexity for a long time — not decaying, exactly, but settling, the way silt settles in still water, until only what is useful remains.

They do not have children often. This is the thing that takes the longest to notice, and once noticed, cannot be unnoticed. There are children in Ansh-Velur — you will see one, occasionally, walking beside an adult, or crouched at the edge of a basin looking into it for a very long time — but there are not many. The population does not grow. It does not shrink either. Each generation replaces itself almost exactly, and the children, when they come, are regarded with a calm attentiveness that is neither doting nor distant. The adults teach them what there is to know: how to cut stone, how to weave, how to fit a sluice, how to sit in a doorway for an afternoon. The children learn. They become adults. The adults become old and then, one day, they are not there anymore, and a house is quietly cleaned and kept ready, and eventually someone else lives in it. There is no ceremony around any of this that a traveler would recognize as such. Things simply continue.

They eat sparingly. There is some cultivation on the shelf — the broad-leaved plants are not all decorative; certain of them bear a dense, starchy fruit that ripens slowly and is gathered in small quantities. They keep no animals, but they harvest a kind of pale fungus that grows in the basins, skimmed off the stones at the waterline, and this provides what the plants do not. They drink the water, of course. The water is clean, and so cold at its source that it numbs the tongue, and they drink it from cups of fired clay that fit the hand.

They do not worship, as far as anyone can tell. There are no temples. There are no priests. There are no carvings on the walls of their houses that could be called religious, though some of the stones are cut with patterns — long parallel grooves, or nested squares — that seem to have some meaning the carvers declined to explain. If you ask one of them about the patterns, they will consider the question carefully, as though it had not been asked before, and then say something like: *my grandfather cut that one*, or *the stone wanted that shape*, and then they will be finished with the subject.

They are not unhappy. This is the important thing, and the hardest thing to convey, because a reader from outside will want them to be unhappy. Will want the stillness to be a kind of trap — will want to find, beneath the quiet, some slow grief or suppressed longing, some knowledge they have forfeited and cannot retrieve. It is not there. They are not waiting to be released. They are not mourning anything. They have found, or been handed, or slowly grown into, a kind of life that does not require the forward motion the rest of us take for granted as the basic shape of existence, and they inhabit it without strain. If you sat with one of them at dusk, by one of the basins, and asked — carefully, in their slow soft language — whether they were content, they would not understand the question as you meant it. Content compared to what? There is no what. There is this.

And yet — and this is the thing that will keep the traveler up at night, in the small clean stone room with its one window open to the water-sounds — they *know*. They know about the rest of the world. They are not naive. Travelers do come, occasionally, and the people of Ansh-Velur listen to them with polite attention and do not argue and do not envy. They know that elsewhere there are cities, armies, machines, sorrows, great projects, children by the thousand, songs with many verses. They know. They have simply chosen — or their ancestors chose, or the shelf chose on their behalf — not to participate. Somewhere, far back, someone decided that enough was enough, and the decision held, and has been holding, and will continue to hold, as long as the cliffs continue to weep and the young rock continues not to age.

They are what happens to people when nothing requires them to become anything else.

When a traveler stays — and travelers do stay, now and then, not often, but enough — the people do not welcome them with any particular warmth, and do not turn them away. A house is found. It was being kept ready. The traveler is shown how to tend their channel, how to skim the basin, which plants bear fruit in which season. No one asks them to stay and no one asks them to leave. Over months, the traveler speaks less. Over years, the traveler moves more slowly. Eventually the traveler is no longer a traveler, and is simply another one of them, sitting in a doorway on a warm damp afternoon, watching the light shift on the wet stone, occupying the space that has been allotted and no more.

This is how the town maintains its number. Not by birth. By absorption. The shelf takes in, now and then, the ones who were already looking for it without knowing. And the ones who were not looking for it leave after a day or two, uneasy, unable to say why, hurrying back down the switchbacks toward whatever they had been doing before, which will now seem, for a long time after, slightly less urgent than it did.
