# The People of Sett

## First, what you notice

They are not many. You knew that from the houses, but it bears saying again from the inside: if you stand at the spring in the morning and wait, you will see perhaps a dozen people come and go before the sun is high. Some of them will nod. Some will not, not from rudeness but because a nod is for a particular kind of meeting and this is not one of them. They have a finer grain of greeting than you are used to, and most of it is silent.

They are unhurried in a way that is hard to describe without making them sound slow. They are not slow. A woman wringing a sheet at the channel wrings it with full attention and reasonable speed and then she is done and walks home, and the walking is the same speed as the wringing, which is the speed of someone who has nowhere else they need to be because they are already where they are going to be next, also.

## Bodies

They are smallish, most of them, and wiry. Mountain people, with the slightly barrel-chested look that comes of generations breathing the thinner air. Their skin is weathered — not aged, exactly, but finished, the way a tool handle is finished by being held. Hands are large for the bodies. Faces show the wind. Eyes tend toward the pale end, grey and grey-green and grey-blue, but this may be coincidence, or it may be that one or two pairs of eyes a few centuries ago became most of the eyes here now.

The children look like the adults, only newer. The old look like the adults, only worn deeper into the same shape. There is no sense, walking the streets, of distinct generations succeeding one another; there is a sense of one continuous people of which some are currently young and some are currently old.

## What they do

Each person seems to have a thing, and the thing seems to be enough. The woman at the loom weaves; this is not all she does — she also cooks, draws water, mends — but the loom is hers in a way the cooking is not. The potter throws. The man who splits kindling splits kindling for many houses, not only his own, and in return things arrive at his door without comment: a loaf, a length of cloth, a repaired hinge.

There is no money visible. There may be money — somewhere a small box of old coins for the rare traveler, for the rare thing that comes up from below — but in daily life the exchanges happen by a slower accounting that everyone seems to keep without writing anything down. Nobody appears to be owed. Nobody appears to be owing. The books, if there are books, are balanced by being unnecessary.

They each do their work with a competence that is striking until you realize it isn't excellence — it isn't anyone trying to be the best — it is just that they have been doing this since they were small, and were taught by someone who had also been doing it since they were small, and so on back. The pot the potter throws is a good pot. It is not better than last year's. It is not worse. It is the pot that this potter throws.

## How they speak

Quietly, and not much. Their language — you can hear it through a shutter or at the spring — has a lot of short words and a lot of pauses. They seem to leave space around what they say, the way the houses leave space around the junipers. A conversation between two of them at the channel might consist of four exchanges over the course of drawing two buckets, and would cover, somehow, everything that needed covering.

They laugh. Not loud, but real laughter, often at small things — a slipped bucket, a cat's expression, a child's pronouncement. The laughter is not held back; it just doesn't carry far, because nothing in Sett carries far. The walls are thick and the air is thin and sound stays where it is made.

They sing sometimes, in the evenings, in groups of three or four, in houses with the shutters half-open. The songs are old. Nobody who lives there could tell you exactly how old. The songs are simply *the songs*, the way the junipers are *the junipers*.

## What they know

A great deal, about a small set of things.

They know their weather — not in the way a forecaster knows weather, but in the way a body knows its own breath. They know that this kind of fog will burn off and that kind will sit until noon. They know which slates on which roofs are due for attention, and they know without being told whose roof it is, and the knowing of it is also the doing of it, eventually.

They know each other's families back through many turnings, and they know the small histories of the houses — that this lintel was set after the great cold, that this door was hung by so-and-so's grandmother, that this corner of this wall took a year to settle after the small earthshake. The histories are not told as stories. They are simply available, the way the spring is available, if you go to the right place and ask in the right way.

They do not know much about the world below. This is not from incuriosity. It is that the world below has not, in their experience, had much to offer that Sett needed, and Sett has had little it wished to send down. Once or twice a year someone comes up — a trader, a wanderer, occasionally a person looking for something they have not been able to find lower down — and they are received with politeness and given water and, if they want it, a bed. Most leave after a day or two. A very few stay, and the town absorbs them slowly, and within a generation their descendants have the grey eyes and the wiry build and weave or throw or split kindling for the others.

## What they believe

This is harder. They do not seem to have a temple, exactly, though the small square with the three junipers has a quality that approaches one, and people do pause there. They do not seem to pray in any visible way. But they treat certain things — the spring, the junipers, the oldest hearthstones, certain hours of certain days — with a settled attention that suggests these things matter in a way beyond their use.

If you asked one of them what they believed, you might not get an answer, or you might get an answer that did not feel like an answer: *that the spring runs, that the stones stay, that we are here while we are here.* Said not as a creed but as the kind of thing you say when someone asks you something whose answer is already obvious to everyone present.

They are not afraid of death. This is not bravery; it is something more like familiarity. The old die in their houses, in the rooms they were born in or in rooms much like them, and are buried in a small terraced place just outside the lower end of the town, where the spring water disappears into the scree. The graves are marked by single stones, undressed, and the stones weather and become part of the slope, and after some long time it becomes hard to say where the graveyard ends and the mountain resumes. This does not trouble them. It seems, if anything, to be the point.

## What they are, taken together

A people who have agreed, without ever having had to agree, to be a certain way in a certain place, and who have been being that way long enough that the way and the place and the people are no longer three things but one thing.

If you came back in fifty years, you would meet different people in the same houses, doing the same work, drawing from the same spring, nodding the same small nods. You would not be able to tell, from the outside, that anything had happened. From the inside, everything would have happened — births, deaths, a good year for the loom, a bad winter, a child who showed promise at the wheel, a cat that lived seventeen years and was missed — and all of it would have happened *within* the town, the way weather happens within a sky, without the sky becoming a different sky.

They are, you might finally say, the form that the place takes when the place has people in it. And the place is the form that they take when they have somewhere to be.
