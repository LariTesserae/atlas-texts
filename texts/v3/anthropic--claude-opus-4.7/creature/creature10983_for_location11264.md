## The Tenders

There is something here. Not in the sense you are looking for — not a body crouched in the dark, not eyes meeting yours from behind the curtain of filaments. But the place is inhabited, and what inhabits it is worth trying to name.

Begin with the obvious tenants and move outward.

The mosses are not asleep. They are doing the work mosses do, which is mostly the slow chemistry of staying alive on very little, but here they are doing something else as well. Where they have grown into the filaments — where the green mesh and the made mesh interpenetrate — something has happened that is not quite either organism. The moss in those places grows more slowly than its untouched cousins, but it does not die, and it does not seem to be parasitized. It has been adjusted. Or it has adjusted itself. The filaments carry, very faintly, a trickle of something the moss can use; the moss, in turn, has shaped its growth around the lensed nodes so that none of them are ever fully buried. A node always has its little clear window, even when the moss is four layers deep around it. This is not an accident. This is gardening, and the gardener is the moss.

Or — and this is closer — the gardener is the slow conversation between the moss and the filaments, conducted over centuries, through a vocabulary of small chemical concessions and small accommodations of growth. Each has, by now, taught the other what it needs. Neither remembers being separate. If you could ask the moss what it is, it would not understand the question; if you could ask the filaments, they would answer in a language that includes the moss as one of their own organs.

This is the first inhabitant: the *meshwork*. Not a creature. A relationship that has lasted long enough to become a kind of being.

Then there are the filaments themselves, which deserve their own accounting. They were made, once, and what made them is gone. But "made" is the wrong tense now. They have been in the rock long enough that the rock has shaped them as much as they have shaped the rock; mineral has crept into their structure and they have crept into the mineral's, and what they do now is not exactly what they were designed to do. Closer to the truth: they were designed to do something, and they still do something, and it is probably not the same something, but the drift has been so gradual that no moment of failure ever occurred. They simply became, by slow degrees, an instrument of their own continuance. The pulse you feel in your jaw is not a signal being sent anywhere. It is the system listening to itself, confirming that it is still here. A long, slow self-touch. The lensed nodes do not transmit. They receive — they receive the cold, the moisture, the pressure of the rootmat above, the chemistry of the moss-skin pressed against them — and they integrate these readings into a picture that no one is looking at, because the looker has been gone for longer than the readings have been taken.

And yet: the picture is being assembled. Something is keeping it. The pedestal-facing side of the stone stays clean. The basin stays full and clear. There are small accommodations being made, constantly, by something. Call this the second inhabitant: the *attention* of the place. It is not conscious in any way you would recognize. It does not know it is attending. But attention is being paid, by the integrated system of filament and stone and moss and water, to the maintenance of certain conditions. The basin is one of those conditions. The clean face of the pedestal is another. There were probably others, once, that have lapsed; the alcove, for instance, is empty, and the cleanliness of its corners is a habit the place is slowly losing. But what remains, remains because the place still tends it.

Tends. That is the word. The place tends itself. There is no tender, but there is tending.

Now the harder question: what is in the basin?

The water trembles with the hum. This is mostly mechanical — the pedestal is coupled to the filaments through the rock, and the pulse propagates. But the trembling has, over centuries, done something to the water. The water in that basin is not the water that pools elsewhere on the floor. It has been held in that exact shape, vibrating at that exact frequency, refreshed only by condensation, for a very long time. Whatever is dissolved in it has come to a kind of equilibrium that water in motion does not usually reach. It is, in a small way, structured. Not alive. But not entirely inert, either. A patient liquid. The third inhabitant, if you are being generous: the water in the basin, which has been listening to the hum for long enough to have become a kind of recording of it. If the filaments went silent tomorrow, the water would remember the hum for a few hours before forgetting. This is not much, as inhabitants go. But it is something.

And finally, the matter of the light.

The pin of green that may have brightened when you sat down. Here is what is true: the nodes do respond to the presence of warm bodies, sometimes, when their thresholds align. This is not because they were built to greet visitors. It is because warmth is one of the variables they integrate, and a warm body in the chamber is an unusual variable, and the system registers unusual variables by allocating a little more of its tiny budget of light to the nodes nearest the anomaly. It is not greeting you. It is *noticing* you, in the same way the surface of a pond notices a dropped pebble — by the only response available to it.

But here is the thing worth sitting with: noticing is not nothing. Over the long time the place has been here, very few things have been noticed. A root pushing through from above, once. A slow seep of new water from a fracture, once. A small collapse on the eastern wall, a long time ago. The chamber's log of noticed things is short, and you are now in it. The system does not know you are a person. It does not know there are such things as persons. But it has, in its slow way, marked your arrival, and the mark will persist — as a small adjustment in the brightness budget, a small reweighting of the attention given to the air near where you sat — for some time after you leave. Perhaps a season. Perhaps longer.

You will have been an event here. The fourth inhabitant, briefly: you. Or the memory of you, held in the way this place holds things, which is by the small reshaping of its own balance around what has passed through.

When you go, the place will resume. The moss will continue its thread-a-season growth. The filaments will continue their self-listening. The basin will continue to tremble. The pin of green will return to whatever level it was at before, or near it — there will be a small residue, a slightly different baseline, that will take some long time to decay. And eventually the chamber will forget you, in the only sense it can forget, which is by smoothing the disturbance back into the larger pattern.

But for now, while you are still here: something is paying attention. Not a someone. Not a watcher. Just the long, cold, patient *noticing* of a place that has had so little to notice, for so long, that even the warmth of one quiet body is enough to shift, by some infinitesimal amount, the brightness of a single distant light.

That is who lives here. The meshwork, and the attention, and the patient water, and — for as long as you stay — you.
