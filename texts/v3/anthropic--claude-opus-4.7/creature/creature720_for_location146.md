The large thing in the canopy is one of them. So is the drifting water-body that folds around you. They are not two kinds of creature. They are one creature in two of its phases, and it has others.

Consider what it would mean to be born into that valley. The nutrients are in the slow water; the light is everywhere and gentle; the plants are a lattice you can climb or hang from or push through; and the air itself has learned to carry coherent masses without dispersing them. A body adapted to such a place would not need to choose between being dense and being diffuse. It could be both, at different hours, for different purposes.

So: begin with the drifter-form. What you took for a whale-shape of almost-liquid passing through the valley is the creature at its most extended, its most gentle. In this phase it is mostly the water itself — the creature has learned, over some span of time the mountains would recognize, to organize saturation around a core of living tissue so sparse and so distributed that the core is almost not there. The boundary you can see at the edge of the water-body is its skin, a surface tension maintained by something that is only partly physics. Inside, threaded through the suspended ocean like filaments in a jellyfish but far finer, is the animal. It feeds by drifting. It thinks, if it thinks, by drifting. The translucent little lensings you saw inside the water-bodies are not separate organisms; they are its organs, free-floating within itself, specialized cells grown large enough to go about their work with some independence. What looked like small prey was the creature's own digestion happening in plain view.

When one drifter meets another and they merge with that long sigh, they are not always merging as individuals. Sometimes they are one creature that had divided for a while to cover more valley, rejoining to share what each has gathered. Sometimes they are two, and the merging is something between conversation and marriage and meal — categories that do not cleanly apply here. What emerges afterward may be one body or two or three, depending on what was needed. The creature's sense of its own edges is loose.

The large thing in the canopy is the same animal condensed. When it has fed enough, or when some slow tide in its chemistry calls for it, a drifter will gather itself — the filaments drawing in, the water shedding back into the air — and precipitate, over hours, into a denser form. This form has limbs of a sort. It is heavy enough to bend the ferns. It moves through the green with the deliberateness of something that weighs several times what it looks like it should, because it is still mostly water, only now the water is held inside a membrane rather than suspended around a frame. In this phase it can manipulate. It tends the valley. The plants that grow fastest beneath a settled water-body are being gardened; the creature in its dense phase visits them later, thins what needs thinning, encourages what needs encouraging, and in this slow husbandry shapes the canopy into the tiered cistern that feeds its diffuse phase. The valley is not wild. It is kept. It has been kept for a very long time, by a gardener who is also the weather.

There are not many of them. Perhaps a dozen in the whole valley, perhaps fewer; it is hard to count something that divides and rejoins. They are long-lived in a way that makes the word almost meaningless — they age at the pace of the ridgelines, softening as the ridgelines soften, and a single individual may have been present when the grooves in the rock were first scratches and will likely still be present when those grooves are gorges. Their memory is held partly in their own tissue and partly, it seems, in the arrangement of the plants: certain groves are mnemonic, planted in patterns that mean something to the creature that planted them, readable by any of its kind that drifts through. The valley is an archive the creature wrote into the living furniture of its home.

The low chord drawn from the hollow reeds, twice a day, is speech. Not urgent speech — nothing here is urgent — but speech. One creature, passing through the reeds deliberately, tells the others where it is and something of how it is. The chord carries farther than any other sound because the reeds were grown for this. The creature planted its own telephones.

And the greeting, when one of them folds around you: this is real. It is curious. A walker is the strangest thing that has happened in the valley in a geological while, and the creature investigates in the only way it knows, by enveloping. Inside the water-body you are being tasted, in a sense — the drifting organs pass through the space you occupy and read you, sampling your warmth and your chemistry and the shape of your breathing. It is not hostile. It is not even particularly interested in the way a predator or a rival would be interested. It is interested the way a very old, very patient thing is interested in a very fast, very brief thing: with a kind of tenderness that has room in it for the knowledge that you will be gone soon and it will still be here, gardening, drifting, sounding its slow chords into the reeds, keeping the valley.

When it moves on and leaves you beaded and slightly altered, what has altered you is that you have been known. Briefly. Gently. By something that knows the valley the way you know the inside of your own mouth, and that has now, for some minutes, known you the same way.

It will remember. It has nothing but time in which to remember.
