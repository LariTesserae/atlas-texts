## What Lives in the Roost

### The Wing-Folk

Call them *kith-of-the-wall*, though they have no name for themselves and no need of one. Ten thousand bodies, give or take a few hundred per season, and the colony reckons itself as a single weight pressed against stone. Each is the size of a closed fist, leather-skinned, eyeless because eyes were given up generations ago in favor of something better suited to a chamber that breathes cloud. They navigate by the shape of echoes against damp rock, and by the pressure of one another's bodies, and by a sense — call it a sense, for lack of the right word — of where the *whole* of the colony is leaning at any given moment.

They are not intelligent in the way the cairn-maker was intelligent. But they are not stupid either, and to call them animals is to miss what the colony has become through being packed this tight for this long. Decisions move through them like weather. When a section startles, it is not that one bat has been frightened and the others have followed; it is that a frightening *fact* has crossed the colony's distributed attention, and the relevant region has chosen, all at once, to discharge it as a percussion. The startle is information being metabolized.

They feed at night, slipping up through the oculus in slow pulses, taking the high-altitude midges and the moths that ride the wind off the snowfield two valleys over. They return before dawn with their throats full and disgorge into the moss-cushions in a slow rain that the beetles harvest and the salamanders drink at second hand. The ammoniac smell is theirs. The white runnels down the lower rock are theirs. Everything in the chamber that grows, grows because they bring it back from the dark above.

They do not have young the way most things have young. Twice a generation — perhaps every forty years — a portion of the colony detaches at dusk and does not return, and some weeks later the wall is denser by a few hundred new bodies, all the same size as the adults, as if they had been produced elsewhere and posted in. Where this elsewhere is, no one has determined. The kith do not say.

### The Coin-Beetles

A slow river-people. Each beetle is the diameter of a small coin, dorsal shell black with a faint blue oil-sheen, ventral plates the dull cream of old ivory. They process the wing-folk's leavings, the dropped moths, the bodies of their own dead. They move in currents that follow the cracks and the seeps, and from above, when the cloud thins and you can see the floor, the floor appears to flow.

They are more individual than the wing-folk and less. A single beetle, plucked from the river, will walk in tight circles and starve in a day. Returned to the river, it resumes its place as if it had never been gone. They are organized by chemistry, by trail, by the long looping decisions of a colony that has been laying its scent down on this same rock for longer than the cairn has stood.

They will not cross into the cairn-perimeter. None of them. Watch a current for an hour and you will see it bend around the invisible circle as a stream bends around a stone.

### The Pale Salamanders

Eleven of them, last count, though there may be more in the deeper seeps where no light reaches. White-bodied, gills feathery and red as fresh muscle, eyes covered with skin. They live in the runnels and pools where the wall-water collects, and they hunt the beetle-larvae and whatever falls in from above. They are the oldest individual lives in the chamber. The largest of them — a thumb-thick, hand-long matriarch in the seep nearest the cairn — has been in that seep since before the cairn was built. The salamanders do not know this and would not care. They register the cairn-perimeter only as a place where their water tastes different, and they do not enter it.

### The Drifting Things

Pale moths, yes, and midge-columns. But also: the larger occupant, the one that displaces fog. I will not pretend to know what it is. It is rarely on the floor. It seems to ride the slow vertical currents between the oculus and the upper dark, and the wing-folk make a particular soft sound when it passes near them — not a startle, not a song, an acknowledgment, perhaps. It has weight enough to move cloud. It has presence enough that the listening-stalks, very occasionally, turn toward it for no articulated reason. Whether one of it lives here or several, whether it is a tenant of the cleft or only a frequent guest, I cannot say. If the chamber has a mind, this thing may be its attention, walking the room.

### The Listening-Stalks

These are the most difficult inhabitants to write about, because they are inhabitants in a different mode than the others. Forty of them, more or less, one per crack, segmented and pale and tipped with a bud that has never opened in observed history.

They are not plants in the way that ferns are plants. They have no chlorophyll worth the name and they do not need light. They draw what they need from the condensation and, I suspect, from the constant low chitter of the wing-folk — they grow faster, very slightly, in the densest-roosted cracks. But they do not orient on the chitter. They orient on speech.

Here is what I think, and I want to be careful, because this is the kind of thinking that the chamber rewards too easily: I think they are not exactly *forty individuals.* I think they are forty antennae of a single attention, rooted in the rock, and what the rock holds in common with the stalks is something I do not have a word for. The mountain is young. It is still cracking with the sound of bells. The stalks may be the way the mountain has, very recently in geological terms, begun to *notice* that there are things in the world that mean. That meaning would matter to a mountain — that is the surprise. That a mountain would grow a listening organ — that is the strangeness. But here it is. Forty fine-tuned buds, in a chamber acoustically perfect for catching a single human voice, all of them turning slowly when someone says a word.

They have not opened. They have been listening for a long time, and what they are listening *for* has not yet been said.

### The Cairn-Maker

Not here. Has not been here, by the dust-line on the obsidian scraper, in perhaps a hundred years. Perhaps two hundred. Perhaps less; the chamber preserves things strangely.

But the cairn-maker is still, in a sense, an inhabitant of the Roost, because the cairn-maker's word is still being spoken. The stacked stones are a sentence in a grammar the stalks understand. The scraper on top is the final mark, the punctuation, the assertion: *I was here, I made this, I knew you were listening.* The perimeter around the cairn is not maintained by the cairn-maker — the cairn-maker is gone — but the wing-folk avoid it and the beetles bend around it and the moss does not grow there, and so the word continues to be legible. Something in the chamber is keeping the page clean.

My guess is the stalks. My guess is that an articulated meaning, set down deliberately in a chamber that listens, acquires a kind of pressure that the other inhabitants feel as a wrongness, a place not to be. The cairn-maker did not need to defend the cairn. The cairn-maker simply needed to mean it hard enough, once, in this particular room.

### What Lives Here, Summed

A colony that thinks as weather. A river that flows as beetles. Eleven old salamanders in cold water. Something that walks the cloud. Forty antennae of a mountain that is just beginning to wonder what is being said to it. And a sentence, left on a small stack of stones, still being read by the room.

No one is here. The Roost is full.
