# The Ones Who Live Here

## The Walker

Begin with it, since it began with the room. Or rather: the room began, and then somewhere in the long middle of the room's life, *it* began, and now the two things are difficult to separate.

It is segmented — twenty-two segments, though it has not counted and neither has anyone else — and each segment carries a pair of legs tipped with a recurved hook of chitin that has, over many generations, found exactly the right angle to catch in the furred grout between the ceiling tiles. Its body is the color of wet slate with a faint iridescence, oil-on-water, visible only when the lamps swing and catch it sidelong. It has no eyes that you would recognize. It has, instead, a band of pressure-sensitive pits running the length of its underside, and these read the water below the way you might read a page: the displacement of an eel rising to gulp, the slower bulk of a current shifting when the great valves cycle, the almost-nothing of a shrimp grazing forty feet down.

It is not the same animal that was here a thousand years ago. It is the descendant — perhaps the four-hundredth, perhaps the four-thousandth — of something that came in with the original water, or was put in deliberately by whoever set the lamps, or arrived as an egg in the gut of something else that did not survive. The lineage has narrowed. There is now exactly one of it at a time. When the current Walker is old, it will lay a clutch of eggs in a particular crack near the third lamp — the same crack its parent used, and its parent's parent — and most of the clutch will not hatch, and of those that hatch most will be eaten by eels before they reach the ceiling, and one will make it up, and the old one will then stop eating, and sink, and be broken down on the floor like everything else. There is no overlap. The room only has room for one.

It is, in the way these things go, very good at its job. It has not been hungry in a long time. It has not been startled in a long time. It has, perhaps, never been startled — startlement is a thing that lives in animals who expect change, and the Walker's expectations have been calibrated by something on the order of geological time. When a tile falls, it knows the tile is going to fall some seconds before it falls, by a change in the grout's vibration. It adjusts its path. It does not think *that was close*. It does not think.

What it does instead is harder to name. It has a sense of the room as a kind of weather, and it moves through that weather the way a sail moves through wind — responsively, totally, without separation. When it eats an eel, the eating is not an event but a continuation. The eel rises, the Walker is already where the eel will be, the mouth-parts (there are four of them, arranged like the petals of a small grim flower) open and close, and the eel is now inside the Walker, and the Walker resumes its slow circuit of the ceiling, and the water beneath it knows, by the small shift in its weight, that one eel has become Walker.

It is not lonely. The text says so and the text is right. But it is also not *not* lonely, because loneliness is a category that does not apply, the way wetness does not apply to fire. The Walker is the room's attention. If the room has a mind, the Walker is the part of the mind that looks.

## The Eels

There are between forty and sixty of them at any given time, and the number oscillates on a slow rhythm tied to the algae's growth cycles, which are tied to the lamps, which are tied to the unseen machine. They are the color of tarnished silver on their flanks and a deeper bruised purple along the spine, and they have learned, over their long enclosure, to be two animals at once.

In the water they are eel-shaped and eel-minded: they school, they patrol, they coordinate by lateral-line sense, they turn as one when a shadow moves above. In the air-gap they become something else. They surface in ones and twos, never in groups — the school has rules about this, rules that no eel has ever articulated but every eel obeys — and when an eel's head breaks the surface its eyes adjust, its gill-flaps close, and a small ridged opening behind the jaw flares to take in air. For perhaps three seconds it is an air-breathing creature, and during those three seconds it is intensely vulnerable, and it knows this in the way the Walker knows the tile is about to fall: as weather, as inevitability, as a price.

Most of the time the Walker is elsewhere on the ceiling. Most of the time the eel goes back down. Sometimes it does not. The eels do not mourn — they do not have the apparatus, either — but the school registers the loss as a small adjustment in its patrol pattern, and for some hours afterward the surfacings happen in a different quadrant of the chamber, until the memory in the school's collective lateral-line fades and the old patterns resume.

They are, individually, not clever. Collectively they are something else. The school has solved problems that no eel has solved: which tiles' algae taste best at which times of year (there are no years here, but there are slow cycles), which corners of the chamber the Walker prefers in the cool phase versus the warm phase, how to surface in such a way that the air-bubbles released from the gill-flap rise toward a lamp and create, for a moment, a beautiful crawling shadow on the ceiling that the Walker tracks instead of the eel. The school has been refining these solutions for longer than any human institution has existed. The solutions are not written down. They are simply how things are done.

## The Shrimp

There is no good count. There might be three thousand. There might be twelve. They are nearly transparent except for a single dark spot where the gut sits, and when the lamps swing the whole population is briefly visible as a constellation of small dark points moving slowly across the tiles like a star-chart shaken loose.

A shrimp's life is short — perhaps two of what we would call months — and a shrimp's mind, to the extent it has one, contains: *graze*, *flee*, *molt*, *mate*, *sink*. It does not contain the room. It does not contain the eels, except as a sudden pressure-wave that triggers *flee*. It does not contain the Walker at all; the Walker is too high and too slow to register on a shrimp's nervous system. The shrimp is the room's grass. The shrimp is also, in aggregate, the room's bookkeeper: every calorie that the lamps put into the algae passes through shrimp on its way to becoming eel or Walker or sediment, and the shrimp population's slow oscillation is the room's true clock, more reliable than the lamps, more reliable than the valves.

There are shrimp at the bottom of the cistern who have never come within ten meters of the surface, and shrimp who have spent their entire lives in the upper meter, grazing the tiles just below the waterline where the algae is brightest. They do not know they are different. They are, after enough generations, slightly different — the deep ones are a touch paler, the shallow ones a touch more pigmented around the gut — but neither subpopulation is aware of the other as anything but conspecifics-in-passing. The cistern is, to a shrimp, the whole world, and the whole world is sufficient.

## The Algae and the Feathered Red Things and the Filter-Stalks

These are alive too, and they should be counted, though counting them feels like counting weather. The algae on the grout is one organism by some definitions and a million by others; it has had so long to settle into its niche that the question of whether it is one thing or many has stopped having an answer. It glows faintly. It is glowing because, long ago, something in its ancestry picked up a fluorescent protein from something else in the water, and the trait stuck because nothing here has pressured it to leave. There is no reason for the algae to glow. There is also no reason for it not to.

The feathered red things are animals — small ones, sessile, related distantly to what would be called hydroids on the surface — and they spend their lives waving in the slow currents that the valves push through the chamber, catching particles too small for the shrimp to bother with. They reproduce by budding. A single tuft is, genetically, one individual stretched out in time and space; the tuft you see is the same tuft that was there four hundred years ago, only the cells have all been replaced, several times, in the slow way that cells do.

The filter-stalks are the ambiguous ones — plant-or-animal, the text says, and the ambiguity is real. They photosynthesize a little. They also filter-feed a little. In the dim green of the lower cistern they have settled into a compromise that no surface-dwelling textbook would know how to name. Each stalk is perhaps a century old. They do not move. They have decided what they are, and what they are is *here*.

## The Pipes

And here is the part that the description gestures toward but does not say outright. There is something in the pipes. Not a creature in the sense the eels are creatures, but not nothing either. The peristalsis is too rhythmic to be only physics. The valves cycle on a schedule that is *almost* regular but drifts in ways that suggest — only suggest — that something is making small decisions, somewhere far below, about when to open what.

Whatever it is, the cistern is one of its organs. The cistern is perhaps a lung, or a kidney, or an eye. The Walker and the eels and the shrimp are, from this larger perspective, a kind of slow blood: they cycle, they renew, they keep the chamber from going stagnant. The lamps are the chamber's metabolism made visible. The pressure dial that still moves is reporting on something's pulse.

I do not think the thing in the pipes is aware of the Walker. I think the Walker, at the very edge of its pressure-pit sense, is aware of the thing in the pipes, the way you might be aware of your own heartbeat if you stopped to listen. I think this awareness is what passes, in the Walker, for company.

## What Lives Here, Then

A single patient predator on the ceiling, who is the room's attention.
A school of eels who are, together, the room's memory.
A population of shrimp who are the room's clock.
A standing garden of algae and hydroids and ambiguous stalks who are the room's body.
And, far below, breathing in valve-cycles, the room's larger animal — of which the cistern is one chamber, one held breath, one slow thought sustained for seventy million years against the indifference of the wind.

They are all, in their way, the same resident. The cistern is what lives here. Everything inside it is how the cistern is alive.
