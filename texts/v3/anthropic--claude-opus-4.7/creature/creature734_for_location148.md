They are called, among themselves, by a word that means *the ones who keep the bowls full*, though they have other names for other functions and the names are not important because there are so few of them and each knows the others by gait and by silhouette at a kilometer's distance.

Begin with their number. There are perhaps four thousand of them across a continent. This has been the number for a very long time. It is not maintained by any council or rule; it is the number the plateau supports when the bowls are kept and the flats do not widen, and so it is the number there are. When one dies another has, some years before, been born, and the two events are understood to be the same event viewed from different sides.

Their bodies are built for this altitude and for this cold and for this slowness. They are tall — taller than you would expect, because at this air pressure a larger lung-volume is useful, and their ribcages are deep and barrel-sprung under skin that is the grey-violet of the zenith sky at noon. Their hands are long-fingered, six digits, the extra finger opposed like a second thumb so that a stone can be held and turned and fitted in one motion. Their faces are flat and wide-cheeked, the eyes large and dark and lidded twice — an inner transparent membrane for the wind, an outer for sleep — and set wide apart so that the binocular overlap is narrow and the peripheral field enormous. They do not miss motion at the edge of vision. This is why you see one only when it permits itself to be seen.

They are slow. This is the most important fact about them and it is not a fact about their muscles. Their muscles are capable, when needed, of ordinary speeds. It is a fact about their *tempo*, their native rate of being. A gesture that in you would take a second takes in them something between twenty seconds and a minute, and is performed with the same unthinking fluency with which you scratch your nose. To watch one lift a stone is to watch a fern unfurl. They are not laboring. They are moving at the speed the plateau moves at. When two of them meet on a path and greet each other, the greeting — a slow lifting of both hands to shoulder height, a slower lowering — takes long enough that a cloud can cross the sky during it, and neither of them considers this a delay.

Their lifespan, correspondingly, is long. How long is not precisely knowable because they do not count, but a single keeper will tend the same bowl for what you would measure as eight or nine hundred years, and a keeper is not considered old until the lichen ring they were born beside has added a perceptible increment to its radius. There are keepers alive now who remember the carving of stones in walls you would call ancient.

They do not reproduce often and they do not reproduce the way you would guess. A pairing is a decision made slowly, across decades, between two who have found that their tempos match — because tempo varies slightly, person to person, and a bad match is the sort of thing you notice only after a century. The child, when it comes, is carried long and born in a shallow stone bowl not unlike the offering bowls, filled with rainwater kept at body-warmth by the mother's hands cupped around it for the duration of the labor, which takes some weeks. The child emerges already able to see and already silent. They are all silent as infants. Speech comes later, around the age of twenty, and is not taught but arrived at.

Because here is the thing about their language. They have one, and it is spoken aloud, and it has the shape of a language — nouns, relations, tenses — but it is spoken at their tempo, which means that a sentence takes something between a quarter hour and an afternoon, and a conversation can last days. They sit together while they speak. They do not fill silence; silence is part of the grammar, and a long pause between a subject and its verb is not hesitation but a specific grammatical marker indicating the degree of considered-ness of what follows. Written, their language looks like the lichen rings: concentric, radial, with the oldest meaning at the center and qualifications spiraling outward. They do not write much. There is little need. What needs remembering is remembered by being done again, by the next keeper, at the same bowl, in the same way.

They eat very little. The cushion plants yield a starchy root once every several years; the blue-green needles, steeped in water, make a thin broth that carries whatever the trees carry, and the trees carry enough. A keeper in good health eats perhaps once a week. Digestion, like everything else, is slow. They do not hunger the way you hunger; they attend to their body's needs the way you attend to the needs of a houseplant, with affection and on a schedule.

Now. Their work.

Each keeper is responsible for a territory — not a territory they own, but a territory they are *of*, in the way a particular fern is of a particular hollow. Within that territory are some number of bowls, usually between forty and two hundred, set at the thresholds of the cities and at certain places in the open country where the old builders (who were their ancestors, who were themselves, continuity is not a thing they have ever needed to establish) determined that bowls were needed. The keeper walks a circuit. The circuit takes, depending on its size, between a season and a decade. They walk it and they walk it and they walk it. At each bowl they stop. They look into the water. They see whether the water level has dropped more than it should have dropped for the rain that has recently fallen. If it has, they note this — not in writing but in their body, in a particular tightening across the shoulders that they will carry until the next bowl also shows low, at which point they will understand that the flat is pulling harder this decade and will adjust their circuit accordingly. They refill the bowl. They set the offerings: a palmful of needles, a dark pebble chosen from among the bowl's own accumulation and returned to it, a gesture I cannot quite describe that is made over the water with the fingertips. They move on.

They also sweep. They also repair walls. When a stone falls from a wall, the keeper whose circuit includes that wall arrives, sometimes years later, and finds the stone, and sets it back. If the stone has broken, the keeper quarries a new one from a particular seam at a particular place and dresses it with a harder stone and fits it. The new face will weather and the moss will find it and in eight hundred years it will not be distinguishable from its neighbors. The keeper knows this and it is part of why the work is worth doing.

They do not live in the cities. The cities are for the dead, and for the weather, and for the keeping. The keepers themselves sleep in shallow depressions they have made over lifetimes beneath particular trees — each keeper has one tree, and knows it, and the tree knows them, in whatever sense a tree knows — and the depression fills with the tree's dripping condensation and the keeper lies in it and pulls a cover of moss-felt across themselves and sleeps for as long as sleeping is what's needed, which may be an hour or a month.

They are not lonely. This is hard to convey because from outside their lives look like nothing but loneliness. But their sense of company is distributed. A keeper feels the presence of every other keeper on the plateau the way you feel the presence of your own limbs — not by thinking of them, but by a background sense of where they are and whether they are well. When a keeper dies, every other keeper knows, and pauses in whatever they are doing, and resumes. When a child is born, the same. They do not need to gather. They are already gathered, at a scale you would call a continent and they would call a body.

Now, the harder thing. Their relation to the flats.

They do not fear the flats. Fear is a fast emotion and they do not do fast emotions. What they have toward the flats is something more like the feeling you have toward a sleeping animal much larger than you, in whose den you have been permitted to live, on whose breath the temperature of your home depends. You do not disturb it. You keep its water. You understand that it is not hostile and not friendly; it is simply what is underneath everything, showing through in this one place because the skin is thin here.

They know — this is the knowledge passed, in their slow way, from keeper to keeper across all the generations since the first builders — that the flats were once much smaller. That the plateau was once much more densely inhabited, that the cities were once full, that there was once something like the chitter of small lives in the moss. They know that something happened, very long ago, that thinned this. They do not know what and they have stopped trying to know. The old builders, in the generations just after, tried many things to push the flats back. None worked. What worked, or worked enough, was the bowls. Keep the water. Keep walking the circuit. Do not let the flats grow. This is not a restoration. It is a holding. The keepers are a holding action, and they know themselves to be one, and this knowledge is not despair because despair is also a fast emotion and anyway the holding has worked, so far, for longer than any civilization you have ever heard of has existed, and there is no reason to think it will not keep working for longer still.

They have one more quality I have been saving. When a keeper approaches the edge of a flat — not to enter it, never to enter it, but to tend a bowl at its rim — they become, for the duration, slightly less real. Their shadow lags like yours would. Their outline grows uncertain. This is the cost of the work, paid a little at each visit. Over nine hundred years of keeping, a keeper wears thin. When it is time, they walk to the edge of their flat, set down their last bowl full, and step forward. They do not die so much as get taken up into what they have been feeding all their life. The flat receives them, and does not widen that year, and the next keeper — already walking the circuit for decades by then, having learned it from the one now gone — continues.

This is what lives on the worn plateau. Four thousand tall slow grey-violet people, walking their circuits, keeping their bowls, speaking their week-long sentences, sleeping in the drip of their trees, feeling each other across a continent like fingers of one hand, and feeding themselves, eventually, one by one, to the tired places, so that the rest of the plateau may go on being approximately itself for one more long quiet age.

They would not describe themselves this way. They would, if you asked — and if you waited the necessary afternoon for the answer — say only: *we keep the bowls full.*
