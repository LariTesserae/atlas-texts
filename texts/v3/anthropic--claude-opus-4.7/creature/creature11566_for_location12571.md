# Those Who Attend

They were not always here. This matters, and they know it matters, though they would not put it the way I am about to put it.

They came up the slopes perhaps eight or nine generations ago — the count is uncertain because they do not count generations the way lowland peoples do; they count *attendances*, which is something else. They came from somewhere lower and warmer, a river-valley people who farmed a grain that no longer grows for them. The story they tell, when they tell it, is that one of their grandmothers many-times-back dreamed of a place where the rock was thinking, and walked uphill until she found it. The story they don't tell, but that you can feel in the shape of their tools and the cut of their wool, is that something further down went wrong — a sickness, a war, a king — and a small group walked away from it and kept walking until the air got thin enough to forget in.

They are small people. Not dwarfish, just small — the tallest man in the Hatchcity would come up to the shoulder of a lowland soldier. Their hands are disproportionately long and fine, and their fingers have an extra knuckle's worth of flex, or seem to; it may just be the whittling, which begins in the second year of life and continues until the hands stop. Their eyes are large and dark and adjusted to the gloaming — in bright sun they squint and weep, and so they do not go far down the slopes, and the slopes do not come up to them. Their skin is pale under the grey wool and takes a long time to tan or burn; it has the slightly translucent quality of people who live mostly indoors and mostly in dim light, though they are outside constantly. It is the light itself that is dim.

Their hair is uniformly dark and uniformly fine and they wear it long and braided close to the skull, often with a moth-down tuft worked into the end of the braid — not for decoration exactly, more the way a lowlander might keep a wedding ring on. The down is shed naturally by the moths in late summer and gathered from the walkways with small bone combs.

They are quieter than you would believe a human being could be. This is not training, or not only training — it is what happens to a body raised in a place where loud sound is a kind of violence done to the air. Their footfalls on the walkways are barely audible even to each other. They have a way of breathing through a half-open mouth that produces no whistle. When they laugh, which they do, the laugh is in the eyes and shoulders and the soft *huh* of breath; the throat is not involved. Children learn this before they learn to walk. A baby that cries — and babies do cry — is wrapped immediately in a sound-dampening shawl of layered felt and carried out past the perimeter to a small stone hut downslope, where it can cry itself empty without lifting the canopy. There is no shame in this. The mothers walk back up smiling, holding the quieted child against their chest.

The tokens they carry — the small carved things they exchange when they meet — are the most interesting thing about them, and the hardest to explain. Each person carries between four and perhaps thirty, depending on age and station, in a soft leather pouch at the hip. They are carved from flutterwood heartwood, each one no larger than a thumb-joint, and each one represents — well. Not a name. A *facet*. A way the carrier is, today, or has been, or expects to be by evening. There is a token for *I am tired and would prefer not to be spoken to*, and a token for *I am tired but the company of another tired person would be welcome*, and these are distinct tokens carved by distinct hands and they are not interchangeable. There is a token for *I am thinking about my mother*, and one for *I have just understood something I did not understand yesterday*, and one for *the moths were unusually warm this morning, did you notice*. When two people meet on a walkway they pause, each draws a token, they look at each other's tokens, and either they pass on or they sit down together on the walkway-edge and do not speak for a while. The tokens accumulate over a lifetime. When someone dies, their pouch is opened and the tokens are distributed among those who knew them, each person taking the one that best represents how they will remember the dead. The pouches of the very old are sometimes nearly empty by the time of death, the tokens already given out, the dying person having spent their last months handing pieces of themselves to specific others.

There is no writing. There is no need for writing. The tokens carry what writing would carry, and the rest is held in the carved spoons and the woven mats and the shape of the houses, which are themselves a kind of slow text being rewritten by every rebuilding.

They eat very little, and most of what they eat is the moths — not the live ones, which are sacred and not to be touched, but the ones the flutterwood has caught and dried on its sticky leaves, which they harvest with long bone hooks in the late afternoon. The dried moths taste, I'm told, like nothing exactly — like a kind of dense cool nothing, faintly sweet, faintly mineral. They also eat a tuber that grows in the moss and a small bitter berry from a shrub that only fruits in years when the canopy is thick. They drink rainwater caught in the soapstone cisterns and a tea brewed from the inner bark of the flutterwood, which is mildly euphoric and which they drink only on certain evenings — evenings when, I think, the listening is going to be harder than usual.

Because here is what they are, finally. What they really are.

They are *listeners*. The entire architecture of the town, the silence, the soft bells, the felt-wrapped mallets, the absence of metal — all of it is in service of a practice they do not name and probably could not name, which is the practice of being present to the mountain making itself. They believe — though *belief* is the wrong word, it is more like *they have arranged their lives in accord with* — they have arranged their lives in accord with the proposition that the moths are dreaming the rock, and that human beings, by being quiet enough and attentive enough, can be present at the dreaming. Not participate in it. Not direct it. Just *witness* it, which they take to be a thing the mountain needs in order to keep happening.

This is why they came up here. The grandmother in the founding story dreamed not of safety or wealth but of a place that needed witnesses, and walked toward it. They consider themselves, very quietly, to be doing a job. The job is to sit on the walkways at certain hours and let the moths land on them and feel the rock rising and not interrupt it. The carving and the cooking and the rebuilding of walkways are what they do around the job, the way a lighthouse keeper trims the wick and sweeps the stairs and is mostly engaged in the lighthouse.

What is unclear, and what they themselves are not sure about, is whether the mountain knows they are there. Whether the witnessing is received. There is a small minority view among the older people that the mountain does not know and does not need to know, that the witnessing is sufficient in itself — that a thing being seen is changed by being seen even if the thing has no concept of being seen. There is a larger view that the mountain *does* know, in the slow way rock knows things, and that the long hum under audibility in the deep middle of the night is the mountain acknowledging them, the way a sleeping animal will shift its weight when you sit down beside it.

They will live perhaps sixty years each, in the thin air, and they will die quietly in their low houses with their pouches mostly empty, and they will be carried out past the perimeter and laid in a hollow in the soapstone where the moths come down in great numbers and cover the body for three days, and on the fourth day there will be only clean bone, which is taken back into the town and carved into more tokens, and the tokens will be given out, and the person will become a vocabulary in other people's pouches, and the mountain will rise another finger's width, and someone will be whittling.

They do not think of themselves as remarkable. They think of themselves as people who happen to live where the work needed doing. If you asked one of them — softly, on a walkway, having shown the right token — whether they are happy, they would not understand the question in the form you asked it, but they would understand what you meant, and they would think for a long time, and then they would give you a token from their pouch and walk on. And later, when you looked at the token, you would find it was a small carved figure of a person sitting very still while something enormous and slow happened around them, and you would understand that this was the answer, and that it was yes.
