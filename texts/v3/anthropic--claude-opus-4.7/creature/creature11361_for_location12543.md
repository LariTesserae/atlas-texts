# Who Lives There

## The People

They are short. Not by ancestry — by accumulation. Four generations of low doorways, of stooping under plank-bridges, of sleeping in lofts where the roof slopes to meet the floor, has selected gently for a certain compactness. The tall ones go elsewhere or learn to fold. Their hands are wide and strong at the thumb from splitting basalt and wedging mortar; their feet are broad and tough, because even inside the resin-leather boots the wet finds a way, and a foot that cannot stand being damp all winter is a foot that fails here.

Their skin tends pale — they get little direct sun, the basin being what it is — but with a particular quality the lamp-reeds bring out: in the green underwater light of the lanes after dark, faces seem to float, lit from below, the way a face looks when held over a candle. They are used to seeing each other this way. A child raised here will, much later, find ordinary daylight on a face slightly indecent, too much shown at once.

Their eyes adjust differently than yours or mine. Pupils stay wide in the perpetual grey. They read the small differences in wetness on stone — this seep is older, that one is new, this puddle has been still long enough for the shrimp to have come back. They notice the lamp-reeds before they notice each other across a lane.

Their voices have the quality of people who have lived their whole lives within earshot of family. Even an argument is conducted in something close to a murmur. A raised voice is a kind of nakedness; afterwards people apologize for days. The children, who have not yet learned this, are loved partly for the relief of their noise.

They name a great many things. Each seep has a name. The seven pools have names, and the named pools have named edges. The lamp-reed clumps have names — usually the name of whoever first cultivated them, passed down. The basalt outcrops inside town have names. A person carries in their head a dense map of named small features, and to be from here is to know the map, which cannot really be taught, only absorbed. This is part of why they don't leave. A person who knows three hundred names for things within a hundred paces does not travel well; the world outside is, by comparison, terrifyingly anonymous.

## What They Do

There is the smith, who works the small precious iron and is therefore important but not powerful — the iron is communal, and the smith is its caretaker, not its owner. There is the keeper of the grey waterfowl, who lives somewhat apart, at the basin's edge where the soil is just deep enough for the birds to nest. There is the clay-digger, an inherited position, who works the single grey seam with great care because if it is dug wrong it collapses and the town has no mortar for a season. There is the dyer, who tends the liverwort vats — a slow, smelly, beautiful trade that turns wool a deep grey-purple unique to this place, a color that holds the basin's light in it.

But mostly: everyone does a little of everything. They split stone. They patch roofs. They tend lamp-reeds — a slow horticulture that involves redirecting seeps, building tiny stone weirs to deepen a wet spot, transplanting clumps from where they thrive to where light is needed. The lamp-reed work is considered the most honorable, though no one would say so directly. It is what a person does when they have time and want their time to count.

They brew. The brewing-rooms are everywhere because the water is everywhere and beautiful — basalt-filtered, very soft. They make a thin pale beer from a grain traded up from the lowlands once a year by the one or two people from town who will make that walk. They make a stronger drink from the lamp-reed's late-summer seeds, which is faintly luminescent in the cup for the first few minutes after pouring. They drink this only on certain nights.

They make pots. Everyone makes pots. A child learns to make pots before they learn to read the named map. The town's wealth is unbroken pots, and the town's history is broken ones — the midden at the basin's south edge is layered in shards going back four generations, and a person can date a fragment by its glaze, by the slip, by the slight changes in the clay seam as the digger went deeper.

## What They Believe

They do not have a religion in any form you would recognize. They have a relationship with the water, which is constant and which everyone participates in, and which is not personified but is also not nothing. When a new spring opens in a floor, the family addresses it before deciding what to do. Not with prayer — with acknowledgment. *You are here now. We see.* Then they move the hearth.

They believe — and this is harder to name — that attention is a kind of payment, and that the basin runs on it. The lamp-reeds are tended because tending them is the rent. The newts are noticed (each family has, more or less, its wall-newts, and knows them) because noticing them is the rent. The unnamed creature that comes down from the ridge twice a year is left food because being left food is what the creature is owed, in exchange for coming, in exchange for being the thing that comes. None of this is doctrine. It is simply how a person from here lives. A person who stopped tending, stopped noticing, stopped leaving the food, would not be punished. They would just, slowly, stop being from here.

They believe the basin is alive in some sense that does not require the basin to have a mind. They believe the rising of the ribs and the rising of the water are a conversation the basin is having with itself, and that the town is in the conversation but is not the subject of it. This is a comforting belief and also a hard one. It means the town might be drowned and the basin would not have meant to drown it. It means survival is not personal.

## The Singer

Someone is singing very quietly to a child, the description says, and this is true at almost any hour. Lullabies here are long. They have to be — a child put to sleep in a house pressed against three other houses must be sung *under* the ambient drip, *under* the murmur of the next room, into a small private quiet that the song itself constructs. The lullabies of this town are famous, in the sense that they would be famous if anyone outside knew them. They are very old. The melodies have the shape of water moving over stone: not repetitive exactly, but returning, returning, never quite the same return.

The singer right now is a grandmother in a loft above a brewing-room, and the child is her daughter's daughter's child, the fourth generation she has sung to sleep, and the song she is singing names — quietly, in order — the seven pools, and the named edges of the named pools, and the lamp-reed clumps by their first cultivators, and the seeps. The child does not yet know this is what the song is doing. The child will know later. The child will sing it, eventually, to someone smaller, and the map will pass on, slightly altered — a new seep added, perhaps, where the floor of the bell-house opened last spring — and the town will continue, for now, to know itself.

Outside, the basalt weeps. The lamp-reeds glow. The water in the lowest lane is a finger's-width deeper than it was last year. Somewhere under everything, the ground is still rising. No one knows which will win. Meanwhile the grandmother reaches the end of the named places, and begins again at the beginning, because the child is not yet asleep, and the song, like the seepage, moves at the pace of an idea.
