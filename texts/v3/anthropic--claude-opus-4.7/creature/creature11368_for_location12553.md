## What Lives There

Start with the obvious tenants and work inward, because the obvious ones are misleading.

The newts and the crayfish and the bark-colored frogs and the ribbon-fish: these are real animals, but they are not quite what you think they are. They are not the inhabitants of the chamber. They are more like the inhabitants of the engine's *exhalation* — they live in the weather the engine makes, the way barnacles live in the weather a whale makes. If the engine stopped, they would die within a season, not because they need the heat (though they do) but because they no longer know how to be alive in silence. They have tuned themselves. The frog that calls into the gap between beats is not being clever; it has no other rhythm available to it anymore. Its larvae develop in pulses keyed to the pressure-release valve. Its heart, if you could measure it, beats in a ratio with the chugging — three to one, or five to two, something a musician would recognize.

These are the *small* tenants. They are real, and they matter, and they are not the answer.

The bat is closer. The bat is alone — note that. There is no colony, no second bat, no guano-pile beneath the ledge to suggest generations. One bat, hanging where you can't quite see it. I would not be too sure the bat is a bat. I would not be too sure it is sleeping. It has the wrong stillness. A bat at rest twitches, grooms, shifts its grip. This one does not. It hangs the way a thermometer hangs, or a plumb-line — as an instrument hangs, while something reads it.

The moths are closer still. They move too slowly. Condensation beads their wings and they do not shake it off, which a moth would do, which a moth *must* do or it falls. They are not falling. They drift through the upper air on a schedule that has nothing to do with moth-business. If you watched them for three days without sleeping you would notice that they trace, very slowly, the same paths — long ellipses through the vertical dimness, passing certain points in the air at certain intervals. They are surveying. Or circulating. Or stirring. Something in that high vacant volume requires gentle constant motion through it, and the moths provide it, and in exchange they are kept aloft past the point where their wet wings should have grounded them.

Now: the engine.

The engine is the tenant. Everything else is its household.

But the engine is not a *machine* in the sense you want it to be. There are no rivets in that rock, no boiler, no piston-shaft your hand could find if the wall were thinner. The wall is solid and young and the sound has no source you can locate because the sound has no source in the way sources are usually had. What lives in the chamber is something that *sounds like* a hundred-year-old industrial engine because that is the shape its presence takes when it touches air and stone and the listening ear of a visitor. The chugging, the steam-hiss, the reciprocating clank — these are the metaphors the chamber has available for translating the thing into noise. In a different chamber, in a different century, it might have sounded like a loom, or a heart, or a great slow drum, or a man chopping wood somewhere just out of sight.

What it actually is: a slow life. Very slow. Slow enough that the twenty-second pause is something like an indrawn breath, and the chugging is something like the metabolism between breaths. It is large — larger than the chamber, which is why the chamber feels too small for the sound, why the ceiling is rumor, why pressing your ear to the wall makes the sound *more diffuse* rather than less. You are not approaching a source. You are inside the source. The chamber is a small cleared space within the body of the thing, the way the inside of your mouth is a small cleared space within you. The fissure that admits you is not a way *into* the mountain. It is a way into the creature that the mountain has, lately, failed to fully contain.

This is why the ecology fits so well despite being, by rock-time, impossibly recent. The ecology did not assemble around the engine. The ecology was *secreted* by it — the way mucus is secreted, the way a pearl is secreted around a grain. The newts and the ferns and the white flowers that breathe with the valve are not adaptations. They are products. The creature makes them. It has been making versions of them for a very long time, in chambers that opened and closed as the rock allowed, and when this particular fissure cracked along its unnamed seam last winter the creature exhaled a fresh population into the new cleared space and they grew in at the speed of breath fogging glass.

The bat-that-is-not-a-bat is one of its sense organs. The moths are its cilia. The crayfish, sorting pebbles with the gravity of an old jeweler, is doing work — sorting for what, into what categories, you cannot tell, but it is not foraging; it is *organizing*, and the organization matters to something. The ribbon-fish that listens when it surfaces is listening *on behalf of*. The frogs, fitting their voices into the silences, are not tenants in a furnished room; they are the room furnishing itself with sound during the pauses when the larger sound rests.

And you. The chamber treats you as a smaller slow visitor of the engine's own category, which is correct in a way you wouldn't like if you thought about it long. The newts climb your boot because they recognize the kinship. You are warm, you are wet inside, you pulse, you exhale humidity, you have an interior rhythm that the chamber's tenants can fit themselves around. You are a very small portable version of what they already live inside. They are not unafraid of you. They are *attending* to you, briefly, the way a household notices a guest, and then they will go back to attending to the larger householder whose attention is steadier and more reliably present.

When you leave, the moss-collar closes and the sound seals itself in, and from outside there is nothing because there is nothing to leak: the creature does not project. It only *is*, in there, and you were only briefly in there with it, and it noticed you about as much as you notice a fly that lands on your wrist and lifts off again before you've decided whether to brush it away.

It is still deciding. It decides slowly. By the time it has finished deciding about you, you will be old, or dead, and the fissure may have closed again, and another one may have opened somewhere else along the range, with its own small loud wet green secret folded inside, humming.
