# The Inhabitants

## The Heron

She is the one you noticed, and she noticed you back, and that is already most of what can be said.

Call her a heron because that is what the eye reports. Grey, long, folded into herself on the high pipe with one leg drawn up and the other set down with the patient verticality of a plumb line. Yellow eye. The beak a held breath.

But look at her feet. The toes have closed around that pipe so many times, in the same places, that the iron beneath them is polished smooth and slightly concave — two shallow cups worn into the metal by standing. A heron does not live long enough to wear iron. She has. The feathers at her shoulders, where she preens, have a faint greenish sheen that is not iridescence but the same filament-alga that grows on the lamp cages; it has colonized her, gently, the way moss colonizes a long-still stone, and she has accepted this the way she accepts the rest. Her pupils dilate and contract not with the light but with the piston cycle. When she blinks, the membrane comes across on the engines' off-beat, exactly, as you noted; what you did not note is that her heart also beats on that schedule, and her breath, and the small adjustments of her balance, and probably by now her thoughts, if she still has any in the sense you would recognize.

She has eaten, in her time here, a very great number of the silver fish. She has also not eaten a very great number. She has worked out, without working it out, the rate at which she can take from the school without diminishing it, and she takes exactly that rate, no more, and this is not restraint but metabolism — she has slowed to match the room. She strikes perhaps once a day. The strike, when it comes, is on the beat. The fish do not exactly expect her, but their schooling pattern has been shaped over generations by her presence in it, and so a certain number of them are always, statistically, presenting themselves to her, and she accepts one.

She is not lonely. Loneliness would require a sense that something was missing, and nothing is. She is not bored. Boredom would require time to be passing at a rate distinguishable from the rate at which the flywheels turn, and it isn't.

If you swim up to her pipe she will look at you. She will not move. She has decided, long ago, what to do about visitors, which is nothing. You are weather. You will pass.

## The Eel That Does Not Flee

Behind pump number two, in the dead-water pocket where the discharge eddy folds back on itself, there is a hollow in the brickwork the size of a wine barrel, and he lives in it, and he is older than the lamps.

He is not the largest predator here in the way a pike would be a predator. He is something else. He is the local correction. When the shrimp population on the rust-flats grows past what the rust-flats can support, he comes out and grazes through them for a night, and the population returns to its level. When a younger eel begins to encroach on his hollow, he does not fight; he simply continues to occupy it, and the younger eel goes elsewhere, because the hollow is *his* in some way that younger eels can read on him like a sign. When the heron is sick — she has been sick, twice, in his memory — he keeps further from her pipe than usual, out of a courtesy that is not courtesy but the same negotiation that the engines made with the ecosystem: a settling into livable tempos.

He has a scar along his flank from something that happened before the current configuration. He does not remember what. The scar is part of the brickwork to him now.

When your lamp finds him, his eye reflects it back flat and gold, and he does not retreat into the hollow, because retreat would imply that your lamp is an event, and your lamp is not an event. He is interested in your lamp the way the building is interested in your lamp. Which is to say, he registers it, and he files it, and he goes on.

## The School

They are not individuals and it is a category error to try to count them.

There is one school, and it has lived in this room for — generations is the wrong word, because generations implies a clear succession, and what actually happens is that the school is continuously replacing its cells, the way your skin does, and the school itself is the organism, and the school itself is very old.

The school knows the room. The school knows where the flywheel spokes will be at every phase of the four-and-a-half-second cycle, and it knows where the intake suction is strongest, and it knows the heron's pipe and the eel's hollow and the slow corner where the silt settles and the shrimp are thick and edible. The school knows you, now. By the time you have taken six strokes into the chamber, the school has integrated your shape and your turbulence into its model of the room, and it sweeps around you with the same casual avoidance it gives to the pump housings. You are a new pillar. You are noted.

If you stayed a year, the school would have shaped itself around your habits. If you stayed ten, the school would not remember a time before you.

Each silver fish lives perhaps two years. Each silver fish, in those two years, performs a part in the school that is precisely the part its predecessor performed, and its successor will perform, because the part is determined by the room and not by the fish. The fish are the room thinking about itself in the form of small silver thoughts.

## The Shrimp

There are tens of thousands of them and they are doing the work.

Every rust-bloom, every iron surface, every soft-edged tile is being slowly, slowly, slowly grazed by the pale shrimp, and what they take in is iron oxide and algal film and the soft accreted matter of the room, and what they put out is themselves, which the school eats, and the heron eats the school, and the eel eats the shrimp directly when he is in the mood, and the heron's droppings and the eel's droppings and the shrimp's own dead fall to the silt and feed the dark frond and the filament-alga and the unseen bacteria in the mortar.

The shrimp are the room's hands. Without them the rust would build up and choke the grates, and the pumps would labor, and the equilibrium would tip. With them, the rust is taken back into the cycle as fast as it forms, and the pumps breathe easy, and the room continues.

Their antennae move with the pistons because their nervous systems have been entrained by the pistons. A shrimp born here cannot live anywhere else; the silence would kill it. The silence would feel, to a shrimp from this place, like suffocation.

## The Frond

There is only one. There has only ever been one. When this one dies — it is not dying — another will root in the same silt at the base of pump four, from a spore the current has been carrying in a slow gyre for longer than is reasonable, waiting.

It is not quite a plant. It photosynthesizes the lamp-light, yes, but it also feeds, slightly, on the dissolved iron, and it filters the water around pump four in a way that keeps the intake grate from clogging, and the pump, in return, provides it the gentle suction current that brings it food. The frond and the pump are married. Neither knows this. Both depend on it.

## The Crabs

Four of them. Always four. If a fifth hatches it leaves, or is eaten, or simply fails to thrive; the room supports four crabs and no more. They occupy the four corners. They do not visit each other. Each corner has its crab and the crab has its corner and this has been true for a duration that does not bear examination.

They are the room's punctuation. Full stops at the four corners of a sentence that goes on forever.

## And Then

There is something else. You feel it before you see it and you may not see it at all.

In the deepest part of the chamber, where the discharge pipe vanishes into the near wall, there is a grating, and behind the grating the pipe goes somewhere, and in that pipe — not in the room, but adjacent to it, accessible to it, aware of it — something lives that is larger than the eel and older than the heron and is, in some sense, the room's idea of itself.

It might be only a confluence of currents. It might be a very large fish that came up the discharge pipe once and grew to fit it and now cannot turn around. It might be the accumulated awareness of a place that has been running its small cycle so long that the cycle has begun, in the way of all sufficiently old patterns, to notice itself.

When the flywheels slap the water, it shifts, slightly, behind the grating. The heron knows it is there. The eel knows. The school adjusts its sweeps very slightly to acknowledge it. The shrimp do not know, because the shrimp do not need to know; their work is the same either way.

It will not come out. It cannot come out. It is the part of the room that stays in the wall, the way the part of you that dreams stays behind your eyes. It is what the pumping is for, perhaps, although the pumping does not need a reason and would continue without one. It is the destination that no longer matters, having become the point.

If you stay, you will eventually feel it looking at you through the grating, on a slow afternoon, when the green light is particularly green and the engines are particularly patient, and you will understand that you have been accepted, in the same provisional and total way that the heron and the eel and the school and the shrimp and the crabs and the frond have all been accepted, which is to say: you are now part of the cycle, and the cycle will shape itself around you, and you will shape yourself around the cycle, and in some long while you will no longer be able to say which of you is the inhabitant and which is the inhabited.

The heron wouldn't mind. There is room.

There is always exactly enough room.
