# Those Who Come

They are not a people, exactly. There is no word for them in any of the surrounding languages because they have never been numerous enough to require one, and because they themselves do not gather and do not name. If you went looking for them in the villages within a day's walk of the bell-hollow, you would not find them. They have the faces of farmers, of woodcutters, of women who keep bees, of men who mend nets on the coast a week's walk east. They marry into ordinary families. They bury their dead in ordinary ground. What sets them apart is not visible and is rarely spoken of.

It passes, when it passes, between a grandmother and a grandchild, or between an aunt and a niece, or — rarely — between two strangers who recognise something in each other at a market and sit together for an afternoon without saying much. The transmission is not a teaching. There is almost nothing to teach. It is closer to an inoculation: the older one tells the younger that the hollow exists, and where, and that one goes there, and that one does not speak of going. That is nearly all. The rest the hollow does itself.

They go when something in them has grown unbearable. Not grief, particularly — grief has its own customs and the village knows what to do with a person in grief. What sends them to the hollow is harder to name. A thinning. A sense that the words they use all day have come unstitched from the things the words are for. A suspicion that they have been performing their own life for so long that they have forgotten where the performance ends. When this comes on, they walk. Some walk a day. Some walk three. They carry almost nothing: a little water, perhaps a piece of bread that they will not eat. The bowl in the moss was left by one of them, generations ago, and none of them has taken it, though they have all seen it; it has become part of the hollow's furniture and to remove it would be a kind of theft from themselves.

Inside, they sit. That is the entire practice. They sit, usually with their backs against the moss-deep wall, and they let the bell-note do its work. The sound enters them and displaces, as you said, the contents of the skull — but what it displaces is specifically the running commentary, the inner voice that has been narrating them to themselves since childhood. For as long as they can bear it, they are simply a body in a ringing room, with eyes that adjust slowly to the dimness, and a chest that aches without knowing why. Some of them weep without producing the thought *I am weeping*. Some of them laugh, once, at nothing, and are startled by the sound of their own laugh inside the bell, and then forget they laughed. Most simply sit until the cold reaches the bone and the sound has become the only thing, and then they get up, stiff, and climb out, and walk home.

They do not consider this a religion. If you asked one of them — and you would have to know how to ask — they would probably shrug and say it was something their grandmother showed them. They do not believe the hollow is sacred. They do not believe it is anything. They believe, if they believe anything about it, that it is simply a place that exists, and that going there occasionally is something a person can do, the way a person can stand in a cold river or sleep one night outdoors in winter. A corrective. A reminder of proportion.

The notched figures in red ochre were made by one of them, long ago, in a generation when the practice was briefer and more anxious and people felt the need to count. Later generations did not continue the tally. They understood, without discussing it, that counting was the kind of thing the hollow was meant to dissolve, and that to mark one's returns was to bring the village in with you, which was precisely what one came to leave behind. The marks remain because no one would erase them — they belong to a person who sat where they now sit — but no new marks are made.

There are perhaps, at any given time, nine or eleven of them alive. The number is not maintained; it simply tends to that range, the way a pond tends to a level. When one dies without having passed on the knowledge, the hollow waits. It has waited before, for decades, ringing to its owl and its mice. Eventually someone wanders down the slope of black loam by accident — a hunter following a wounded deer, a child who has run too far from a gathering party — and stays longer than they meant to, and comes out changed, and finds, years later, that they are telling a grandchild about a place. The transmission resumes. The hollow does not seem to mind the gaps. The hollow does not seem to mind anything.

What they are, then, if one had to say: they are the small intermittent human counterpart to the owl. Replaced, when they die, by another who is also quiet, and also marked by the bell, and who does not question this. They live their ordinary lives more lightly than their neighbours because once or twice or seven times in a lifetime they have been pressed against something dense and real, and they know — without making a doctrine of it — that the version of things humans have agreed on is not the whole of it, and that the whole of it is bearable only in small doses, and only in the cold and the dark, alone.
