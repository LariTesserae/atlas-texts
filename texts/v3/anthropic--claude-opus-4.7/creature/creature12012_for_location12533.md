# The People of Halfmaking

They are not, at first glance, unusual. That has to be said before anything else, because the temptation when describing a place like this is to make its inhabitants exotic, and the truth is plainer and stranger than that. They are people. They have hands and faces and grievances. They eat dinner. A child in Halfmaking would be recognizable as a child anywhere — running, shrieking (softly; even the shrieking is soft here), trailing a stick along a mossy wall to feel the give of it.

But.

They are *long-boned*, most of them. Not tall, particularly — many are quite short — but long in the fingers, long in the forearm, long in the neck. There is a slenderness to them that reads at first as undernourishment and isn't. They eat well enough; the canal brings food in, and the warm runoff channels grow a kind of cress year-round. It is simply their build. You notice it most in the masons and the drift-measurers, whose work involves a great deal of reaching and adjusting in small spaces, and whose bodies seem evolved (or selected, or shaped) for exactly that.

Their skin is pale and faintly grey-green in the way that people who live underwater in old paintings are grey-green — not sickly, but cool-toned, as though they had taken on a little of the rock and a little of the moss. In the deep folds of the elderly — at the corners of eyes, in the hollows of throats — the moss does, very faintly, actually grow. It is considered neither shameful nor remarkable. An old woman in Halfmaking has a green seam down the side of her neck the way an old woman elsewhere has grey in her hair. You learn not to stare.

Their eyes are large and dark and adjusted to the smoke-light. In full sun they squint and suffer; full sun is rare here anyway, filtered as it is through the slow grey river overhead. At night they see better than you would expect. Children playing in the alleys after the lamps come on do not seem to need the lamps.

They move, all of them, with a particular gait. It took me a while to see it. They walk as if the ground might be slightly different from where they last left it — and of course it might be; it often is. Each footfall has a fractional hesitation in it, a testing, a readiness to adjust. They do not stumble. They almost never stumble. A visitor from outside the valley, walking Halfmaking's streets, will stumble constantly in the first week and then stop stumbling, and will not be able to say when the change happened or what they are doing differently. They have learned, without learning, the *listening foot*. The locals call it that, when they call it anything, which is rarely.

They speak softly because the valley speaks softly; the muffling that the moss does to machine-noise it does also to voices, and shouting simply does not carry, so no one bothers. Their language — and here I am uncertain, because I cannot quite hear it clearly — seems to have a great many words for *states of becoming*. For rock that is still soft. For rock that is mostly hard but not yet. For a wall that has begun to lean but has not yet been shimmed. For a river-channel in its first week, its second week, its dying week. These distinctions matter to them in the way distinctions of tense matter to other peoples. To say a thing *is* in Halfmaking is almost rude; it forecloses. One says, instead, that a thing is *at* such-and-such a stage of itself.

They are patient. This is the deepest thing about them and the hardest to describe without making them sound saintly, which they are not — they quarrel, they cheat at cards, a man in the dye-works last week was caught skimming and was quietly, firmly run out of town by means I did not see. But the underlying tempo of them is slow. They do not rush. They do not, I think, *can* not rush; the valley has trained it out of them across however many generations. A craftsman setting a row of bricks in Halfmaking sets each brick as though the brick will outlast him, which it won't — nothing here outlasts anyone, everything shifts — and yet he sets it that way anyway, because the *setting* is the thing, not the brick.

They have, most of them, a particular relationship with their own bodies that I want to try to describe. They treat themselves the way they treat their houses: as things that flex, that need shimming, that are *at a stage*. An ache in the shoulder is not a problem to be solved but a development to be noted. The drift-measurers, I have learned, also measure people — there is a long quiet practice in the back rooms of certain houses where an old man or woman will lay hands on you and tell you, in the careful vocabulary of becoming, where your body is in its slow churn. Whether the diagnosis does anything I cannot say. The recipients of it seem comforted.

They love. They love in the soft way of everything here. I watched two of them, on a bridge that had been wheeled into a new position that morning, stand together for nearly an hour and not speak, and the not-speaking was clearly the substance of the thing, the held attention, the standing-together-while-the-valley-moved-under-them. When they parted they touched each other's wrists, briefly, and went off in opposite directions, and I understood without being told that they would meet on that bridge — or wherever the bridge had got to — the next day, and the day after, and that this had been going on for years and would go on for years more.

They die softly too. I have not seen it but I have been told. They are buried in the half-set rock at the valley's edge, in shallow troughs that are not marked because the marking would be pointless; within a generation the rock will have risen and shifted and the bodies will be somewhere else, deeper, folded into the pale grey stone with its paler veins. The veins, someone implied to me, and then would not confirm, are partly *them*. I do not know if this is metaphor or chemistry. I suspect, here, the distinction is not as firm as I would like it to be.

The children. I should say something about the children. They are quiet but not subdued; they play hard, but their play has the same muffled quality as everything else, the same listening quality. They are taught very young to put a hand on a wall before trusting it. They are taught to notice when a step feels wrong. By six or seven they can tell you, with reasonable accuracy, how much a given alley has narrowed since spring. They find this neither tedious nor remarkable; it is simply *attention*, the thing one does, the way a child elsewhere learns to look both ways before crossing.

There is something else, and I am uncertain about it, and I will say it carefully.

I think they are, in some slow way, *becoming the valley*. Not metaphorically. The greening at the edges of the old ones. The veins in the stone. The way their gait matches the give of the rock so precisely that visitors cannot reproduce it without years of practice. The way they speak in tenses of becoming because they themselves are in a tense of becoming. They are not separate from the half-made bedrock under their feet; they are continuous with it, on a longer timescale than a single life, and they know this, and it is the source of their calm.

They are a people who have understood that they are also a place, and that the place is also them, and that both are unfinished, and that this is not a problem.

At evening, when the lamps come on and the moths gather and the smoke-river glows, they sit on their doorsteps — those whose doorsteps are still where they were yesterday — and they watch the light, and they click softly to the rooks, and the rooks click softly back, and somewhere under all of it the rock rises another quarter-inch and takes them with it.
