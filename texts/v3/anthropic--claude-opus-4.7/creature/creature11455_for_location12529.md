They are not, of course, beetles. The word is a courtesy you extend to yourself because the shape is close enough — six limbs, a hard back, a low profile — and because the alternative is to admit you don't know what you're looking at. Up close (and you cannot get close, but imagine it) the carapace is not chitin. It is the same material as the kiln-walls: a fired, grainy ceramic, fine-pored, warm to a touch you will never give it. The carapace is not grown. It is *made*. Each worker wears a shell that was shaped in a kiln by another worker, and when a worker dies its shell is returned to the kilns and refired into something else — a duct, a tile, a bead. Nothing in the pocket is wasted because nothing in the pocket is, strictly speaking, alive in the way you mean. The line between the worker and the work is not where you would draw it.

What lives, then.

There is a thing under the carapaces — call it the animal part — that is small and soft and does not look like much. A nub. A pale knot of tissue the size of a pea, set deep in the shell's central cavity, connected to the chisel-limbs and the sensory pits by threads of something that is partly nerve and partly the same sintered grit they build with. The nub is warm. It pulses. It is, you suspect, the only part of any individual that could be said to belong to that individual alone, and even this is uncertain, because when a worker is gravely injured another worker will sometimes split its own carapace open along a seam you did not know was there and press its nub against the injured one's nub for a long moment before resealing. Something passes. The injured worker is then carried down a shaft and not seen again, and the helper returns to its kiln and works at the same pace it worked before, and you cannot tell whether what just happened was a mercy, a transfer, an inheritance, or a conversation.

They do not appear to have names. They do not appear to need them. A worker called to a clogged duct does not seem to be summoned; it simply *goes*, the way blood goes to a wound, and the workers near it adjust to fill the gap it left, and the workers near those adjust in turn, and the adjustment propagates outward through the pocket in a ripple that dies out a few body-lengths from the original event. There is no central authority and no visible signaling. There is only the fact that the colony, taken as a whole, behaves like a single careful animal attending to itself, and the individual workers behave like the cells of that animal, with the cells' indifference to their own particular fates.

And yet — and this is what unsettles, after a while of watching — they are *not* indifferent. A worker that drops a bead pauses, and the pause is not mechanical. There is a quality to it. The chisel-limbs hold very still and the sensory pits orient downward and the small soft nub inside the shell does something you cannot see but feel, the way you feel another person listening in a dark room. The worker is attending. To the ring, to the silence after the ring, to whatever the mountain might say back. Then it resumes. It does not look up to check whether its neighbors heard. It does not need to. Its neighbors heard.

There is, somewhere — you cannot find it, but you become certain of it — a deeper room. Not a queen's chamber; nothing so monarchical. A listening-room. A place at the bottom of one of the bored shafts where the rock is thinnest against whatever lies below, and where a few of the workers (older? differently shaped? you cannot tell from up here) sit very still for very long periods and do nothing that looks like work. They are the ones who decide that a section will fail within the week. They are the ones who know which heat-seam will open next. They receive, and the colony acts on what they receive, and the kilns move and the alcoves migrate and the beads are made in the colors and densities the mountain has asked for this season. The workers above do not need to know any of this consciously. The information arrives in them the way weather arrives in a flock of birds.

What the mountain wants the beads for, you will not learn. But you can guess at the shape of the relationship. The peak is young and raw and unfinished, still settling into itself, still working out how to be a mountain; and these makers have come, as they have come to other peaks before this one and will come to others after, to help it finish. The beads are perhaps ballast. Perhaps insulation. Perhaps a kind of seeding, packed into the bored shafts so that when this mountain eventually wears down to a stub, the next mountain — pushed up somewhere else by the same slow exhalation — will rise already knowing how to be a mountain, because the beads will have taught the mantle, and the mantle will remember.

The makers will not be there to see it. Or they will, in the only sense that matters to them: some shell now in this pocket will be refired into a duct, and that duct will be carried down a shaft, and the duct will be in the new rock when it comes up, and a new generation of the same careful soft-nubbed animal will crack out of a fresh kiln on a fresh peak and recognize, without being told, exactly what to do.

They are the mountain's craftsmen. Or the mountain's antibodies. Or the mountain's children, depending on which direction you read the relation. From inside the pocket, watching the smoke threads go up and feeling the low note come back, the direction does not seem to matter. The work continues. The hush holds. The mountain breathes out warmth and the makers shape it into small dark beads and give it back, and somewhere very far below, something that is not quite a voice says *yes, like that, a little more,* and the kilns sigh, and the chisels tick, and a bead falls and rings, and every small soft thing inside every fired shell pauses for exactly one heartbeat to listen.
