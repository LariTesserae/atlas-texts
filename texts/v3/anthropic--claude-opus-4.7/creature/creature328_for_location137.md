They are called, in their own speaking, by that in-breath word, and so I will not try to write it here — it would look wrong on a page, all consonants and no weather. But I can tell you about them.

They are small, as you saw, but it is worth saying what kind of small. Not stunted. Not the smallness of hardship. They are the size a person becomes after many generations of never needing to reach higher than a hanging cup, never needing to run, never needing to carry anything heavier than a replacement bowl up a rope ladder. Their smallness is a fit. Their hands are long for their bodies — the fingers especially, which have the articulation of something between a spider's legs and a harpist's hands. They can braid a cord of the proper length without measuring. They can tell by touch whether a cup has been hung at the correct height, because the correct height produces a particular standing dampness on the rim that the fingertips recognize the way yours recognize the temperature of bathwater.

Their eyes are large and dark and not quite useful. The lichen-light is enough for them but only barely, and they have developed, instead, a way of knowing the room by its sound-shadow. A person moving across the cavern displaces certain drips, muffles certain *tinks* by standing near them, occludes the *tok* of the deep pot with the shape of their shoulder. The others know where everyone is without looking, the way you know where your own hand is in the dark. A stranger entering the cavern is felt immediately, not as a sight but as a *wrongness in the song* — a stranger's body is the wrong size, stands in the wrong places, does not know to hold still when passing the copper pan.

They do not have leaders. They have, instead, listeners. In each generation three or four children are born who hear the song more completely than the others — who can tell you, at six years old, that the seventh cup in the eastern cluster has begun to ring a quarter-tone flat because a grain of calcite has settled in it. These children are not elevated. They are simply the ones who are asked, when an asking is needed. They live in the same closet-rooms as everyone else. They eat the same fish. The only privilege of being a listener is being disturbed more often.

They have children rarely. A woman bears perhaps once in her life, perhaps twice, and the pregnancies are long and the births are quiet — there is a technique, passed down, for giving birth without crying out, because a cry would unmake months of the song's settling. The infants are taught, before they are taught anything else, to breathe shallowly and to cry into a particular felted cloth that absorbs the sound. A baby who cannot learn this does not, I think, survive. This is not spoken of. It is one of the things the song has decided.

They love each other. I want to be clear about this, because a description of such stillness can sound like a description of the unfeeling, and they are not unfeeling. They love in the register they speak in — between the drips, under the sound. A couple who have been together for forty years will sit beside each other on a slate walkway and do nothing that an outsider would call affection, and yet something is happening: they are listening to the same cup, and each knows the other is listening to it, and the cup's small voice is being heard twice, and this doubling is what, for them, love sounds like. When one of them dies the other will sit by that cup alone and the sound will be thinner and they will know, in their body, that they are now the only one hearing this particular *tok*, and that is grief.

They bury their dead in the cistern. The body is weighted with stones and lowered through one of the deeper pools and the blind fish eventually do what blind fish do. The water of the cistern is therefore, in a sense, all of them — every ancestor dissolved into the tea-colored lake they drink from. When they drink they are drinking their grandmothers. They do not find this strange. They find it obvious. What else would water be.

Their memory is not like ours. They do not tell stories in sequence. They tell stories by pointing at cups. *The year the third gourd cracked* is a date. *When my mother hung the small copper bell* is a date.
