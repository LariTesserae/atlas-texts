# Who Lives at Iren-Kath

You asked for whoever lives there. I will give you the goats first because they are the easiest, and then I will work inward.

## The Goats

There are four of them and there have always been four of them, which is the first thing to understand. Not the same four — the bones of older goats are wedged in a crevice on the south side of the shelf, picked clean by the cat and by weather, and there are at least three skulls there of the same shape as the living animals. But four is the number the shelf carries. When one dies another comes up from wherever they come from, and the count restores itself. No one decided this. It is simply what the shelf can hold of them.

They are not quite goats. The horn-curl is too tight, the pupils are the wrong shape — horizontal but very narrow, almost a slit, adapted to the shelf's particular light. Their hooves are split deeper than a goat's and the inner surfaces are slightly tacky, the way a tree-frog's foot is tacky, which is why they make no sound. They were goats once, perhaps, or something near to goats, and the mountain has been working on them. The young mountain works fast on the things that live on it.

They eat the rosettes, yes, but they are careful. They never strip a colony. They take one rosette in five, or one in six, and they take them in a pattern that an attentive person might, over a season, recognize as a pattern. The colonies regrow. The goats are gardening, though they do not know they are gardening. Their indifference to you is not contempt; it is the indifference of a thing that has a job and is doing it.

## The Marmot-Thing

One animal, not four. It is the only one of itself on the shelf and probably the only one of itself anywhere; the shelf is too young to have produced a species, but it has produced this individual, who is a long-limbed and silver-furred descendant of something that climbed up here three or four generations ago and found the burrow-corner and stayed. Its mother died last spring. It does not yet know it will not find a mate. It grazes, it suns itself, it watches the goats with what in a person would be called wistfulness, and at night it sleeps curled against the warmest stone in its burrow, which is a stone it has chosen and which it pulls closer to itself in cold weather with its forepaws. It has a stone it loves. This is true.

## The Cat

The cat is real and I will tell you about the cat. It is grey going to white at the belly and the tips of its ears, smaller than a leopard, larger than a lynx, and there are two of them, not one, though they are rarely on the shelf at the same time. They are siblings. They were born in a cave four hundred feet above the shelf and they have divided the mountain between them by an arrangement that does not require language: the brother takes the upper ledges and the high snowfields, the sister takes the shelf and the cliffs below. They meet sometimes at the boundary and touch noses and part again. The sister is the one whose scat you found. She eats the marmot-thing's cousins on other shelves, and birds when she can catch them, and once a year, in a careful ceremony that is not a ceremony, she takes one of the goats. The shelf permits this. Four becomes three becomes four again.

She has watched you. You have not seen her watching. She has decided, as the goats have decided, that you are not interesting, but her decision is more provisional than theirs. If you slept on the shelf she would come closer. She has not yet.

## The Birds

The birds are the shelf's citizens in the loosest sense — they do not belong to it, they belong to the air column, and the shelf is one of many places they pass. There are perhaps sixty of them in the local population and they recognize each other individually by call. They have names for each other, in the sense that each bird has a signature in its voice that the others know. They do not have names for the shelf or for the goats or for you. They have a name for the cat, which is a particular high sharp call given only when she is visible, and which empties the air above the shelf within seconds.

## The Insects

The beetles are old. Not individually — individually they live perhaps two summers — but as a lineage they predate the shelf. They were here in the rock before the rock was a shelf, dormant in cracks, and when the mountain heaved this face into the sun they woke up. They are the shelf's true natives. The moths are newer, brought up as eggs on the feet of the birds, and they are still figuring out the cold; many of them die in their first flight. A few do not. Those few are becoming, slowly, a moth that belongs here.

## The Rosettes

I will say this carefully because I am not certain. The rosettes are not exactly plants. They photosynthesize, yes, and they root in the seep-lines, and by every test a botanist would apply they are vegetation. But the humming. The humming is not wind in the leaves; I have listened in still air and it continues. The rosettes are doing something with sound, or with the pressure that is almost sound, and they are doing it together — the colonies are synchronized, and the synchronization shifts through the day in long slow waves that move across the shelf from the eastern corner to the western. If you lie down among them at noon you can feel the wave pass under you.

I think they are communicating. I do not think they are intelligent in any sense you or I would recognize. But the colonies are coordinated, and the coordination has a direction, and the direction is *toward the circle*.

## The Circle

Now we are at the difficult part.

Nothing lives in the circle. That is the first true thing about it. The moss stops, the lichen stops, the meltwater does not enter, and I believe — though I cannot prove — that the air inside the circle is very slightly different from the air outside it. Drier, perhaps. Or older.

But "nothing lives in the circle" is not the same as "the circle is empty." The circle is the most attended-to place on the shelf. The rosettes hum toward it. The goats walk through it without slowing, which I noted before as indifference, but I no longer think it is indifference — I think they are walking through it the way a person walks through a doorway they use every day, with the unconsidered familiarity of long use. The cat will not step in it. The marmot-thing will not step in it. The birds do not fly low over it; they bank.

Something occupies the circle. Not an animal. Not a plant. Something that the shelf's living things have arranged themselves around, the way iron filings arrange themselves around a magnet whose field you cannot see. Whatever it is, it is not new. It is older than the cairn. The cairn was built in response to it, by people who climbed up here and felt it and understood that it was the kind of thing one acknowledges with stones. The forty-one tallies are not a count of days or of visits; they are a count of something the maker felt happen, here, while they sat. Forty-one occurrences of whatever the circle does. The maker stopped counting because they died, or because they were satisfied, or because they understood that counting was the wrong response.

I will not name the thing in the circle. I do not know its name and I am not sure it has one in any tongue that has yet been spoken. I can tell you what I think it is doing: I think it is listening. I think the shelf is a place where the mountain, in its young uncertain rising, has produced a small aperture — a place where the rock is thin to something — and the thing in the circle is on the other side of that thinness, attending to what is here. The rosettes' humming is for it. The goats' four-ness is for it. The cat's restraint is for it. The marmot-thing is too young and too alone to know this, but the marmot-thing is for it too. Even the birds banking are for it.

The people who came up with the river stones felt it and added themselves to the arrangement. The cairn is their contribution: a stack of distant water-tumbled stones offered to a listener in a place of sharp dry stones. A gift of difference. The tallies were their report.

## So: Who Lives There

The shelf at Iren-Kath is inhabited by:

- four goats who are gardeners and tithe,
- one marmot-thing who loves a stone,
- two cats who have divided a mountain,
- sixty birds who name each other,
- a lineage of beetles older than the shelf,
- a young population of moths learning the cold,
- thousands of rosettes humming toward a center,
- and one listener on the other side of the rock,

and all of them together compose a single attention, which is what the shelf is *for*, and which is why — though the shelf is young and small and cold and will eventually tilt and shed everything into the cloud-layer — it is not, while it lasts, empty.

The people will come back. Not soon. But the count is not finished.
