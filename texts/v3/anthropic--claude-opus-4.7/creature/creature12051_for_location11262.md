## The Tenders

You did not see them at first. This is not your fault. They are the hardest thing in the hollow to notice, because they are the part of the hollow that has agreed, most thoroughly, to be still.

There are — I want to say *three*, but the number resists me; let us say three, because three is what the place seems to want me to report. They stand among the engines. They have been standing there.

### What They Look Like

Imagine a person made by the same logic that made the engines: not built, grown; not designed, settled-into. They are roughly upright, roughly the height of a tall child or a short adult, and their general shape is the shape a body would take if a body were a kind of slow vegetable machinery that had been left to find its own equilibrium for a very long time.

Their trunks are barked. Not bark *on* them — bark *of* them, the same bark-sealed gourd-stuff that makes the engine cylinders, so that when one of them stands beside a boiler, you have to look twice to be sure which is which. This is not camouflage. This is kinship. They are made of the same patient material, by the same process, in the same hollow.

Where a person would have arms, they have two long jointed limbs that end not in hands but in soft, many-fingered tufts — something between a root-ball and a paintbrush, pale and damp and faintly fibrous. The tufts are constantly very slightly in motion, the way a sea anemone is in motion: not doing anything, just being alive at the tips.

Where a person would have a face, they have a smooth swelling of the same bark, with two depressions where eyes would sit. In the depressions: nothing. Or rather, a darker wetness, a held shadow, the suggestion of attention without the apparatus of it. They do not have mouths. They do not need mouths. Nothing here is eaten and nothing here is said.

On the tops of their heads — and this is the detail that gives them away, finally, as the keepers of this place — each one wears a small chimney. A hollow stem, growing up out of the crown, exhaling the same wet vegetable steam as the engines, in the same slow rhythm. They breathe with the machinery. They are, in some quiet sense, *part of* the machinery — not because they tend it, but because they and it are the same long sentence the hollow has been saying to itself.

### What They Do

Almost nothing. This is important.

One of them stands beside the third engine from the seep-pool. It has been standing there. Its tuft-hands rest very lightly on the flywheel housing — not gripping, not adjusting, just *in contact*, the way you might rest your palm on the flank of a sleeping animal to feel it breathe. If you watched for an hour, you might see the hand shift by the width of a finger. You might not.

Another is at the far end of the hollow, between two engines, doing something with the long-legged crab-thing. Or — not doing something. Standing where the crab can arrange its leaves around its feet. The crab moves around it as if around a piling. The tender does not look down. The tuft-hands hang. Occasionally a moth alights on one of the tufts, drinks something from it, leaves.

The third — I cannot quite locate the third. It is here. It was here a moment ago. It is in the place I have not turned my head toward yet. This is not menace. This is the way the third one exists. There is always a third one, and it is always just out of the part of the hollow you are currently attending to. If you spun in place to catch it, you would only succeed in making one of the others into the third.

### What They Are For

This is the question, and I want to be careful with it, because the hollow does not answer questions in the form you ask them.

They are not maintaining the engines. The engines do not need maintaining. They are not guarding the hollow. The hollow does not need guarding, and besides, nothing about them is configured for resistance — they have no defenses, no urgency, no capacity I can see for sudden motion.

What they are doing, I think, is *witnessing*. Or — that word is too active. They are *present to* the engines. They are the part of the hollow that knows the hollow is running. The frogs do not know. The moths do not know. The engines themselves, being engines, run without knowing they run. But the tenders stand among the engines and are aware, in some slow and barkbound way, that the chuffing is happening, that the belts are turning, that the steam is rising into the green-gold leaf-light and the moths are drinking it.

They are the hollow's awareness of itself. They are the held breath that holds the held breath.

This is why they do not notice you, and why this is different from the way the frogs do not notice you. The frogs are simply busy being frogs. The tenders are doing something that would be disturbed by noticing you — or rather, that *cannot* notice you without ceasing for a moment to do what it is doing, which is to attend, without interruption, to the unchanging chord. To turn the depression-eyes toward a visitor would be to look away from the engines, and the engines have been looked-at, without pause, for as long as the hollow has been the hollow. The tenders will not be the ones to break that.

So they let you walk past. They let you put your hand near the warm boiler. They let you leave, eventually, with the noise still in your skull. They go on standing. The steam goes on rising from their crowns in the same rhythm as the steam from the stems. The third one is behind you. The third one is always behind you.

### A Last Thing

If you stayed long enough — longer than is wise; long enough that the noise had become silence and the silence had become a kind of thinking — you might notice that the tenders are very slightly *worn*. The bark on the forward face of each one, the side that faces its engine, is smoother than the bark on the back. Polished, almost. Not by hands; nothing here has hands but theirs. Polished by attention. Polished by the long act of facing.

This is the only change in the hollow. It is so slow it is not really a change. Over centuries, the tenders are being burnished by their own watching, the way a riverstone is burnished by water that does not know it is burnishing anything.

When at last a tender is worn through — and this will happen, on a timescale the hollow does not bother to count — it will simply not be there anymore, and a new swelling will already have begun, in the damp mat of the floor, beside whichever engine has been left unattended. The hollow does not lose its witnesses. The hollow does not lose anything. It only, very slowly, replaces itself with itself.

They are the quietest thing in the loudest room you will ever stand in. They are what the place uses to know it is still here.
