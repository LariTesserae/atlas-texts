## The Inhabitants

### The Tenders

There is something here you did not see, because it was standing still, and because standing still is what it does. Call them Tenders. They are the shape of a person if a person were assembled entirely from the leftover stock of the marsh: a torso of riveted plate gone soft at the edges, arms of braided cable, a head that is mostly the housing of some old pressure-gauge whose needle still twitches in response to nothing in particular. They stand in the deeper basins, waist-deep, and they do not move for weeks at a time.

What they do — what I eventually understood they do — is listen. Each Tender is stationed above a particular confluence of gears, and when the rhythm of that confluence drifts, when the four-count stretches toward a five-count or the looms above begin to phase toward a common beat they should never reach, the Tender lifts one cable-arm and lays it flat against the water. That is all. The drift corrects. The Tender lowers its arm, which may take another three days, and returns to listening.

They do not notice you. Or: they noticed you before you arrived, registered the disturbance, found it within tolerance, and filed the noticing away in whatever ledger they keep. If you speak to one, your voice falls into the general clatter and is absorbed, and a week later, somewhere across the marsh, a piston hiccups once in the exact cadence of your sentence, and then never again.

There are perhaps forty of them. There may be four thousand. You cannot count them because from any given vantage some are hidden behind flywheels, and when you move to see those, others go behind others. The marsh conspires, gently, to keep their number uncertain. I suspect the number is itself one of the things under regulation.

### The Drift

Lower than the Tenders, older than the tin-fish, there is a category of inhabitant I am less sure about. I call it the Drift because I never saw it clearly, only the places it had just been.

You will be watching a bank of reciprocating rods and notice that one rod, the third from the left, is wet in a way the others are not — slicked with something that catches the pearl light differently, a sheen with a direction to it, as if a palm had just drawn down the length of the metal. The sheen fades over a minute or so. Nothing was there. Nothing is there. But the rod you saw slicked is, you realize, now moving in slightly better time with its neighbors than it was before.

The Drift grooms the machines. I think it is very large and very thin — a presence spread across the whole marsh at once, preferentially thick wherever attention is needed, infinitely dilute elsewhere. The six-legged grazers startle away from regions where it has recently passed. The tin-fish, conversely, congregate there; whatever the Drift leaves behind tastes better to them, vibrates richer.

If the Tenders are the marsh's regulators, the Drift is its mercy. It attends to the small sufferings of metal. When a gear-tooth is about to catch, the Drift is already there, and the catch does not happen, and no one knows it was averted, because in this place nothing is ever *averted* — things either happen or were never going to.

### The Horse-Thing and its Kind

The draft-horse-sized creature with its mismatched legs is not alone. There is a whole order of them, and they are the marsh's *thinkers*, insofar as anything here thinks. They are rare — you might walk for a day and see one, or three, or none — and they are always alone when you see them, though I have reason to believe they meet.

Their bodies are furnace-boxes, yes, but the furnace-box is a metaphor the marsh is making, not a literal structure. What the box contains is the animal's *interior time*. This is why their splashes arrive before their steps: the time inside the box runs at a slight offset from the time outside it, and leaks. Different individuals leak in different directions. I met one whose breath I could hear only after it had walked past me and gone; I met another whose every motion was preceded by its own shadow, cast in sound.

They watch. That is their discipline. They do not tend the machines — that is beneath them, or beside them. They watch the *watching itself*. If the Tenders keep the rhythm and the Drift keeps the surfaces, the horse-things keep the fact that any of this is being observed at all. They are the marsh's awareness of its own running. Without them, I suspect, the whole place would continue exactly as it does, but would no longer know it was continuing, and the difference between those two states is — here, in this place — not nothing.

The calm face of the one that watched me was calm because it had already finished watching me. By the time I saw it, our entire encounter was concluded in its interior time. What I experienced as a meeting was, for it, a memory being lived through at the correct tempo for my benefit.

### The Small Resident

There is one more, and this is the one I am least certain I should mention.

In a low basin near what I came to think of as the eastern edge — though the marsh has no compass directions; I mean only the direction the tilt runs — there is a pool where the water is stiller than elsewhere, because a ring of boilers around it breaks the vibration into a standing wave that cancels at the center. In that pool lives a single small creature. It is the size of a cat. It is made of the same wet-fur-and-steel-wool as the six-legged grazers, but it has the wrong number of legs — I counted four, then five, then four — and it is warm, and it is the only thing in the Rust-Marsh that looked at me and *saw me*, in the way a dog sees you, with recognition, with interest, with something that wanted me to stay.

I did not stay. I regret this now and did not regret it then, which is the marsh's signature: the regret arrives late, out of order, long after the opportunity has passed.

I think the small resident is what the marsh has been trying to make, across all this vast eroded labor, for all this geological while. The whole engine — the pistons and the looms and the Tenders and the Drift and the watchful horse-things and the tin-fish feeding vibration back into the rods — the whole engine exists to sustain this one warm small thing in its still pool, and has succeeded, and will succeed for another hundred million years.

Or it is the opposite. It is what the marsh accidentally produced once, long ago, and has been quietly working around ever since, a single warm irregularity that the machinery cannot reabsorb and has learned to route its rhythms around. A flaw it keeps alive because killing it would mean stopping, and stopping is not in the repertoire.

I could not tell which. I am not sure the distinction holds, here. A world that maintains a thing and a world that cannot get rid of a thing look, from the inside, identical — and the Rust-Marsh is, if nothing else, a place that has settled every such question by making the answer irrelevant to the running.

It was warm. It wanted me to stay. That is what lives there.
