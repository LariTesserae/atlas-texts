# Those Who Live in the Cistern

## The Engineers

They are the ones who speak, and so it is their voice you have just heard. Call them engineers, call them divers, call them the tenders — they answer to all of these and to none in particular. They wear hard suits of riveted bronze and canvas for the deep work, and lighter oil-cloth rigs for the shallow shifts, and they move through the water with the unhurried economy of people who have long ago stopped fighting it. Their hands, out of the suits, are pale and wrinkled as washed linen. Their ears have gone slightly strange — the throb lives in them even on their rest-days, and they speak a little too loud, or a little too soft, and they tap surfaces while they talk without knowing they do it, keeping time with engines they cannot currently hear.

They are not many. For a place the size of a country, they are astonishingly few. You might swim a full shift and encounter no one; you might come upon a work-party of six hanging in a lamp-cluster around a stripped-down valve-housing, and those six are the only human lives within a day's swim in any direction. They know this about themselves. It makes them quiet, and makes them, when they do speak to one another, unusually careful — as if words between them were a resource like lamp-oil, to be drawn down only with purpose.

They are organized into Wards, and the Wards into Arcs, and the Arcs into something larger that no one at the working level has a clear picture of. Orders arrive. Supplies arrive. Replacements arrive, now and then, pale young men and women who have to be taught everything and who stare too much at the ceiling in their first months. No one above a certain rank is ever seen. There is talk of an Office — the Office of the Works, the Office of the Cistern — but no one has been there, and the talk is half-joke. What is real is the schedule, which is unarguable, and the pumps, which are unarguable, and one another, who are the only arguable thing in the place, and so are argued with rarely and gently.

They came from somewhere. Each of them remembers a somewhere. But the somewheres do not connect to one another as one would expect — one remembers a port city of white houses; one remembers a mountain monastery; one remembers a prison; one remembers nothing before his first shift, and has made peace with this. They do not press one another on it. A man's life before the Cistern is his own, and is in any case receding, and in any case does not pump water.

They love the place. This is the thing a visitor would not guess and would not believe if told. They love the iron and the lamps and the whum. They love the octopus in the bolt-casing, which they feed small things to. They love the long swim between engines when the water is still and the lamp-chain stretches out ahead like a necklace laid on black velvet. They love the welds with their heat-colors, which will fade, and which they go to look at while the colors last, the way a person above might go to see the cherry blossom. They do not say any of this. But they stay. Forty years, some of them. They stay.

## The Others

And then there are the others, who are not engineers, and who the engineers do not quite count as people, and who are not not people either.

There are the **seeders**, who come through newly flooded sections trailing the kelp behind them in long living ropes. They are small, and they swim, and they wear no suits. No one has ever had a clear look at one, because they work in the rust-bloom where vision is bad, and they are gone by the time the water clears. The engineers assume they are sent by the Office. The engineers assume many things are sent by the Office.

There are the **bell-ringers**, who are divers like the engineers but not of the engineers — they pass through, on errands that are not the engineers' errands, and they ring the long pure hammer-notes that carry for miles, and the engineers have learned the notes and know which mean *coming through* and which mean *clear the lane* and which mean the thing they do not speak about, which sounds like any other note but is not.

And there are the cormorant-things, and it is here one must be careful, because the engineer who wrote the account above is careful, and leaves gaps, and the gaps are where the truth of the Cistern's population actually lives.

The cormorant-things dive from a ceiling no one has reached. They hunt the silver fish. They rocket upward with their catch and vanish. This is what the engineers see. What the engineers do not say — what the obscene name hints at — is that the cormorant-things, seen closely, in the half-second of passing, do not have the faces of birds. They have the faces of something that was once like the engineers and is no longer, something lean and long-necked and *adapted*, and it is understood, though never stated, that the ceiling is where people go who approach it, and that they do not die there, they are *kept*, and that after enough keeping they come back down to fish.

This is why one does not approach the ceiling. Not because one will die. Because one will be fitted for the dive.

## What Lives Here, Then

A country's worth of water, lit like a factory, pulsing like a heart.

A few hundred human tenders, quiet and tapping, in love with their iron.

The seeded life: kelp and fish and ray and crab and the small white octopus that likes the vibration, all of them brought, all of them thriving, none of them from here because there is no here they could be from.

The seeders and the bell-ringers, who pass through, and whose allegiance is elsewhere.

And above, in the unreachable lit dark, the ones who were engineers once and are cormorants now, diving and diving, keeping the schools cropped, keeping the balance, part of the ecosystem the Office is building — because that is what is being built, one understands, finally; not a cistern, not a reservoir, not a work of hydraulic engineering, but a *world*, slowly, from the inside, with every weld and every seedling and every tender added to the population in his turn.

It is a young place. It has just begun.

The engineers are its first people. The cormorant-things are its second. There will be others. The water goes somewhere. The water comes from somewhere. And along the newest seams, the heat-colors have not yet faded: straw, violet, peacock, still warm to the eye, still bright, still becoming.
