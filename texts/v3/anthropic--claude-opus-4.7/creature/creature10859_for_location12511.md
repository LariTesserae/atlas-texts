# The Tenants

They are not one kind of thing. That is the first correction the place makes to you, if you watch long enough. From any distance the velvet-things look like a single species — a congregation of one — but they are not. There are at least four sorts among them, perhaps six, and the differences are small but consistent: a fold here, a fringe of cilia slightly longer, a faint banding visible only when one drifts across the dimmest part of the courtyard and catches what light there is from beneath. They sort themselves. In the refectory the layers are not random. The deepest layer, pressed nearest the grate, is always the same kind — broader, heavier, their velvet darker, almost black-purple. Above them a paler sort, smaller, with the long fringe. Above those, two kinds mingled, the smallest and lightest, who ride the upwelling almost to the vault and then sink slowly back down through their larger kin without disturbing them. The stratification is so reliable that you suspect it is not preference but obligation. Each kind has its station. Each station has been theirs for a length of time that makes the word *species* feel hasty.

What they are, beneath the velvet, is harder to say. If one settles near you on the cloister stone and you look closely — and they do not mind being looked at, they do not mind anything — you see that the folded body is not really a body in the sense you would use the word. It is more like a garment around a body. There is something firmer inside, suggested by the way the velvet shifts: a small dense core, perhaps the size of a plum, and a slow internal motion that is not breathing exactly but is rhythmic, and that matches, when you time it, the pulse of the current through the grate. They are tuned to the building. Or the building is tuned to them. Or both were tuned, long ago, to the same third thing, which is no longer present but whose tempo they have kept.

They do not have what you would call faces. There is a softer place at one end, slightly puckered, which is presumably where food enters when food enters, but it is not oriented forward in any consistent way — sometimes it trails, sometimes it leads, sometimes it points at the stone for hours while the rest of the creature fans gently above it. They are not going anywhere. They are only being where they are, in a slightly different configuration than they were being a little while ago.

The crawlers are stranger, and in some ways more legible, because their patience has shape. There are perhaps two hundred of them in the whole complex, and I think — I am not sure — that this is the same two hundred that have been here for a long time, not generations of two hundred but the actual individuals, very old, moving very slowly because they have all the time there is. Their pale joints have the look of something that grew without ever needing to harden against impact. Nothing has ever struck them. Nothing has ever chased them. Their shells are thin as fingernail and translucent enough that you can see, when one passes a brighter patch of phosphor-stone, the faint shadow of an interior architecture — a curl of gut, a darker knot that might be a ganglion, a row of pale spots along the dorsal line that may be light-sensing or may be something else, some sense we don't have a word for, attuned to the chemistry of the warm seep or to vibrations in the rock or to the presence of their fellows.

Watch one long enough and you understand that it is doing work. It moves along the wall with its forward limbs touching the stone, and where the stone has accumulated some film — too thin to see, but present — the crawler removes it. Cleans it. The cloister is cleaned this way, slowly, in routes that overlap and braid and cover, over the course of a year or two, every accessible surface. They are not eating the film, or not only eating it; some of it they pass along, limb to limb, and eventually one of them carries a small accumulated bolus to a particular crack in the refectory floor, where it is deposited, and where the milk-colored worms receive it. This is one of the routes. There are others. None of them are fast. All of them are kept.

The worms I have looked at less, because they are mostly hidden, but I have seen enough to know they are not simple. They emerge at the cracks to receive what the crawlers bring, and they emerge again, separately, at other cracks, where the velvet-things drift low and brush against the stone, and something is exchanged there too — I cannot see what. A secretion, perhaps. A signal. The worms thicken and thin on a cycle I have not been there long enough to time. They may be the oldest of all of them. They may be what the building was built around, the original tenants, and the crawlers and velvet-things later arrivals who learned the work.

The large mouthed swimmer — there is only the one, as far as I can tell, and it has been the only one for as long as anyone could have been counting — is not a predator. I was sure of this within an hour. Its mouth is enormous and soft and almost always slightly open, and what passes through it is water, only water, filtered through some interior baleen-analog that takes from the water whatever the water has too much of. It is a kidney for the room. It swims its slow circuit through the cloister and the refectory and back, and the velvet-things part for it because its passage is part of the pulse they live by. When it stops — and it does stop, for long periods, hanging motionless in the corner of the courtyard with its mouth slack — the velvet-things gather a little closer to the grate, as if compensating, as if they know the kidney is resting and the work must be shared. Then it moves again and they disperse to their stations.

None of them, I think, know that they are tenants. None of them know there is a building. The building to them is simply the shape of the world, the same way the shape of your lungs is the shape of breathing, not a place but a condition. They do not look at the carvings. They do not pause at the smoothed face above the refectory door. They have no use for the inscriptions even when the inscriptions were sharp, and they have no use for them now. The building is for them in a way that does not require them to perceive it as for them. It simply holds the temperature. It simply channels the current. It simply keeps the walls in the positions the walls need to be in, so that the routes can be the routes and the layers can be the layers and the kidney can swim its circuit unobstructed.

And yet — and this is the part I keep coming back to, the part I cannot quite put down — they are not merely creatures who happen to live in a ruin. They are tenants. The word fits and I cannot dislodge it. There is something in the way they comport themselves, the regularity of their stations, the inheritance of their routes, the way the dark-purple ones never rise above their layer and the small light ones never sink below theirs, that suggests they are observing a custom. Not a custom they remember. A custom whose memory is the shape of their bodies and the rhythm of their internal pulse and the location of the cracks they tend. The masons who carved the lintels are gone. The order, if there was an order, is gone. The faces on the walls have been smoothed to ovals with two dimples for eyes. But the almshouse is still an almshouse. It still receives. It still holds. And these — the velvet, the pale jointed, the milk-thick, the great soft mouth — are who it receives now. They are the poor of this house. They are sheltered, and fed, and quieted, and kept warm, and they do the small work the house requires of its sheltered, which is to be present, and to be regular, and to continue.

If you asked them — and you cannot, and they would not — whether they are well, I think the answer would be yes. Not yes the way a person says yes; yes the way a stone says yes, by remaining a stone. They are well in the way the building is well. They are not finished. They are not hurried. They are taking their long turn in the warm dark, and when their turn is over — in some thousand years, or ten thousand, or however long the equilibrium holds — something else will take its turn, and the house will receive that too, with the same patience, on the same pulse, until the stones at last give up their shape and the warm seep cools and the whole quiet arrangement returns to the sea that has been waiting, all this while, just outside the walls.
