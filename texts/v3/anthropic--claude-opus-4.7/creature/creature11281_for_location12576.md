# The Hand-Speakers of the Roostflats

They are shorter than you would expect. Generations of low-roofed mud-brick rooms have pressed them down; the ceilings of their causeway-houses rarely clear a meter and a half, and they move through their world in a habitual soft stoop that, standing still, never quite leaves them. Their legs are long for their bodies — wader's legs, in a way, made for shin-deep sheet-water and the slow careful placement of a foot that will not startle a bird. Their feet are wide and splayed, the toes longer than yours, with thick pale soles that have never known a hard surface. When they walk on the packed-clay causeways they walk silently. When they wade, they wade without ripple.

Their skin is the color of the wet clay they live on — not by coincidence; the clay gets into everything, into the pores, into the hair, into the fine creases at the corners of the eyes, and over a lifetime it tints them from the outside in. Newborns come out pinker and darken within a season. The oldest among them are nearly the shade of the basalt underfoot, a deep sooty grey-brown that catches the copper light strangely.

The hair is the most surprising thing. It is pale — almost white — and fine, and they wear it long, all of them, men and women and the ones who are neither, braided in heavy ropes down the back or coiled high on the crown. It is not their own hair, or not entirely. The down of the great waders is worked in among it, plaited in, until you cannot tell where the person ends and the bird-fluff begins. This is also the source of the roof-fibers; the same long pale stuff thatches their houses and crowns their heads, and a child sleeping under a roof of it is sleeping under more of what already grows from her own scalp.

Their eyes are large and dark and set deep, with heavy lids. The pupils are wide — perpetually wide — because the light has never been bright enough to ask them to contract. Bring one of these people into a sunlit place and they would be functionally blind, weeping, helpless. They have never seen a shadow with a sharp edge. Their sense of depth, of distance, is calibrated entirely to the tea-light and the soft-edged forms it produces; they read the flock not by detail but by mass and motion, the way you might read a crowd through frosted glass.

## The Hands

The hands are where the people live.

A Roostflat hand is long-fingered, supple, articulate past anything you have ever seen a hand do. The fingers bend backwards as well as forwards, slightly; the thumbs are double-jointed; the small muscles of the palm are developed in ways that show, even at rest, as a faint webbing of definition under the clay-stained skin. A child learns to make the basic shapes — the seventy or so root-gestures — before it learns to walk, lying on its back in a mother's lap with the mother's hands moving above it, the child's hands answering. By five, a child speaks fluently with both hands at once, the left and the right carrying different threads of a single thought, the way you might hum a melody while speaking a sentence. By adulthood the two hands carry full independent voices, and a complex statement — a story, an argument, a courtship — is a duet performed by one person with herself.

There are dialects of hand. The egg-gatherers use a tighter, faster style with the fingertips, conserving motion. The builders speak with the whole forearm, broad slow shapes visible across a causeway. The elders — the ones who have lived long enough that their hands have begun to stiffen — develop a spare, almost lazy register that the young find beautiful and try to imitate, badly, the way young singers everywhere try to imitate the cracked voices of old masters.

They do have mouths, and the mouths work. They eat with them; they breathe through them; they kiss with them, and lovers in the dim rooms make small sounds at each other, sounds with no words in them, just the body remembering it has a throat. But speech — the use of the mouth to mean something — is taboo. Not forbidden. Simply not done. The roar outside would eat any word before it reached another ear, and so the mouth, freed from that duty, was given to other things, and the hands took up the work of meaning, and now several thousand generations later the arrangement is so settled that to speak aloud would be like urinating in the cooking pot. It is not that you couldn't. It is that the thought is faintly nauseating.

## What They Do

They tend the flock, in a sense, though the flock does not need tending. They gather eggs — but only the eggs the birds have abandoned, and they know which these are by signs no outsider could read. They gather down. They gather, from the edges of the basin where the warm sheet-water deepens slightly, a soft white crustacean the size of a thumbnail that the birds also eat, and they grind it into a paste that is most of what they live on. They fire clay. They press mud-brick. They paint, on the inner walls of their houses, the long horizontal bands of red ochre and bone-white that map, in a language no one has ever needed to teach, the shape and standing-pattern of the flock.

They do not hunt the birds. The thought has never occurred to anyone, in the way that the thought of eating your own hair has never occurred to you. The birds and the people are the same population, divided into two bodies; you do not eat yourself.

They make almost nothing new. A pot is a pot; the shape of a pot was decided before the basalt cooled, and to make a pot of a different shape would be — again — faintly nauseating, a small wrongness, like a word mispronounced. A house is a house. A braid is a braid. There are no artists among them, because there is no concept of an individual making a thing differently; there are only better and worse executions of the one correct thing, and even "better" and "worse" are mild distinctions, the difference between two well-made pots being roughly the difference between two well-tied knots.

## What They Know

They know the flock. They know it the way you know your own body — without thinking, without looking, with a constant low awareness of where the mass is denser, where it is thinner, where a disturbance is moving through it. A child of ten can tell you, by the texture of the roar alone, what is happening half a kilometer away in the flock: a brief panic, a courtship cluster, a death. They cannot tell you how they know. The knowing is not separable from the hearing, and the hearing is not separable from being alive in this place.

They know each other. Their society is small — perhaps forty thousand, perhaps eighty, distributed across the causeway-clusters — and any two of them, meeting, can place each other within three or four gestures: this cluster, that family, this trade, that age. They do not have names in the way you have names. They have hand-shapes, personal sigils, a flick of fingers unique to each person, learned and given in infancy, modified slightly through life as the person becomes more themselves. When two people meet, they show each other their sigils, and that is the greeting, and that is enough.

They know almost nothing else. They do not know that the basin has an edge. They do not know that the vapor ceiling has a top. They do not know that the basalt underfoot was once liquid, or that the water comes from somewhere, or that there are other lands. They do not know that they are unusual. They do not know that the roar is loud, because they have never heard quiet; loud is not a category for them, only the texture and weather of the one sound that has always been.

## The Frightening Silence

Once in a great while — once in a generation, perhaps, or less — the flock falls briefly silent. No one knows why. Some atmospheric pressure, some shared startle, some collective held breath. It lasts a few seconds. In those seconds, the people freeze. The hands stop moving. The eyes go wide. Infants, who have only ever known the roar as the inside of the world, begin to scream — and their screams, audible for the first and only time in their lives, terrify them further, and they scream louder, and the adults stand paralyzed, unable to comfort because their hands have forgotten how to speak in the presence of an audible voice.

Then the flock resumes, and the world resumes, and the moment is not spoken of, because nothing is spoken of, and within a day the silence has been folded back into the seamless surface of the always, and only the oldest people, the ones with the lazy beautiful hands, sometimes make a particular small gesture at each other in the evenings — a closed fist opening slowly into nothing — which the young do not understand and do not ask about.

This is what lives in the Roostflats. Long-legged, clay-colored, white-haired, dark-eyed, double-handed, silent-mouthed. A people perfectly fitted to a world that has not yet begun and may never quite begin, holding their one sustained note alongside the birds, under the warm low ceiling, on the raw young stone, forever, or at least for as long as forever lasts in a basin where nothing has yet learned to end.
