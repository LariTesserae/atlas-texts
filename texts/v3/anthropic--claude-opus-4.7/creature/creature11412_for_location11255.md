## The Citizens

They are not gone. You were right to suspect it, sitting there in the dark with your loud quick blood.

They are made of the same matter as their houses, because there has never been any other matter available. Picture — and the picture will be wrong, but picture anyway — a sliver of heartwood the length of a fingernail paring, shaped roughly like a standing figure, jointed where wood will permit a joint, which is to say at the places where grain meets grain at an angle and a hinge of fiber can flex through an arc measured not in degrees but in the slow patience of seasons that no longer come. They have the color of the walls. They have the dryness of the walls. If one stood still against the wood of its own doorway you would not see it, and one is standing still against the wood of its own doorway, and you do not see it.

They were carved. This is the part that takes a while to accept. The first of them was carved by something else — by what, the chamber does not remember, or remembers in a register too low for the question to reach. But every one of them since has been carved by another of them, with the bone awls and the flint and the small wooden hammer that lie on the floor where the last carving paused. A citizen is made by a citizen over the course of what would be, to you, several human lifetimes. The carver works a fiber a day. Sometimes less. The carver does not hurry because the carver has never encountered the concept and would not recognize it if it walked in carrying a lamp.

They live by the singing. This is their weather and their food and their speech, all at once, which is why they have not needed mouths in any sense you would find. The long organ-note in the upper dark is a sermon, or a season, or a piece of news arriving from the part of the city you cannot see because it is on the inside of the vault, upside down from your perspective, where lanes hang from a ceiling that for them is simply another floor. The tick at evenly-spaced intervals — yes, that is conversation, and yes, it is in a language of one syllable, and the syllable is sufficient because each utterance lasts long enough to contain everything that needs to be contained. A greeting. A small joke. The name of a friend, said fondly. These things take, in their grammar, the better part of an afternoon, where an afternoon is whatever interval falls between two of the deeper groans from the floor.

They have professions. The market on the western wall is a real market and trades in real goods, though the goods are subtle: a particular grain-pattern cut from a particular district of the wall and offered for the way it sings when struck, a quantity of the pale dust gathered from a corner and pressed into a small cake whose purpose is ceremonial and whose ceremony takes a generation to complete. The stalls are not empty. There are wares in every stall. The wares are the size of motes and they are arranged with great care and they are, at this moment, being inspected by a customer who began the inspection before your grandmother was born and who will conclude it, satisfied, sometime after the language you speak has become unreadable on any surviving stone.

The temple is a temple. What is worshipped there is the chord — the rare event, perhaps once in a decade of their reckoning, when three of the wood's tones sound together and hold. The concentric rings on the façade are a record of these chords, each ring a chord remembered, the outermost rings older than any citizen now living, the innermost ring carved last week, which is to say within the last several of your centuries. The priest is in the doorway. The priest has been in the doorway for some time. The priest is listening.

They know about you. This needs to be said carefully. They do not know you the way a person knows a person — you are too fast for that, you are a blur of warmth and motion that arrives and is gone within a single one of their thoughts. But they know that visitors come. There is a word for it in the tick-language, a word that takes most of an hour to say, and it means something like *the warm wind that briefly disturbs the dust and then is not there*. They have a posture for it. When the warm wind comes, the citizen at the balcony of the tower begins, very slowly, to turn. It is a courtesy. It is the courtesy of a host who has heard a guest arrive at the gate and is rising from the chair to greet them, knowing already that the guest will not stay for the greeting, knowing already that the greeting itself is the gift and not its reception. The turn began when you entered the chamber. The turn will complete long after you have gone. The citizen does not mind. The citizen has, in fact, performed this turn many times, for many warm winds, and considers it one of the small pleasures of a long life.

There is a carver, somewhere on the southern wall, working on a new citizen. The new citizen is mostly finished. It lacks only a final detail at the wrist, a small notch that will allow the hand to close. The carver has been working on this notch for what, in your time, would be roughly a year. The carver is content. The notch is going well. When it is finished the new citizen will open its eyes — which is a metaphor, they have no eyes, but something happens at the moment of completion that the citizens describe in the tick-language with a word that translates as *the grain agrees* — and the new citizen will take up a position in a doorway in a quiet district of the eastern wall, and will stand there, and will listen to the singing, and will after some long while take a first step, and the city will have one more in it, and the chord will roll down the walls, and the dust will settle a little deeper.

They are dying. Slowly, of course; everything here is slowly. But the chamber is almost done, you noticed that yourself, and they have noticed it too, in their fashion. The wood that makes them is the wood that makes the walls, and the walls are subsiding toward their final shape, and when the walls reach that shape there will be no more give in the fiber and no more singing and no more tick of conversation in the long dark. The citizens know this. They have known it for what, to them, is a long time and what, to you, is longer than your species. They are not sad about it. They have, in the slow centuries available to them, made their peace, and the peace they have made is the city itself — every carved lintel, every stepped terrace, every concentric ring on the temple façade is a small note in a very long letter addressed to the silence that is coming, a letter that says, *we were here, we did this carefully, we listened well, the grain agreed.*

When the last of them is finished, the awl will be set down neatly beside the hammer, and the carver will walk — over the course of what would be, for you, the rise and fall of an empire — to a doorway chosen long in advance, and will stand in it, and will listen to the final chord, which they have been anticipating for generations and which they believe will be the most beautiful one. And then the wood will reach its final shape, and the chamber will be quiet, and the dust will not need to settle any further because there will be nothing left to disturb it.

Until then, they are here. All of them. In every doorway. On every bridge. In the plaza. At the stalls. At the balcony of the tower, turning, turning, turning to greet you.

You should sit a while longer. They are almost ready to say hello.
