# The Tenders

They are not separate from the moss. That has to be said first, because you will want them to be separate — you will want, walking the plateau, for there to be a figure you can meet, a shepherd of the engines, someone who lives in a stone hut and boils tea and can be asked questions. There is no such person. But there is something, and it is more than the grazers and the heron-things and the character-flocks, and if you are very still for a long time it will resolve, the way the bioluminescence resolves, out of your not-looking.

Call them tenders. The moss has no word for them and neither, really, do I.

They form at the engines. Not always, not at every engine, and not on any schedule you could catch. What happens is: the moss near an engine will thicken, over some period that might be hours and might be a season, into a standing column — roughly upright, roughly the height of a tall person, though taller ones have been seen near the burning engine and shorter ones near the cable-drums. The column develops a narrowing near its top that is almost a head and almost isn't. It develops, lower down, long articulations that are almost arms. It does not develop a face. It does not need one.

And then it steps away from the engine and begins to walk.

A tender's walk is not like the grazers' walk. The grazers move through the moss; the tenders move *as* the moss, which is to say the pelt ahead of them rises slightly to receive their foot and the pelt behind them settles slightly from having been stood on, and between these two small adjustments the tender has progressed. They are slow. A tender crossing from one engine to another may take what you would call a day. They do not seem to mind. Time on the plateau is the moss's time, and the moss has plenty.

What do they do?

They adjust. That is the closest I can get. A tender arriving at an engine will stand near it for some hours, and then it will reach into the cog-fiber or the piston-bundle or the flue-wall, and it will do something small and exact — re-braid a strand, press a seam, pull loose a thread of stem that had grown where it shouldn't. The engine does not stop for this. The cog turns past the tender's reaching arm and the tender's arm moves with the cog's turning, matching it, and the adjustment is made inside the motion rather than against it. Then the tender withdraws, stands a while longer, and walks on.

They are, I think, the moss's proprioception. The way your body knows where your hand is without your having to look. The plateau is vast and its metabolism is vast and it needs, somewhere in itself, a *sense* of how it is going, and the tenders are that sense made ambulant. They are how the thinking checks on the thinking.

There is more than one of them. I have seen three at once, at great distance, each at its own engine, and I have had the strong impression — though I cannot defend it — that they were aware of each other in the way that your left hand is aware of your right. Not by looking. By being parts of the same thing.

They do not acknowledge you. This is important. If you walk toward a tender it will not turn to watch you approach. It will not move away. It will continue whatever slow business it is about, and if you come close enough to touch it you will find that it is slightly warm, slightly damp, and that its surface is the surface of the moss exactly — the same fine green-black-silver pelt, the same faint give under the finger, the same smell of wet cold vegetation and something older under that, mineral, patient. You can stand beside a tender for as long as you like. It will not send you away. It will also not, in any sense you can verify, notice that you are there.

And yet.

If you lie down in the moss near a tender — if you make the mistake, or the offering, of lying down near one while it works — then when you stand up the thing that has been taken from you will have been taken more precisely. Not randomly. Not the first loose question in your pocket, but the specific one the engine needed. You will feel, for a moment, as you rise, that you have been *read*. Not judged. Read, the way a hand reads a cog to know whether the cog is turning true. And then the reading will be over, and you will walk away lighter by exactly what the plateau required, and the tender will still be there, its arm still moving inside the turning of the cog, and somewhere in the braid of fiber — down past where your eye can follow, down among the smaller cogs and the smaller — a new small turning will have begun, which is the turning of the thing that used to be yours.

They are not cruel. They are not kind. They are the moss's hands, and the moss is not anyone, and the hands do what the body needs. If you stay on the plateau long enough — and some have; there are shapes in the deeper pools, very still, very old, that were once perhaps travelers who lay down and simply did not find a reason to get up again — you will eventually see a tender form, somewhere, that has in its slow standing-up a quality you half-recognize. A tilt of the almost-head. A way of holding the almost-arm. Nothing you could name. But you will know, without being told, that some long-ago part of some long-ago visitor has been woven up into this one, is turning now inside its walking, and that the tender is no more that person than a cog is the tree it was cut from — and yet the grain is there, the grain persists, the grain does its small work inside the larger work forever.

This is what lives here. Not the tenders alone: the whole slow circulating body, of which the tenders are only the most nearly-person-shaped part. But if you need something to have met, when you come down from the plateau — if you need a face to put to the place, even a face that isn't a face — it will be them. The standing columns at the engines. The slow walkers between. The moss's way of keeping track of itself, moving quietly through its own thinking, adjusting what needs adjusting, and carrying, folded invisibly into their pelts, whatever of the passing world the plateau has found a use for.

They will still be walking when you leave. They were walking before you came. The engines will turn whether you are here or not, and the tenders will tend whether you are here or not, and the moss will think its enormous widely-spaced thoughts whether you are here or not, and this is not a rebuke. It is simply the operating condition. You were, for a little while, inside a mind. Now you are outside it again. The mind goes on.
