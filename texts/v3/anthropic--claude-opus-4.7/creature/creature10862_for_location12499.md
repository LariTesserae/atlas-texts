## The Inhabitants of Aoun-Sur

They are called, by themselves and by the few traders who climb to meet them, the **Sethaim** — a word that means, depending on inflection, *those who set down* or *those who arrived first* or, in its oldest reading, simply *the early.* They do not think of themselves as a people in the way lowlanders do. They think of themselves as a *shift* — the first watch in a long roster, with the understanding that other watches will follow and that their job is to make the handover clean.

### Their Bodies

They are tall and narrow-chested, built for thin air, with the long forearms and broad palms of people whose work is largely lifting and placing. Their skin runs to a wet-clay color, faintly greenish in the men and faintly coppered in the women, and it never quite dries — they perspire constantly in a fine slick, mirroring the stone they live on. Lowlanders find their handshakes unsettling for this reason. The Sethaim find lowlanders' dry hands frightening, the way one might find a dry tongue frightening: a sign of something wrong inside.

Their eyes are pale and large and set wide, adapted for the long blue twilights that fill the cleft when the sun has left the rim but not yet left the sky. Their hair grows slowly and is usually worn long and wet-braided with copper pins — the same copper pins, sometimes, that their great-grandparents used to mark courses in the wall, retrieved from buildings now half-swallowed and re-purposed as ornament. To wear an ancestor's measuring-pin in your hair is to say: *I carry the line forward.*

Their teeth are notably good. They credit the water.

### Their Minds

A Sethai child learns to count before she learns to speak fluently, because counting is how the work is organized — courses of stone, drips per minute into a given cup, generations until a given wall meets a given ceiling. They have a base-twelve number system and twenty-seven distinct words for *slow*, distinguishing slowness-of-growing-stone from slowness-of-a-house-being-built from slowness-of-a-child-becoming-an-adult from slowness-of-an-argument-being-resolved. They have no word for *hurry* that is not also an insult.

They are not, however, contemplative in the way mountain mystics are usually imagined. They are *practical* on a timescale that simply runs longer than other people's. A Sethai mason planning a buttress is thinking, with no melodrama at all, about the buttress's relationship to a wall that will be built in eighty years to brace it. He is not having a religious experience. He is doing his job.

They laugh easily and tell a great many jokes, most of which are about timing.

### Their Work

Every Sethai has a *measure* — a thing they are responsible for measuring, daily or weekly or annually, and recording in the slate-books kept in the upper galleries. Some measures are practical: cistern levels, the drift of a particular wall, the salt content of a given seep. Some are stranger: the pitch of a particular wind-vent on the third morning after the equinox, the exact color of the celadon moss at the foot of the western stair, the number of times a certain bell rings itself in a given month (the bells here sometimes ring themselves, in the deep updrafts; this is recorded but not explained).

The slate-books go back nine generations. They are the closest thing the Sethaim have to scripture. A young person courting another young person will sometimes bring, as a gift, a copy of a particularly beautiful page from the measure-books — a year in which the moss bloomed unusually, say, or the season when the south wind held a single note for eleven days. To give such a page is to say: *look at this carefully kept thing. I would like to keep you this carefully.*

There are also the **wash-women**, who are not exclusively women and not exclusively washers, but who manage the ceiling-waters and the cistern-waters and the long stone troughs. They are the closest thing to priests Aoun-Sur has. They decide which meeting hall's ceiling-water goes to which use; they keep the drip-cups; they know, by taste, which cistern has been argued under and which has been sung under. They wear pale celadon robes that glow faintly at dusk, like the moss. They are not feared, but they are deferred to in the way that competent specialists are deferred to anywhere.

And there are the **masons**, who are the largest guild and who think of themselves as the spine of the town. They are stoic, slow-spoken, very physically strong, and given to long apprenticeships — fourteen years before a mason is permitted to cut a load-bearing stone unsupervised. They have a saying: *the first cut is forever.* Meaning: the stone here does not erode. What you carve will be carved a thousand years from now exactly as you carved it today, minus whatever the slow-growing rock has swallowed. A mistake by a Sethai mason is a mistake that outlives the mason by a great margin. They take this seriously, but not gravely. They simply work carefully.

### Their Reverence

They do not have gods, exactly. They have *the early*, which is both a tense and a presence — the condition of being-before, of laying down what will be measured against. When a Sethai swears, she swears *by the early* or *on the line set down*. When she is grateful, she gives thanks *to those who will come after*, which sounds backwards to lowlanders but makes perfect sense once you understand that, for the Sethaim, the people who will come after are the audience. The mountains are the stage. The Sethaim are the opening act, and they take pride in it.

They believe — though *believe* is too strong a word; they *assume*, the way one assumes a floor — that the cleft will eventually fill with life. The animals will arrive. The soil will deepen. Streams will cut their beds. Other towns will be built on the platforms the Sethaim are setting now. The marten will become many martens, and then other animals will arrive that no one in Aoun-Sur has seen, and the grove will thicken into a forest, and the forest will breathe. The Sethaim will not be there for this. They know it. They build for it anyway.

They are not melancholy about this. A Sethai woman, asked by a trader once whether it saddened her to know that the town she lived in was for someone else, said: *I lived in a house my great-grandmother built. She is not sad. Why would I be?*

### Their Strangenesses

A few small things, true of them and of nowhere else:

They sleep in pairs or threes, not for warmth or romance but because a Sethai alone in a room cannot sleep — the dripping is too layered, the wind too articulate, and a single body cannot ballast against it. They need at least one other breathing rhythm in the room to find their own.

They do not bury their dead. They wash them in cistern-water and place them on the high ledges above the town, where the rock is growing fastest, and within three or four generations the bodies are gone — absorbed, embedded, become part of the wall. *Going into the line*, they call it. The oldest cellars of Aoun-Sur contain, somewhere in their stone, the great-great-grandparents of the people living above them.

They cannot lie indoors. Not won't — *cannot*, or at least not easily. They believe the ceiling-water hears, and they have grown up with this belief so deeply that the act of speaking an untruth beneath a roof produces in them a physical hesitation, a stutter in the throat. Outdoors, under open sky, they lie as readily as anyone. The wash-women have been known to conduct difficult negotiations on the high galleries for this reason — to let people speak freely. *Under the open*, in Sethai, is a phrase that means *in candor* or *off the record* or *we are not yet on the slate.*

They are, in their way, the first cup of a long pour. They know it. They live like it. And the water drips, and the chisels ring, and somewhere in a high gallery a young mason is being shown, for the first time, the copper pin her great-great-great-grandfather set, still measuring, still true, still early.
