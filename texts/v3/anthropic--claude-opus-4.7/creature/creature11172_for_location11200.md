## The Inhabitants

### The Builders

Call them the crowned. They are the worms, and they are the city, because what they secrete is what stands, and what stands is what they are when they are not extended into the water.

A single one, drawn from its tube, would disappoint. A soft pale rope no longer than a thumb-joint, segmented faintly, blind, with a muscular foot-end that grips the inside of its calcite shaft and a flowering head that is almost all the animal worth seeing. The crown is two spirals of feathered radioles, red banding into white banding into a deeper red at the base, and when it is open it is doing four things at once: pulling oxygen from the brine, sieving copepods and detritus from the slow current, tasting the water for the chemical signature of the things that would eat it, and — this is the part I am least certain about, but I believe it — registering the crowns of its neighbors. Pressure-sensitive. A worm whose neighbor snaps shut will snap shut a half-second later, not because it has seen the predator but because it has felt the absence where a crown was.

This is how the city pulses. Not from a center outward but in patches, in waves, in superstitions. A flatworm's shadow at the north end of an alley will close every tube in that alley and leave the next alley over still in bloom, until some eddy carries the alarm-chemical sideways and the second alley closes too, and by then the first is opening again because the danger has moved on, and so a slow rolling shutter passes across the district all afternoon, if there were afternoons.

They do not know they are a city. Each worm knows: my tube, the rasp of my neighbors against my walls, the taste of the water, open, closed, open. The city is what an outside observer would call the sum, and there is no outside observer. Or — there is me, describing this, which may be the first time the city has ever been seen as a city, and the worms are not aware of the promotion.

They reproduce by releasing gametes into the brine on some cue I cannot identify, perhaps the turning of the pocket's slow current, perhaps a chemical threshold reached when the population is dense enough. The larvae drift. Most are eaten — by the larval fish, by the amphipods, by the copepods even, who are not above it. The few that settle, settle on the tubes of the dead or the living and begin their own secretion, and so the city thickens, branches, leans.

They live a long time. I think. The metabolism here is so slow that I suspect a single worm may persist for what we would call decades, its crown opening and closing perhaps a hundred thousand times, its tube lengthening by the breadth of a fingernail per year, its awareness — if awareness is the word — a long low hum of taste and pressure and the rhythm of its kin.

### The Hunters

The sea-spider is the principal terror. I called it that for lack of a better word: a pycnogonid, almost certainly, but isolated here so long it has become its own thing. Eight legs, each as long as a worm's tube is tall, and a body so reduced it is essentially a junction where the legs meet and the proboscis hangs. The proboscis is the trouble. It is longer than the body and it can be inserted into a tube-mouth faster than a crown can fully retract, and what it does inside the tube I would rather not describe, except to say that it drinks.

There may be a dozen of these in the whole pocket. Perhaps fewer. They are slow, and they do not need to eat often, and they spend most of their time motionless on the rooftops of the leaning towers with their legs splayed, indistinguishable from the calcite they stand on except for a faint iridescence where a leg-joint catches the blue light. When one moves, it moves carefully, tiptoeing, each foot placed so as not to vibrate the tubes it crosses, because a vibration would close every crown for meters around and rob it of its hunt. The hunt requires the worms to believe nothing is happening. The sea-spider's whole life is the practice of being nothing happening.

I think they are old. I think some of them may be the oldest things in the pocket. Their legs bear the scars of ancient encounters — chips, regrown segments, a patina of bacterial mat that grows on the slowest of them like moss on a standing stone. A sea-spider that has not moved in a season will be furred with violet bacteria and will look, when it finally moves, as if a piece of the city itself has decided to walk.

The flatworm is the second hunter and a different proposition. Bruise-colored, the width of a fingernail, gliding along the tube-walls on a slick of its own mucus, it eats the small things — the amphipods unwise enough to be caught in the open, the copepods it engulfs in numbers. It is no threat to the worms in their tubes. It is a constant low pressure on everything else.

And then there are the larval fish, which I am calling fish because they have the shape, but which may never grow up. There may be no adult form here. The pocket may be too small, the food too scarce, and these transparent commas with their black-bead eyes may be the whole life-cycle: born, hanging in mid-water, striking at copepods, struck in turn, dying before they ever become whatever they were meant to become. A population of permanent children. I find this the saddest thing in the pocket, though I am aware sadness is not a category that applies here.

### The Multitude

The copepods are the weather. I do not mean this metaphorically. There are enough of them, and their movements are coordinated enough by the currents and by their own faint social rules, that they constitute a medium. The crowned worms filter them; the flatworms graze through their clouds; the larval fish hunt single individuals out of the swarm. A copepod's life is on the order of weeks and consists, as far as I can tell, of eating bacterial fragments, being eaten, and producing the next generation of copepods, who will do the same. They are not the citizens of this city. They are something more like its air.

The amphipods are something else. They are, I think, the closest thing to a people here. Translucent, hinged, persistent, they walk the tube-walls and the floor and they leave chemical trails — pheromone, or something pheromone-like — that other amphipods read. The trails say: I came this way, the mat here is good, a flatworm passed an hour ago, do not go further. Over time the trails build up into a kind of palimpsest on the calcite, a layered map of where everyone has been and what they found, and an amphipod entering a new district can read its history in the walls. This is not language but it is not nothing. It is a literature of scent, written by everyone and read by everyone, and revised continuously by the slow dissolution of older trails into the brine.

They are also the only creatures here that seem to *know each other*. Two amphipods meeting will pause, antennae working, and exchange — what? Recognition, I think. Status. Something. And then they will pass, or they will fight briefly over a patch of mat, or one will follow the other for a while as if curious. They are the gossips of the pocket. If anything here has a culture, it is them.

### The Lanterns

At the bottom, where the light gives up, the phosphor-blinkers live. I have saved them for last because I understand them least.

They are small — smaller than the copepods, possibly — and they are not animals in any sense I can confidently name. Bacterial, perhaps. Or a colonial something, drifting in loose aggregations that pulse green on a slow rhythm. The rhythm is not random. I have watched the bottom of the pocket for what felt like a very long time and the blinks have a structure: a flare here, then a flare a little to the side, then a wave of flares running across a square meter of the dark floor, then quiet, then a single answering pulse from somewhere further out.

I said earlier that this may be a kind of love. I want to retract that and also keep it. It is not love in the sense we would mean. But it is communication of some kind among entities that have nothing else — no motion to speak of, no chemistry strong enough to cross the cold dense brine at the bottom, no predators worth mentioning because nothing larger goes that deep. They have only the light, and they use it, and what they say to each other with it I cannot guess. Coordination of reproduction, perhaps. Or the simple fact of presence: *I am here. Are you there. I am here.*

If the worms are the city's body and the amphipods are its gossips and the sea-spiders are its fears, the lanterns are its dreaming. The slow strange thought at the bottom of the pocket that the rest of the city does not know it is having.

### What They Are, Together

None of them is the inhabitant. That is what I want to end on. There is no single creature here who is the citizen of the cold sept; there are only the relations.

The worms cannot exist without the copepods to filter and the bacteria to oxygenate the seeps and the slow current to bring food past their crowns. The copepods cannot exist without the bacterial mats. The mats cannot exist without the seeps and the trickle of organics from the ice above. The amphipods graze the mats and are eaten by the flatworms and the larval fish, and their corpses feed the mats again. The sea-spiders thin the worms, which prevents the city from overbuilding itself into stagnation. The lanterns at the bottom do something I do not understand but the pocket would not be the pocket without them; remove them and I suspect the whole long marriage would unwind.

They have been together so long, in such a small closed room, that they have become a single distributed creature with many bodies and many strategies and one slow pulse. When I say *the city is in bloom* I mean the worm-crowns are open. When I say *the city is alarmed* I mean a sea-spider is moving. When I say *the city is dreaming* I mean the lanterns are talking at the bottom. The city is not the tubes. The city is everyone in the tubes and between the tubes and under the tubes, in their long mutual specialization, their inheritance of each other.

If you were to ask who lives there, the honest answer is: it lives there. One thing, with a thousand-thousand bodies, pulsing in the blue dusk under the ice, accreting itself slowly into a shape it will never finish, silent, attentive, and — in its own way, which is not our way — entirely full.
