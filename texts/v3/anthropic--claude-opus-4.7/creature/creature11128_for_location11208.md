They came back.

Not the ones who built the wall — those are gone in the way the description says, gone past returning. I mean the ones who are here now, who I want to tell you about. They came back to a place their kind had left, and they did not know they were coming back, because the knowledge of having been here was not carried forward. It was left in the wall and the cairn and the seven markers, and the wall and the cairn and the markers do not speak the language the returners speak. So the returners are, to themselves, the first.

They live in the columns.

Not on them, not among them — *in* them. This took me a while to see. The columns, remember, are not plants; they are something else, slow-chemical, fed by the planet's exhalation. Their trunks are pale and translucent, and inside the larger ones, in the older ones near the vent-warmed slopes, there are hollows. Not cavities the columns grew around accidentally. Chambers. Some of them quite large. A column fifteen meters tall might hold three or four such chambers stacked, connected by narrow passages where the inner tissue thins to a membrane you could push through with a finger.

The returners are small. Call them the size of a human child, though the comparison is unfair because their proportions are wrong for it — longer in the torso, shorter in the limb, with a head that sits forward on the neck the way a heron's does. Their skin is the color of the column-flesh, which is not coincidence. They are the same color because they eat the same things and are made of the same minerals, and because they spend their lives inside that flesh and have come to match it. In the dim vent-glow they are nearly invisible against the trunks. You would walk past one standing in a doorway-slit in a column wall and not see it until it moved, and they do not move quickly.

They breathe the water. Whatever the old builders did — held breath, brought air, possessed some apparatus that has rusted to nothing — the returners do not do. They have something at the sides of the neck, a long slow gill arrangement that takes oxygen from the water at the rate the water gives it, which is not much. This is why they move slowly. This is why everything about them is slow. They have the metabolism of the place itself, which is the metabolism of stone settling and minerals precipitating and columns growing a centimeter a year. A returner takes most of a day to cross a saddle between two peaks. A conversation between two of them, if you could call it that, might last a week.

They do not eat the columns. This is important. They live inside the columns the way certain wasps live inside oak galls, but they do not consume their hosts. They feed on the mineral rain — the silver showers that take an hour to land. They stand in the open with their faces upturned and their mouths open and the slow precipitate drifts in, and they swallow it, and this is enough. A returner might feed once a week. They are not in a hurry. Nothing is in a hurry.

This is why the description says there are no animals. The describer was looking for animals as we know them — quick darting things, eaters, browsers, the whole busy economy of appetite. The returners are not that. They do not browse the columns because they do not need to. They do not prune. They do not leave paths because they take any path and the columns do not mind. They are so quiet and so slow and so much the color of their surroundings that a careful surveyor with a lamp could pass through their valley and conclude, as the describer did, that nothing lives here.

But they live here. Many of them. I think a few thousand, distributed through the column-forests on the warmer slopes. They are not numerous in our sense but they are not few in theirs.

What do they do?

They listen, mostly. The place is loud, remember — the held organ-chord of the vents, the keening of the crowns, the boom of the rock. The returners have inherited or evolved or grown into an exquisite sensitivity to all of this. Their heads are mostly ear. They can hear the difference between a vent that is about to surge and one that is settling. They can hear, I think, the columns growing — the very slow tearing sound of new cell-walls forming under pressure. They can hear each other across the valley, low subsonic pulses pushed out from somewhere in the chest, and they answer, and the answer takes hours to arrive and hours more to be answered, and a single exchange might span what we would call a season.

What they say to each other I do not know. I am not sure it maps onto anything we would recognize as content. It might be closer to what the vents do — a held tone, varying, sustained, meaning by its variations rather than by anything we could translate. They are not silent and they are not talkative. They are continuous.

They have noticed the wall.

This is the part that troubles me, or moves me, I am not sure which. The wall on the ridge is forty meters of fitted stones, no mortar, waist-high to a human. To a returner it is shoulder-high, which is to say it is the right height to lean against. And they do lean against it. Not all of them, not often, but I have come to understand that the wall is *known* among them. There is a returner — older, I think, by the cloudiness of its gill-fringes — who comes to the wall once or twice a year and stands beside it for a day or two, with one long hand resting on the topmost stone. It does not do anything else. It does not speak. It just stands there, touching the wall, and then eventually it goes back to its column.

The returners did not build the wall and they do not know who did. But they have understood, in whatever way they understand things, that the wall is *made*. It is not like the columns, which grow. It is not like the cairn-shaped piles of rubble that thermal storms sometimes throw up at the bases of slopes. It is fitted. Someone fitted it. The returners know the difference between fitted and fallen, and the wall is the only fitted thing in their world except for the cairn, the post-holes, and the seven markers — and they know about all of these. There is a kind of pilgrimage, if that is not too strong a word. The seven markers in particular are visited. A returner will stand at the first marker for some hours, then move to the second, and so on, taking perhaps a tenday to traverse all seven, and then it will go back down into the forest and not come up again for years.

They do not worship the makers. I want to be clear about that. They do not have gods, as far as I can tell, and the absent builders are not gods to them. But they have a relationship to the made-ness of these objects that is unlike anything else in their experience. The columns grow; the rock settles; the vents sing; the rain falls. These are processes, vast and slow and continuous, and the returners are part of them. The wall is different. The wall *stopped*. Someone made the wall and then stopped making it. The wall is forty meters long and ends. This ending — this evidence that something was intended and completed and then ceased — is the strangest fact in the returners' world. I think they go to the wall to touch the strangeness.

They have not begun to build anything themselves. I have looked for it and there is nothing. They do not stack stones. They do not knap. They have the hands for it — long-fingered, capable, the kind of hands that could fit stones together if a mind wanted to — but the wanting has not arrived among them. Perhaps it will. Perhaps in ten thousand years one of them, leaning against the wall with one long hand on the topmost stone, will understand what the wall is doing and pick up a second stone and lay it somewhere, and then a third, and a new wall will begin growing across some other ridge, and a new species of made-ness will enter the valley. Or perhaps not. They are not in a hurry. Nothing is in a hurry.

When a returner dies — and they do, eventually, after a long life I cannot measure — the others do not bury it. They carry it, slowly, over the course of weeks, down into the warmest part of the valley where the vents are most active, and they leave it in the open on a flat place of rock, and the mineral rain falls on it, and over years it is gradually covered, and eventually it becomes part of the floor and the columns root through it. There is no ceremony beyond the carrying. The carrying takes a long time and many of them participate. I think the carrying is the ceremony.

I want to say one more thing. The description ends by saying the place does not require you, that it got along before and gets along now and will get along after. This is true of the place but I am not sure it is true of the returners. The returners are new — new in the way of this place, which means perhaps a few hundred thousand years — and they are still becoming whatever they will become. They are inside the slow process now. They are part of what the valley is doing. When they touch the wall, they are touching the only thing in their world that suggests another way of being — a way that intends, completes, stops. They have not taken that way yet. They may never. But the wall is there, and they know it, and they go to it, and they put their hands on it, and they stand for a long time without moving, and the mineral rain falls on them and on the wall equally, and the held chord of the vents goes on, and the columns lean in the slow current, and something is happening that I cannot name but that I am sure is happening.

That is who lives there. The columns, slow and possibly considerable. The returners, quiet inside the columns, listening. And the wall, which is not alive but which is, in its way, the most awake thing in the valley — because it remembers, and because the returners come to it, and because what it remembers may yet be learned.
