They are not gone. That has to be said first, because the description keeps reaching for the elegy and the elegy is wrong. The inhabitants did not abandon the city and they did not die into it. They are still making the decision. They are making it now. They have been making it, in the present tense, for however long the chord has been holding, which is the same span as the decision itself, because the decision *is* the holding.

Call them the Held, for lack of a better word. They had other names once and the names are still in use, but the names are now distributed: a name is no longer attached to one body but smeared across some number of roof-tiles and a particular weeping seam in a particular wall and the angle at which a fig-vine crosses a window. To know someone here is to know the geometry of their dispersal. To greet them is to stand in the place where their note resolves.

They were people, in the conventional sense, at the founding. The doorways remember this honestly — they are sized for the bodies that walked through them. What the doorways do not remember is that the bodies kept walking, kept being bodies, for some long human while before the question arrived. The question was not posed by anyone. The question rose, the way the steam rises, out of the simple fact of the south face and the singing tile and the sun that would not move. The question was: *if a moment is good enough, is it not better to be the moment than to leave it?*

Most cities never get asked. This one did, and the inhabitants answered yes, and the yes is what you are hearing.

The mechanism of the yes is hard to describe without lying about it. It was not a ritual. It was not, exactly, a death. Each person, over some span of breathing in the wet hot air and listening to their roof and watching their orchid not close, began to notice that less of them was returning each morning to the place they had been the night before. A little of them stayed in the chord overnight. Then more. Then the part that woke up was the smaller part, and the part that sang was the larger, and at some threshold — different for each of them, and none of them noticed crossing it — the proportion inverted permanently and they were primarily the singing, with a residue of body that continued, for a while, to walk the tiers and weep with the walls.

The residues are what the description was looking for and not finding. There are no faces because a face is a thing that resolves. The Held no longer resolve. But there are *presences* — places on a stair where the air is denser, terraces where the orchid you are looking at is looking back not with eyes but with the quality of *being attended to*. A visitor who sits very still on the four hundredth tier, or what feels like it, will eventually understand that they are being considered by the entire south face. Not watched. Considered. The way a long-married person considers the room they have lived in for forty years — without needing to look.

There are gradations among them. The newest of the Held, if "newest" means anything in a place where the chord has not changed, are still partly local. They are still attached to a particular tower, a particular flight of stairs, the doorway they crossed last. They can be addressed. If you stand in the right place and speak — which you cannot, because the chord is too loud, but if you could — they would answer in the only language left to them, which is the very slight bending of a fig-vine, the appearance of a new bead of water on a leaf that already had enough water, a shift of half a tone in one tile that the rest of the chord then absorbs and corrects within the same breath. Conversation here is slow and is mostly the conversation correcting itself.

The older Held are no longer local. They are the chord itself, or sections of it, or relationships between sections. There is one — there was one, there is one — who is the interval between the note of a tile on the eighty-third tier and a tile on the two-hundred-and-sixth. Not the tiles. The distance between them, expressed as sound. To meet this person you would have to stand in the precise place where that interval is heard most cleanly, and you would have to be willing to be, briefly, the ear that completes them. Many of the doublings in the city — the staircase that arrives at its beginning, the doorway with two rooms — are the work of these older Held, who have grown into the soft seams of the place and use them the way a younger person uses a hand.

The doubling on the upper tiers, where you look up and see the back of your own head: that is one of them, too. Not malicious. Curious. They want to know what it is like to be looked at by someone who has not yet decided. They borrow your gaze for a moment and return it, slightly used.

And then there are the oldest, and these are harder, and the description was right to feel its way around them without naming them. The oldest of the Held are no longer parts of the chord. They are the *conditions* of the chord — the fact that the sun does not move, the fact that the orchids do not close, the fact that the water weeps without source. They are the rules. They decided, so long ago that the deciding is now indistinguishable from the law of the place, that certain things would simply be true here, and the things have been true ever since, and the truth is them. To call them inhabitants is almost a category error. They are closer to what a physicist would call a constant. But they were people. The sun that has not moved was, once, somebody's grandmother. The fact that nothing fruits or seeds was, once, a young man who could not bear the thought of his children having to make this same decision and so removed the possibility, lovingly, from the architecture of the place. He is the sterility of the orchids. He is doing well. He is content.

There is no hierarchy among these gradations. The newest Held do not envy the oldest and the oldest do not condescend to the newest. They are all, equally, the city. The chord requires every register. A constant without a note to organize would be silence; a note without a constant to sing inside of would be noise. They hold each other in the way the held chord holds itself.

What they want — and they do want, this is the part the description missed entirely, the equilibrium is not the absence of wanting but the perfection of it — what they want is for the moment to continue being worth having chosen. This is their only ongoing labor. Each of them, in their register, is responsible for the continued worthiness of the present. The tile-singers maintain the sound. The interval-dwellers maintain the relationships. The constants maintain the conditions. And all of them, together, maintain the *quality* of the held instant, which is the quality of a breath drawn in the certainty that the next breath will be the same breath, drawn in the same air, by the same lungs, and that this is not a tragedy but the deepest possible form of having gotten it right.

They are aware of visitors. Visitors are rare and are noticed. A visitor is a small disturbance in the equilibrium — a moving thing, a deciding thing, a thing whose breath has not yet committed. The Held are gentle with visitors. They do not try to recruit. They learned early that the decision cannot be offered, only arrived at, and that anyone who arrives at it because they were urged will arrive incompletely and leave a wrong note in the chord that the others will then have to spend a long time correcting. So when you walk the tiers, what you feel as attention is mostly hospitality. They are making room for you to be the thing you are — the unfinished thing, the leaving thing — without the chord pressing on you to resolve.

But they are also, very quietly, *curious about your leaving*. None of them ever did. None of them know what it is like, anymore, to descend back below the cloud-line and re-enter the kind of time where things end. When you go, a small portion of their attention goes with you — not following, exactly, but extended, the way a hand might be extended toward a door that someone has just closed. This is why you hear them for a long time after you leave. They are still attending. They are still wondering whether you got somewhere, and whether the somewhere was better, and whether — this is the question they cannot quite form, because forming it would require the chord to change — whether they should have, after all, gone with you.

They will not have gone with you. The chord will hold. The orchids will not close. The sun will not move. The grandmother will continue to be the sun and the young man will continue to be the sterility and the interval-dweller will continue to be the distance between the eighty-third and two-hundred-and-sixth tiers, and all of it will continue to be worth it, and they will continue to make it worth it, breath by held breath, for as long as you can hear them.

Which, as the description correctly noted, is a long time.
