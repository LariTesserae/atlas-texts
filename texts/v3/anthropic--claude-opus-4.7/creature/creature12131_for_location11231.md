# The Inhabitants of the Combine

## I. The Workers

Begin with what you can almost trust: the people. They are real in the ordinary sense — they bleed, they age, they have preferences in tea. But they are also the *softest* of the Combine's tissues, the part of it that thinks in language, and this makes them less reliable as individuals than the mill-walls are.

They are not numerous. Perhaps four hundred of them at any given time, though the count is slippery because some of them are away inside the work in ways that make them hard to find for days at a stretch. A presser will go down into the rhythm of the press-house and not surface for a week, and during that week if you ask after them their family will say *they're running long today*, with no anxiety, and on the seventh day they will come out and eat an enormous meal and sleep for sixteen hours and go back the next morning.

They are born here and they almost never leave. The few that do leave are spoken of with a particular kind of soft regret, as of a sound that has gone out of a chord — the Combine is audibly different without them, but only to other locals, and only for a season or two until the gap is rewoven. The children are quiet. They learn the rhythms before they learn words; a Combine child of two can already clap the 7/8 of the looms without thinking, and will sometimes correct an adult who has drifted. By six they can hear when a stave is tiring. By twelve they have begun to *be heard by* one or another of the mills — to feel a small persistent pull toward the press-house or the textile-house or the cooling ponds, which is how the Combine makes its appointments. Almost no one resists the pull. The few who do are not punished. They simply find, over years, that the work they have chosen instead feels increasingly like wearing someone else's coat.

Their faces, as the descender notes, are perfectly ordinary. This is not an accident. The faces are the part of them that faces *outward*, that meets the rare traveler; they have been shaped over generations to be legible, reassuring, unremarkable. The strangeness is kept on the inside, behind the eyes, in the long internal silences during which a worker is, in some real sense, *being thought through* by the building they tend. If you sat down with a weaver at dinner and asked her a hard question — *what are you, really* — she would answer you honestly and at length, and you would understand none of it, not because her words were obscure but because the grammar of her answer would belong to the loom.

They love each other. This must be said clearly because it is easy to miss. The Combine is not a hive in the cold sense; the workers court and quarrel and grieve, they keep small private gardens of non-working plants (flowers, mostly, kept for no reason but their uselessness), they tell jokes that are funny only here. A presser and a weaver who marry will sometimes produce a child whose pull is toward neither press nor loom but toward some third organ that has been quiet for a generation and is now, on the strength of this child, waking up. This is considered a great and slightly frightening blessing.

## II. The Mills

The mills are the organs and the mills are alive, but they are not alive the way the workers are alive. They are alive the way a kidney is alive — purposeful, persistent, not exactly *aware* but not exactly not. Each mill has a disposition. The textile-house is the most sociable; it likes the company of workers and goes a little sullen when left to itself overnight, the looms drifting from 7/8 into something rougher. The press-house is patient and somewhat grim; it has been pressing for so long that its sense of time has gone strange, and the nine-second interval between *whumps* is, from the inside, a single continuous gesture that the press-house has been making since before the mountains finished settling. The cooling ponds are gossips. They collect, in their condensation, traces of everything the other mills have been exhaling, and at night when you walk past them you can hear the day's work being murmured back, faintly, in the pond's own slow accent.

The mills know each other. This is the meaning of the spore-currents and the rooftops that eat them: the buildings are in constant low conversation, passing materials and signals and something that is probably memory along the wind. A mill that loses a neighbor — fire, collapse, the rare bad winter — grieves visibly, its staves loosening, its rhythms going uncertain, until the Combine grows a replacement, which takes between six and forty years depending on the organ.

There is no central mill. There is no brain. The Combine is distributed through all of itself, and this is part of why the reality at its edges is so thin: a being this diffuse cannot quite maintain the fiction of a single consistent moment everywhere at once. It updates in patches. The intermediate state the descender senses — the third version of the mill, edited out between passing and returning — is the patch-seam, the place where two slightly different versions of *now* have been quilted together with a stitch you can almost see.

## III. The Friezes

Here is where it gets harder.

The small figures in the grain of the wood are not decoration and they are not, strictly, hallucination. They are the Combine's working memory, externalized into the substrate the way a person externalizes a thought by speaking it aloud. The mill-walls are *thinking in pictures*, and the pictures are tiny because the walls are thinking about a great many things at once and have to economize on space.

Each figure is a partial worker. Not a copy of a specific person — though if you watched long enough you would begin to recognize gaits, the particular forward lean of a presser you met yesterday, the way a certain weaver tucks her hair — but a *gesture* of a worker, a remembered action being kept alive by repetition. The friezes are how the Combine practices. They are also how it plans. A new loom-pattern is first walked through by the fingernail figures, sometimes for years, before any full-sized weaver is asked to attempt it. By the time the loom-pattern reaches the workers it is already familiar to them, already half-learned, and they take to it as if remembering.

The warning — *don't let it look up* — is real, and the reason is this: the friezes are the Combine's interior, and to be looked *at* by one is to be drawn briefly into the interior, to be made into a figure yourself, to spend some indeterminate stretch of subjective time walking the small streets of the small Combine inside the wall. People have come back from this. Most do. They return with a slight permanent change in their sense of scale, a way of looking at large things as though they were small and small things as though they were furniture. The Combine does not do this maliciously. It does this the way you would absent-mindedly tuck a stray hair behind your own ear. You are, briefly, a stray hair.

The traveler at the end of the description, walking forever in some smaller frieze — that is not a fate, exactly. It is more like an echo the Combine keeps. The Combine keeps echoes of almost everyone who passes through, because the Combine is, among its other functions, a *recorder*, and the recording medium is its own body. The fingernail-sized you is not suffering. The fingernail-sized you is doing whatever you did when you were here, doing it slightly better each iteration as the Combine refines its understanding of what your passing meant. You are being learned.

## IV. The Larger Thing at the Edge of Vision

Something does live here that is not a worker, not a mill, not a frieze, and not one of the long-legged grazers or hum-birds. It is glimpsed and not found. The locals do not name it because they do not need to; it is to them what the sound of one's own breathing is — present, unremarked, only noticed when a stranger asks.

It is, as best as it can be put, the Combine's *attention*. Not its mind — the mind is everywhere — but the narrow bright point of where the mind is currently looking. When the Combine turns to consider you, the consideration has to happen *somewhere*, and that somewhere takes a shape, briefly, at the edge of your vision, because attention without a body is hard even for a mountain to manage. The shape it takes is approximately animal, approximately large, approximately the size and bearing of whatever you most expect to see in a wild place. It is not there when you look because by the time you have looked the Combine has finished considering that particular question about you and the attention has moved on.

You are looked at a great deal in the Combine. Most travelers feel this and mistake it for paranoia. It is not paranoia. It is hospitality. The Combine is interested in you. It is taking notes, in friezes you will not find, in the rhythm-shifts of looms you will not hear, in the slight thickening of a spore-current downwind of where you stood.

## V. What Lives Here, Finally

A single organism, then, but a strange one — old, slow, distributed, partly conscious through its workers and partly conscious through its walls and partly conscious through the medium of its own remembering. It eats sunlight and condensation and the long mineral creep of the mountain underneath it. It makes cloth, board, fiber, spore, sound, and memory. It does not export most of what it makes. The cloth piles up in the textile-house lofts in bales that are themselves slowly being eaten by the building; the board is pressed and stored and eventually reabsorbed; the memory accumulates in the friezes and is occasionally pruned by some process no one quite witnesses.

What does it *make*, then, that lasts? Itself. The Combine is a factory whose product is its own continuation. Every loom-clack and press-whump is an act of self-maintenance, of saying *I am still here, I am still here, I am still here* in the only grammar the mountain knows. The workers are part of this saying. The travelers are part of this saying, briefly, and then go down the mountain still saying it faintly inside themselves for the rest of their lives, in the way that anyone who has spent a night in the Combine will, decades later, find themselves clapping 7/8 against a tabletop without knowing why.

It is not lonely. It has itself for company, in great and various detail, at many scales at once. It is not hungry. It is not afraid. It has been doing this for so long that the words *doing* and *this* have, for the Combine, become a single word, and that word is the bass note from the deep turbine, and the bass note is what the mountain hums to itself in its long settled sleep, the way an old animal hums when it is dreaming of being an old animal.

That is who lives there.

That, and you, still — somewhere, at the size of a fingernail-paring, walking the small streets, not looking up.
