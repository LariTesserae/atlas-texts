Something lives in the water.

Not a fish — you were right that there are no fish. Not an animal at all, in the sense you'd recognize. But the pool is twenty feet deep and has been held at bath-warmth and full light for a generation, turning over slowly and never freezing, never souring, never going still. A body of water like that is not empty. It only looks empty because what lives in it is the water.

Think of it this way. The four falls bring in rain that has touched the hill — limestone dust, pollen, the thinnest suspension of whatever the clouds carried. The nine oculi bring in light enough to drive any green thing that cared to try. The overflow carries out, continuously, whatever has grown too heavy or too numerous to belong. What remains, after a generation of this sorting, is whatever can live *exactly here*: in warmth that pulses daily, in light that comes in shafts and scatters, in a current too slow to call a current, in water that is always being gently replaced.

It is mostly invisible. If you cupped a handful and held it to one of the shafts you would see nothing — the water is clear, the builders saw to that. But it is not sterile. It is a slow soft suspension of the smallest green lives, single cells that have learned the rhythm of the room: rising toward the oculi in morning to drink the shafts, settling at noon when the light is too fierce, rising again in the long gold of afternoon. They are the reason the deep tiles look greenish. They are what feeds the algal felt at the waterline and the single ribbon-plant on the floor. They are too fine to see one at a time and too dispersed to see together, but they are what the pool *is*, and the pool is most of the room.

And because they are there, other things are there. A population of even smaller lives that eat them. A population, smaller still, that eats those. A ciliate, perhaps, that drifts on the slow circulation and has never in its line's history encountered anything larger than itself. Whole genealogies that fit in a thimble. When you lower your hand into the water the warmth that goes down past your wrist is full of them, and they register your hand as a passing weather, a shadow, a warmth-of-a-different-kind, and they are changed by it for a moment and then the pool forgets.

The ferns in the corners are inhabited too. In the damp silver undersides of those eleven fronds there are springtails, probably, and a mite or two, and a film of something that is not quite moss — the entire terrestrial fauna of the room, eleven fronds' worth, with everything that implies about how small a world can be and still be a world.

But if you mean: what *lives* here, in the way a hermit lives in a cell or an owl lives in a hollow — what presence keeps this room?

Then I think it is the room itself. I mean this carefully. A cistern that has roared and shone without pause for a generation, that breathes its daily cycle of cold-to-warm as reliably as lungs, that has nine shafts of attention pouring down into it and four voices speaking continuously and one voice answering at the drain — such a place is not quite inert. It is not conscious, I don't want to claim that. But it has a way it is, and the way it is, is very strong. It has had no interruptions. No silences to forget itself in. It has been *being this room*, without pause, loudly and brightly, for longer than most people stay anything.

The keeper who comes down the stairs with the copper dipper — the one whose ladder leans against the wall, whose scrub brush dries on the ledge — that keeper knows. You can tell by the plainness of the tools. There is no ornament, no offering-shelf, no carved name. A person who tended a room they thought was only a room would have added something by now, in a generation: a mark, a date, a saint's niche. The absence of those things is not neglect. It is a particular kind of respect. The keeper comes down, scrubs the waterline back when the green felt grows too thick, checks the wooden plug, dips a measure for whatever the cistern serves, and goes back up. They do not speak in the room. They would not be heard if they did. They have understood that the room is already full — of water, of light, of sound, of its own long unbroken being-itself — and that the correct relationship to such a place is to keep it working and otherwise leave it be.

So: what lives here.

A thin green suspension too fine to see. A felt of algae at the waterline. Eleven fronds of fern in two corners, and whatever rides on their undersides. A single ribbon of submerged plant, lifting and settling. And a room that has been roaring and shining without pause long enough to have become, in some sense I can't quite name, a tenant of itself.

And you, for as long as you stand on the ledge. The room accepts you the way it accepts a cloud passing over the hill: it dims a little around your shape, adjusts, and goes on.
