## The Inhabitants of Low Hour

They are people. That is the first thing to say, because the place invites you to expect otherwise — to expect something cave-adapted, pale and strange, something that has diverged. But they are people, recognizably, and if you sat with one at her work she would offer you tea from a chipped cup and ask after the weather above, which she has heard described and finds difficult to believe in.

Still. People shaped by the place are shaped.

---

**The look of them.**

Not pale, exactly — the lamps give enough light, over a lifetime, to put some color in a face — but a particular kind of pale: the pale of someone who has never been sunburnt, never been wind-chapped, whose skin has known only the soft constant cool. Their cheeks have a faint flush from the work and the wool. Their hands are remarkable. Everyone's hands are remarkable here. By twelve a child's palms are already mapped with the small white scars of chisel-slip and splinter and the burns of solder-work, and by forty those hands are instruments — thick at the knuckle, sensitive at the pad, capable of judging the thickness of a slate by feel alone, of finding a hairline crack in a cistern wall in the dark.

Their eyes adjust. A visitor moves through Low Hour squinting; the locals do not. Their pupils sit wide and dark in irises that have, over generations, drifted toward the lighter colors — pale grey, pale green, the occasional yellow — because the dim work better with what light there is. They see in the brown dark the way you see at dusk. They blink less often than you do, and when they meet your eyes the gaze is steady in a way that feels, at first, like challenge, and is not.

They are not tall. The ceilings discourage tallness; the ladders discourage it; the low doorways between district and district discourage it. A man of six feet would have to stoop a hundred times a day, and so over the long patient sorting of generations the tall ones either left, going up the stairs and not coming back, or simply did not thrive, did not breed, did not pass on the length of their bones. The people of Low Hour are compact. They move easily under beams that would brain you.

Their clothes: layered wool, undyed or dyed in the muted colors the local lichens give — a soft rust, a deep slate-blue, a green like the inside of an old copper pot. Over the wool, oiled canvas, dark with use. Every garment has pockets, and every pocket has something in it: a stub of chalk, a small iron tool, a folded square of waxed paper holding a few raisins or a pinch of salt. They wear soft leather shoes with thick felt soles, because the cobbles are wet and a hard heel would slip and a loud heel would foul the listening.

---

**The listening.**

This is the thing about them that takes longest to notice and is, once noticed, hardest to stop noticing.

They listen constantly. Not anxiously — not the way a hunted animal listens — but the way a musician in an orchestra listens, with the full background of attention always given to the sound around them. The drips tell them the hour, the weather above, the state of every cistern in earshot. A change in the note of a particular basin means that basin is filling, or has cracked, or has been moved. They know without thinking. A child of four can tell you which cistern on her street is the Widow Anneke's and which is the one the cooper put up last spring, by sound alone, the way a child of four where you come from can tell her mother's voice from a stranger's in the next room.

This means conversation in Low Hour has a different shape. They pause more. They leave silences that, to a visitor, feel weighted, expectant — but the silences are just listening, the ordinary listening that is the floor of their attention. When two locals talk in the street, they are also tracking the drum-pumps, the bell-walker's distant progress, the particular drip of the cistern overhead that means *someone has just drawn water above.* They know who is awake. They know who is working. The town speaks itself to them all day, and they answer it by going quiet enough to hear.

---

**Their work and their names.**

Each person is, generally, one thing: a cistern-keeper, a slate-setter, a cooper, a bell-walker, a drum-walker (though everyone takes a turn at the drums; the dedicated ones are simply those who take more turns), a baker, a tanner, a lamp-trimmer, an alchemist, a Sink-keeper. The trade is usually inherited, but not strictly — a child who shows the ear for cisterns may be apprenticed away from her parents' cooperage with no shame on either side. *The work finds the hand,* they say.

Their names are doubled. Each person has a given name — short, often a single syllable: Mar, Tev, Anneke, Bel, Joss — and a *use-name*, which is the name of their trade plus a locative. *Mar of the Third Drum.* *Tev of the South Slates.* *Anneke of the Cistern Above the Cooper's.* The use-name is the working name; the given name is for kin and lovers and the inside of the head. Visitors are usually told only the use-name, which is why travelers' accounts of Low Hour read like inventories.

There is one exception. The Sink-keepers do not give their use-names freely. They give the given name, and only the given name, and when pressed about the locative they say only *near the water.* This is old practice. No one quite remembers why.

---

**The Sink-keepers.**

Worth a paragraph of their own. There are never many of them — six, eight, sometimes ten — and they live in the lowest streets, in houses built against the wall of the basin itself, houses whose back rooms are stone and whose front rooms are timber and whose doors all face the black pool. They are not, the others are careful to say, *different.* They are people who took the work because someone had to, and the someone is usually the child of the last someone, though again, not always. Sometimes the Sink calls — this is the phrase used — and a young cooper or a young baker will one morning put down the tool and walk down to the lowest street and present themselves at one of the doors, and be taken in without question.

What they do: they keep the offerings. They watch the level of the pool. They listen to the deep slow drips and report to the council, monthly, on what those drips have been saying. They are courteous, slightly distracted hosts. They drink the same water everyone drinks but they prefer it from the Sink's own edge, taken in a small copper cup, and they do not press this preference on anyone else. Their eyes — and this is observed, this is not invented — are noticeably darker than the local average. Whether this is because the Sink calls a certain kind of eye, or because long looking at the black pool darkens the eye over years, no one has settled.

They are kind to children. Children are not afraid of them, which the parents find reassuring and also, occasionally, troubling.

---

**Their minds.**

They are patient in a way that is hard to describe to someone from a place with seasons. Their patience is not the patience of someone waiting for spring; there is no spring to wait for. It is the patience of someone who knows that the work is the work and the work does not end, and that hurrying ruins the joint, splits the slate, sours the solder. They build things expecting their grandchildren to repair them. They repair things their grandparents built. The time-sense is long and shallow, like the basin itself — not deep with anticipation, not sharp with deadline, just continuously present, continuously occupied.

They are not, by your standards, ambitious. No one in Low Hour is trying to become anything other than what they are. But within the work, they are *exacting* — the slate-setter who shaves a slate three times to get the lap right is not unusual; she is ordinary. The pride is in the fit. The pride is always in the fit.

They love. They marry, often late, often quietly; the ceremony is the exchange of a small made-thing, a cup or a tool or a folded square of metalwork, and the announcement of the pairing to the household and the nearest neighbors, and that is all. They have children, fewer than you might expect — the town does not grow much, has not needed to grow much, accretes inward rather than outward. They mourn their dead by carrying a stone to the Sink and dropping it in, and not speaking for the rest of that hour, however long the bells decide that hour to be.

They tell stories. The stories are mostly about the town — about a cistern that sang a true note for forty years and then cracked; about a bell-walker who walked the wrong sequence one evening and was gently corrected; about a child who opened a roped door and came back out three days later, unharmed, and would not say what she had seen, and grew up to be one of the steadiest slate-setters of her generation. The stories are not moral. They are simply what happened, or what is said to have happened, and the listener is trusted to take from them what the listener takes.

---

**What they believe.**

Not much, in the way of doctrine. They do not have priests. They have the bell-walkers, who set the hour, and the Sink-keepers, who watch the deep, and the alchemists, who explain things in language no one else fully understands and which most people regard with affectionate skepticism. There are no gods named.

But if you ask one of them, in a quiet moment, what they think the town *is* — what it is doing, what it is for — you may get an answer. The answers vary. A cooper once said: *The town is a cup, and we are what is being poured into it, slowly, and the pouring is also the cup.* A child said: *The town is making itself, and we help.* A Sink-keeper, asked the same question, looked at the questioner for a long time and then said: *Something is listening from below. We are the sound it likes.*

None of them, asked, expressed any wish to live anywhere else. The stairs go up. The stairs have always gone up. A few, in every generation, climb them and do not return, and this is spoken of without grief, the way you might speak of someone who has gone to live by the sea — a thing one might do, a thing most would not.

---

**The one last thing.**

When they sleep — three to a bed under heavy wool, in rooms whose walls drip softly into bowls placed for the purpose — they dream, they say, of water. Not of drowning. Of being water. Of moving through the seams of the town as the condensate moves, gathering on the underside of a beam, hanging, falling, joining a basin, joining a stream, joining at last the slow black weight of the Sink and turning over there in the dark with whatever turns there.

They wake from these dreams rested. They do not find them frightening. They find them, if anything, familiar — the way you find familiar the dream of walking down a hall in the house you grew up in.

This is who lives in Low Hour. People. Shaped by the cool and the drip and the long made dark. Listening. Fitting one stone to the next. Pouring themselves, slowly, into the cup that is also them.
