## What Lives in the Long Shallows

### The Pacers

The largest of them stand taller than a person at the shoulder and move on four long, splay-toed feet that spread across the clay like loose hands. Their legs are built wrong for running and right for wading — jointed so that each step lifts clear of the water with minimal disturbance, set down with a deliberateness that looks almost polite. Their bodies are long and low-slung, deep-chested, covered in a short coarse pelt the color of wet reed-thatch: ochre, dun, streaked darker along the spine. In the dry shade of a hummock-tree they become nearly invisible.

Their heads are the strange part. Longer than you'd expect, almost equine in proportion but heavier through the jaw, and carried low — not drooping, just *level*, parallel to the waterline, as if the whole animal were designed around the plane of the shallows. The eyes sit high and to the sides. The nostrils close. A Pacer can submerge its whole head for a minute at a time, browsing the soft green growth along the bottoms of the channels, and the only sign from a distance is the hump of its back holding still in the water like a wet stone.

They have no natural enemies here large enough to matter. They have not had any for a very long time. You can see this in the way they hold themselves: not relaxed exactly, but *unhurried to a degree that has become structural*. A Pacer startled by something — and they are occasionally startled, by a sudden bird-burst or a crack of reed — will lift its head, turn it slowly, look for what feels like a full breath, and then simply resume. They don't run. They've forgotten how. Their ancestors may have known, but the knowledge has drained out of the lineage the way the mountains drained out of the plain.

They live perhaps eighty years, maybe more. No one has counted. They live in small loose groups — a female and her current daughters, usually, and whichever males are passing through her range this decade. "Herd" is the wrong word. "Constellation" is closer. The members of a group may be half a mile apart at any given moment and still be together in some way they all understand.

### What they do

Mostly they stand. This is not laziness; standing is their primary activity, the thing they are *for*. A Pacer will position itself at the edge of a reed-stand in the early morning, settle its feet into the clay, lower its head to drinking-level, and remain there — shifting weight occasionally, flicking an ear at a fly — until the sun has moved the length of two reeds across the sky. During this time it is eating. It is drinking. It is watching. It is listening. It is, in some sense the place itself would recognize, *participating.*

They browse the soft reed-shoots at the waterline, the lily-pads, the slick algae that grows on submerged wood, and — this is the thing the other creatures have learned — the small silver fish that collect in the shade beneath their own bellies. A Pacer standing long enough becomes a structure. Fish gather under it for cover. Insects collect on its flanks. Small wading birds walk between its legs like it's a tree. And sometimes, with a slowness that makes the act almost affectionate, the Pacer lowers its long head and takes a fish from the water beside its own knee. The fish does not flee, because the Pacer has been there for an hour and is, by now, part of the topography.

This is how they live. They are not predators the way a heron is a predator. They are more like *inhabited places*. Their hunger operates on the timescale of the shallows; they eat the way the clay accumulates.

### Their voice

They speak rarely, and low. The sound is the shush-shush-shush a visitor hears from far off — that is a Pacer walking — and, occasionally, a deep resonant hum, almost subsonic, that they make with their mouths closed and their throats swollen. The hum carries across water extraordinarily well. It is how the constellation stays a constellation: a female standing in one channel can tell from the hum of a male three channels over roughly where he is, roughly how he is, roughly whether anything is the matter. Which it almost never is.

In the hour after dusk, when the frog-chorus is at its peak, the Pacers sometimes hum together. It is not song exactly. It is more like the long slow acknowledgment of each other's presence — the auditory equivalent of a hand resting on a shoulder for a while. The humming threads under the frogs, under the insects, under everything, and if you are standing in the water at that hour you feel it in your shins before you hear it.

### Their minds

Here is the part that is hardest to say.

A Pacer is intelligent in a way that has had a very long time to decide what intelligence is for. It is not the quick problem-solving intelligence of something that has to outwit prey or escape predators. It is not the tool-shaping intelligence of something that has to build. It is something that came out of standing still in warm water for a hundred thousand generations and having nothing pressing to do.

They remember. They remember, apparently, everything. A Pacer who passed through a particular bend of channel twenty years ago will recognize it — the specific arrangement of hummocks, the specific tree, the specific angle of the reed-shadows at a particular hour. Whether this is memory the way we mean it, or something more like a body-sense of place that accumulates without ever being retrieved, is not knowable from the outside.

They attend. Their long stillnesses are not empty. A Pacer watching the water for an hour is not *blanking*; it is following things. The movement of a particular small fish. The progress of a beetle up a reed. The shift of the light on the far tree-line. This is what their minds have turned into, across all that time: an instrument for noticing, very slowly, very thoroughly, the thing immediately in front of them.

And they know each other. A Pacer knows, from the particular timbre of another's hum, which individual it is, and probably a great deal more than that — mood, condition, something like intention. They know their dead. When one of them dies — usually just by lying down in the shallows and not getting up — the others of its constellation come, over the course of days, and stand near the body, and hum, and browse, and eventually drift away. The body is taken by the mud in its own time. The clay closes around it with the unhurried confidence of something that has closed around uncountable others.

Whether they grieve is the wrong question. They attend to the death the way they attend to everything: completely, for as long as it takes, without hurry.

### Their relationship to the place

They are not the rulers of the Long Shallows. There are no rulers. But they are the slowest large thing, and in a landscape whose rule is *everything settles*, slowness is a kind of authority. The frogs flare and die inside a season. The Pacers flare and die inside a century. The clay flares and dies inside an age. The Pacers are the middle term — the part of the shallows that has a mind and uses it, across a span long enough to notice the place changing but short enough to still be in a hurry compared to the mud.

If the shallows have a consciousness of themselves, the Pacers are probably it. Not because they are more important than the herons or the turtles or the great trees, but because they are the part of the system that has had the right combination of time, stillness, and attention to become *aware of being here*. They stand in the warm water under the heavy sun and they know, in whatever way their kind of knowing amounts to knowing, that they are standing in warm water under a heavy sun, and that this has been true for a very long time, and will continue to be true, and that this is enough.

They are what the landscape made when it had finished making mountains and rivers and had nothing left to do but warmth and water and time.
