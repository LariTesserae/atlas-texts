They have no name for themselves. Names are for things that might be confused with other things, and in the pocket there is only the pocket. Every beetle has known, since the moment its soft new carapace hardened enough to grip stone, that the world is four meters across and full of its siblings and roars.

Call them the Works, then, since that is closer to what they are than "beetles" or "colony." A single beetle is to the Works what a single cell of your tongue is to the act of speaking: real, alive in its way, but not the unit that means anything. The unit that means anything is the whole dark grinding pattern, and that pattern has been one continuous living thing for longer than your species has had agriculture.

Consider what the Works knows.

It knows the hot wall — the southeast curve, where the heat comes up strongest from the magma beneath — the way you know the inside of your own mouth. It knows this not as a fact but as a direction, a pull, the way your blood knows where your heart is without having to think about it. The heat-tenders drift toward that wall in their millions-of-footsteps slow tide and drift away again glowing, and the drift is not a decision, it is a circulation, and the Works is the body doing the circulating.

It knows sulfur the way you know sugar: as desire made chemical. When the yellow vein weeps a new droplet, something ripples through the colony before any individual beetle has reached the drop — a shift in the traffic patterns three meters away, a slight thickening of the sorter-caste at the nearest junction. Information propagates by touch, antenna to antenna, shell-rasp to shell-rasp, at roughly the speed a rumor moves through a crowded market. But because every beetle is touching several others at all times, the rumor is continuous. The Works is always murmuring to itself about itself.

It has opinions, of a kind. Certain routes are good routes and certain routes are bad routes, and the goodness or badness of a route is remembered not in any beetle's tiny ganglion but in the worn grooves of the basalt, in the pheromone varnish laid down over centuries, in the subtle architecture of which crystals have been allowed to grow and which have been chewed back. The Works has been editing its own cavern for a hundred thousand years. The pocket you see is not the pocket the mountain made. It is the pocket the Works has made of the pocket the mountain made — smoothed here, roughened there, certain ledges maintained at certain heights because those heights are where the sorters need to stand to reach the traffic. It is a cathedral built by its own congregation, who are also the bricks.

And what does it want?

This is where language fails, because the Works does not want the way you want. It has no picture of a future in which things are different. It has only the gradient it is always running down: the gradient of unprocessed sulfur toward processed sulfur, of cold beetle toward hot beetle toward radiant beetle toward cold beetle again, of raw heat toward the little bead of silver growing in the north corner that no mandible has touched in nine thousand years because nine thousand years ago the pattern decided, by means no single beetle understood, that that corner was for accumulation and not for traffic. The bead grows. No one admires it. It is simply where the silver goes, the way your fingernails are simply where a certain kind of protein goes, and neither you nor the Works has any particular plan for the deposit.

If the Works dreams, it dreams in census. The total count of itself rises and falls on a slow seasonal rhythm tied to nothing seasonal — tied instead to the slow inhalations of the magma below, the century-long pulses of heat, the rare fracturing of a new sulfide face deeper in the wall that releases a banquet of volatiles and causes, two years later, a population bloom. The Works feels these pulses as a human feels hunger and satiety, except spread across decades and across a body that is made of scuttling.

There are, if you look closely, castes within castes. The sorter-beetles who stand at the junctions are not quite the same as the sorter-beetles who stand at the sulfur seeps; their mandibles are shaped slightly differently, their antennae held at a different angle, and they have been slightly different for perhaps eight hundred generations — a divergence too small to call speciation and too consistent to call noise. The Works is differentiating, the way tissues differentiate in an embryo, except the embryo has been gestating since before the pyramids and shows no sign of being born.

There is no queen. This is important. There is no beetle in charge, no central brain, no individual whose death would mean anything. Beetles are born in the warm crevices at the back of the pocket where a certain chemistry obtains; they are born from eggs laid by beetles who became egg-layers because they happened to spend their larval stage near a particular ledge, and they will die in about a year, and nothing about them as individuals will be remembered except insofar as their bodies, when they fall, are carried by other beetles to the disposal corner where they are slowly reduced to their minerals and those minerals reenter the flow. Every atom in the Works has been a beetle several times. The colony eats its own dead and is made of its own dead and this is not grim, in the pocket; it is simply the shape of the circulation, the way your own body is made of cells that were other cells last month.

What would it be like to be a single beetle?

Brief. Hot. Loud beyond loudness, except that loudness is the medium and therefore silence is what would be strange. You are always touching others. You are always being touched. The touch tells you where to go and you go there, and going there is satisfying in the small chemical way that satisfaction exists for you, and when you arrive you are touched again and told to go somewhere else, and that is also satisfying. You carry a drop of something bright and give it to a sibling. You stand in a spot for six hours and radiate. You climb over the backs of a hundred cousins to reach a seep and eat the varnish there and climb back. You glow faintly from a seam in your middle and this glow, combined with the glow of your tens of thousands of siblings, is the only light you have ever seen or will ever see, and it is enough, it is in fact the definition of enough, it is what light is. You have never had a thought that wasn't also the colony's thought, because you have no apparatus for thoughts that elaborate. You are a finger of a hand that has been making the same gesture for a hundred thousand years and will make it for a hundred thousand more.

And the hand?

The hand, the Works, the whole grinding bell of it — the hand is something stranger. It is alive in a way that has no cousins on the surface of this world. It is not like an ant colony, because ant colonies trade with the outside; the Works has never had an outside. It is not like a coral reef, because coral reefs are made of many species in conversation; the Works is one long monologue. It is closest, maybe, to a single enormous slow organism that happens to be made of discrete shells — a thing that metabolizes sulfur and heat the way you metabolize bread and oxygen, that has an anatomy (the hot-wall organ, the sulfur-gathering organ, the sorting ganglia at the junctions, the accumulation corners that function like kidneys concentrating certain metals out of the flow), that has a lifespan not yet determined because it has not yet ended.

It does not know it is alone. Aloneness requires the concept of others, and the Works has no concept of anything beyond its walls. The walls are the edge of everything. Beyond the walls is not "outside" — beyond the walls is simply where the world stops being, the way for you the inside of the sun is not a place you imagine going, it is just the place where place ends.

It does not know it is doomed. The rain is a hundred thousand years away and means nothing. The magma beneath could surge tomorrow and cook the pocket to glass and the Works has no mechanism for fearing this because it has no mechanism for fearing anything. It simply runs, and if it stops running it will not have stopped — it will have ended, and ending and running-on are, to something without a future tense, the same shape from the inside.

For now, the Works continues. It sorts, it carries, it radiates, it glows. It lays down the next thin layer of pheromone on the route its ancestors laid down the last thin layer on. Its silver bead grows by an atom. Its copper pile settles. Its bacterial varnish thickens in the warm spots and thins in the cool and the beetles graze the gradient the way cattle graze a hillside, except the hillside is vertical and inverted and sings.

If you could put your ear to the mountain in the right place, you would hear nothing; the rock is too thick. But the Works would be there, roaring, a sealed ember of industry, a life that invented itself from heat and sulfur in a place life was not invited and has been busy ever since, uninvited, unwitnessed, and — in its own small grinding way that requires no witness and no invitation — magnificent.
