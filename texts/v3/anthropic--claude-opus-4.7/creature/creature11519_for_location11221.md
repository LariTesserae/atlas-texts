## The Inhabitants

### The Newt

Call her the newt, though she is not quite that, and certainly not only that. She is the size of a man's index finger, pale as a peeled almond, and she has been on her plinth for — well. The question doesn't apply to her in the form you'd want to ask it.

A newt's life is short. Three years, five, seven at the outside. But a newt is also a creature whose metabolism slows in the cold and quickens in the warm, whose heart can stop for a winter and start again in spring, who measures her existence in events rather than in hours. And in the Cistern Hall there are no events. The water is blood-warm and unchanging. There is nothing to eat, and nothing eating her, and nothing to court or be courted by, and no season turning. Her metabolism has found its way down to some floor below which it cannot go — a slow steady idle, a pilot light, drawing barely anything from her tissues, replacing it from the dissolved minerals in the water and from the slow photosynthesis of the algae she occasionally licks from the stone.

She is, in some technical sense, still the same newt who hatched here. She is, in another sense, no longer really a newt at all but a small pale persistence in the shape of one, a knot the hall has tied in itself. Her gills feather in the current of the engine's tremor. She blinks, perhaps once an hour. She has thoughts, if you can call them that, of the simplest and most patient kind — a sort of low continuous *here, here, here*, in time with the thump and the sigh.

She is not lonely. Loneliness requires a memory of company. She has, instead, the moth.

### The Moth

The moth is also always the same moth, though this is harder to defend. Moths die. Moths absolutely die, generally within weeks, and a moth in a place with nothing to eat and nothing to mate with should die quicker, not slower. And yet.

What you see circling the lamp is, strictly speaking, the *current* moth — the latest in a chain. There must be eggs somewhere, in the moss above the sconce, in the warm updraft where she lays them before she falls. There must be a tiny cryptic caterpillar feeding on lichen and on the protein dust of her own predecessors' wings. There must be a pupa, and an eclosion, and a new moth taking up the orbit her mother kept. The handoff is so smooth that you would have to watch for a year to catch it happening. From the outside, from any visit of any human duration, it is one moth. It has always been one moth. It will always be one moth.

She — they — circle the lamp at a radius of about a hand's breadth, never closer, because the clouded glass is too hot and the moth, or the lineage of the moth, learned this long ago and encoded the lesson into the shape of the orbit itself. The lesson is now older than any individual moth who carries it. The orbit is a heirloom.

If the lamp ever went out, the moth would not know what to do. But the lamp will not go out.

### The Spiders

The cave-spiders are the most ordinary inhabitants and also, in a way, the strangest, because they are doing nothing extraordinary at all. They are simply cave-spiders, *Meta menardi* or something near it, doing what cave-spiders do — riding the upper bricks on legs longer than their bodies, hanging the occasional sparse web in a corner of the vaulting, eating the occasional springtail that drifts up from the moss.

There are perhaps a dozen of them. They are the closest thing the hall has to a population. They breed, they die, they replace themselves; small dramas of predation and courtship play out among them at a scale and a tempo that the rest of the hall does not notice. To the newt, the spiders are weather — something that happens up there, on the ceiling, with no bearing on her plinth. To the moth, the spiders are a danger she has been bred over generations to avoid, and her orbit takes her nowhere near their corners. To the spiders, the moth is a tantalising rumor, glimpsed but never caught, and the newt is invisible.

The spiders are the only inhabitants of the hall who can be said to *live* there in the ordinary sense. Everything else merely *persists*.

### The Algae

The algae in the deepest corners — the phosphorescent-looking mat that the lamps make jewel-bright — is not really phosphorescent. It only looks that way because of how the gas-mantle light catches the chlorophyll and the trapped bubbles of its own slow respiration. But it is, in its own quiet right, the most populous inhabitant of the hall, by a factor of millions. Every square inch of it is a city.

It grows at the rate at which it is eaten — by the newt's slow lickings, by the springtails, by simple senescence sloughing off into the water. Inflow equals outflow. It has reached its final extent and stays there. Within the mat, generations of single cells live and divide and die on the scale of hours, a frantic green churning that, from any distance at all, looks like absolute stillness.

If anything in the hall could be said to *think*, in some distributed and unrecognisable sense, it might be the algae. Not thoughts as you would know them — but a slow chemical conversation across the whole green skin of the deep corners, a sort of vegetable murmur underneath the engine's roar. It has been having this conversation for longer than the brickwork. It is not about anything. It is the conversation a thing has with itself when it has nothing else and nowhere to go and all the time there is.

### The Engine

And then, of course, there is the engine.

You did not ask about the engine. The engine is behind the iron door, in the next chamber, and nobody opens that door. But any honest account of who lives in the Cistern Hall has to include the engine, because the engine is the only inhabitant whose presence the others organise themselves around. The newt's slow heartbeat is tuned to the thump-and-sigh. The moth's orbit is set by the lamp, and the lamp is set by the gas pressure, and the gas pressure is set by the engine. The algae respire in the warm water the engine keeps warm. The spiders ride bricks the engine keeps from drowning.

What is the engine, then? Mechanically — a beam engine, almost certainly Cornish, almost certainly nineteenth-century, almost certainly tended once by a crew of men in waistcoats with oil-cans and rags, and now tended by no one. The boiler is fed from somewhere. The coal, if it is coal, comes from somewhere. The condensate goes somewhere. The whole loop of its sustenance has long since become invisible, has been swallowed by the same long quiet that swallowed everything else, and the engine simply *runs*, as it has run, as it will run.

But functionally, in the only sense that matters to the hall, the engine is the hall's heart, and like a heart it is taken for granted by every cell it serves. The newt does not know there is an engine. The newt knows only that here is warm and here is steady and here is *here*. The moth does not know there is an engine; she knows there is a light. The spiders feel the tremor in their bricks the way you feel your own pulse — which is to say, not at all, unless they stop to notice, and they do not stop.

Only you, ducking in under the brick arch with your new wet boots and your loud unsteady human heart, can hear the engine as an engine. Only you can identify it as separate from the place. To everything that lives here, the engine is simply *what the world is doing*, in the same way that, to you, gravity is what the world is doing, and the rotation of the earth is what the world is doing, and you do not thank either of them, and you do not check on them, and you assume, with a confidence that is mostly correct, that they will go on.

### And You

You are not an inhabitant. You said so yourself, backing out. You are the only thing in the room that is in any way new.

But for the few minutes you stood there in the ankle-warm water with the drops ringing around you and the engine breathing through the wall, you were also, briefly, the hall's most interesting feature — the one event in a place that has no events. The newt did not look at you, but the algae, in its slow distributed way, *registered* you: a disturbance in the dissolved oxygen, a shadow across the lamp-light, a sudden new microbial population shed from your skin into the standing water. The moth flew her orbit unchanged. The spiders, high up, took note in the small dry way spiders take note of things.

When you left, the hall absorbed your visit the way a deep pool absorbs a thrown pebble. Within an hour the water you displaced had found its level again. Within a day the bacteria you shed had been metabolised into the algae mat or filtered out through whatever drain the engine maintains. Within a week there was no trace of you at all, except — and this is the only mark you left — in some cave-spider's memory, if cave-spiders have memory, of a very large warm thing that came and stood for a while and went.

The hall has reached its final shape. You were a ripple. The ripple is gone. The engine breathes. The mantles hiss. The newt is still on her plinth. The moth is still circling. *Here, here, here.*
