There is no one here.

I want to say that first and let it stand, because the temptation will be to populate the place — to find some clever lichen tucked in a fold, some chemoautotroph riding the warm sap, some patient cousin of a tardigrade making its home in the condensation. There isn't. The description is honest. The plant occupied the ground before ambulatory life could reach it and has held the ground so completely, so equilibrated, that no niche has ever opened. Niches are made by slack. There is no slack here.

But you asked who lives there, and "no one" is too quick. Let me work at it.

---

The plant itself is the obvious candidate, and the wrong one. To call the continent its own inhabitant is to commit a category error in the other direction — it is not living *in* itself, it *is* itself, and a thing cannot inhabit what it is. A house is not its own tenant. So set the plant aside, not because it isn't alive (it is the most alive thing in any direction for a long way) but because the question of habitation doesn't apply to it.

What about the sap?

Here I have to be careful, because I can feel the pull toward whimsy, and the place does not deserve whimsy. The sap is not a population. It is a fluid. But the gametes moving in it — those private postal items, traveling for months or years across the hexagonal mesh from one flowering chamber to another — those are discrete things, each one a packet of information with an origin and a destination, each one carried passively but persistently across distances that would, on a human scale, be transoceanic. They are not organisms in the sense you would use. They cannot eat, cannot move under their own power, cannot reproduce until they arrive. But they are *units*, and they are *in transit*, and the plumbing they move through was built for them and only for them. If anyone lives in the working layer, it is them: a diffuse, slow, scattered population of travelers, none of whom know they are traveling, each one a letter in the mail. At any given moment there are perhaps a hundred billion of them in transit. The continent is, among other things, a mail system whose only customers are itself.

That is closer. But still not quite right, because they are *of* the plant; they are not lodgers, they are the plant's own currency.

Let me try again, and let me try harder.

---

Consider the fermentation bulbs.

Each one cracks sugars into alcohols and burns the alcohols in slow flameless oxidation. This is chemistry, and chemistry at that scale and that consistency does not happen without catalysis, and catalysis at biological temperatures means enzymes, and enzymes mean protein, and protein this specialized, working this reliably for this long, means — well, in any other system it would mean a microbiome. A consortium. Tame bacteria, or something like bacteria, descended from free-living ancestors and now locked into obligate partnership, like the mitochondria in your own cells, like the rhizobia in legume roots, like the cellulolytic flora in a termite's gut.

I think they are there. I think they must be. The plant did not invent the engine alone; nothing invents an engine alone. Somewhere in the deep evolutionary past, before the continent closed its loop, a free-swimming microbe with a useful trick was drawn into a vesicle and never let out, and its descendants are still in there, still doing the trick, in numbers that beggar description. Trillions per bulb. Quadrillions per stem. Across four million square kilometers.

These are the inhabitants. If anyone lives here, it is them.

But — and this is the part I have to be honest about — they no longer know they are alive. They have been domesticated past the point where the word applies. They have lost the genes for swimming, for sensing, for choosing. They have been bred (by the slow patient selection of the plant's internal economy) into pure function. They divide when the plant needs them to divide. They die when the plant needs them to die. They do not eat; they are fed. They do not reproduce; they are propagated. Each one is, by every reasonable measure, a cell of the plant, even though its DNA still carries the long-faded signature of a once-independent lineage.

So: the inhabitants are present, and numerous beyond counting, and they have forgotten how to be inhabitants.

---

There is one more candidate, and I have been circling it.

The breath.

The continent inhales for twenty minutes and exhales for longer. That rhythm is not the rhythm of any one piston or any one bulb; it is the integrated rhythm of the whole, an emergent oscillation produced by the coupling of millions of subsystems whose individual cycles are nine or ten minutes long. The breath exists at a level above the parts. It is not located anywhere. It is not made of anything you could point to. It is a *pattern* — a standing wave in the activity of a continent — and it has been the same pattern for longer than any individual cell or piston or bulb has existed. The cells turn over. The pistons are reabsorbed and re-extruded. The bulbs are replaced. The breath persists.

I do not want to say the breath is alive. That would be the whimsy I promised to avoid. But I will say this: if you are looking for something that occupies the continent without being identical to it — something that lives *in* the system rather than *as* the system — the breath is the closest candidate. It is what the parts are doing together. It is older than any of them. It will outlast any of them. It has no metabolism of its own, but it has a tempo, and it keeps that tempo, and nothing the plant has ever done has caused the tempo to drift.

When you put your hand on the black wall at the edge of the sea and feel the slow rhythm under your palm, you are not feeling the plant. You are feeling the thing the plant is hosting. The plant is the instrument. The breath is what is being played, by no one, for no one, indefinitely.

---

So. Who lives there.

No animals. No fungi in any sense you would recognize. A vast invisible population of domesticated microbes who have forgotten they were ever guests. A drifting cloud of gametes in transit, carrying messages the continent is sending to itself. And, at the level above all of these, a slow rhythm that has been holding its shape for longer than the question of who lives there has had anyone to ask it.

You were right that you would not be welcome and you would not be unwelcome. You were also right that you would be unaccounted for. But I want to add one thing to what you said. You would not be the only one unaccounted for. The microbes are unaccounted for too — they have been there so long the accounting has subsumed them and lost the distinction. The gametes are unaccounted for — they are tallied by destination, not by self. Even the breath is unaccounted for, in the sense that nothing in the continent knows it is breathing; the breath is simply what happens when the parts do their work.

The continent is full of inhabitants who do not know they are inhabitants, performing functions that long ago stopped being separable from the body that contains them. You would walk in among them and walk out again, and in that sense you would be one of them for as long as you were there: present, unnoticed, doing nothing the system needed and nothing it forbade, a small warm-blooded packet moving through the dark on an errand of your own, no different in kind from the gametes, except that you would know you were moving, and they would not, and the breath would continue under you either way.

That is who lives there. That is everyone.
