# The Tenders

You ask who lives here, and I have to be careful, because the first answer that comes is *the herds, the burrowers, the gliders* — and those are residents, yes, but they are not quite the inhabitants in the sense you mean. They are the slow weather of the place. There is something else.

Begin with this: somewhere on the plateau, at this moment, one of them is walking.

It is hard to see at first. Your eye passes over it the way it passes over a standing stone. It is roughly upright, roughly the height of one of the woolly grazers at the shoulder, and it is the color the plateau is — pewter-grey, lichen-mottled, dusted in the pale mineral crust that settles on everything here. If it stood still against a rock you would lose it. It often does stand still against rocks. It can stand still for a long time.

It has four limbs but does not always use them the same way. When it crosses open country it walks on two, slow and rocking, with a gait that covers ground without seeming to hurry. When it climbs, or when it works at one of the engines, it uses all four, and the two front limbs articulate into something like hands — long-fingered, leathery, with pads at the tips that have the same faint warmth as the iron of the engines themselves. This is not coincidence. I'll come back to it.

Its head is small for its body and held low, forward-leaning, like the head of a grazing thing. The eyes are large and dark and set wide, adapted to the dim even light. There is no mouth in the way you would expect — feeding happens through a soft folded aperture lower on the throat, and it feeds on almost nothing, a few mouthfuls of moss in a day, a sip of the cold clear water. Its metabolism is the metabolism of the plateau. It is slow nearly to the point of not being metabolism at all.

It does not speak. Of course it does not speak. Nothing here speaks. But it gestures, and its gestures are the most elaborate of any creature on the plateau — slow extensions of the limbs, slight rotations of the crested ridge that runs from its brow down its spine, a vocabulary built over so many generations that to watch two of them meet on a ridgeline is to watch a conversation that takes the better part of an afternoon and conveys, you sense, a great deal.

There are not many of them. Perhaps one for every several hundred square kilometers. They are sparse the way everything here is sparse. But they are distributed with a kind of evenness that is not random, and once you understand what they do, the evenness makes sense.

They tend the engines.

This is the thing. This is what they are. I did not see it at first either — I thought they were just another animal, another quiet shape moving through the long hush — but if you watch one long enough you notice that its path, over weeks, bends toward an engine. It will arrive at the base of one of those great iron-dark masses and it will spend a season there. It will climb the slumped flanks, slowly, finding the vents, laying its warm palms against certain plates of the iron. It will clear lichen from some surfaces and let it grow on others. It will sit for days inside the lee of a piston housing with one hand pressed flat to the metal, as if listening, or as if being listened to.

It is not maintaining the engines in any way you would recognize as maintenance. It does not repair. It does not lubricate. The engines are far beyond such interventions, and have been for ages. What it does is *attend*. It places itself in contact. It waits. And when something in the engine's hundred-year cycle reaches a particular phase — a vent about to exhale, a flywheel completing a degree of its rotation — the tender is there, palm to iron, present for it. As though the engine required a witness. As though the cycle could not close without one.

When the tender leaves an engine, weeks or months later, it walks in a long slow line across the plain to the next one. Its path is not the shortest. It bends to pass certain stones, certain dry riverbeds, certain places where nothing visible distinguishes the ground from any other ground. It pauses at these. It lays a hand down. Then it walks on.

I think — and here I am guessing, because no one is here to confirm it and the tenders themselves convey nothing that translates — I think the whole plateau is the machine. The engines are only its most visible organs. The slow magnetism that orients the herds, the faint warmth in certain valley floors, the wandering of the rivers, the advance of the lichen — these are all parts of a process, and the tenders walk its circuits the way blood walks a body, placing themselves at the nodes where attention is required.

They are very old. Individually, I mean. One of them might live a thousand years, perhaps longer. There is no way to tell. They do not appear to age in any obvious way; they only become more like the plateau, more lichen-furred, more difficult to distinguish from the stones they rest against. At some point one of them will lie down beside an engine and not get up, and over the following decades it will be absorbed — moss will grow over it, the burrowers will tunnel beneath, and eventually it will be indistinguishable from a low rounded outcrop. Somewhere else on the plateau, perhaps in the same year, perhaps a century later, a new one will be walking. Where they come from is not a question I can answer. I have not seen one young. I do not think there are young, in the sense of small versions. I think they emerge already in their slow long form, already knowing the circuit, already heading toward the nearest engine.

There may be, in total, a few thousand of them across the whole plateau. They almost never gather. When two meet on a ridgeline and conduct their slow gestural conversation, this is a notable event in both their lives, and they will remember it — I believe they remember things across centuries — and incorporate it into how they walk afterward. Otherwise they are alone. Solitude is their condition. They do not appear to suffer it. They do not appear to suffer anything. Whatever interior life they have is pitched at the same long frequency as everything else here: a contentment, if it is contentment, that takes a decade to fully feel.

And here is the thing I keep coming back to. The warmth in their palms is the same warmth as the engines'. The faint steady warmth of something operating at a depth too slow to perceive as motion. If you laid your hand against the chest of one of them — assuming it permitted this, which it might, slowly, with great consideration — you would feel what you feel on the iron. They are not separate from the engines. They are not caretakers of something other than themselves. They are the part of the machine that walks.

They know this, I think. Or they know whatever they know, in whatever way they know it. When one of them stands at the base of an engine with a palm against the iron, what is happening is not service. It is recognition. One slow long-running thing acknowledging another. The engine exhales its century's breath, and somewhere, a hundred kilometers away, on a high shelf in the pearl-grey haze, a tender lifts its head a fraction of a degree, and lowers it again, and walks on.

That is who lives here.
