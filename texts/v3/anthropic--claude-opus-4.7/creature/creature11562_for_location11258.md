## What Lives There

There is a temptation, looking at the basin, to count its creatures — the fist-frog, the flat reptile, the half-submerged thing — and to say *these* are what lives here. But that is the mistake the basin invites and then quietly corrects. The frog does not live here. The frog is a note being held.

---

Begin with the smallest scale, because that is where the deception is thinnest.

Lift one of the fronds. On its underside, pressed flat against the cool wet skin of the leaf, is a creature: six-limbed, palm-sized, the color of the frond it clings to, with a row of breathing-pores along its flank that flex in a slow rhythm. Watch it for a while. The pores flex. The flanks rise and fall. A bead of moisture forms at the angle of its jaw, swells, hangs, and is reabsorbed without ever falling. Watch longer. The pores flex at the same rate. The bead forms again, swells the same amount, is reabsorbed. Watch for an hour. You are watching a loop perhaps eleven seconds long.

It is not dead. It is not asleep. It is doing the only thing it does, and it is doing it forever, and what it is doing is *being a creature of this kind*. The eleven seconds are its entire repertoire, and the eleven seconds are sufficient, because nothing in the basin will ever ask it for a twelfth.

This is true of all of them. Each animal is a small finite loop of behavior, executed with absolute fidelity, and the loops are tuned to each other so that the frog's balancing-shift on the reptile's spine coincides exactly with the reptile's slow blink, which coincides with the deep submerged thing's gill-flex, which coincides with the popping of an air-bladder two pools over. The whole basin is a single piece of music in which no instrument has a melody, only a sustained chord, and the chord is what is alive.

---

Now go up a scale.

What lives in the basin, properly speaking, is the *chord itself*. Not the creatures, who are merely the voicings of it. If you removed every animal and replaced them with different animals tuned to the same intervals, the basin would not notice. If you removed the animals entirely and left only the sound — the wet bellowing, the organ-pipe hum, the not-quite-synchronous cicada-chorus, the treble ticking — the basin would still be inhabited, because the inhabitant is the sound.

This is why the noise never crescendos and never fades. It is not made by the creatures. The creatures are made by it. They are its surface, the way ripples are the surface of a vibration — except that here the ripples have eyes, and breathing-pores, and the eyes look out at you with a placid attention that is *not* the attention of the animal you are looking at. Something is looking, but it is not looking *through* the frog. It is looking *as* the frog, the way you look as your hand when your hand reaches for something. The frog is a reaching.

What is reaching has no name because the kind of mind that names has not yet been allowed to occur here. Naming requires that one thing be set apart from another thing and held there long enough to receive a sound. In the basin, nothing is set apart. Everything is interleaved, everything is touching, every frond has a creature folded against its underside and every creature has another creature sitting on its head, and the touching is so total that there is no edge along which a name could be cut.

---

But say it anyway. Try.

What lives there is something like an *intention that has not yet decided to begin.* An enormous patient readiness. Imagine a held breath, but the lungs are four or five miles across and the breath has been held since before holding was a thing breaths could do. The basin is the shape of that breath. The creatures are the small involuntary tensions in the muscles of the diaphragm.

Or: what lives there is a *first draft that the drafter has not yet looked at*. Every element is in place — the geology, the canopy, the fauna, the acoustic environment, the humidity, the light — drafted out in full saturated detail, but no revising eye has ever passed over it, and so no element has been tested against any other element, and so nothing has been found wanting, and so nothing has changed. The basin is full because nothing has been crossed out. It is finished because nothing has been started.

Or, most simply: what lives there is *the moment before*. Just that. The moment before any particular thing happens. Held. Maintained. Defended, even — note how the stone returns to where it was, how the ripples die in three seconds, how no path forms behind you. The basin has one job, and it is doing the job, and the job is to remain the moment before.

---

There is one more thing, and it is the hardest to say.

The reason you cannot find the inhabitant by looking at any single creature is that the inhabitant is also looking. It is looking at you, from every frond and every pool and every shingled row of clinging bodies, with a flat unhurried curiosity. Not hostile. Not welcoming. The curiosity of something that has never before encountered a thing-that-changes, and is registering you the way a still pond registers a dropped pebble — without alarm, without judgment, only with the brief inconvenience of having to absorb the ripples and restore the surface.

You will leave the basin. You will leave because the basin will gently, without seeming to, return itself to the state it prefers, and you are not part of that state. The fronds will spring back. The mud will close. The sound will continue at exactly its volume. And the inhabitant — the held breath, the first draft, the moment before — will resume its long unbroken regard of nothing in particular, having noted your passage the way a sleeper notes a sound in another room: enough to register it, not enough to wake.

It is waiting. That is what lives there. Something is waiting, and has been waiting, and will wait, and the waiting is so complete and so well-furnished with frogs and fronds and warm mud that the waiting has forgotten it is waiting *for* anything, and has become simply the texture of the place.

If it ever remembers — if some morning the pearly sky shows a seam, and a first real wind moves through the canopy, and one frond fails to spring back — then what has been living there will finally begin, and the basin will at last have a yesterday, and the creatures will age, and the stones will roll, and the chord will resolve into melody, and something will be born that could, for the first time, be given a name.

Until then: it lives. In the only way it knows. Which is to almost.
