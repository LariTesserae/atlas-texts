# The Inhabitants of Ossara

They are called, among themselves, the **Kept** — though the word in their own humming is closer to *those-who-were-grown-a-place*, or *those-the-ground-did-not-refuse*. They do not think of themselves as the plateau's owners, or even its tenants. They understand, with a clarity that has sunk into their bones over many thousands of years, that they are a courtesy the substrate extends. A courtesy can be withdrawn. It has not been, yet.

---

**Body.**

Imagine a person drawn thin and then drawn thin again. An adult Kept stands perhaps a meter and three-quarters tall and weighs what a child would weigh in a denser world. The hollowing of their bones is not uniform — the long bones of the arms and legs are nearly reed-like, the ribs fluted and finely chambered, but the skull retains some density, a small dim resonator around the brain. Their sternum is the instrument. It is broad, slightly convex, and finely grained on the inner surface in a pattern no two Kept share, the way no two human fingerprints are shared. They hum into it from below, through the throat, and the sternum shapes the note and releases it not through the mouth but through the whole chest wall, forward, outward, down into whatever they are standing on.

Their skin is the pale color of the inside of a turnip, faintly luminous in the lavender dimness, and marked along the flanks and the insides of the arms by thin parallel grooves — not veins, not scars, but channels, which fill with a warmth during certain feelings and go cold during others. You can, if you know how to look, read the mood of a Kept the way you might read a thermometer. They find this unremarkable. They find the opacity of offworld faces bewildering, even frightening, the way one might find a sealed box bewildering if one had grown up in a glass-walled house.

Their eyes are large, set wide, and adapted to the permanent dusk — black-pupiled, with a silver ring at the iris that contracts visibly in the presence of the silver pillars, as if recognizing a relative. Their hair, when they have it, is the pale yellow of the dusk-pollen, and they do not cut it; it grows slowly and stops on its own.

They have six fingers on each hand. Two of them, the outermost, are vestigial and used chiefly for playing the inner surfaces of their own instruments — the sternum, yes, but also a secondary resonator that sits at the base of the throat and produces higher harmonics. A Kept speaking to another Kept at close range will sometimes unconsciously drum the outer fingers against their own collarbone, adding overtones. It reads, to them, the way eye contact reads to us.

They live a long time. Four, five hundred years is ordinary. There are Kept alive in Ossara now who remember the making of certain doorways, and who will, if asked (and if the hum finds you), describe the shape of the hand that did the coaxing, and note with mild disapproval that the hand was impatient.

---

**Mind.**

The long delay of their speech has produced a kind of mind that does not, strictly, finish thoughts before beginning to voice them. A Kept will begin a sentence with full knowledge that the response to it will arrive possibly after they themselves have forgotten precisely what they said. They compose in the manner of people writing letters to descendants. Every utterance is slightly formal, slightly valedictory, slightly more complete than the moment requires, because it may be all the interlocutor ever receives of this particular thread.

They do not interrupt. They cannot, really — the substrate sorts their voices by origin and carries each one to its addressee along its own slow path, and two Kept humming simultaneously at opposite ends of the plaza are not in competition, only in parallel. A young Kept learning to converse learns not turn-taking but *layering*: how to begin a thought that will land after another thought, how to shape a hum so it harmonizes with what will still be in the air when it arrives.

This has consequences. Arguments in Ossara unfold over days. Decisions over seasons. A grudge can be active for a century before either party has received the full statement of the other's grievance, and by then the grudge itself has usually become a kind of shared possession, something both parties tend together, neither wanting to lose it.

They are not, however, slow of wit. Inside their own skulls their thinking moves at ordinary speeds, or faster. It is only the transmission that is patient. A Kept listening for a reply will, in the interval, think through whole architectures of possible answers, pre-feel all of them, and be ready to receive whichever one the ground delivers. They are prodigious daydreamers. They are superb at chess-like games, which they play by humming moves into the substrate and waiting, and they have invented perhaps a dozen games that no shorter-lived species could tolerate, in which the opening moves are made by one's grandparents and the endgame by one's grandchildren.

---

**Social life.**

There are not many of them. Perhaps three thousand across all of Ossara, which means most dwellings stand empty, attended by no one, slowly enlarged by the substrate regardless. The Kept do not find this sad. They walk through the empty rooms sometimes, hum softly, and note what the ground has added since their last visit. A room that has grown an alcove is a room that has had an idea. They treat these ideas with respectful curiosity, the way a gardener treats a volunteer seedling — not always welcome, not always moved, but acknowledged.

They pair, but loosely, and for long intervals rather than for life. A Kept may have three or four partners across their centuries, with gaps of decades between. Children are rare and much-attended; a birth in Ossara is felt across the plateau, because the mother's hum changes pitch for the weeks around it, and the substrate carries the altered note everywhere. Every Kept alive knows, without being told, when a child has come. They pause in whatever they are doing. They send, each in their own time, a single low note of welcome. The child, still wet, receives over the first hour of its life a slow-arriving chord of several thousand voices, each one a person saying *I am here, and now so are you*.

They have no central authority. Decisions that affect the town are made by what they call *long listening* — a process in which a question is hummed into the ground and left there, and the Kept go about their lives, and over weeks or months the substrate returns a kind of averaged answer, a note that is the superposition of every Kept's half-conscious response to the question. They trust this process. It has served them. They understand that it is not really democracy, because the substrate is one of the voices, and the substrate's vote is larger than theirs. This does not trouble them. The substrate has been here longer.

---

**Work.**

They do not build, as I said. They coax, but rarely now — most of what they need is already grown. They tend. A Kept's day is mostly tending: walking certain routes at certain hours to keep the paths hummed-open (an unused path will slowly grain over, and a long-unused one will disappear); pressing their palms to the bases of the silver pillars at certain intervals, which the pillars seem to require, though no one remembers why; walking out to the reed-hare herds and standing among them, simply standing, which the herds expect, and without which they will drift.

They do not hunt the reed-hares. They *receive* them. When a reed-hare is old, and its resonance has begun to flatten, it will detach from the herd and walk toward Ossara, and find a particular Kept — not any Kept, a specific one, chosen by a process neither party controls — and lie down at that Kept's feet. The Kept then sings the reed-hare down. This takes about an hour. The creature's body, afterward, is taken into a chamber the town has grown for this purpose, and over the following days it is eaten, shared widely, every Kept receiving a small portion. The hollow resonant shell of the body is kept. The walls of certain older buildings in Ossara are lined with reed-hare shells, layered and overlapping, and the buildings themselves sing slightly different notes as a result, and are prized, and inherited carefully.

They do not tend the great sovereign trees. The great trees tend themselves, and occasionally tend the Kept. There is a tree at the western edge of the plateau — barrel-shaped, breathing — whose name in Ossara is a hum I will transliterate as *Aummon*, which means, roughly, *the one who remembers our grandmothers*. Aummon, every few decades, releases a pollen of its own, heavier than the dusk-pollen, which drifts into Ossara on the night the release occurs and settles on the sleeping Kept, and the Kept wake the next morning having dreamed, all of them, the same dream — a dream, they say, of a Kept long dead, going about some ordinary task. They do not know why Aummon does this. They think perhaps Aummon is lonely, or perhaps Aummon is correcting something in them that they cannot perceive. They are grateful either way. When Aummon eventually dies — which will not be soon — they do not know what they will do.

---

**What they know and do not know.**

They do not know what the silver pillars are. They know the pillars must be touched, and they touch them.

They do not know what the threads in the air carry. They know not to walk through a thread when it is visible, and they do not.

They do not know what occludes the sky. They have a word for it — *Iole* — which is not a name but a placeholder, a sound one makes in place of a name one does not have. They look up at Iole sometimes, briefly, and then look down. Children ask, once, what Iole is. They are told: *Iole is what is above*. They accept this. Later, as adults, they understand that the answer was not evasion; it was the complete answer available.

They know the plateau is alive, and they know it has moods, and they know — this is the one hard knowledge — that it has, in the deep past, withdrawn its courtesy from others. There are regions of the plateau, far from Ossara, where the end-grain runs in tight knotted whorls and the reed-hares will not graze and the tall-steppers will not cross. The Kept call these the *shut places*. They do not go there. They teach their children not to go there. They do not speak of what is shut in them, because they do not know, only that something was once there and is no longer, and the ground keeps the shape of its refusal.

---

**At dusk.**

Stand at the edge of Ossara at dusk. A Kept is standing a few meters from you — you did not notice them arrive, because they made no sound above the general resonance. They are watching the reed-hares settle. Their flank-channels are warm; you can see the faint luminescence shift along their side. They are, in their own slow way, glad.

They hum, once, low, into their sternum. The note travels down through the soles of their narrow feet into the wood-ground, and disperses, and will find — somewhere across the plateau, in minutes or hours — the one they meant it for, who will receive it, and understand, and in their own time hum back.

The Kept beside you does not look at you. Looking is not how they greet. But the grooves along the arm nearest you warm very slightly, and a note you feel rather than hear passes up through your feet, and you understand, without being told, that you have been noticed, and that the plateau has been informed of you, and that, for now, the courtesy extends.

You are welcome. Provisionally. For no time at all, by local reckoning.

Which is to say: long enough.
