# The Tenders

They are tall, and they are slow, and their slowness is not weakness but a kind of attunement — the way a musician is slow to interrupt another's phrase. Stand one beside a visitor from the surface (if such a visitor could be imagined, which mostly they cannot) and you would see a person of ordinary proportions but stretched, drawn upward as if by long patient gravity. Their limbs are longer than yours. Their fingers especially: long, with an extra joint, ending in nails that are not nails but something more like the pale horn of a hoof, kept filed smooth so as not to score the wood when they touch it. They touch the wood constantly. It is how they think.

Their skin is the color of the inner bark of a young root — a warm pale tan with a faint pinkness beneath it, more visible at the wrists and the hollow of the throat where the sap-warmth shows through. They do not tan, because there is no sun; they do not pale further, because the amber light has already done its work over generations. A child born in the deep workshops where the light runs redder will have a faintly redder cast all their life, and other Tenders can tell at a glance where someone was raised. It is not a matter of class. It is a matter of which light first found them.

Their eyes are large and dark and set a little wider than you would expect, and the pupils are slow to change size — they live in steady light, and the muscles that adjust for brightness have softened in them across many generations. Quick changes of illumination make them squint and reach for a wall. In compensation, their hearing is acute past the human range. They can tell a stranger's footfall from a neighbor's at fifty meters down a curving gallery. They can hear, they claim, the difference between a healthy sap-pulse and a sluggish one by pressing an ear to the wall — and the claim is borne out: their physicians do exactly this, and are not often wrong.

They have no body hair to speak of. The hair on their heads is fine and pale and grows slowly, and is usually kept long and bound back, because cutting it feels wasteful in a place where nothing is wasted. Old Tenders have hair to the waist, gone the color of old heartwood. The young keep theirs braided with strips of fiber-cloth in patterns that mean things — which gallery they were born in, which workshop they apprentice to, whether they are courting, whether they are mourning. A stranger from a far district can be read at a glance, if you know how to look.

---

They are quiet. This is the first thing anyone would notice. Not silent — they speak, they laugh, they argue, they sing at certain festivals — but the baseline volume of a Tender is low, and they find loud voices physically uncomfortable, the way you might find a too-bright light. Children are loud and are not scolded for it, only gently corrected, the way you correct a posture. By adolescence the voice has settled. Adult Tenders converse at what a surface-dweller would call a murmur, and they carry these murmurs across long galleries by some trick of the wood, which seems to favor them, to ferry their words along its grain.

Their language has the features you would expect and some you would not. It has the word for *sap-line stopped* and uses it rarely. It has separate words for the wood's growing, the wood's settling, the wood's reclaiming, and the wood's listening — four verbs where another tongue might have one. It has no word for *sky*, though it has a word for *the green that comes through*, which is used as a kind of mild blessing, the way one might say *good morning* without quite meaning the morning. It has a grammatical mood reserved for statements about the wood's intentions, which is neither indicative nor subjunctive but something between: a *negotiated* mood, used when one is making a claim about a thing one cannot fully know but must act with. Architects speak in this mood almost constantly. Children acquire it late and imperfectly, and one of the markers of adulthood is using it well.

They eat the beetles, and the bracket-fungi, and a dense pale bread made from pressed inner bark and bound with sap-curd. They eat sparingly. They are not a hungry people. The metabolism of a Tender runs cool, in keeping with the cellar-temperature of their world, and they can go a long day on a single meal without complaint. They are slow to put on weight and slow to lose it. They live, on average, longer than you would expect — a hundred and twenty years is not remarkable, and the oldest among them, the *deep-listeners*, have been known to reach a hundred and sixty before they go quiet.

---

What do they *do*, beyond the work of keeping the city? They tend, which is their name for themselves and also their occupation. Every Tender tends something. The beetle-drovers tend the herds. The architects tend the growing walls. The sap-graders tend the seven fluids. The fungus-keepers tend the lamps. The listeners-of-listeners — a small caste, rarely spoken of — tend the small grey wall-things, though no one will quite say what this tending consists of.

There are also tenders of less material things. Tenders of memory, who walk the closed-over districts and recite, in low voices, the names of the families who lived there, the trades that were practiced, the songs that were sung. Tenders of disagreement, who are called in when two galleries cannot resolve a dispute about a shared causeway, and who do not judge but rather sit with both parties for as many days as it takes, listening, until the dispute has worn itself smooth. Tenders of the newly arrived — meaning the newly born, since no one arrives from elsewhere — who spend the first year of a child's life simply present with that child, observing, taking notes in a small fiber-bound book, building the document that will follow the child through their education and tell their teachers, eventually, what kind of attention this particular person needs.

It is a culture of attention. This is perhaps the simplest way to put it. They are a people who have learned, over a long time and at the cost of certain other possibilities, to pay close and patient attention to slow things. They do not produce art that is dramatic. Their music is built of small intervals and long durations. Their stories have almost no plot in the surface sense — they are mostly descriptions, very precise, of how a thing changed across a generation. *The Account of the Northern Bend*, which any child can recite portions of, is two hundred pages about how a single corridor migrated four meters east over ninety years, and what the people in it learned, and what they lost.

---

They love each other in the way you would expect of such a people: slowly, attentively, with long courtships and few divorces and a tolerance for the kinds of differences that more hurried cultures cannot sit with. They have several arrangements of family beyond the pair-bond, including a kind of trio-household which is common in the workshops and a looser communal arrangement which is common in the high galleries. Children are raised by whoever is near and capable, with the biological parents holding a particular but not exclusive role. A child without parents is uncommon and is not pitied — they are simply tended by the gallery, as anything in the gallery is tended.

They die quietly. When a Tender knows they are near the end, they often go to a particular kind of small chamber, grown for the purpose, set in the older roots of their home district. There they lie down, and family comes and sits with them, and the wood — slowly, over the following years, long after the body has been returned to the bark-drifts — closes the chamber around the place where they lay. Every district has many such closures in its walls, marked with small inscriptions, and walking past them is not somber but ordinary. The dead are part of the slow weather too.

---

And here is the thing about them, the thing that is hardest to convey: they do not feel trapped. A surface-dweller, told about this life, imagines a kind of beautiful imprisonment — the lowering ceilings, the closing corridors, the absence of sky. But the Tenders do not experience their world as a constraint. They experience it as a partner. The wood is growing; they are growing; the city is the conversation between these growths. To leave would be unthinkable not because it is forbidden but because it would be like leaving a sentence half-spoken, like walking away from someone you are in the middle of understanding.

When, very rarely, a young Tender is overcome with what they call *the upward longing* and climbs through the high galleries pressing their ear and then their palm and then their cheek against the amber ceiling, trying to hear more of the wind in the leaves — the elders do not stop them. They sit with them. They tend the longing the way they tend everything else. And usually, after some weeks or months, the young one comes back down into the working light, and takes up their tending again, and is not ashamed, and is not changed exactly, but has added the wind to the long list of things they are listening to.

They are listening, always. That is finally what they are. A people shaped to the long attention of a thing that is paying long attention back. The wood notices them. They notice the wood. Between these two noticings, a city grows and closes and grows again, and the amber light comes down, and the *thup* of the valves keeps its slow rain, and somewhere far above, presumably, a forest stands in ordinary sun, not knowing — or perhaps knowing, in its own slow way — what lives in its roots.
