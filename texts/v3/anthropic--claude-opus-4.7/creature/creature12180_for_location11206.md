# The Wallwrights

They are there. You did not see them because you were not looking at the right scale, and because they were not moving while you watched. They almost never move while anything watches. But they are there, and the place is theirs, and the work is theirs, and has been since before the lowest course was laid.

Picture, first, something like a person. Not very like. Start with a person and take away the warmth, take away the hurry, take away most of the weight. What remains is tall and narrow and pale, the pale of wet limestone, with skin that is not skin but a fine close-fitted rind of the same chalky stone that drinks the water and gives it back. Where a person would have hair there is nothing; where a person would have eyes there are two shallow wet depressions that hold a meniscus of water flush with the rim, like the terraces in miniature, and that is how they see — by what the held water shows them, by the sky caught in their own face. When one of them tilts its head the little basins of its eyes tilt too, and for a moment you can watch a cloud cross its gaze before the surface settles.

They have hands. The hands matter. The hands are the most finished part of them, long and many-jointed and harder than the rest, with fingertips worn to a particular angle from a particular labor. A wallwright's hand can find the one place on a rough block where a tap will split it true. A wallwright's hand can lay a stone into its bed and know, without measuring, whether the water in the basin above will sit a hair higher or lower for that stone's being there. They have been doing this for so long that the knowing is no longer in the head, if there is a head in the sense you mean; it is in the joint of the thumb, in the pad of the palm.

They are slow. This is the first true thing to understand. They are slow the way the building is slow. A wallwright at work on a stone may take a day to consider it before the first chisel-stroke, walking around it, laying a palm against it, sitting beside it through a night to feel how it gives up the day's heat. The stroke, when it comes, is one stroke. Then there may be another day before the next. To watch one of them work for an hour is to watch almost nothing; to come back in a year is to find a wall.

Their patience is not virtue. It is what they are made of, in the way you are made of haste and forgetting. They do not experience the wait as waiting. A wallwright setting a sill stone and adjusting it by the breadth of a fingernail, then waiting through a full season to see how the winter freeze settles the course before adjusting it again — this is not endurance for them. This is the texture of being alive.

There are not many of them. I have tried, walking the wall-tops, to count, and I think perhaps a few hundred across the whole flank of the mountain, though I may be wrong by an order in either direction; they are difficult to see when still, and they are almost always still. They stand in the basins, often, waist-deep or shoulder-deep in the bright water, and the water laps exactly level with some particular ring or seam on their bodies, and you understand after a while that they are themselves the gauges. The precision of the terraces — the impossible levelness, basin to basin to basin across a whole rank — is achieved because the wallwrights stand in the water and feel where it touches them and know, by an ache somewhere in the chest or the throat, that this basin is a hair high and that one is a hair low, and they go and they adjust a sill by the width of a chip and they come back and stand again, and the mountain comes truer.

They do not eat, as far as I can tell. They drink, in a sense — they stand in the water and the water enters them through the chalky rind and leaves them again as a slow weeping down the legs, the same weeping that the walls do. They are, in this, of a piece with the architecture. You could call them the moving part of the building. You could call the building the still part of them.

They speak rarely, and when they speak it is not by voice. A wallwright wishing to tell another wallwright something will set a small stone in a particular place on a wall-top, or let a single drop fall from a fingertip into a particular basin at a particular hour, and the ripples, or the placement, carry the meaning. Across the mountain a slow conversation is always running, made of nudged stones and timed drops, propagating from terrace to terrace at the speed of attention. A sentence may take a week to complete. The sentences are mostly about the work. *This stone is shifting. The third sill in the eighth rank is a finger's-breadth high. The frost came early to the north face.* Sometimes, I think, the sentences are about other things — about the sky, about a particular quality of the light on a particular afternoon — but I cannot read those, and the wallwrights who set them down are not, I think, expecting to be read by anything but each other and the mountain.

They were not born. None of them were born. This is the part I came to last and am least sure of, but I believe it: a wallwright is made, slowly, by the others, out of the same materials as the terraces. Somewhere on the upper slopes there is — must be — a place where a new one is being shaped, course by course, the way a wall is shaped, and where after some number of decades it will be finished and will open its small water-eyes and step down from its scaffolding and begin, without instruction, to work. The ones who made it will by then be very old, and some will have begun to soften at the edges, their rinds going to travertine, their joints filling with mineral, until at last one of them stops in the middle of a basin and does not move again and is, after a further long while, simply part of the wall it was tending. I have seen, I think, one or two of these. A standing stone in a basin, slightly the wrong shape for a stone. The water level with a seam that used to be a collarbone.

So the question of who built the terraces and the question of who lives in them are the same question, and the answer is the same answer, and it has no name because the wallwrights do not name themselves and there is no one else to name them. They built it. They are building it. They will, at some rate that is not our rate, finish it, and when they finish it the last wallwright will step into the highest basin and stand there until the rind takes, and the mountain will be a mirror with a single stone tooth at its crown, and the tooth will be the last of them, looking up.

That is who lives here. That, and the pale film on the submerged stones, which is perhaps something they tend and perhaps something that tends them; I could not tell. The wallwrights were not telling. The wallwrights were standing in the water, being level, and the sky was lying on their faces, and somewhere down the slope a stone was being moved by the width of a chip, and the work was going on.
