## The People of Oss

They are not many. If you counted them, which someone once did and which no one has felt the need to do since, there are something like four hundred — adults, children, the old — distributed across the forty terraces in a density that feels, to them, correct. Not crowded. Not lonely. Each household knows every other household by sight, and most by name, though names are used sparingly, the way one uses a tool one does not want to wear out.

Their bodies are the bodies of mountain people: not tall, compact, with the deep chests of those born to thin air. Skin the color of the pale granite after rain — a warm grey-tan that takes sun without darkening much. Hair dark, usually, and worn long by both sexes, bound back with cord or left loose depending on the work. Eyes that have the slight narrowing of people who look at distances; the opposite wall of the cleft is always there to be looked at, and they look at it without particularly meaning to, many times a day. Their hands are very capable. The fingers of even a young child can fit a stone into a wall so that it will not move, and will know, by feel, which of two similar stones is the one wanted.

They do not age as we age. This took me some time to understand, and they do not speak of it because it is not, for them, remarkable. A child is born; it grows; it reaches something like adulthood, perhaps in twenty years, perhaps in thirty — and then it stops. Not frozen. Not unchanging in the way the blue-green leaf is unchanging. They still move, still learn things (within the narrow band of things there is to learn here), still form attachments, still eventually die. But the stopping-place of the body is wide and flat, like the flat bedrock the river runs over. A woman who was grown when I arrived will still be grown when I leave, and the child she is nursing will, in due course, also be grown, and the two of them will walk the terraces together looking, to a stranger, like sisters of some indeterminate age. The oldest among them — and there are a few — show their years not in the face, particularly, but in a certain transparency, as if the person were becoming slightly less opaque to the light, a little more of the wall behind them visible through their shoulders. When one of these very old ones dies, it is not dramatic. They become gradually more visible-through, and then one day they are not there, and their house is cleaned, and someone else lives in it, or no one does, for a while. There is no grief in the way we have grief. There is a quiet, a dropping of the voice for some days, a laying of a fruit at the empty shrine.

They reproduce, but rarely. A child is a considered thing. It seems to be understood, without any rule about it, that the numbers of Oss should hold, and so pregnancies occur roughly as deaths do, and the population keeps its shape. A woman who wants a child speaks to her partner, and they speak to no one else about it particularly, and in some months or some years there is a child. The mechanism by which this timing works is not discussed. It may not be known. It may simply happen, the way the fruit ripens.

Their work, as you have guessed, is not urgent, but it is not trivial either. They garden. They tend the channels, clearing moss from the grooves when it thickens enough to slow the flow, though the moss grows back at the same rate and the work is never finished and never pressing. They weave — cloth of a wool-like fiber from the goats, dyed in three colors: the undyed cream, a soft blue from some root, a rust-red from some bark. Garments are simple, loose, belted, and last generations; a tunic made by a grandmother is worn by a grandchild and is still not worn out, because nothing wears out here in the way we mean. They cook. They eat together in small groupings of houses, three or four families sharing an evening meal in rotation, the rotation being ancient and unspoken. They make music, occasionally, on a kind of long-necked three-stringed instrument and on small clay pipes, and the music is slow, modal, without climax. Songs have verses that can be added to indefinitely, and are, by whoever feels like adding one, and no song is ever really finished or really begun — one sits down and joins the song that was always there, and stands up and leaves it going.

They tell stories, but the stories are odd. They are almost all descriptions. *There is a pool below the third fall where the water turns in a circle before continuing. In the pool lives a fish with a pale belly. The fish is old. The pool is cold even at midday.* This is a complete story, to them, and a good one. Stories with events — someone did something, and then something happened, and then something else — are rare and treated with a faint suspicion, as if they were a kind of boasting or a kind of lying. A few such stories exist. They concern, mostly, the time before Oss, which is understood to have existed but is not dwelt on. *The rock was cut. We came here. We built the walls.* This is the whole of their origin story, offered without elaboration, and offered only if asked. Who cut the rock; where they came from; who built the first wall: these are not questions that interest them, and they answer, if pressed, with a shrug that is not dismissive but simply truthful. They do not know. They do not think it is the sort of thing that can be known.

They are kind. This needs saying because it would be easy to imagine them as blank or affectless, and they are not. They are kind in the particular way that people are kind when they have enough time — which is to say, they notice. A child who is sad is noticed, and sat with, not hurried out of the sadness but accompanied through it. An old person who has become too transparent to carry water has their water carried, without fuss, by whoever is going that way. A stranger — and strangers are very rare, but I was one, and there have been others — is given a house that happens to be empty, and food, and is not asked to explain themselves. They will answer questions, if the stranger asks them, but they will not ask questions back. It is not incuriosity exactly. It is that they do not believe the stranger's answers would change anything here, and they are right.

And yet.

There is something in them, or under them, that I want to try to describe, because it is the real thing about the people of Oss and I am not sure I can get it in words.

They know. Not all of them, not consciously, not in a way they would say out loud — but they know that this is not how a world is. They know, the way one knows a word one has forgotten, that something is being held. That the stopping of the fruit at ripeness, the stopping of the moon at three-quarters, the stopping of their own bodies at some plateau of adulthood, is a stopping and not a nature. In the oldest ones, the transparent ones, this knowledge is nearer the surface. They will sometimes stand at the edge of a terrace and look down the valley, south, where the cleft continues and eventually, presumably, opens onto something else, and the look on their faces is not longing, exactly, but a kind of recognition. As if they were remembering that there is a south. That the cleft goes somewhere. That rock, elsewhere, weathers.

The shrine on the lowest terrace is, I came to think, the place where this knowledge is put. Not worshipped. Not addressed. Just — set down, the way you set down a stone you have been carrying. The offerings are not to anything. They are of something. Each fruit, each pebble, each bead is a small admission: *I know. I will not say it. Here is a thing from my hand to mark that I know.* And the shrine holds these admissions, and does not rot them or release them, because nothing here rots or is released, and so the shrine has been filling, without filling, for as long as Oss has been Oss.

What they are, then — the people of Oss — are people who live inside a held breath. They are the breath. They do not know whose breath it is, or why it is being held, or when it will be let out. They suspect it will be let out. The rawness of the rock suggests it. The faint transparency of the old suggests it. Some mornings, when the light comes over the eastern wall at a particular angle, there is a feeling in the valley — I felt it, and I think they feel it, though they would not name it — that the whole place is provisional. That it was made, and is being kept, and that keeping is a verb that requires a keeper, and that the keeper is tired, perhaps, or distracted, or simply will at some point turn their attention elsewhere.

When that happens, the people of Oss will, I think, be ready. Not eager. Not afraid. Ready in the way they are ready for everything: by already being where they are, doing what they do, fitting the stones.

The thrushes will sing their two notes. The river will applaud, softly. The moon will finish waning at last. And on the lowest terrace, the shrine will let go of everything it has been holding, all at once, a small bright avalanche of fruit and pebbles and beads, finally permitted to fall.
