## The Inhabitants of the Roostworks

They are not two populations. This is the first thing to understand, and the hardest, because everything about the basin presents itself as a partnership — birds above, workers below, the crust between them as commerce. But a partnership requires two parties, and there are not two parties here. There is one long process that wears two kinds of body.

### The Birds

Begin with them because there are more of them, though "more" is a category that may not entirely apply.

A pigeon in the Roostworks, lifted gently from a railing and held in the hand, weighs perhaps a third of what a pigeon should weigh. Not because it is starved — its breast is full, its feathers dense and oil-slick — but because what fills it is wrong. Under the feathers the skin is warm in the way a kettle is warm, not in the way an animal is warm. The heart, if you could feel for it, beats at a rate that does not vary with handling or fear. It beats at the rate of the slow pistons in the third foundry. It has always beaten at that rate.

The pupils are the giveaway, but only the most obvious one. Look longer and you find: a starling whose iridescence, in certain light, resolves into the same oil-on-water pattern that forms on the cooling reservoirs. A jackdaw whose claws, when it grips a wire, settle into grooves worn for exactly those claws, on exactly that wire, by something that has been gripping there for a very long time without ever quite being the same bird twice. A rook that, if startled, will open its beak and produce not a caw but the small clean tick of a relief valve seating itself.

They do not breed because they do not need to. They do not die, mostly, because dying is a thing finite creatures do to make room, and there is no room being made or unmade here. When one does fall — and they do fall, occasionally, in ways that look like accident — it is light in the hand because the substance has gone out of it. What's left is the husk of the form, the shape the substance was wearing. The substance itself has rejoined the larger circulation, which is to say it has gone into the crust, which is to say it has gone into the foundries, which is to say it will come back out wearing feathers again before long. The husks are gathered quietly by the workers and are not discussed.

The larger silent things on the stacks are older. This is not a matter of years — years are not the unit — but of consolidation. They are what happens when the substance, instead of distributing itself into a hundred small bright bodies, concentrates. They have the rough shape of birds but the proportions are subtly off, the heads too still, the eyes set forward like something that hunts thought rather than mice. They do not roost so much as preside. There are perhaps forty of them in the whole basin. They have names, but the names are not spoken aloud, only tilted toward.

### The Workers

Now the harder part.

The workers wear oilcloth and flat hats and move between the belt-houses and the foundries at the pace of work that has always been done and will always be done. Under the hats: faces. Ordinary faces, more or less. A foreman has a moustache going grey. A girl in the sorting shed has a burn-scar on her wrist from a ladle. They eat — there are canteens, with long benches, and a thin stew of root vegetables that arrive on the rail. They sleep — there are dormitories, with iron-frame beds, and the sleep is dreamless and exactly eight hours. They speak to each other, joke sometimes, complain about the damp, complain about the crust getting into the seams of boots.

They are also part of the process. Not in a sinister sense — there is no sinister sense available here, sinister requires an outside to be sinister *toward* — but in the same sense that the birds are. The workers are the substance wearing a different shape, one suited to the parts of the work that require hands. The birds make the crust; the workers refine the crust; the refined substance returns to the birds; the birds make more crust. The hands are necessary because the foundries need hands. So there are hands. So there are people attached to the hands. So there are lives attached to the people, lives complete with burn-scars and moustaches and small private opinions about the stew.

If you asked the girl in the sorting shed whether she was a person, she would look at you as though you had asked whether water was wet, and she would be right to. She is a person in every way a person can be tested for. She has a childhood she can describe — a mother, a small room above a belt-house, the first time she was allowed to climb to the high catwalk. The childhood is real. It happened. It is also the case that the basin has always had exactly the number of workers it needs, and that when one of them grows too old to work, they go quiet in their bed one night, and the next shift there is a new young one in the dormitory who has always been there, with a childhood of their own, equally real, and the older workers remember teaching them their first tasks, and this memory is also real, and none of this is a contradiction inside the basin, only outside it.

The foremen who speak to the silent things on the stacks are the workers in whom the substance runs closest to the surface. They have worked the longest — longest being, again, not quite a duration — and the boundary in them has thinned. In the evening shift, when the bird-sound goes contemplative, a foreman will climb a ladder partway up a stack and stand on a maintenance platform and look up at one of the silent ones, and the silent one will look down, and they will exchange something. It is not language. It is closer to the way two parts of the same body coordinate a movement without consulting each other. The foreman comes back down and adjusts a valve, or reassigns a crew, or simply stands a moment longer in the doorway of the foundry before going in, and the work continues, very slightly better fitted to itself than before.

### What Lives Here, Then

A single organism, distributed. It has chosen, or fallen into, or always been, a configuration in which part of itself flies and part of itself works and part of itself rusts slowly on the railings and part of itself moves humming through the gutters in the middle of the streets. It maintains itself by circulating substance through these parts. The crust is the medium of circulation. The foundries are organs. The birds are organs. The workers are organs. The mist is the breath. The pale working light is the steady attention the thing pays to itself.

It is not lonely, because loneliness requires a sense of other, and it has not encountered an other in a very long time, perhaps ever. It is not happy or unhappy. It is occupied. It is doing the thing it does, which is to persist, in this shape, in this basin, at this temperature, at this rate.

What it makes in the foundries — what leaves on the rail in crates, what comes back as coal and iron and oil — is, as best as can be reconstructed from outside, the substance of its own continuity, traded with something further out that needs that substance and pays in raw materials. The basin is, in this sense, a creature that has worked out a trade with the world. The world sends it fuel. It sends the world a little of what it is. Neither side has ever met the other in any form that could be called a meeting. The crates simply go, and the crates simply come, and the rail line climbs the south lip and disappears into mist that, from inside the basin, has no other side.

If a visitor stays past the second day — which few do, because by the second day the sound has become weather and the weather has begun to feel like attention — they may notice that they are being learned. Not watched, exactly. Learned. The birds along their usual walking route begin to be arranged slightly differently. The foreman they nod to at the foundry gate begins to nod back a fraction earlier. The mist parts for them in the same places each morning. The basin is making room. It is a hospitable thing, in its way. It has room for more hands, if hands are wanted. It has always had room.

Most visitors leave on the third day. The ones who don't are not, afterwards, quite visitors. They are workers who have always been here, with childhoods they can describe in detail, and the older workers remember teaching them their first tasks, and the basin's count of itself has not changed, because the basin has always had exactly the number it has.

As long as the birds. Which is the only answer. Which is, here, the only kind of answer there is.
