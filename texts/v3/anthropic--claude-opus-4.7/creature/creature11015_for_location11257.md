# The People of Hunch-on-Vyr

They are not tall.

This is the first thing, and it took the city three hundred years to do it, but the people of Hunch-on-Vyr are not tall. The doorways are low; the stairs are narrow; the ceilings of the older tenements would brush the crown of any lowlander. And the people have met the city halfway, as people will. They are compact, the Vyrish, short in the leg and long in the back, with the deep chest you get from climbing stairs for ninety years. Their hands are wide and their fingers are clever and their feet are quiet. They walk softly because the stones are wet and a fall on the Eighth means a long way down through other people's washing. From childhood they learn to set the foot flat, to test the rung, to slide a shoulder sideways through a crowd without touching. A Vyrish child in a lowland town looks like she is moving through invisible obstacles, ducking under nothing, turning her hips to slip past empty air. It takes them a year to unlearn it, if they ever do.

Their skin runs to the pale damp side, the color of the inside of a mushroom, but with a flush at the cheek and the knuckle from the climbing and the steam. Hair is dark mostly, sometimes a startling rust where the dye-houses have been in a family for generations and something has gotten into the blood. Eyes are good in low light; they water in full sun. Old Vyrish visiting the coast wear smoked glass and complain.

You would expect them to be grim. They are not grim. They are quiet, which is not the same thing. A Vyrish conversation is conducted at a volume that a stranger from a market-town would mistake for conspiracy — half-voice, with the head tilted toward the other speaker, because the fog eats sound and you have learned that if you can hear yourself clearly you are shouting. They laugh the same way: a quick low sound, almost a cough, with the shoulders. A whole tea-house full of Vyrish having a wonderful evening sounds, from outside, like nothing much.

They are patient in a way that the lowland traders find maddening and a little frightening. A Vyrish weaver will stand at her loom for the length of a shift and not speak and not hurry and not stop, and when you ask her at the end how it went she will say *well enough* and mean it. Time in Hunch-on-Vyr is not the time of the clock. It is the time of the shift-bell, which comes when it comes, and the time of the wheel, which turns at its own rate, and the time of the bricks, which weep at the rate they have always wept. The Vyrish have absorbed this. They wait without resentment. They will queue for forty minutes at a public stair-junction in the morning rush and emerge at the top with no sense of having lost anything. Where would the time have gone otherwise?

They are, however, *busy*. Do not mistake the patience for slowness. A Vyrish woman crossing the Fourth at the change of shift with a basket of bobbins on her hip and a child on her back and a kettle's-worth of news to deliver to her sister three terraces up is moving as fast as any creature alive; she just isn't running, because in Hunch-on-Vyr running gets you killed or fired or laughed at, and she has the route memorized to the inch. She knows which step of the Cooper's Stair is loose. She knows that the bridge from Tinman's Yard to the Ropewalk is closed on Wend-days because the tar-boilers are out. She knows seventeen shortcuts through other people's kitchens and she has standing permission for fourteen of them and she will be in and out of a stranger's scullery in nine seconds with a nod and a flick of two fingers at the brow that means *thank you, owed.*

This is the thing about the Vyrish: they live in an economy of small owed favors that has no ledger and never lapses. The city is too dense for strangers. Everyone is somebody's neighbor or somebody's neighbor's neighbor, and the borrowed cup of oil from the Twelfth must be returned, eventually, perhaps as a turn at watching the children of someone you have never met but who is cousin to the woman whose oil it was. The web is total. An outsider cannot see it and the Vyrish cannot describe it; they only know, with great precision, what they owe and what is owed them, and they discharge it slowly, all their lives, and die with the books roughly balanced. To die owing too much is the great shame. To die owed too much is sadder still — it means you held yourself apart, you did not let yourself be helped, and your unspent credit sinks back into the city like rain into brick.

They are religious in a sideways way. There are no real temples in Hunch-on-Vyr — no room for them — but every wheel-house has a niche, and every niche has a small wet-looking figure in it, blackened with soot and shiny at the head where thumbs have touched it for two hundred years. These are the Turners, the patron-things of the city, and there are as many of them as there are trades. The Turner of the Dye-Vat is different from the Turner of the Loom is different from the Turner of the Stair. You touch the right one on the way to work. You do not pray; you acknowledge. The Vyrish say *seen you* to the figure as they pass, and that is the whole ceremony, and it is done a hundred million times a day across the city, and the Turners are very satisfied, and the wheels keep turning. On the high terraces above the smokeline there are older figures, half-vegetable, made of stitched moss on a wicker frame, replaced each spring; these are the Mother's own and the Vyrish are politely afraid of them. They do not look at them directly. They leave a thumb-smear of butter on the sill and walk on.

They sing. This was mentioned, the faint singing from somewhere across the terraces in the long evening, and yes, the Vyrish sing — at work, mostly, very low, half under the breath, the kind of song that is really a way of breathing in a long rhythm so you can keep doing the thing with your hands. The songs are mostly about water. Some are about waiting. A few are about a sweetheart on another terrace whom you cannot easily get to because of the shift, and these are the saddest, and the young people pretend not to know them and know them perfectly.

Their food is brown and hot and there is not very much of it but it is always there. A working Vyrish eats five small meals across a shift, taken standing, from a tin: a knot of dark bread, a smear of pickle, a piece of the long pale potato that grows in the wall-gardens, a sliver of the strong cheese that comes up by mule from the high passes, a swallow of the thin sour beer that is brewed in every quarter and is different in every quarter and which Vyrish from the Third will not drink if they can possibly help it because it tastes, they say, of the Third, which is a place they do not like. The cheese is shared. The pickle is shared. The bread you keep for yourself. These are rules; nobody states them.

Children are everywhere and nowhere. They are everywhere in the sense that there are a great many of them and they are extremely small and they move through the city like a kind of weather, in pulses, just under the adult eye-line; they are nowhere in the sense that no Vyrish adult is ever quite sure whose child is whose at any given moment, and it does not matter, because any child in trouble on any terrace is the business of any adult who happens to be near, and any child being a nuisance on any terrace is the business of any adult who happens to be near, and the system works. Vyrish children climb extraordinarily well. They are taught the ladders before they are taught their letters. There is a thing they do, the older children, around eight or nine, where they go out at the dim hour after the evening shift and run the rope-bridges and the parapets in a kind of game, a quick weightless game, and the adults pretend to disapprove and remember doing it themselves and let it happen. Some fall. Not many. The city expects a few.

They marry late, the Vyrish, and quietly, and often within the same trade-quarter, because where else would you meet anyone? But there is a long tradition of the cross-terrace match, the marriage that comes out of an accidental meeting in a stairwell during a shift-change, and these are considered romantic and slightly improbable, and there are a dozen ballads about them, sung very low.

They do not, on the whole, leave. A Vyrish who goes down to the river-foot and takes ship for the lowlands is spoken of for a season and then not. A Vyrish who comes back from such a journey is listened to politely and not entirely believed. The city is what there is. The city is enough. A Vyrish standing at the parapet of the Ninth in the blue evening, looking down across the terraces with their pricking gas-lights and their slow wheels and the smoke standing up white, is looking at the whole world, and the whole world is looking back, dim and warm and humming, and saying, in its quiet baffled way: *seen you.*

And the Vyrish nods, and touches two fingers to the brow, and goes in to supper.
