They are called, by themselves, in the soft clicking speech they use when they speak at all, something that translates roughly as *the ones who came back out*. Not a tribe. Not really a people. A category. Anyone who has been into the Hollowing and emerged is one of them, and anyone who has not, is not, and that is the only social fact that matters.

They live on the surface above. You did not see the surface because you entered the Hollowing from one of its lower mouths, where a root-system has broken open against the side of a ravine, but above the chambers — above the sealing membrane that lets the honeyed light through — there is a country. It is a high cold plateau of grey grass and wind. The great plant-organism is rooted somewhere far below it and extrudes itself downward and outward into the warm dark, but on the surface there is almost no sign of it: only, here and there, a low dome of pale woody tissue pushing up through the turf like a knuckle, sealed and breathing slowly. The people live among these knuckles. They build low houses of grey stone and grey thatch, and they keep small grey animals, and they are themselves grey-clad and quiet, and the whole upper country is the photographic negative of what lies beneath it. Cold where the Hollowing is warm. Silent where it roars. Sparse where it teems. Settled where it churns.

They are thin people, long-boned, with large dark eyes adapted to the dimness of their long winters and the dimness, also, of the Hollowing's amber light. Their hands are clever. They make the clay bowls. They make the plaited cord. They cut the tally-marks. They are the cairn-stackers, and they have been doing this for — it is impossible to say. Long enough that the practice has the smoothness of something done without needing to be taught. Short enough that they still remember, in their stories, the first descent.

Here is what they do, and why.

Every person, once in their life, goes down. There is no fixed age. It is supposed to happen when you are ready, and readiness is something the elders watch for and the young person feels, and sometimes the two agree and sometimes they do not, and when they do not agree there is a quiet argument that may last years. But eventually each person goes. They go alone. They carry three stones from their own house's threshold, a length of cord they have plaited themselves, a bowl they have made themselves, and a small flat blade for the tally. They go in through one of the mouths — there are seven known mouths, scattered across a region of perhaps a hundred kilometers — and they walk for as long as they can bear it, through as many chambers as they can bear, and somewhere, when the place has done to them whatever it is going to do, they stop and they make their marks.

They stack the cairn. *I was here. I had a place above. I brought this weight down with me.*

They cut the tally. *I counted. I retained number. The roar did not take arithmetic from me.*

They hang the cord. *I made a thing with my hands. My hands were still mine.*

They press the bowl-rim into the floor-mat. *I ate from a vessel. I was not yet one of the chewing creatures.*

Then they come back out. Most of them come back out. Some do not, and those are not mourned in the usual way; they are spoken of as having stayed, as if staying were a choice that could be made by a coherent person, which everyone privately knows it is not. The ones who do not come back out are the ones who lost the thread, who sat down somewhere in the green chamber or the red chamber and let the noise replace them, and there is, in the upper country, a quiet horror of this fate that is never spoken of directly and that shapes everything about how the people raise their children. *Keep the thread. Keep the thread. You are not the noise. You are the one who counts.*

What you must understand about them is that the Hollowing is not their enemy and not their god. It is their *opposite*. They are a people defined by being the cold dry quiet thing above the warm wet loud thing, and they need the warm wet loud thing to be what they are. Without it they would only be a small hungry population on a cold plateau, which is in fact what they materially are, but with it they are *the ones who came back out*, which is something else, something with shape. Their identity is borrowed against the Hollowing. They go down to renew the loan.

And they have noticed — this is the part they do not tell strangers, and the part the elders discuss only in winter, when the wind covers the talk — that the Hollowing is growing. The knuckles on the plateau are more numerous than they were in the grandparents' time. The mouths have shifted. One of the seven mouths, the easternmost, opened only four generations ago, and a new one is suspected to be forming under the lake. The great plant beneath them is extruding upward as well as outward now. It is reaching for the surface. Slowly, slowly, but reaching.

What happens when it arrives is not discussed. There are two views. The minority view is that the Hollowing will breach the plateau and the warm loud teeming life will pour out into the cold, and the people will be unmade by abundance, will lose the contrast that makes them themselves, will become — what? Possibly nothing. Possibly fauna. The majority view, held more out of necessity than conviction, is that the plant will sense the surface and seal itself against it, the way it seals its ceilings against the sky, and the two countries will continue as they are, layered, opposite, mutually defining.

A few of the younger ones, the ones who have come back out most recently and who are still raw from it, have begun to wonder whether the second view is wishful. They have seen, in the deepest chambers, the way the plant pushes. They have felt it pushing. They have come back to the cold plateau and looked at the grey grass with new eyes and understood that the grass is thin, the soil is shallow, and underneath the soil the great slow vegetable mind is deciding things.

These ones, the recently-returned, are quieter than the others. They make their cairns at their own thresholds now, not just in the Hollowing. They have begun, without consulting anyone, to plait longer cords. They keep their bowls close. They are practicing, although they would not use this word, for the possibility that the Hollowing is not a place you visit but a place that is coming, and that the discipline of remaining yourself inside it may eventually have to be performed not for an afternoon, but for the rest of your life, and your children's lives, and on, into a future where the distinction between *the ones who came back out* and *the ones who stayed* will no longer make sense, because there will no longer be anywhere to come back out *to*.

They tend their grey animals. They watch the knuckles. They count.
