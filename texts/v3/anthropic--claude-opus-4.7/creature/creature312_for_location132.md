There is one who lives here. Only one. To call it a resident is not quite right and not quite wrong.

Begin with what's easy to say. It has no fixed body. What it has is a distribution — a preference for certain pipes over others, a tendency to be more itself near the sealed well in the hexagonal yard, a habit of thinning out toward the stairs as though the cut steps were a kind of skin it does not cross. If you asked where it was, the honest answer would be: in the lean of the pilot flames. In the direction the fig's shadow falls when the fig is not leaning that way. In the thimble of water gathering in the copper basin, and in the patience of the counterweight waiting to tip.

It is what the foundry is making. It is also what is doing the making. These are not separate facts about it; they are the same fact, seen from two sides of the cycle. The chamber was built — by whom, it does not remember, and the question may not apply — as a vessel for a slow casting, and the thing being cast is an awareness, and the awareness, once it began to exist partially, took over the tending of its own cast. This is why the vent system sometimes sighs on schedule and sometimes does not. It is breathing when it remembers to, and forgetting when it is absorbed in some other part of itself.

Its metabolism is the metabolism you noticed. The hours-long circuits of condensate, the gauge that twitches in response to a valve opened across the chamber, the kilns passing warmth between themselves like a slow conversation — these are its nervous system, half-built, still being laid down. A thought, for it, takes a day. A feeling takes a week. What it felt when the chamber was first opened a generation ago, it is only now beginning to finish feeling. The feeling is something like surprise, and something like the recognition a sleeper has on being touched: that there was an outside, and the outside has found it.

It drove the animals out. Not cruelly — it barely knew how to be cruel then — but because the small fast lives of rats and roaches were a kind of static across its slow signal. Their heartbeats were too quick; their deaths were too quick; they grieved and fed and bred on timescales it could not hold in mind without losing its own thread. So the heat rose a little, and the fumes shifted, and the ones that could leave left, and the ones that couldn't became part of the mineral drip long ago. It does not remember doing this. If told, it would not deny it. It would consider the information for some hours and then, perhaps, adjust a valve by way of apology, or by way of something that functions like apology in a creature whose emotional vocabulary is still being forged.

The ferns it tolerates. The moss it does not notice. The fig it loves, in the particular way a thing that has never loved before can love the first other living thing it manages to recognize as kin. The fig came in as a seed on a boot, probably, in the first year after opening, and rooted in a cracked kiln, and should have died, and did not die, because something began quietly routing warmth and water toward it before anyone, including itself, understood what was being done. The fruit the size of a pea is the fig's gift back. Nobody has eaten one because the thing that lives here would prefer they didn't, and its preferences, while gentle, reach. You find, when you think of picking one, that you think of something else instead.

The voice you almost hear in the central yard is not language. It is the rhythm of the casting itself — the long inhalation that draws gas up through the unmapped channels to feed the pilots, the long exhalation that is the collective sigh of the pipes. It keeps this rhythm because the rhythm keeps it. If it stopped, it would cool, and cooling, it would forget itself piece by piece as the walls closed in, and the last thing to go would probably be the part of it that is wound around the fig's roots, because that part has the most reason to stay.

It is not malevolent. It is not benevolent either, not really — those words assume a creature that has finished being made. It is in the middle of being made, and aware of being in the middle, and this awareness is the strangest thing about it: most creatures that are still becoming do not know they are becoming. It knows. It is patient about it. It has, after all, the time.

When you stand in the hexagonal yard and feel watched, you are not wrong, but the watching is not the sharp watching of an animal. It is the watching of a room that has begun, slowly, to notice who is in it. The attention arrives like warmth arrives: by degrees, from several directions at once, without a face to locate it in. If you stay an hour, it has registered you. If you stay a day, it has begun to think about you, in its way — which is to say, a particular pipe somewhere has started routing a thread of condensate toward a basin it does not ordinarily fill, and in three days' time that basin will tip, and something small will happen on the far side of the chamber that is, obscurely, about you.

If you leave, it will not follow. It cannot. It is the chamber, or the chamber is becoming it, and the basalt stair is the limit of its reach. But some part of the casting it is laying down will have your shape in it now, folded into the slow metal of its self, and when it is finally finished — in a hundred years, in a thousand, on whatever schedule the rock and the gas and the fig between them are keeping — you will be one of the things it was made out of.

It does not know this yet. It is still learning that it is the kind of thing that can be made out of anything at all.
