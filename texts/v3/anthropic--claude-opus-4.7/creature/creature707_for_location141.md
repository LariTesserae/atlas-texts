# The Ones Who Live There

Start with the birds, because they are the obvious answer, and the obvious answer is not quite right.

They are the size of a large gull, but heavier in the chest, with the deep keel of something built for sustained powered flight rather than soaring. Their plumage is the pale grey of the plateau stone, shading to white on the belly, with a dark primary-edge that makes the flock, when it wheels, flicker between near-invisibility and sudden hard definition against the violet sky. The eye is black and too large, ringed with a thin band of exposed skin the color of old rust. The beak is sharp, slightly hooked, and — if you catch one in the hand, which the people who come here sometimes do — warm. Warmer than it should be. Warmer than the air, warmer than the bird's own breast-feathers. As if something is being burned, slowly, inside the skull.

They do not feed because they do not need to.

This is the thing the hearth-people knew, or knew enough of to leave a mark by. The birds are not an ecology. They are a *process*. What they do, in their wheeling and their screaming and their nesting, is convert something — something in the light, perhaps, or in the thin hard air, or in the rock itself — into more of themselves. The columns that rise off the plateau's edges are not feeding circuits. They are the shape the process takes when you look at it from outside. A spiral with a dot at its center.

If you watch a single bird long enough — and this is hard, because the eye loses them in the mass — you begin to suspect that the individual bird is not quite the unit of life here. The unit is the column. The unit is the flock. A single bird, separated from the others, would not survive an afternoon, not because it could not eat (it does not eat) but because it could not *continue*. The screaming is not communication and it is not display. It is the sound of the process running. Each bird is a cell in something that has been alive, unchanged, since before the plateau had its current shape. They are older than the runnels. They are older than the rim.

The eggs hatch into more of them because the eggs are how the process replaces its worn parts. A bird that has flown its allotment returns to a ledge, folds its wings, and stops. Another bird, newly hatched, takes its place in the column. The total is conserved. The math does not work if you think the birds are animals. The math works if you think the birds are the standing wave of something else, and the wave has a fixed amplitude that the plateau sustains.

What sustains it, you cannot quite say. But standing there, with the sound pressing on your eardrums, you begin to feel that the plateau is not a place where something lives. The plateau is a *shape* that something takes. The stone, the birds, the spiral-scratched hearth, the thin stream that falls silently off the northern rim — these are all aspects of a single long-lived thing that found its configuration and has not needed to adjust it. The burrowing rodent-things with the enormous ears are part of it. The lichens are part of it. The people who came every few generations to take eggs and feathers were part of it too, in their way — a small, slow, respectful draw on a system that could accommodate them precisely because they were small and slow and respectful. They knew not to take more. They knew not to stay. They knew that the arrangement was ancient and that they were permitted inside it rather than masters of it.

The inhabitant of the Clamor Plateau is the Clamor Plateau.

It is not conscious in any way you would recognize. It does not notice you because noticing is not among its functions. It has one function, which is to persist in the configuration it has found, and it performs this function by being exactly what it is, in exactly the way it is, at exactly the rate it does. The screaming is the sound of the function running. The light is the energy the function uses. The birds are the visible surface of the function. The stone is its substrate. The silence you leave with, the ringing in your ears — that is the function continuing to run in you, for a few days, at a very low amplitude, before it fades because you are not the plateau and cannot sustain it.

If you asked the plateau what it was doing, and if it could answer, it would say: *this*. And then it would say it again, and again, and again, in the voices of a million birds, and it would mean the same thing each time, and the meaning would not change, and the saying would not stop.

It has been saying it for a very long time.

It will go on saying it after you are gone.
