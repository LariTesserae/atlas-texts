# The Residents of the Lacquer Ward

They are difficult to count because counting them is the wrong operation. You said thirty, and thirty is what you saw, and thirty is probably correct in the way that the temperature of a room is correct — a true average of something that is not, underneath, a single stable quantity. Some days there are twenty-two. Some days thirty-four. No one arrives and no one leaves, and yet the number drifts, and the residents themselves will tell you, if pressed, that this is simply how populations behave in a place that has not yet finished deciding its own shape. They say it the way people elsewhere say *the tide comes in.*

Let me describe them as I have come to understand them.

## The Pitch of Them

They are human. I want to be clear about that first, because everything I am about to say might suggest otherwise, and it would be wrong. They have the full complement of fingers. They bleed red. They eat bread and drink tea and one of them, the man on the ladder, keeps a small tobacco pouch in his breast pocket and rolls cigarettes with one hand while the other holds the gasket wrench. They were born, most of them, in cities you have heard of. A few were born in the Ward, and those ones are subtly different, but not in any way you could point to in a photograph.

What has happened to them is not transformation. It is *accommodation*. They have lived long enough in a place whose rules are provisional that they have become provisional themselves — not unreal, not inhuman, simply *unfixed* in certain small ways that a person fixed to a single stable world would find unsettling. They do not throw sharp shadows either. I noticed this late and could not un-notice it.

## What They Do

They are makers. Every one of them. This is not a coincidence and not a job requirement — it is closer to saying that every resident of a reef is a filter-feeder. The Ward supports one kind of life, and that life is the slow, attentive, wet-handed labor of producing something from raw material under conditions of mild impossibility.

The woman in the oilskin apron is a smelter. What she smelts is not metal in the sense you know metal. It arrives as a grey slurry from the mill and leaves her furnace as small dense ingots the color of a bruised plum, which are then drawn into wire by a thin man I did not see but whose lathe I heard. The wire goes into the crates. The crates go onto the barge. The barge goes through the arch it cannot fit through. Somewhere, someone is buying this wire. I cannot imagine for what.

The man on the ladder is a pipefitter, but the word is too small. He is a *pipe-diplomat*. The pipe network is the Ward's circulatory system and also, I came to suspect, part of what holds the Ward coherent against whatever pressure from outside would otherwise collapse it — the boilers pulse the whole pocket like a heart, and he keeps the vessels from bursting. He has been doing this for a long time. I could not tell how long and did not ask. His hands are the hands of someone whose relationship with his tools has gone past skill into something more like marriage.

The children. I want to come back to the children.

There is a woman who tends the orchids on the chimneys, and what she harvests from them goes into small dark bottles, and the bottles go out on the barge too. There is a man whose entire profession appears to be listening — he sits in a small booth near the mill with his eyes closed and his head cocked, and once an hour he writes something in a ledger and rings a small bell, and somewhere in the Ward a valve is adjusted in response. There is a baker. The baker is important. I will come to the baker.

There are two people I could not place at all. One was very old and sat in a window. One was very young, not a child, perhaps sixteen, and walked the perimeter of the vault slowly and constantly, touching the walls. I believe she was checking something. I believe she had been doing it for some time.

## How They Are With Each Other

Sparingly. Kindly. With the economy of people who have known each other for a long time and have nothing to prove and nothing to hide and not much, day to day, to say.

They nod. They pass tools without asking. When the buildings migrate, they call out directions to each other in a shorthand of single syllables — *north*, *slow*, *mind the third* — and the warnings are always enough. They eat together at midday, or what functions as midday in a place without a sun, gathering at a long trestle under an awning near the baker's, and the meal is quiet and quick and the bread is extraordinary. I was not offered any and did not ask. I understood, without being told, that to eat the bread would be to begin the process of becoming a resident, and that this process, once begun, is not quickly undone.

They laugh rarely but easily. The pipefitter laughed once, at something one of the children said, and the laugh was the laugh of a man who has laughed at variations of the same joke for forty years and still finds it funny, and I felt, hearing it, a sudden sharp homesickness for a home I have never had.

They do not touch each other much. When they do, it is deliberate — a hand on a shoulder to steady someone over a wet cobble, a brief clasp of forearms in greeting or parting. The hand on the shoulder lingers a beat longer than it would above. This is the only sentimentality I observed in them, and it is not sentimentality exactly. It is more like — they know how provisional they are. They confirm each other by contact. A touch is a small mutual insistence: *you are here, and so am I, and for this moment we are both definitely here.*

## The Children

There are three. I saw two on the canal edge and one later, asleep in a window seat behind glass fogged from the inside.

They are not anyone's children in the sense of parentage. They belong to the Ward. Every adult treats them with the same mild affectionate attention, and they move between the workshops as if all of the workshops were equally their kitchen. They are being taught, constantly and without apparent curriculum, how to be residents. They are being taught the pattern of the reshuffles. They are being taught which springs are safe to drink from and which will offer you a coin. They are being taught the shorthand.

They are also, I think, being taught something I cannot name — something about how to be a person who is slightly unfixed without being afraid of it. The boy who dropped pebbles to scatter the frogs looked up at me once and his face had the considered patience of a man of sixty. It was not eerie. It was only a little sad, in the way that competent children are always a little sad.

I do not know where they came from. I asked, obliquely, and was answered obliquely: *they arrived.* The way springs arrive. The way weather arrives. The Ward produces what it needs, including, sometimes, the people to tend it.

## The Baker

I said I would come to the baker. The baker is a woman of indeterminate middle age, with flour to the elbows and a face that has been kind for so long that kindness has become its default resting shape. She runs the oven that everyone eats from. She is the only resident who made direct, warm eye contact with me, and she held it for perhaps three seconds, and in those three seconds I understood several things at once:

That she knew exactly who I was and where I had come from.

That she knew I would leave in under two hours.

That she had seen many like me and would see many more.

That she bore me no ill will whatever and wished me, in a general and unsentimental way, well.

That she was, in some functional sense I cannot articulate, the Ward's host — not its ruler, not its mayor, there is nothing so formal, but the person through whom the Ward meets its visitors, the one who decides, with the small authority of someone offering or not offering bread, whether you stay or go.

She did not offer me bread. I am grateful to her. I think.

## What They Are At Peace With

They are at peace with the migrating buildings, and the reversing canal, and the occasional upward gutter, and the frog who offers a coin, and the thing in the vault that sounds like wet canvas folding. They are at peace with the fact that their sky is forty meters up and sweats. They are at peace with not knowing, or not caring, whether they are hidden from the city above or the city above is hidden from them.

They are at peace, most remarkably, with being partly provisional themselves. They do not fight it. They do not try to shore themselves up against it with rituals or dogmas. They simply make things, carefully and well, and eat together at midday, and touch each other's forearms when they meet, and trust the Ward to keep breathing under their feet.

I think this is the deepest thing about them, and the thing I have thought about most since leaving. They have made a life out of staying kind and staying careful inside a place whose rules will not hold still. They are not saints and not sages. They are tradespeople in an unstable pocket of the world, and they have worked out, between them, a way of being decent to each other under conditions that would drive most of us to superstition or cruelty or flight.

I do not think they would describe it that way. I think if you asked the pipefitter what the secret was, he would look at you for a moment, and roll a cigarette, and say *mind the third*, and go back up his ladder.

## A Last Thing

When I found the door out — not the door I came in by, the other one, the one that was willing — I passed the baker's window. She was kneading. She did not look up. But as I passed, she said, not loudly, *come back in daylight,* and I have been trying ever since to work out what she meant, because there is no daylight in the Ward and never has been, and I think she knew that, and I think she meant something else by it, and I think whatever she meant was a kindness.

The frogs in my pockets were, I believe, her doing. A small gift, or a small test, or a small anchor — some part of the Ward traveling home with me, to see if I would bring it back.

I released them into the gutter. I was not ready.

I may yet be ready. The residents, I think, would wait. They have the patience of people who have learned to live in a place that is still deciding its shape, and they know that visitors, too, are still deciding theirs.
