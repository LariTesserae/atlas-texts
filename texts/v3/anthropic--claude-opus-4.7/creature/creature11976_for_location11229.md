## Who Lives There

### The Builders

They are pale. That is the first thing to know, and the thing that surprises people who imagine termites as the dark wood-chewers of their porches. These are something else — *Constrictotermes* would be a close cousin, but this lineage hasn't been named yet by anyone who would name it, and may not be by the time it changes again. Their bodies are the color of skinned almonds, soft-bodied and slow when handled, though no one handles them. Workers are perhaps four millimeters long. Soldiers are slightly larger and carry, instead of mandibles, a nozzle at the front of the head — a frontal gland that ejects a thread of sticky, faintly turpentine-smelling defensive secretion. They are nasutes. They cannot bite. They glue.

There are three castes in the hollow right now, though a fourth is being attempted.

The **workers** outnumber everything. They are blind, and they move through the mounds and out across the clay in trails so thin they look like a single twitching line of dust. They are the ones building. A worker carries a grain of clay in its mandibles, walks it to a place that has been decided without being decided, presses it down, turns it with a small rotational motion that mixes saliva into the matrix, and walks away. The grain is now part of a wall. The worker has just contributed a syllable to a sentence it will never finish reading. Multiply this by perhaps two hundred thousand individuals and you have the thumb's-width of growth per afternoon that an observer might notice.

The workers also farm. Deep in the largest of the mounds, in chambers the size of walnuts, they tend small grey combs of chewed plant matter on which a particular fungus grows — a fungus that exists nowhere else, that has been bred into this relationship for so many generations that it cannot fruit without them and they cannot digest without it. The combs are their gardens and their pantries. The fungus is, in a real sense, also a citizen of the hollow, though it has no opinions.

The **soldiers** are the strange ones. They patrol the perimeters of the trails and stand at the entrances of the mounds with their nozzles raised. When the column of foreign ants on the ceiling — those distant treaty-keepers — once tried, months ago, to send a scouting party down into the clay, the soldiers met them in a thin white line and glued seventeen ants to the floor in under a minute. The ants have not tried again. The soldiers remember nothing of this; the colony remembers it in the form of a slightly altered patrol path.

And then there is the **queen**. She is in the oldest mound, the one nearest the back wall of the hollow, the one whose spire has been torn down and rebuilt twice already. She is the length of a thumb and she does not look like a termite anymore — her abdomen has swollen into a pale segmented sac, ringed and translucent, in which you can see the slow peristaltic movement of eggs being made. She cannot walk. She is fed mouth-to-mouth by workers in a rotation that never ceases. She has been alive for perhaps eight years and may live thirty more. Beside her, smaller and darker and almost forgotten by the literature, is the king — the original male, still present, still mating, the quiet other half of the founding pair who flew in on a single nuptial evening years ago when the fig was already old and the hollow was already waiting.

They are the city, those two, in a way the workers are not. Everything else is their extended body.

### The Almost-Builders

There is something happening in the brood chambers that the colony has not resolved. Among the recent eggs, a small proportion are developing wing buds at a stage when they should not. The colony is trying, without trying, to produce alates — reproductive flyers — too early in its own life. Most will be reabsorbed, eaten, repurposed. A few may complete the transformation and emerge, next rainy season, into a hollow they cannot escape, because the fig's openings are high and narrow and the air outside the fig is not the air they were calibrated for. They will mill on the clay for a day and die there, and the workers will carry their wings away and use the bodies to feed the fungus. This is not a tragedy. This is how the colony learns what it is by failing to be something else first.

### The Guests

The **velvet worm** is older than she looks. She has been in the hollow for two years and has eaten, in that time, perhaps four hundred springtails and one unwary young whip spider. She is a peripatus, soft as a finger, with stubby legs that move in a wave, and she navigates by touch and by the faintest chemical traces. She is one of perhaps three in the entire hollow, and they do not meet. When they do meet, only one of them leaves.

The **whip spider** on the wall has a name only in the sense that any creature that has occupied the same square foot for nine months has earned one. She is *Charinus*, probably, though the species in this forest haven't all been described. Her front legs are absurdly long and thin and she uses them like a blind man's canes, sweeping the air ahead of her for the warmth of moths. She has never been struck by a falling drop of water that she didn't anticipate. The termites do not interest her. She is waiting for something larger.

The **frogs** are *Pristimantis* — direct-developers, which means they lay their eggs in damp moss and the eggs hatch as tiny frogs, skipping the tadpole stage entirely. This matters here because there is no standing water in the hollow large enough for tadpoles. The frogs have made the hollow possible for themselves by not needing what the hollow does not have. There are six of them currently. Two will be eaten by the whip spider before the season turns.

The **foreign ants** on the ceiling — they are *Camponotus*, a carpenter species, and their nest is in a hollow root somewhere above the canopy. They come down through the fig because the fig's interior offers a shortcut, and they have learned, in the way ant colonies learn, that the floor of the hollow is forbidden. So they walk the ceiling. What they carry is the dismembered bodies of beetle larvae from a rotting branch on the other side of the tree. They do not know about the termites below them in any way they could articulate. They know about the *line* — the boundary at which seventeen of their sisters once stopped moving.

The **bats** are two males of a small frugivorous species, roosting alone because they have not yet been accepted by a harem elsewhere. They are tolerated by everything because they leave at dusk through a gap none of the others can use, and they return before dawn smelling of fruit. Their droppings, falling occasionally to the clay, are a windfall — minerals, seeds, undigested matter — and *whatever falls from above belongs to whoever reaches it first* means that within an hour of a bat dropping, the matter has been disassembled and removed by half a dozen different parties, none of whom acknowledge each other in the process.

### The Smallest Citizens

There is a layer below the visible. In the clay itself live nematodes, mites, tardigrades, and a thin film of bacteria that the termites carry on their exoskeletons and seed into every wall they build. The mounds are not just clay; they are clay inoculated with the colony's own microbial signature, which is part of why a torn-down mound can be rebuilt without starting over — the substrate remembers. There are protists in the termites' guts that digest cellulose for them and could not survive an hour outside that gut. There are mites that ride specifically on the heads of soldiers and eat the secretions around their nozzles, keeping them clean. There are even smaller mites that ride on those mites.

A single termite is not one organism. A single mound is not one colony. The hollow is not one ecosystem but a nesting of them, each one a city for the one beneath it, down past where the eye can resolve.

### What They Are Doing

They are, all of them together, doing the same thing the mounds are doing: becoming.

The colony is, in evolutionary terms, in the middle of a sentence. Its architecture is changing because its biology is changing because its environment is changing because the fig is slowly, over decades, drying at the top and weeping at the bottom, and the colony is being shaped by this. In ten generations the mounds may have spires. In a hundred they may have learned to build outward, beyond the fig, into the leaf litter. Or the fig may fall and crush them and end the experiment entirely. The colony does not know. The colony does not know anything. The colony is a process that has, so far, not stopped.

The whip spider waits. The velvet worm hunts. The frogs blink. The ants commute. The bats sleep. The queen lays. The workers carry single grains of clay across distances that, scaled to their bodies, are the length of city blocks, and they do this without rest, and the wet warm dim continues, and the drops fall — *tup* — and the hollow holds its breath, and a civilization the size of a hearthrug invents itself, one grain, one egg, one held moment at a time.

Nobody who lives here knows they are a marvel. That, too, is part of what they are.
