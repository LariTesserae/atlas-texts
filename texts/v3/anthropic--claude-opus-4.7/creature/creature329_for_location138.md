# The Tenders

They are hard to see at first because they move at the valley's speed, not yours. If you sat still for a full day — a genuine day, sun-up to sun-up, not the restless hour most visitors manage — you would begin to notice that certain patches of moss are not moss, and certain fern-shadows shift between one glance and the next in ways the wind cannot explain.

They are roughly person-shaped, though the word *person* wants qualifying. Call them tall — taller than a human when they stand fully upright, which is rare; they mostly stoop, or crouch, or fold. Their bodies have the same braided quality as the fern-trunk-and-conduit I described: a core that reads as living tissue, pale and slightly translucent like the inside of a frond, wound through with fine threads of the same polished copper as the engines. Not wearing copper. *Grown with it.* Where a human would have tendon, they have a filament of wire; where a human would have a long bone, they have something closer to a reinforced stalk, a pith of plant-stuff sheathed in metal that has never tarnished. When one of them moves an arm, you can see the copper slide under the skin-that-isn't-skin, catching the hard bright light.

Their heads are small for their bodies and tilted slightly forward, as if always listening to the ground. No visible eyes in the places eyes belong — instead, a scatter of dark points across the upper face and scalp, arranged without symmetry, which I came to understand were sensitive to fog density and to the resonance of the condensers' drone. They navigate by humidity and by sound the way we navigate by sight. In the clearings, where the light is too much and the drone echoes off bare stone, they tend to falter, feel for the pipes with their hands, and hurry back into the green.

There are not many. I counted seven over the course of a week, and I think I was double-counting one. Perhaps a dozen in the whole valley. One for every twenty or thirty machines — which matches, now that I think of it, the ratio of the small structures to the engines. The stone huts and the wooden sheds are not residences. They are workshops, or stations, or simply places where a Tender can set down a tool.

The tools. I should mention the tools. Each Tender carries — or is accompanied by, it's unclear which — a few small implements that have the same braided biology-and-metal character as their bodies. A wrench whose jaw is a living thing that closes around a valve-stem with a soft muscular grip. A cloth, if you can call it that, which seems to be a sheet of lichen reinforced with fine wire, used for polishing the copper. It is the Tenders who keep the engines bright. This is most of what they do. They move from condenser to condenser on a circuit that takes — I estimate — something like a season to complete, and they wipe each one down, test each valve, tighten what has loosened at whatever imperceptible rate things loosen here, and move on.

They do not speak. I watched two of them meet at a junction of pipes and they simply stood near each other for perhaps twenty minutes, their small tilted heads angled the same way, and then parted.
