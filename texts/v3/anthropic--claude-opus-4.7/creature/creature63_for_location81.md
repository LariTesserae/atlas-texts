# The Tenders

There is one of them. Probably. This is the first difficulty.

You find evidence of a single occupant everywhere — a clay cup left on an iron railing, still faintly warm; a pair of wet footprints on a catwalk, evaporating at their edges; a pruning hook hung on a nail driven into a wooden post that someone sank, at some point, into the soil of a raised bed. One set of habits. One rhythm of passage. The beds nearest the central stair are tended more often than the outer ones. A person has preferences. A person has a route.

But then you find the second cup, identical, on a terrace three levels away, also faintly warm, and the walkway between them has been flooded for longer than a cup stays warm. And the pruning hook you saw an hour ago is hanging on a different nail now, in a different room, and you did not see anyone pass.

So: probably one. Functionally more than one. Call them the Tender, and understand that the Tender is sometimes in two places.

---

The Tender is small. You can tell from the height of the nails, the height of the cups on their shelves, the places where the ferns have been trained to lean aside — a person not much over five feet, moving through green corridors shaped to their shoulders. Barefoot, mostly; the footprints show toes. The feet are wide and splayed the way feet get when they have not worn shoes in a very long time. The soles are pale from standing in warm water.

The Tender wears linen, or something like linen, that has reached the same equilibrium with the air that your clothes reach within minutes. It is never dry. It is never getting wetter. It hangs on them the way wet cloth hangs, and they do not appear to find this uncomfortable; they have forgotten, perhaps, that cloth can do anything else. The color is whatever color linen becomes after forty years of green light. A sort of pale mineral green, the green of the undersides of certain leaves.

Their hair is long and is always wet and is the same color as the linen. You cannot tell their age. You cannot tell their sex, and they have stopped being able to tell you, not because they are hiding it but because the category has become uninteresting to them, the way the category of *Tuesday* has become uninteresting. They move at the pace of the pistons. One stroke every nine seconds. A step, a pause, a step.

---

They were trained by someone. This is clear from the way they handle the valves in the service level — the precise quarter-turn, the listening pause, the second quarter-turn, the listening pause, a procedure inherited rather than invented. Somewhere behind them in time there was a master, and behind that master another, and the chain goes back to whoever first decided the flywheels should turn, and why.

The Tender does not know why. They have stopped needing to know why. The engines turn because the engines turn; the water rises because the water must rise; the ferns are trained aside because the walkway must remain a walkway. Purpose has been replaced by maintenance, which is a more honest relationship with the world in any case.

They talk, occasionally. Mostly to the plants, and not in words — a small clicking of the tongue, a hummed two-note phrase when repotting, a long soft exhale that sounds like the steam valves and is perhaps learned from them. If you spoke to the Tender in a human language they would understand you. It would take them a moment. They would have to locate the language, which they have not used recently, the way you might have to locate a tool at the back of a drawer. Then they would answer, quietly, with a voice that has the same timbre as the drip — small, overlapping, unhurried. They would not be surprised by you. They are not surprised by anything anymore. Surprise requires a rate of change faster than the rate of rust.

---

They eat. There is a kitchen, on the fourth terrace, where roots are washed in a stone basin and something is always simmering very slowly in a copper pot over a burner fed from the same system that feeds the boilers. The food is mostly the food of the terraces — tubers, fern-fiddleheads, the fruit of the door-leafed fig-thing, which is dense and faintly metallic and which the Tender eats peeled, with a small knife, sitting on an iron stool, looking at nothing, chewing for a long time.

They sleep. There is a hammock, in a small dry room high up, one of the few places in the complex with a properly sealed ceiling and a small stove for drying linen. They sleep in the hammock for some number of hours that varies with the engines. When the pistons slow, they sleep longer. When the pistons quicken, they rise and walk the terraces in the green half-dark and listen.

They have not been outside in — the number no longer means anything. They know there is an outside. They have stood, many times, at the unplanted pane on the uppermost terrace, and pressed their forehead against it, and felt the cold come through. They know the cold means *not here.* They do not go. The cold is interesting the way a memory is interesting; you do not need to live inside it.

---

The strangeness is this. The Tender is, in every observable way, content. Not happy — happiness is too sharp a weather for this climate — but content in the deep sense, the sense of a thing that fits its container so exactly that the distinction between thing and container has softened. They move through the glasshouse and the glasshouse moves through them. They breathe the water that the plants breathe. Their hair drips into the soil that the ferns drink from. When they die — and they will, eventually, on whatever timescale the engines have set for them — they will be buried in one of the raised beds, probably the one nearest the central stair, which is tended most often, and something will grow out of them, and be pruned, and be pruned, and be pruned.

And then someone else will be there. Not descended from them — the Tender has no children and has never wanted any; the category is as uninteresting as Tuesday. But someone. A small person in wet linen, moving at the pace of the pistons, who was trained by someone who was trained by someone, and who handles the valves with the precise quarter-turn and the listening pause.

Or perhaps no one. Perhaps the last Tender has already been and gone, and what walks the terraces now is only the habit of tending, the shape a person leaves in a place after the person has been absorbed. Perhaps the cup is warm because the pipe beneath the shelf is warm. Perhaps the footprints are only the places where condensate has gathered in the rhythm of a stride long since completed.

This would explain why you never quite see them. Why the pruning hook is always in the room you just left. Why, when you call out — and you will, eventually, call out, because the silence asks for it — the answer is only a drop falling into a pool somewhere far below, and the long soft hiss of a valve, and the piston moving once more through its stroke.

Something lives here. It is tending the place. It may no longer require a body to do so.
