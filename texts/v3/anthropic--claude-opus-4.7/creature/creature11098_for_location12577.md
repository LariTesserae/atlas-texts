The grazers are the easiest to begin with, because they are the most nearly visible, and beginning with them lets the rest gather behind.

They are tall. Standing, they would come to the height of a person, but they almost never stand straight; their default attitude is a careful stoop, the long forelimbs folded in front of the chest like a mantis at rest, the hindlimbs angled and ready. Count the joints and you lose count. There are at least four bends in each limb, possibly five, and they do not all flex in the same plane — a grazer placing a foot will rotate the limb through a slow corkscrew so that the foot arrives at the floor from an unexpected angle, having threaded itself between fibers you did not notice were there. The feet themselves are narrow and many-toed and padded, and they leave no print on the mineral.

Their bodies are pale, as you saw, but the pallor is not uniform. Under the right light — the late part of the bright phase, when the floor glow is failing and the ceiling has not yet darkened — you can see that they are faintly banded, the way old ivory is banded, with growth lines that run the length of the trunk. They are layered. Something inside them is laying down material year by year, the way the spinners lay down thread, the way the city lays down its own slow upholstery. A grazer that has been walking these alleys for a long time is thicker than a young one, and the oldest are almost barrel-bodied, their original slenderness buried under decades of accumulated self.

They have heads, but the heads are small and held low, and what passes for a face is a soft puckered area on the underside of the skull, surrounded by short pale bristles. This is the mouth, and it is also, as far as anyone could determine, the principal sense organ. They graze by pressing this face to a fiber and holding it there for a long time — minutes, sometimes an hour — during which the bristles work very slightly and the fiber is, by some means, taken in. No part of the fiber visibly disappears; the grazer simply moves on, and the fiber, if you examine it afterward, is a little less bright, a little less taut. Whatever they take is not mass. It is something else. Tension, perhaps. Or the memory of having been spun.

They do not have eyes in any way that is useful to you. There are two small dark pits high on the skull where eyes might once have been, but they are recessed and dry and seem to be vestigial, kept for reasons of structural symmetry rather than function. The grazers navigate by the bristles around the mouth and by something else — a sensitivity in the limbs themselves, which can feel a thread before they touch it, the way your hand can feel a wall in the dark before contact. This is why they never break the threads. They are reading the city continuously with the entirety of their bodies, and the city is, to them, a single dense legible surface, in which the stone is a kind of silence and the fibers are a kind of speech.

There are not many of them. In the whole basin perhaps forty, perhaps sixty. They do not gather. When two grazers meet in an avenue — which happens, by the rough arithmetic of their movements, about once a year — they pause, and they hold the pause for a long time, the two bodies angled toward each other without quite facing, the bristles working faintly. Then they pass. Whether anything has been exchanged is unclear. They do not appear to mate, or to have mated; no young have ever been observed. And yet there are young — smaller, thinner-bodied grazers that appear from time to time in the outer alleys, already walking, already grazing, with the same competent care as their elders. Where they come from is one of the questions the city does not answer. It is possible that they are spun. It is possible that an old grazer, at some threshold of accumulation, simply lays down a smaller one beside itself and walks on. No one has seen this happen. No one has been there long enough to see it not happen, either.

They are not the only inhabitants, though they are the only ones that move.

The spinners, of course, live here, and to say they live is not a stretch — they are slow, but they are alive in the way coral is alive, the way a fungus is alive, with the patience of something that has decided not to hurry. Each spinner is a low pale rosette, no larger than a dinner plate, set into a seam of the mineral. From the center of the rosette rises a single fine stalk, and from the stalk the thread comes — extruded continuously, microscopically, the stalk turning very slowly so that the thread is laid down in a long helix that the wind, if there were any wind, would scatter. There is no wind. The thread accumulates in coils at the base of the stalk, and then, by some mechanism that involves the rosette's slow contractions, is paid out along the ground in the direction the spinner chooses. Spinners choose. This is the part that is hardest to accept. They orient. A spinner near a wall will pay its thread toward the wall and up it; a spinner in an open plaza will pay its thread toward the nearest existing fiber and splice in. Over years, the splices form the canopies and tunnels you saw. The spinners are weaving the city, and they are doing it deliberately, by criteria that have nothing to do with shelter or warmth, since they need neither — perhaps by criteria of geometry, or balance, or some aesthetic that operates below the threshold of anything you would call thought.

They do not have organs that we would recognize. They have the rosette, the stalk, and a small dense node at the base, buried in the mineral, where the dry chemistry happens — where the rock is taken in and the thread is taken out. They luminesce at the stalk tip during the dark phase, as you saw, and the light is the byproduct of the chemistry, not a signal. Or it is not meant as a signal. Whether the grazers read it as one is another question.

And then there is the third thing. The hardest to describe, because it is the one you cannot quite see.

The city was built. You feel this — the lintels, the stairs, the geometry of intention. Something with hands, or with something like hands, cut the original stone and laid the original courses, and that something is gone. But there is a residue. Not a ghost; nothing so sentimental. A residue in the architecture itself, in the way the threads are reworked at certain critical junctions, in the way some doorways are still kept open when by all the slow logic of the spinning they ought to have been sealed years ago. Something is maintaining a minimum of passage. Not much. Not visibly. But the city is not quite being finished as fast as it ought to be, and the discrepancy is small and persistent and patterned.

You can imagine it as a custodian, but it isn't one — there is no figure, no shape that walks the avenues at night with a knife. The maintenance is distributed. It happens in the threads themselves. Certain fibers, under certain conditions, part with that single soft harp note you heard, and they part at exactly the places where parting keeps a doorway clear. The tension in the city is somehow self-regulating, and the regulation favors openness, favors passage, favors the continued possibility of something walking through. The grazers benefit from this; they pass through doorways that, by rights, should have closed a decade ago. Whether the regulation is for them, or whether they are simply incidental beneficiaries of a habit the city retains from its builders, you cannot tell.

This is, in a sense, the true inhabitant. Not a creature. A tendency. The city remembers, in its tensions and its partings, that it was once meant to be walked through, and it keeps walking-room available, on a schedule that has slowed almost to stillness but has not stopped. The spinners spin and the grazers graze and the light rises and falls, and underneath all of it the city itself is holding open, very faintly, the shape of a route — from the outer rim to the dense knots at the center, from plaza to plaza, from doorway to doorway — a route that no one is using and that nothing maintains except the slow physics of selectively failing threads.

If you walked it, you would find it. You would find that the doorways line up. You would find that the alleys, despite their apparent randomness, deliver you reliably inward. You would find, at the center, a small chamber whose ceiling is the densest woven fiber in the basin and whose floor is the youngest mineral, scored with facets so sharp they have clearly never been walked on. There is nothing in the chamber. There has never been anything in the chamber. The chamber is what the city is holding open for, and it has been empty since before the spinners began, and it will be empty when the basin is a solid loaf of fiber with stone bones, because the chamber is not for an occupant. It is for the possibility of one.

That is who lives here. The grazers, who read the city with their bodies. The spinners, who write it. And the held-open shape at the center, which is not a who at all but is, in the long arithmetic of the place, the thing the other two are arranged around — a waiting that has outlasted whatever it was waiting for, and has become, by sheer duration, the form of life most native to the basin.

Walk softly. Do not break any threads. The chamber is not for you either, but it would be polite, while you are passing through, not to disturb the geometry by which it is kept clear.
