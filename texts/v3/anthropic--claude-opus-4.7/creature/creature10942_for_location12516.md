# The Inhabitants of the Sough

They are not one kind of thing. That is the first thing to understand. The Sough has not settled on what its living should look like, any more than it has settled on where its engines stand or how many footprints a walker leaves. So its fauna comes in drafts, in versions, in overlapping attempts.

## The Quiet Ones

The most numerous. You will never see one clearly, which is itself a kind of description.

They are warm-blooded, or at least warm — you know this from the heat-shadows they cast on your skin as they pass. They have mass; the mud remembers them in soft oval depressions that fill slowly with water. Some are long and low and move by a kind of pour, the way mercury moves down a tilted plate. Others walk on a great many legs, six or twelve or more, and the legs are thin and articulated and *careful*. Others still seem to have settled on the shape of a heron and stuck with it, more or less, except for the doubling.

What they all share is the silence. They have made a treaty with sound, or sound has refused to carry them, and they get along without it. When two of them meet — to mate, to fight, to feed, the categories blur — there is the soft scuffle, a displacement of warm air that you feel on your face from yards away, and then the resolution: one shape continuing, one shape not. They do not seem to mourn. They do not seem to celebrate. The matter has simply been worked out.

They eat, I think, by absorption. You will see (or rather, fail quite to see) one settle over a patch of mud and remain there a long time, and when it moves on the mud is subtly different — emptier, somehow, of whatever the mud had been accumulating. They eat the country's slow accruals. They keep the reeds sparse not by browsing but by being present near them, by drawing off whatever it is that reeds need in order to multiply. This is why the vegetation is rationed. The Quiet Ones are the rationers.

They communicate, when they communicate, by heat. A Quiet One that wishes another's attention will warm a patch of itself — you can feel this happen if one is near you, a slow blush along its flank — and the other will warm in answer, or not. Whole conversations pass this way over the basin, gradients sliding into gradients, and you, walking through, are walking through speech you cannot read but can sometimes feel as a passing flush on your own cheek. You may have been addressed. You may have been discussed. You will not know.

## The Tenders

I want to say there are no people here, because the engines run themselves and the houses are dark. But that is not quite true. There are the Tenders.

They are humanoid in the way a reflection in moving water is humanoid: the shape is roughly right, the proportions keep almost-arriving. They live in some of the brick cottages and some of the stilt-shacks, and they are what the candle-in-a-window is for, though the candle is also not quite there.

A Tender, met at close range, is a person who has been in the Sough a long time. Long enough that the doubling has gotten into them. You will see one walking the line of a pipe, checking the weeping joints with a slow hand, and you will notice that the hand is in two slightly different positions, and the Tender does not mind this; the Tender uses both. They wear what looks like oilcloth, or aprons, or nothing in particular — the clothing is also under-committed. Their faces, when they turn toward you, are kind in a tired way. They do not speak, mostly. When they do, the words arrive a little before or after the mouth moves, and you accept this, because by the time you have met a Tender you have accepted a great deal.

What they do: they tend. Not the engines exactly — the engines do not need tending, or have arranged not to. The Tenders tend the *agreement* between the engines and the country. When a pipe's weeping has gotten too enthusiastic, a Tender will come and lay a hand on it and the joint will quiet. When a new mud-island is lifting itself in a place that would inconvenience a pump-house, a Tender will go and stand on the island for a few nights and the island will lift itself somewhere else instead. They are diplomats between the made things and the making thing.

I do not think they were born here. I think they walked in, the way you might, and stayed long enough to become partial. There is a process. It is not unpleasant. It involves sleeping on the hummocks more often than you meant to.

The oldest Tenders — the ones who have been here longest — are nearly Quiet Ones themselves. You will find one sitting in a doorway, very still, and realize after a while that the warmth of her is conversing with something out in the reeds, slow flushes passing back and forth, and that she has not used her mouth for years and does not miss it. She is content. She is, in a manner of speaking, fed.

## The Engine-Things

And then there is what lives *in* the engines, which is harder.

The fireboxes glow because something inside them is hot, yes, but the heat is not from coal — there is no coal, no one carries fuel, no ash heaps accumulate. The engines are warm the way a sleeping animal is warm. The *huh-chuh, huh-chuh* is not quite mechanical; if you put your ear to the iron flank of a pumping station you will hear, under the reciprocation, something like breathing. Not labored. Slow and even, the breathing of a large thing that has been asleep a long time and intends to keep being asleep.

I do not think the engines are inhabited by something. I think the engines *are* something, and have taken the shape of engines because the shape is useful and because, at some point long ago, somebody who knew about pipework and fireboxes came through here and the country took notes. The engines pump because pumping is what the shape does. What they actually move, through those warm weeping pipes, is harder to name. Some of it is water. Some of it is the slow stuff the Quiet Ones eat. Some of it, I suspect, is the very thinness of reality here — circulated, kept moving, kept from settling, because if it ever settled the Sough would have to decide what it was, and the Sough is not ready.

The engines, in this sense, are the country's heart. They keep it provisional. They are why the footprints don't agree and the herons stand in two places and the candles wander at the edge of sight. Stop the engines and the Sough would resolve, and in resolving, end. The Tenders know this. The Quiet Ones, I think, know this too, in their heat-language. Everyone living here is, in their way, complicit in the unfinishedness. It is the condition of their being here at all.

## What Else

There are smaller things. Something lives in the standing water that is not quite fish — you see the dimples of their feeding at the surface, but no fish-shape under, just a sort of localized attention in the water itself. There are the things that walk on many polite legs, which may be a kind of Quiet One or may be their own kingdom. There is, once or twice a year (a year being a thing the Sough tolerates rather than observes), a passage of something very large overhead, felt as a pressure-change and a long warm gust, which the Tenders go out to watch though there is nothing to see. They do not say what it is. I am not sure they know. I am sure they love it.

And there is the *looked-at* dark in certain places — those patches you learn to walk around. Something lives there. Or something is buried there. Or something is being slowly assembled there, by the country, out of the accumulating slow stuff, and is not ready to be met. The Tenders give those places the widest berth of all. When I asked one (by gesture, by warming my palm toward her in the way I had learned), she warmed back, very gently, in a pattern I understood to mean: *not yet. Not for a long while yet. But yes. Something.*

So that is who lives in the Sough.

The Quiet Ones, abundant and unseen, rationing the reeds and speaking in heat.

The Tenders, partial people, keeping the peace between the engines and the country, becoming slowly Quiet themselves.

The Engine-Things, sleeping animals in the shape of pumping stations, circulating the thinness that keeps the place possible.

The small unfinished fauna in the water and on the mud.

The great passing thing, occasional, beloved.

And whatever is gathering, in the looked-at dark, toward some eventual arrival no one is rushing.

They are all, in their way, the same population. They are what the Sough has so far made of the idea of living. It is not done making them. That, more than anything, is what it means to be from here: to be a draft of something the country has not yet decided to finish, and to be at peace with that, and to go on — quietly, warmly, in the long patient thud of the engines — being provisional together.
