# The Inhabitants of the Cinder Tier

They are called, by themselves and by no one else, **the after-people**, though only in the sense that a stone in a riverbed might be called "after-water" — descriptively, without much weight. They do not know whose city they live in. They know it was not built for them. This has been true for so many generations that it is no longer a wound, or even a curiosity. It is simply a feature of the world, like the warmth coming up through the floor.

---

They are tall and thin, which is what altitude and patience do to a lineage given enough time. Their skin is the color of unfired clay, dusted permanently with the brass-sift, which has worked its way so deeply into the creases of their hands and the corners of their eyes that the dust and the person have become, in some literal sense, the same substance. When an after-person dies — rarely, and late — the body is laid on a warm floor in one of the disused halls, and within a season it has gone to sift, and the sift is left where it lies, and the wind takes it where it will.

Their eyes are large and dark and slightly nictitating, an adaptation to the sift-wind that no one alive remembers acquiring. Their hair, when they have it, is fine and pale and tends to lift slightly in the static of the dust, so that an after-person at rest in a dim room often appears faintly haloed. They do not find this remarkable. Visitors — and there have been visitors, though not in any of the current generation's memory — have apparently found it striking.

They are not numerous. Perhaps four thousand of them along the four-hundred-kilometer spine, which works out to a density that is essentially gaseous. Whole neighborhoods of the city contain no one. A person might walk for two days along the ridge and meet three others, exchange the small courteous nod that serves as their primary greeting, and walk on. They are not lonely. Loneliness is a condition of expecting company; they do not expect it, and so they do not lack it.

---

Their **language** is spoken softly, because the sift eats sound and there is no point in shouting. It has accreted slowly over a very long time and is full of words for textures of dust, gradations of warmth, the precise quality of a furnace's hum in its various phases. It has no word for *hurry*, though it has several for different kinds of stillness. It has no word for *ocean* or *forest* or *rain*, and the old loan-words for these things, if they ever existed, have been worn smooth and lost. It has a particularly rich vocabulary for the warm things — not names for kinds of them, but names for *postures*: the way one drapes a gantry-rail differs from the way one drapes a cooling pipe, and these have different words, and the words are used in poetry, of which the after-people have a great deal, almost all of it short.

They do not write, exactly. They mark. A finger drawn through sift on a windowsill leaves a line, and the line will last until the wind moves it, which might be hours or might be weeks. Important things are marked in places where the wind seldom reaches — the lee sides of certain interior columns, the undersides of catwalks. There is a column in the lower city that has been marked and re-marked for so long that the sift has fused into a low relief, and the relief is, if you know how to read it, a kind of chronicle. No one reads the whole of it. People add to it and move on. The after-people's sense of history is cumulative rather than narrative: things have happened, and the evidence is here, and one does not need to put them in order.

---

They **work**, in the loose sense that they tend. Every after-person, in the course of a life, learns the care of some portion of the still-running machinery — not how it functions, which is unrecoverable, but how it sounds when it is well and how it sounds when it is not. A tender of a furnace-bank can tell from a quarter-kilometer away whether her charges are in good humor. If they are not, she will go and stand near them, and listen, and sometimes adjust a valve whose purpose she does not know but whose position she has learned to read. The machinery responds to this attention. Whether it would run without it is an open question, and not one the after-people are inclined to test.

There are tenders of furnaces, tenders of relays, tenders of the great cooling-fins on the cliff-edges, tenders of the chimneys (a respected and slightly eerie profession, owing to the long things). There are also keepers of the **draping-halls**, who do nothing but maintain the temperature of certain floors at precisely the gradient the warm things prefer, and who are considered, gently, to have the best job.

A few after-people tend nothing, and this is also fine. They walk the ridge. They mark columns. They sit with the warm things. The economy, such as it is, runs on the understanding that everyone will, over a long enough span, contribute approximately what they take, and since what anyone takes is very little — heat, sift-filtered air, the occasional mineral lick from the deep galleries — the math works out without anyone needing to do it.

---

Their **relationship to the warm things** is the most important thing about them, and the hardest to describe. They do not consider the warm things to be animals, exactly, nor people, nor furniture, nor gods. The closest equivalent in older languages might be *neighbors* — but neighbors of a particular kind, the kind one has lived beside for so long that the question of whether one likes them has dissolved into the question of whether one likes one's own knees. An after-person sleeping in a draping-hall does not displace the warm things; she lies down among them, and one or two will shift slightly to accommodate her, and her body heat will contribute marginally to the floor, and in the morning she will rise and the warm things will not have moved more than a few centimeters in the night, and she will brush a little of their topside-felt from her sleeve without thinking about it, and they will not mind.

It is believed — though "believed" is too strong a word; *assumed*, perhaps, in the way one assumes a floor will hold — that when an after-person dies and goes to sift, some part of what they were enters the warm things, since the warm things subsist on heat and the dying body releases its last heat into the floor. No one teaches this. Children seem to arrive at it on their own, around the age of six or seven, the way children in other worlds arrive at the idea that the moon is following them. It is not a doctrine. It is just the shape the thought takes, here, in this warmth, among these creatures.

The warm things, for their part, are slightly more attentive to after-people than to other visitors. When an after-person walks past, the warm things on nearby surfaces lift the edges of themselves a few millimeters, in something that is not quite a wave and not quite a wing-stretch, and then settle again. The after-people do not return this gesture, because they do not need to. It has already been returned, in the slow accounting of the place, a thousand thousand times.

---

They **have children** rarely and late, and the children are quiet from the start — not solemn, but quiet, as if they understood on arrival that this is a place where sound is a finite resource. Children of the Tier learn to walk on warm floors before cool ones, and so they develop a particular gait, a kind of unhurried rolling step that distributes weight across the foot, because warm slag conducts heat into the sole and one learns not to linger. Adult after-people walk this way too, even on the rare cool surfaces, and it is by this walk that they would be recognized, if there were anyone left to recognize them.

They tell their children very little about the founders. There is little to tell. The founders left. Something pulled them away or something pushed them; the after-people do not speculate, because speculation, in a place that has finished becoming itself, feels like a kind of bad manners. What the children learn instead is the names of the furnace-phases, the postures of the warm things, the routes along the ridge that are safe in sift-wind and the ones that are not, and the long courteous protocols by which two people meeting in a corridor decide, without speaking, which of them will step aside.

---

They are not waiting for anything. This is perhaps the most important fact about them, and the one most likely to be missed by an observer from a faster world. They are not waiting for the founders to return, or for the furnaces to fail, or for a sign, or for water, or for the end. They have heard, dimly, through stories whose provenance is lost, that there are places where people wait. They find this difficult to imagine, and slightly sad, in the abstract way one might find a distant illness sad.

What they do instead of waiting is **attend**. They attend to the furnaces, to the warm things, to the sift, to one another, and to the long quiet weather of the Tier. Attention is their primary verb. It is what they are for, as far as they have ever needed to decide what they are for, which is not very far.

At night, when the static-sparks travel up their arms and the warm things below have gone so still they could be hearths, the after-people sit in their thin constellation of lit windows along the ridge, and some of them mark columns, and some of them speak softly to children, and some of them simply sit, and the furnaces breathe up through the floor, and the Tier holds them, warmly, in the long slow palm of itself, and they are — in a way that almost no other people in any other place have ever quite managed to be — at home.
