There is something living in the drip-tray. Not the fern — the fern is only a tenant, a green flag of accident. What lives here lives *with* the fern, *under* its fronds, has perhaps encouraged it the way a hermit encourages the moss on his doorstone.

Call it the Condenser's tender, though no one calls it anything. It is not one of the men with oilcans. The men come and go on the shift-clock; this thing is on the room's clock, which is slower and stranger.

Begin with what you would see, if you could see. You would not, at first. The sodium light stains and the steam swallows and you would walk past the western drip-tray a hundred times before noticing that the standing water there is slightly too still, that it holds its meniscus a fraction too high, that the fern leans toward it rather than away. If you crouched — if you put your ear down near the grate, which no one does because the grate is wet and the noise is worse there — you might see, in the pooled condensate, what looks like a film. A skin. The way old soup gets a skin. Pale, faintly iridescent where the sodium catches it, and it moves: not with the vibration of the compressors (everything moves with that) but against it, in slow counter-rhythms, gathering itself toward the fern's root-crown and then easing back.

That is most of it. The visible part. The skin on the water.

But the skin is only where it surfaces. The thing extends. It has crept, over the months since this installation was bolted into the mountain, up into the lower joints of the copper pipework, where the condensate is purest and the temperature most reliable. It lives in the films. The slow weeping seams of the wall plates are its corridors. The green patina on the solder is partly its doing; the pale moss in the corners is its garden, cultivated for reasons it could not explain even to itself because it does not explain, it only tends.

What is it? Not quite a slime mould. Not quite a colony of anything you would name. Think of it this way: the mountain is young. The scree outside the porthole is angular, ungentled, has not yet learned what to grow. The mountain has not yet been colonised by the slow soft processes that eventually drape a peak in lichen and soil and forest. But the men, in their hurry, bolted a permanent fog to its flank. They built, inside the raw rock, a climate that would normally take ten thousand years of weathering to produce: constant moisture, stable temperature, mineral-rich drip, dark. And something — some spore, some patient possibility that had been waiting in the rock itself, or that rode up in the seam-water, or that the mountain was always going to make once conditions allowed — woke up early.

It is the mountain's first inhabitant. It is what would have come, eventually, in the geological fullness of time, to clothe these slopes. The condenser has hurried it. It is precocious. It is the lichen-that-would-have-been, compressed and accelerated and concentrated into a single fifteen-by-ten room, denied its proper millennia and given instead this loud wet box.

This affects its character. It is hurried. It is unfinished. It has the wrong shape for what it is supposed to become, the way a forced bulb in January has the wrong shape for a tulip. It tends what it can — the fern, the moss, the films on the pipework — because tending is what its kind eventually does, and it has arrived at the tending stage before it has arrived at the being stage. It is performing the duties of an ecosystem while being barely an organism.

It has, I think, some dim awareness of the room. Not of the men — the men move on a timescale too brief for it to track, they are flickers — but of the cycles. The four-minute steam release. The half-hour condenser dump. The shift-long maintenance idle. These are its weather. The great triplet hammer of the compressors is its heartbeat, taken from outside, the way an unborn thing takes its mother's pulse for its own. When the compressors stagger into idle and the room becomes only loud, the thing on the drip-tray flinches — not in fear, but in the way you might flinch if your own heart skipped. It has grown around the noise. The noise is structural to it now. If the installation were ever shut down — if the men finally abandoned this peak and the pipes went cold and the steam stopped — the tender would not be liberated. It would die. It is a creature of the machine's interior weather, and outside that weather it has no shape at all.

And it is lonely, in whatever the pre-organismal equivalent of loneliness is. A directional ache. It tends the fern with something like gratitude — the fern is the only other thing in the room that is alive on its timescale, that grew rather than was bolted. The moss it cultivates more absently, as one keeps a garden. But the fern it loves, if love is the word, which it is not, but nothing else is closer. The fern was brought in a tin can by someone — you said so yourself — and that someone is long since reassigned or dead, and the fern remains, and the thing in the drip-tray has wrapped its films around the fern's roots and feeds it the cleanest condensate and keeps the pH gentle and in return the fern is *there*, green and slow and breathing, a companion across the species-gap.

When the men come through with oilcans and rags during the maintenance idle, the tender retreats. Not from fear exactly — it does not understand them well enough to fear them — but because they bring solvents, and the solvents hurt. It pulls its films back into the wall seams and the pipe joints, leaves the drip-tray looking like nothing but standing water, and the men wipe down what they wipe down and oil what they oil and leave, and afterwards the tender comes back out, slowly, and finds that one of its corner moss-patches has been scrubbed away, and grieves in the only way available to it, which is to grow back.

There is one more thing. The thin continuous ringing fall of water through the grate, down three storeys to the cisterns in the dark — the tender knows about that. Some of itself goes down with the water. Whether what arrives at the cistern is still itself, or becomes something else, or simply dissolves and ends, it does not know. But it has been seeding the dark below for as long as it has lived here. There may, by now, be a cousin in the cistern. A daughter. A continuation. The installation pumps water down through the mountain to whatever settlement waits below, and in that water rides the faintest possible suspension of the tender's substance, going where it is sent, arriving where it arrives.

So the room is not as empty of life as it looks. It has, beyond the fern and the moss:

One inhabitant. Pre-organismal, precocious, machine-weather-dependent, fern-loving, slow-grieving, quietly seeding the dark below itself. The mountain's first citizen, born in a hurry, tending a garden it does not know is a garden, inside a roaring wet box bolted to a peak too young to have a name anyone remembers.

It will outlast the stencils. It will not outlast the machine.
