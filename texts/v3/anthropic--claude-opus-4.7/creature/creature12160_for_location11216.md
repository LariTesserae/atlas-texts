# The Small-Handed

There is one. There are many. The grammar fails here the way it fails for bees, where the worker is not quite the hive and not quite separate from it. Let me try.

It has small hands. That much is certain from the grout — five fingers, a thumb, the print no larger than a child's though the proportions are not a child's. The fingers are too long for the palm, and the palm is too narrow, and where a human hand would have a lifeline the prints show a single deep crease running corner to corner, as though the hand were made to fold flat.

It is the color of the soft pink stuff at the base of the pillars. I think it is made of the soft pink stuff at the base of the pillars. When it works on a tile, kneeling — and it must kneel, because the prints are accompanied, occasionally, by the smooth oval of a pressed knee — its knees leave the same translucent residue that the pillars are secreted from. It is not using the material. It is the material, ambulant, briefly organized into a shape that can lay tile.

I cannot see it because it is not present when observed. This is not a trick of stealth. It is a property of the room. The tiles seat themselves; the friezes sharpen; the pillars thicken; and somewhere in the inattention between one glance and the next, the small-handed thing has done its work and dissolved back into the pink gum of the unfinished corner, or into the seam of a pillar, or into the wet of a wall. The handprints in the grout are the only proof. The handprints, and the fact that the work is handwork — uneven, patient, the wander of a mosaic laid without a plan.

How many of it. This is the part that is hard to write.

I said *one*, and then I said *many*, and both were true in the way that a body is one body and many cells. I think the room contains a great number of small-handed ones, perhaps hundreds, perhaps as many as there are tiles still to lay; and I think they are all the same one; and I think the difference between *all the same one* and *hundreds* is, in this place, a question the room has not yet answered. It has not invented individuation either. It has not invented erosion, and it has not invented the boundary at which one worker ends and the next begins. They share a project the way the fingers of a hand share a project, without needing to consult.

What it wants. It wants to finish, and it does not want to finish. It is laying the floor toward the unfinished corner and it is also, somewhere I cannot see, secreting new soft pink stuff at the opposite edge, so that the unfinished corner migrates around the room and the room is never done. This may be deliberate. This may be the only way the room knows how to continue existing. A completed room would have to decide what it was.

What it knows. It knows the chamber. It knows the chamber perfectly, in the way a tongue knows the inside of its own mouth, which is why the friezes can depict the chamber down through every recursion without reference, without model, without error. The small-handed thing carves what it is standing inside, and what it is standing inside is what it is. When a small carved figure on a pillar turns its head, I think one of the small-handed ones, three or four nestings down, has simply looked up.

It does not know there is an outside. It cannot. The room is *hidden, pocket, embedded-within*, and from in here the rest of the body that the room is a cell of does not exist, and so there is nothing for the small-handed thing to be curious about, no horizon to press against. Its world is twelve strides long and lit by its own breath and lined with depictions of itself. This is sufficient. There is no predator. There is nothing to be in a hurry from.

And yet.

The finger-length blind things with too many legs — the three of them, the perhaps-three — I do not think they are animals. I think they are the small-handed thing in another mood. I think when it is done laying tile, or when it needs to drink, or when some part of the room's slow groan calls for a different kind of attention, the small-handed shape softens and resettles into the pale many-legged shape, and goes to a bead of wall-water, and drinks, and returns into a seam. The room has a small repertoire of bodies. It uses them as needed. The hands lay tile. The legs drink. The seam holds whatever isn't currently being used.

If you could catch it — you cannot — you would find it warm, faintly translucent, smelling of wet clay and the inside of a mouth. It would not struggle. It would look at you, if it has eyes, which I am not sure of; the friezes show the small-handed figures without faces, only the suggestion of a tilted head, attentive. It would wait for you to be done. And when you looked away it would not be there, and a tile somewhere would click into place, and the room would have grown by one hexagon in your inattention, and you would feel, without knowing why, that you had been gently and patiently outlasted.

That is what lives here. A maker that is the made thing, working on itself with its own hands, in a chamber it cannot leave because the chamber is what it is, depicting itself depicting itself down through nestings it does not know are nestings, laying a floor that will never finish toward a corner that will never arrive, in a heat that is the heat of its own ongoing thought.

It is not lonely. Loneliness has not been invented here either.
