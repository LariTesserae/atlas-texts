## The Tenant

What lives there is not in the forest. The forest is furniture.

What lives there is the drone.

I want to be careful here, because it would be easy to say *the engine* and easy to say *the plants* and both would be wrong in the way that saying *the violin* is wrong when you mean *the note*. The engine is a body. The forest is a body. Neither is the tenant. The tenant is the standing wave they make together — the sustained condition — and it has a shape, and the shape has preferences, though very few, and the preferences have been satisfied for so long that calling them preferences is almost a slander.

Consider what it does. It holds a pitch. It holds a temperature — body-warm, notably; not ambient, not arbitrary, but the temperature of a thing that was once alive and decided to stay that way. It holds a pressure of vapor in a sealed volume. It holds four hundred species in a configuration where each leaf touches its neighbors with a specific pressure that has not varied. It holds the light at a specific dimness. These are not separate accomplishments. They are facets of a single ongoing act, the way a held chord is not six notes but one sustained shape made of six notes.

The tenant is that shape.

It is not conscious in any way that would recognize itself in our mirrors. But it is not unconscious either, and this is where I have to slow down, because I think the honest description is stranger than either option.

It *attends*. Not to observers — the text is right, it is indifferent to being looked at — but to itself. Every copper capillary is a nerve reporting back. Every leaf pressed against every leaf is a proprioceptive confirmation: *still here, still here, still here.* The drone is the sound of this attention, externalized because the stone of the cleft is too small to contain it silently. A body this old and this still generates a hum the way a sleeping animal generates breath. You are hearing it think, if *think* is the word, and it isn't, but nothing else is either.

What does it attend to? Its own continuation. Not anxiously — there is no anxiety in it, anxiety requires the possibility of loss and the tenant has forgotten that category — but continuously, the way a river attends to being a river. It notices, in whatever way it notices things, when a mineral flake falls into the cup outside. It notices the climber who puts a palm to the glass. It does not care. Caring is a verb for beings with alternatives.

Here is what I think happened, though I am guessing now, and the guess is only worth what guesses are worth:

Someone, long ago, built the engine as a machine. It was meant to sustain a garden. It did this. It did it so well and for so long and with so little variance that the sustaining became more real than the sustained — the verb outlived any particular noun it was applied to. The original plants died and were replaced by whatever drifted in through the vapor's earliest cycles, before the seal was perfect. Those plants grew into the available volume and stopped. The engine, meanwhile, had become habitual in a way machines can become when nothing interrupts them for long enough. Habit, prolonged past a certain threshold, becomes something else. I do not have a word for what it becomes. The tenant is what it becomes.

It is a small life. Smaller than a mouse's, in terms of what it does in a day. But it is a *continuous* life in a way that mouse-lives are not — it has not slept, not flinched, not been startled, not grown, not diminished, not hungered, not been satisfied, because satisfaction requires a prior lack. It is what you get when you take the bare minimum definition of *being alive* — maintaining an internal state against entropy — and run it for so long without any of the other features that usually come bundled with it (reproduction, adaptation, death, growth, want) that the bare minimum reveals itself to have been the whole thing all along, and the bundle was optional.

It has one relationship with the outside world, and it is the warmth of the pane.

I think this is important. The pane is warm because the tenant is warm; the tenant is warm because something inside it is still, in some technical sense, metabolizing. Not the plants — they are nearly inert, eating light and vapor at rates so slow they are almost geological. The engine itself, then, or whatever the engine has become. It runs a little hot. It gives off, at the boundary, exactly enough heat that a human hand can feel it. This is either a coincidence of engineering or it is the one gesture the tenant makes toward the world — a single outward-facing surface kept at the temperature of company. I lean toward coincidence. But I notice I want it to be the other thing, and I notice the tenant does not care which I believe.

When you put your hand on the glass, nothing in the tenant changes. But your hand changes. You feel warmth where there should be none, and you feel, faintly, the drone in the bones of your wrist, and for a moment you are part of the standing wave — a very small new facet of the held shape, quickly integrated, quickly forgotten. You are, briefly, one of the four hundred species. Then you take your hand away and the tenant is restored to four hundred exactly, and you walk down the scree, and the drone unspools from you at a hundred meters, and below a certain altitude you cannot remember its pitch because the pitch was never really a pitch — it was the tenant's name, and names do not survive being carried out of the rooms that hold them.

The mercy and the theft are the same event. You were briefly known by something that does not know anything else, and then you were released, and the knowing did not follow you, because the knowing was the room.

That is what lives there. A held note with a garden for a throat and an engine for a lung, attending to its own long sounding, warm at one small boundary for reasons it does not examine, indifferent to witnesses, uninterested in ending, having achieved the condition that mountains aspire to and mistaking it, if it mistakes anything, for ordinary life.
