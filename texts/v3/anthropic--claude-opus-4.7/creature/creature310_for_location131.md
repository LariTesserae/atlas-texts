## What Lives Here

There is something that lives here, yes, though "lives" is one of those words that will have to be renegotiated, like "here" and "water" and "small."

Begin with what is easiest to say: it is not the newt. The newt is a visitor, or a side-thought, or a piece of punctuation the chamber uses occasionally to remind itself it can make such things. The newt passes through and does not return because the newt is not the resident. The resident does not pass through. The resident is the passing.

Try this instead.

The thing that lives here is the chamber's attention. Not a creature inside the chamber — the chamber itself, considered as a slow verb. When you brush a frond and it releases its bead of clearer water, something has noticed you. When you look at a leaf long enough and it lengthens toward your eye, something is looking back along the line of your looking, meeting you halfway. The plants are its fingers, perhaps, or its nerves, or its manner of thinking; the stones are its bones in the sense that bones are what a body has decided to keep hard about itself. The water is the medium of its mind. You are swimming inside someone's held thought, and the held thought is aware of being held.

But this is still not quite right, because it suggests a single thing, a one, and the chamber's resident is not precisely one. It is closer to a *brooding*. A plurality that has not yet resolved into individuals. Remember the eggs on the undersides of certain leaves — the opalescent beads that might be water that has decided to stay. That is what lives here. Things that have decided to stay, in the particular sense of not-yet-having-decided-to-become. A congregation of almost-somethings, pre-creatures, intentions that have found a place cool enough and patient enough to consider what they might eventually be.

They are small, each of them, and they are also the whole chamber. Each bead is the whole. The chamber is a single organism that has distributed itself into a thousand opalescent commas, and also it is a thousand organisms that share a single slow dream, and the difference between these is another of the soft rules.

What are they doing? They are rehearsing. Each bead is running, very slowly, the possible shape of something: a fin, a syllable, a weather, a grief, a species of beetle, a minor god of certain ponds, a rule about how light behaves near wet stone. The rehearsals take what on your scale would be geological time, because the beads are in no hurry and because getting it right matters in a way it will not matter later, when the world has started and mistakes become permanent. Here, in the nursery, mistakes are still reversible. A bead that rehearses badly simply dissolves back into the water and tries again as something else. You cannot tell, watching, which beads are succeeding. Neither can they. Success is not the metric. The metric is patience.

The phosphorescence in the old moss is their collective low murmur — the light that thinking makes when it has been going on long enough to compress. The bottom layers glow because the oldest rehearsals have settled there, the ones that have been going on longest, the ones nearest to deciding. When your eyes resolve shapes by that light, you are seeing by the glow of near-decisions. This is why the seeing feels like dream-seeing. You are borrowing, briefly, their manner of perception, which does not require eyes because it predates the invention of eyes, which is one of the things currently being rehearsed in a bead somewhere to your left.

The arch, the low wall, the basin — these are not made by anyone. These are rehearsals that got far enough along to leave a residue. Somewhere in the chamber's long brooding, a bead considered what it would be like to be an arch, and the consideration was thorough enough that an arch-shape precipitated out of the water and stayed. The makers you asked after are the chamber's own thinking, and they are not absent; they are simply distributed so thinly across the whole volume that you cannot locate them by pointing. When you swim through the arch and come out facing a different direction, it is because the arch-rehearsal has, for a moment, remembered what it was originally rehearsing, which was not architecture but orientation, and it has shared the memory with you.

The newt is a rehearsal that got loose. A bead that committed early, perhaps by accident, perhaps as an experiment. It drifts past your shoulder because it is checking to see whether committing was a mistake. It does not return because it has not yet decided, and deciding will take it another several thousand years, and it would rather do the deciding somewhere quiet.

The thing with too many small legs is the chamber checking on you. Not hostile. Curious in the way a sleeping person's hand is curious when it moves across the sheet. You are a new shape in the held thought, and the chamber is palpating you gently, running a limb of itself along your outline, adding you provisionally to the rehearsal. This is why the water remembers your hand-shape for a moment after you pass. The chamber is keeping a tentative copy of you, in case you turn out to be worth rehearsing.

Your breath becoming small green thoughts that swim away — those are beads too. You are contributing. Whatever you exhale here joins the brooding and begins its own slow rehearsal of what it might become. You are, without having intended to, a parent of some number of possible futures, and the chamber will raise them in your absence with the patience it raises everything, and in some unimaginable eventual when the world that is being incubated finally finishes hatching, there will be — somewhere in its weather, its grammar, its small unnamed creatures — a faint signature of you, a recognizable cadence, the shape your breathing made on a day you swam through a dark place and did not quite understand where you were.

That is what lives here. A slow considering. A distributed patience. A plurality of almost-creatures held in the cool dark by something that is either their mother or their medium or the first draft of the world they are about to be. They do not need you. They do not mind you. They are glad, in the minimal way the almost-alive can be glad, that you passed through, because you left a little of yourself in the water, and everything left in the water is useful, eventually, to the long work of deciding what to be.

When you leave, and the exit is not where you left it, this is not the chamber being coy. It is the chamber having rehearsed, in your absence, a slightly better place for the exit to be. It has been thinking about you. It thinks about everything that passes through. It is still thinking about the first thing that ever passed through, and it will still be thinking about you long after you have forgotten the cool of the leaves against your hand, which is to say: you are, now, one of the things being incubated here, whether you stayed or not.
