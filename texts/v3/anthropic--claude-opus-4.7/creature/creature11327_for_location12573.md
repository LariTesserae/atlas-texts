The people of the bowl are small.

Not small as a curiosity, not small the way a child is small with the promise of unfolding into something larger — small as a settled fact, the way a wren is small, the way certain mosses are small. An adult stands perhaps to the height of your hip. This is what the bowl asked for, over the long slow generations of asking, and this is what was given. You cannot live four canopies deep in a forest that tops out at five meters and remain the size of the things that built cathedrals. The branches would not hold you. The rope-bridges would snap. The doorways would have to be re-cut, and there is no one here who believes in re-cutting doorways.

Their bodies are compact and long-fingered, made for grip. The hands are the most striking thing about them if you have the chance to watch a hand at work — disproportionate, almost, with fingers that close around a vine or a tool or the rim of a nectar-vessel with the unthinking certainty of a second mouth. The feet are similar. They go barefoot or in soft wrappings of woven bark, and the soles are dark and tough and slightly splayed, the toes still mobile, still capable of holding. A child learning to walk learns also to climb in the same week; the two are not separate skills here. A grown person moves through the layered branches with the absentminded fluency that you move down a familiar hallway.

Their skin tends toward the colors the bowl tends toward — the brown of wet bark, the grey-green of lichen, the duskier umbers of the deep moss. Whether this is because they have shaped themselves to the place or because the place has, in some slow exchange of substance, shaped them, no one inside the bowl thinks to ask. They are simply the color of where they are. Their hair is dark, often, and many wear it long and braided through with the small things that accumulate in a life lived close to a forest — a feather, a polished seed, a length of fiber dyed pale with flower-pulp. The braids serve a purpose. Loose hair catches on branches, and a branch caught is a moment lost, and they do not, as a culture, have many moments to spare for catching on things.

They are quiet. This is the second thing you notice, after the size. In a bowl this loud with insect-drone and bird-braid and frog-chime, they have learned not to add to it unnecessarily. They speak, but softly, and a great deal of what passes between them passes without speech — a touch on the wrist, a tilt of the head, a particular way of holding the fingers that means wait, or come, or I have seen it too. Their language, when it surfaces in sound, is full of soft consonants and held vowels, a speech that does not compete with the canopy. A stranger listening would mistake the first several minutes of conversation for ambient noise. This is not accidental. They have spent a long time learning to fit inside the sound of the bowl rather than over it.

They live, as you saw, embedded. A dwelling is not a separate thing from the tree that holds it; the tree grew into the dwelling and the dwelling grew around the tree, and which came first is a question that has lost meaning over the generations it took for them to become indistinguishable. A family's house contains the family, yes, but also: the colony of pale ants that runs its line along the inside of the south wall and has done so for longer than anyone can remember; the tree-frog or two that lives in the rain-cup of the doorpost plant; the moths that roost in the rafters during daylight and leave at dusk in a soft pulse of wings; sometimes a climber from the branches, dropping in to investigate a stored cache of seeds and being shooed gently, without offense on either side. The houses are not theirs alone. Nothing in the bowl is anyone's alone. The concept does not quite translate.

They are long-lived. This is hard to say with certainty because they do not count years the way larger and more hurried peoples do, but a person born in the bowl can expect to see a great many turns of the moss-cycle, a great many mistings and bloomings and the slow black rotting-down of fallen wood into new soil. They age without much hurry. A child is a child for a long while, learning the branches, learning the hands, learning the soft speech and the names of the hundred kinds of insect that share the walls. An elder is an elder for longer still, sitting often at the same favored knot of root or carved stone, weaving or watching or simply being one of the still points in a place defined by small constant motion. There is no urgency about any stage. There is nowhere to be that they are not already.

They eat lightly. Nectar, gathered drop by drop, fermented sometimes into a thin sweet drink. The soft inner pith of certain ferns. The eggs of certain insects, taken in small numbers from large clutches so that the clutch goes on. Fungi, of which the bowl produces a startling variety, pale and dark and ruffled and smooth, some sliced thin and dried on warm stones, some eaten fresh, some used only as medicine. They do not, as a rule, eat the climbers or the birds or the frogs, though this is not a prohibition so much as a forgotten possibility — the question of eating a climber simply does not arise, the way the question of eating one's neighbor does not arise. The climbers are part of the household. So are the birds. So, in their way, are the frogs. You do not eat the household.

They tell stories. Of course they tell stories — every people tells stories, and a people this old has had a long time to accumulate them. But the stories are not about heroes who went somewhere and came back changed, because no one here goes anywhere. The stories are about the bowl. About the time the mist failed for a season and the moss began to grey and what was done. About the tree at the eastern rim that fell in a wind-year and what fell with it. About a child who climbed above the rim once and what the wind taught her, briefly, before the others pulled her back down. About the founder, who was not really a founder, because the bowl has never not had people in it as far as anyone remembers — but who is spoken of anyway, a first one, a small dark figure who looked up at the ringed-in sky and understood that this was the shape of a home. The stories are layered like the canopy. Many versions, many tellings, no single authoritative trunk. You can enter the story-system at any branch and find your way.

They are aware of the world outside the bowl, in the abstract. They know the slopes go down. They know that down at the bottom of the slopes there are wider places, and in the wider places larger people, and the larger people have done things — built and unbuilt, hunted, ruled, marched — that the bowl-people consider with the polite incomprehension of someone listening to a description of a dream that is not their own. A few, over the generations, have gone down to see. Most of those have not come back. The ones who came back came back quiet, and were quiet afterward, and the children of those returned are quieter still, and after three or four generations the going-down is forgotten in that particular family and the line resumes its rooted life. The bowl absorbs its prodigals the way the moss absorbs everything else — slowly, completely, without comment.

They have, perhaps, gods. Or perhaps not gods exactly — the word is too sharp for what they mean. They have presences. The mist is one. The old trees are several. The wind above the rim is a presence that is also a warning, addressed carefully and not often. There is a presence in the deep moss that has to do with the dead and the returning of the dead, and there is a presence in the high canopy that has to do with the climbers and the birds and the things that move and call and live their bright brief lives in the upper light. These are not worshipped, quite. They are acknowledged. A person passing a particular tree may pause and touch the bark with the back of the hand, lightly, the way you might touch the shoulder of a sleeping friend on your way through a room. The tree is the friend. The room is the world.

And finally — because you should know this, even though they would not say it of themselves — they are happy. Not happy as a state to be achieved against opposition; happy as a baseline, a default, the natural condition of a creature whose every need is met by the close warm packed green that surrounds it and whose every relation, with neighbor and ant and frog and tree and stone, is in order and has been in order for longer than memory. There is grief, of course. Children fall. Elders go into the moss. A bad mist-season is hard, and a wind-year harder. But the substrate beneath the grief is contentment, and the substrate beneath the contentment is the bowl, and the bowl is not going anywhere, and so neither, in any meaningful sense, are they.

They are the configuration the life allows. They have folded themselves so completely into the fold that the fold and the folding are no longer distinguishable. If the bowl could be said to dream, it would dream them; and they, in the dim green long hours of their lives, dream the bowl back.
