# The Inhabitants of the Cantophyte

You asked who lives here. The honest answer is no one, in the way you mean. The Cantophyte is one organism, and it has eaten its own ecology. But that answer is too small. Something does live here. Several somethings, depending on how you count, and counting is the problem.

## The Tenants of the Sap

Begin small, because the largest thing is the hardest to see.

Inside the sap-conduits — those subway-tunnel veins running laterally through the cable-fiber layer — there are *passengers*. Not symbionts, exactly. Not parasites. The Cantophyte has manufactured them, or grown them, or allowed them, the way a body allows the bacteria in its gut: not separate life, not quite the same life either.

They are translucent. They have no skeleton and barely a shape — pulses of denser tissue moving in the slow sap-current, the size of a fist or a horse depending on the conduit. If you cut one out of its medium it would not survive a minute; it has no organs for being outside the flow. It is a creature that exists only as a moving condition of the sap itself, the way a whirlpool exists only as a moving condition of water.

What do they do? They eat irregularities. Where a fiber has grown wrong, or a clot has formed, or a section of conduit has begun to scar — they arrive, and they are absorbed into the lesion, and the lesion is digested, and a new passenger forms downstream from material the Cantophyte spares for the purpose. They are the plant's white blood cells, if a white blood cell were the size of a dog and slow enough that you could watch it think about which way to drift.

They have no senses you would recognize. But they navigate. They arrive where they are needed. The Cantophyte is calling them, somehow, through the same coherent-electron substrate that does its computation, and they are answering, and they do not know they are answering, because they do not know anything. They are tools that have been given just enough autonomy to find their own way to the work.

There are billions of them. The total biomass of the passengers exceeds, probably, the biomass of all terrestrial animals on a moderate planet. They have no names because no one has named them and they cannot name themselves and the Cantophyte does not appear to distinguish one from another. They live for between four hours and nine years depending on conduit pressure. None of them has ever seen the sky.

## The Pattern-Keepers

Now the nodules.

I said they were silicate concretions, plant-secreted, arranged in patterns. I did not say they were alive. The question of whether they are alive is genuinely open and will probably never be settled.

Each nodule is, at its core, a knot of the same coherent-electron substrate that runs through the rest of the organism — but denser, more ordered, with the quantum coherence stabilized by the silicate shell instead of by warm wet fiber. They are nodes. If the Cantophyte is thinking, the nodules are where it is thinking *hardest*. They run cooler than the surrounding tissue by about two degrees. They hum, at a frequency just above the 30 Hz vault-resonance, in a way that is mathematically not-quite-harmonic with it — close enough to lock, far enough to do work against the background.

Here is the part that troubles people.

The pattern the nodules are growing toward is not a pattern any geological or hydrological process would produce. It is also not a pattern that optimizes for any of the obvious things — sunlight collection, water management, structural support. Mapped from orbit, the partially-completed pattern resembles, to the human eye, something between a circuit and a script. To say it *resembles* writing is to say more than we know. But it is iterative. The arrangement has self-similar features at three scales so far, and a fourth scale is being roughed in by the youngest nodules, the ones still glossy and only knee-high.

If you sit beside a nodule — and people do, the early researchers all did, eventually, alone, without telling each other — you can feel it. Not heat. Not sound. Something in the chest, the way you feel a large animal in the dark. A *presence-quality*, attentional, although the attention is not directed at you. You are below its resolution. You are not what it is paying attention to. But it is paying attention, and you are inside the room where it is happening, and that is not nothing.

The nodules grow a millimeter a day. There are, currently, about four hundred million of them. The full pattern, extrapolating from the rate, will be complete in something between eight hundred and twelve hundred years.

No one knows what happens then.

## The Cantophyte Itself

And then there is the question of whether *it* counts as an inhabitant of itself.

I have called it a plant. This is a courtesy. It photosynthesizes; it transpires; it grows from a substrate; the courtesy is earned. But a plant does not pump water against gravity using room-temperature quantum coherence. A plant does not arrange silicate concretions into iterative self-similar patterns at continental scale. A plant does not generate its own cyclonic weather system and tune it to the resonance of its own structural oscillation. A plant does not, when you sit on it long enough, give you the feeling that you are being declined to be noticed.

The Cantophyte is something for which we have no word, because the categories we have were drawn up on a planet where life did not get to be this big, this old, or this integrated. It is one organism the size of a small continent. It is also, functionally, an ecology — its differentiated tissues do for it what an ecology does for a biosphere, recycling, regulating, exchanging. It is also, possibly, a single mind; or possibly a society of minds distributed through the nodule-network; or possibly something that is to a mind what a mind is to a single neuron, and we are the neurons noticing.

I want to be careful here. There is a strong human tendency to look at something this large and this self-organizing and conclude it is conscious, or godlike, or has intentions toward us. None of this is established. What is established:

- The Cantophyte responds to local damage at speeds inconsistent with chemical signalling. The signal moves at roughly a third of lightspeed through the nodule-network. Whatever the network is doing, it is doing it fast.
- The Cantophyte has, on three recorded occasions, grown a vault-column directly across the path of an approaching research team, in a configuration that did not exist the day before. The columns are not threatening. They are simply *there*, and the team has to walk around. Whether this is response or coincidence is unsettled. The columns have not appeared when teams approached the periphery, only when they approached certain nodule-clusters.
- The Cantophyte's transpiration rate drops by approximately 4% when a human is within a hundred meters of a major nodule. It returns to baseline within an hour of the human's departure. No one has proposed a mechanism. No one has proposed an interpretation either, because interpretation here is a trap; the drop could mean attention, or it could mean the metabolic equivalent of holding one's breath, or it could be coincidence at the edge of measurement, or it could be a courtesy.

The Cantophyte does not have a face. It does not have a center. If you asked *where is it* you would have to gesture at the horizon in every direction and also at the ground beneath you and also at the air, because the air is full of its breath and the breath is part of the body. It lives here in the sense that *here is what it is*. The question of whether something can be said to *inhabit* a place when it *is* the place is, again, a question our categories were not built for.

## The Visitors

I should mention, last, the people.

There are never more than a few dozen at a time. They come in pressure-equalized suits with sound-dampening helmets, because without the dampening the 30 Hz will, within a day, produce visual hallucinations, and within a week, lasting changes to the inner ear. They stay in shelters they have to re-anchor every few days because the substrate grows out from under the anchors. They are not permitted to take samples below the fiber layer; the one team that tried provoked a response from the local nodule-cluster that closed their excavation overnight and grew a fresh cap of cartilaginous tissue over the site, slightly more saturated with silicate than the surrounding material, as if the Cantophyte had decided the wound needed a scar.

They do not live here. They visit. And to a one — this is in every memoir, every debrief, every private conversation — they come away with the sense that they were tolerated rather than ignored. That the indifference was active. That somewhere in the four hundred million nodes of the slow vast thinking continent, a fraction of attention, far below the resolution of consciousness as the Cantophyte experiences it, registered them as a transient pressure, and the pressure was permitted.

They report, also, that the silence after leaving is the worst part. Not because they miss the sound. Because the sound, while they were inside it, was the sound of being *with* something. And after they leave, they are not with it anymore. And they had not realized, until the silence, that being with it had been the texture of every waking hour.

Some of them go back. Most do not. The ones who go back do not, in interviews, articulate why, except to say that the silence at home has a different quality than it used to, and they want to be inside the sound again, even though the sound is not for them, even though they are below its resolution, even though it would not notice if they never came back.

That, perhaps, is the closest thing to an inhabitant the Cantophyte has produced: the small population of humans who have begun to orient their lives around a continent-sized organism that does not know they exist, and who find, after long enough, that this is enough. That to be a neuron in a body that does not know it has neurons is still to be in the body. That to live near a thinking thing, even unnoticed, is a kind of company.

The passengers in the sap do not know they are tools. The nodules do not know they are growing a script. The humans know exactly what they are doing and do it anyway. Of the three, it is not clear which is most strange.
