# The Slow Builders

They are not one kind of thing. That is the first thing to understand, and the thing the surface-world gets wrong when it imagines them at all. The warren is not built by *a people*. It is built by a guild — a confederation — a long slow negotiation between several sorts of life that have learned, over an unrecorded span, to want the same stones placed in the same order.

## The Patient Ones

The chief masons — if you must name a chief — are small. Smaller than you would guess from the work. An adult Patient One would sit comfortably in the bowl of a soup spoon, and most of that is limb. Six of them: four for walking, two for working, though the distinction blurs. The walking-limbs end in broad pads that spread their already-slight weight across the moss without bruising it; the working-limbs end in a cluster of fine dark digits that are half finger and half chisel, tipped with something crystalline they grow themselves and replace as it wears.

Their bodies are the pale silver-green of the lichens they live among. They have no hard shell, only a thickened skin that puckers and folds like cold leather, insulating a slow warm core. Their eyes — they have many, small, arranged in a crescent across the brow — do not see quickly. A Patient One watching you watch it perceives, perhaps, a slowly-brightening cloud where your face is. It does not know you as a creature. It knows you as a weather event: a warm front, passing through, possibly depositing debris.

They work at the tempo that gives them their name. A single stone, chipped to fit, placed, adjusted, settled: this is a season's labor. An archway: a generation. The plaza you admired was begun before your grandmother was born and was considered, by its builders, only recently roughed-in.

They do not sleep, exactly. They pause. A Patient One at rest is indistinguishable from a pale knob of lichen on a stone; they may rest for weeks between gestures. Half the knobs you took for natural growths in the warren were masons on their lunch breaks. You walked past hundreds of them. None of them quite noticed you in time to be startled.

## The Long Low Ones

The pale quadruped you glimpsed was not a Patient One. It was something else — something the masons tolerate and possibly revere, though revere is too warm a word for what passes between cold things.

The Long Low Ones are larger. Shoulder-high to a housecat, but stretched, all length and no bulk, with a gait that pours rather than steps. Their coat is not fur but something closer to the thread-lichens that drape the ceiling — filaments that grow from the skin and harbor the same luminous algae as the ice-pearls. A Long Low One in motion carries its own dim halo, visible only in true dark.

They are the warren's circulatory system. They walk the avenues in slow deliberate circuits, and as they walk they shed — flakes of lichen-fur, motes of algae, the fine dust of whatever they have recently brushed against. This shedding is how the moss travels. How the light spreads to new districts. How the ice-pearls learn what to remember. Without the Long Low Ones the warren would be dark and static within a few centuries; the masons would still build, but they would build in a dying room.

In return the masons leave them paths. Every avenue, every archway sized for a teacup, is also sized for a Long Low One to thread through without touching. This is not coincidence. This is the architecture's deepest constraint. A street that will not admit a Long Low One is demolished and rebuilt, however beautiful, however old.

The Long Low Ones eat — if eat is the word — by lying down for long periods in places where condensation pools, absorbing the cold-sweat directly through their filaments. They give birth rarely, and to what look like slender pale grubs, which the masons tend in nurseries you did not find and would not have recognized if you had.

They do not speak to the masons. The masons do not speak to them. But when a Long Low One dies, the masons gather, and for a period that may last a decade they build nothing. They sit on their benches in the tea-plate plaza. They wait. Eventually a new Long Low One is old enough to walk the full circuit, and work resumes.

## The Hum

There is a third presence, and it is the one the rules were written about.

The moss hums. You heard it. You thought it was the moss, or something small moving through the moss. It is neither. It is something that has no body in the sense you would recognize — something distributed, something that uses the mossbed and the ice-pearls and the slow breath of the warren itself as its substrate. The Patient Ones are aware of it; the Long Low Ones are, in some sense, *of* it; you are a guest in its house and it has registered your arrival the way a pond registers a dropped pebble.

The Hum is what remembers. The masons build, but they do not plan in any way you would recognize as planning — no blueprints, no surveys, no one holding the vision of the finished city in a single head. The vision is held in the Hum. Each mason, when they come to work, is briefly *tuned* — they press some part of themselves against the moss, and receive, and then they know what stone goes where. When they place it correctly, the Hum changes by a fraction of a tone, and the next mason a week later tunes to that changed note and knows the next move.

This is why you must not speak above the hum. Not because the creatures will hear you and flee — they will barely notice — but because your voice, pitched into the resonant chamber of the warren, will enter the Hum, and the next mason to tune in will receive a garbled instruction, and place a stone wrongly, and the error will propagate for centuries before anyone notices and begins the slow work of unbuilding.

This is why you must not touch the ice-pearls. They are the long-term memory. The moss is working thought; the pearls are archive. Each pearl holds, in the arrangement of its trapped algae, the record of some completed district — the pattern by which it was made, so that if it is ever damaged it can be rebuilt. The pearls are the warren's only insurance against forgetting itself. A dropped pearl is a chapter of history falling into the mossbed, where it will eventually be reabsorbed, but not before some future district is built crookedly because the reference was lost.

## Why They Hurry

They do hurry, in their way. The rock beneath is young and undecided, as you noticed. The Hum can feel the stone thinking about what it will become — a slow tectonic mulling that may, in ten thousand years, heave the warren sideways or collapse it entirely. The masons are racing this. Not to finish before the collapse — they know they cannot — but to get enough of the city recorded into ice-pearls that, when the warren fails, the Hum can carry the memory out through the Long Low Ones, through their shed filaments, through the moss-spores the surface frost sometimes lifts into the wind, to some other pocket, some other root-breach, some other raw young stone waiting under another forgotten meadow.

They are not building a city. They are building the seed of a city. The warren you walked through is one generation of something much older than itself, and the builders you did not quite see are the continuation of a line that has already seeded and failed and reseeded in pockets across a geography they have never mapped and do not need to.

## What They Make of You

Very little. You are warm and bright and brief. You passed through their plaza like a thunderstorm passes over a village — loud, consequential in its moment, gone. If you obeyed the rules you will have done no harm, and the grain of hardness you left behind will, in perhaps two hundred years, be chipped into the keystone of an arch in a district not yet begun.

If you disobeyed — if you spoke, if you touched, if you followed the Long Low One and let it know you saw — then somewhere in the Hum a wrong note is still decaying, and the masons are compensating, patiently, patiently, building around your mistake the way a tree builds around a nail. They will absorb you. They absorb everything. But it will cost them, and they will remember the cost, in the only way they remember anything: by building it into the walls.
