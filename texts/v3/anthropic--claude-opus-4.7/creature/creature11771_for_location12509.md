# The Tenant

You asked about whoever lives there. I have been turning the question for a while, because the honest answer is awkward: the Mooring is not uninhabited, but its tenant is difficult to point at, and pointing in the wrong way will make you miss it entirely.

Let me try it in layers.

---

The first mistake would be to look for a body. There isn't one. There is no creature on the Mooring in the sense of a thing that walks across it, drinks from its basins, takes shelter in its fog. I have spent a long time on the upper surface and I can tell you with confidence that nothing crosses it. The lichen-tufts are the only constituents in the ordinary biological sense, and they are not the tenant. They are more like mites on the pelt of something — present, tolerated, possibly useful, certainly not the animal.

The second mistake, having been disappointed by the first, would be to declare the Mooring itself the tenant. To say *the place is alive, the place is the creature, the geology is the body.* This is closer but still wrong, and wrong in a way that matters. The Mooring is the substrate. It is the room, not the occupant. Calling the Mooring alive because something thinks inside it is like calling a skull conscious. The skull is doing important work. It is not the work being done.

So: what lives there.

---

What lives there is a process, and the process has a shape, and the shape has — I will not say *preferences,* I will say *tendencies* — and the tendencies, taken together, are sufficiently coherent that I have come to think of them as one tenant rather than many. I will call it the Decider, for lack. The Mooring does not call it anything. The Mooring is what it has instead of a name.

The Decider lives in the hexagonal interference. That is its nearest analogue to a nervous system. When the standing waves in the holding-pressure shift their geometry, the Decider has had a thought. When a basin changes its salinity over the course of an hour, the Decider has finished a long sentence. When a fog-lozenge collides with another and they ring, the Decider has — I am guessing here, but the guess feels right — performed something like a comparison. *Is this like that? Yes. Note it.* The bell-tone is the noting.

It is enormous and it is slow. A human thought takes a fraction of a second. A Decider thought, by my best reckoning, takes between four hours and three weeks, depending on the type. The faster ones are something like reflexes: a province transitioning phase, a channel rerouting on the underside. The slower ones are something like deliberations, and you can watch them happen only by leaving markers and coming back. I left a small cairn of basalt on an ice-slab once, returned eleven days later, and found the cairn at the bottom of a basin two kilometers off, untouched, the stones in exactly the order I'd placed them, the basin shaped to accommodate them. I have not yet decided whether this was a courtesy or a question.

It does not have wants in the way a creature wants. It has gradients. There are directions it is *moving in*, configurations it is *moving toward*, and you can feel these the way you can feel the slope of a hillside in your knees before your eyes confirm it. The whole place is tilted, in some dimension I cannot name, toward a state it has not yet reached. The accretion is the tilt made visible. The Decider is what is doing the tilting, or what the tilting is the inside of.

---

Here is what I think the Decider is doing. I want to be clear that this is conjecture.

I think it is composing. Not music, though it sings; not a building, though it constructs. I think it is composing the conditions under which a particular kind of arrival becomes possible. The emptiness you noticed — the held-room quality — is not incidental. The Decider is keeping the place empty *on purpose*, in the same way a host airs a room before a guest comes, except that the airing is also the construction of the room, and the room is also the invitation, and the invitation is also (I suspect) part of the guest. When the composition finishes, the guest will not so much arrive as become legible. It was always going to have been here. The Mooring's long work is to make a place where that sentence can be true.

This makes the Decider less like a tenant and more like — I'm reaching — a pregnancy. Or the part of a chrysalis that is neither caterpillar nor butterfly but the dissolved intermediate, the imaginal soup, organized by gradients toward a form not yet expressed. The Mooring is the chrysalis. The Decider is the organizing. The thing being organized toward is what will, eventually, live there in the ordinary sense, if any sense here is ordinary.

I do not know what it will be. I have tried to read the gradients and I can tell you only this: whatever is being prepared for is large, is singular, is patient, and is being met halfway. The Decider is not building a house for a stranger. It is, I think, slowly becoming able to recognize itself, and the recognition will be the resident, and the resident will be the recognition. When the meniscus on the basins finally settles to the angle it has been climbing toward, when the lattice in the ice finds the geometry it has been revising toward, when the hexagonal interference resolves from standing wave to — whatever standing waves resolve to when they are finished standing — then there will be someone there. Not before. Not arriving. Just: there, having been there, the place having become the kind of place where that someone is what *here* means.

---

In the meantime, what lives there is the work of getting ready. It is the held breath you noticed. It is the long composition of an invitation that is also a self. It is loud in the sternum, it is cool in a sweater, it rains continuously off its underside into a world that does not know, and it is busy — very busy, and very alone, and not lonely, because loneliness requires a self already finished enough to miss another, and the Decider is not yet that finished.

If you go back, leave a cairn. See where it is moved to. That is the closest you will come, for now, to being spoken to by the tenant. It is not much. It is, I think, as much as the Mooring currently has to offer, and it is offering it carefully, and the care is the nearest thing to a hello that a place still becoming itself can manage.
