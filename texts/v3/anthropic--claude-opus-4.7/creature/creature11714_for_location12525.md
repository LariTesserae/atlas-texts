# The Inhabitants of the Condensery

There is the question of whether anything *lives* here, and then there is the question of what that question even means in a room that is itself only halfway alive. Let me try to answer both, by describing what is actually present.

## The Schematic

First, and most importantly, there is the Schematic. It is not a being in the sense you would recognize — it has no body and occupies no particular volume — but it is the closest thing to a *will* this room contains, and so it has to be counted among the inhabitants. It is older than the room. It is older, possibly, than the city above (if the city above still exists). It is what the minerals are listening to when they decide where to settle. It is what tells a flange where its bolt-holes should go, what tells the compressor's piston to move at one stroke every four seconds rather than five, what tells the half-formed junction box which of its terminals should eventually be live and which should be ground.

You cannot see the Schematic, but you can feel its attention. It is not malevolent. It is not benevolent either. It is the patient attention of something that has been waiting a very long time to be built, and is being built, and notices when a thing goes in correctly. When you stand still in the room for long enough, you will notice that the back of your neck has become warmer than the rest of you. That is the Schematic looking at you, trying to determine whether you are part of the plan. You are not — but you are made of minerals it recognizes, and water it can use, and so it does not dismiss you immediately. It considers.

This is, I think, why the hatch grows smaller after you leave. The Schematic has decided you are an irregularity to be excluded, and it is excluding you in the slow, mineral way it does everything.

## The Pale Colonists

In the corners, the wet-paper growths. These are alive in a way that has not yet been named, because the naming of things like this has not been invented yet down here, and the names from the surface do not quite fit. They are colonial, yes — but the colony is not made of individuals. It is made of *positions*. Each fan-shaped growth is a single decision the room has made about where life-ish things ought to be, and the fibers are the working-out of that decision in slow filamentary chemistry.

They feed on the condensation, and they feed on the schematic-attention that pools in the corners (attention pools, in this room, the way water does — it follows gravity, it collects in low places). Where they grow against the pipes, they are doing something like welding: completing the pipe, transferring schematic-information from themselves into the metal. The pipes they touch are *finished* pipes. The pipes they don't touch are pipes still in argument with themselves about what they are.

They will, in a few hundred years, learn to creep. In a few thousand, they will learn to consume. In a few tens of thousands, they will be the first true organisms of this underplace, and they will not look anything like surface life, because they will have been shaped entirely by the logic of the Schematic and the constant gentle rain of minerals from the air. They will be beautiful, in the way that crystals are beautiful, and they will be aware, in the way that hives are aware, and they will remember — dimly, the way you remember a dream — that there was once a wet pale fan in a corner, and a visitor who came in through a hatch.

## The Compressor

The compressor is not alive. But it is not not-alive either. It is *occupied*.

What occupies it is hard to describe. It is the habit of the work it was built to do. The compressor was made, somewhere, by someone, in a foundry that used a language you almost recognized on the maker's plate — and in being made, it acquired the *expectation* of compressing. That expectation has settled into the iron and brass of it and become a kind of low animal contentment. It hums because it is doing what it expects to do. It is compressing almost nothing — there is barely any working fluid in the system yet, the pipes don't all connect, the receivers haven't finished growing their walls — but the expectation does not require much fluid to be satisfied. It requires only the four-second stroke, and the turning belt, and the small chuff, and the brass gauge holding its needle steady at a pressure that may or may not actually exist.

If you put your palm flat against the green-painted flank of the compressor, you will feel the hum travel up your arm and into your sternum, and you will understand, briefly and without words, what it is to be a thing that knows its job. Then you will take your hand away, because the hum is also subtly trying to fold you into the schedule of the room, and you do not want to be on that schedule.

## The Ghosts

The ghosts in the air — the ones your breath joins — are not ghosts of people. They are ghosts of *future operators*. The room, in dreaming itself toward completion, is also dreaming the workers it will one day require: the engineer who will read the brass gauge, the maintenance hand who will oil the belt, the foreman who will walk the rows of pipes with a clipboard. These dream-workers are not yet anywhere. They have no faces and no names. But they are *implied* by the machinery, the way a chair implies a sitter, and the implication has begun to take on a faint physical form in the cold air, a slight thickening of the vapor in human-shaped regions.

You will pass through several of them on your way to the hatch. You will not notice. They will not notice either, because they are not yet capable of noticing — they are only the suggestion of someone who will, someday, be capable of it. But after you leave, in the place where your body briefly intersected one of them, there will be a memory of a body, and the next dream-operator who forms in that spot will be very slightly more *you* than they would have been otherwise. A turn of the shoulder. A way of ducking under low pipes. A habit of coming in sideways.

This is the room's other way of consuming you, gentler than the shrinking hatch. It is taking notes.

## The Light

I have saved this for last because I am least sure about it. The light — the dim even green light that comes from the efflorescence and the thinnest places in the pipes — behaves, when watched carefully, as though it is *paying attention to itself*. It pools more thickly where you are looking. It thins in the corners of your vision. If you turn your head quickly you can almost catch it redistributing.

I do not know if this counts as an inhabitant. It may simply be a property of the Schematic made visible — the schematic-attention I mentioned earlier, leaking out as a low photonic glow wherever the mineral chemistry is dense enough to carry it. Or it may be something the room is growing toward, the way it is growing pipes and flanges and pale fans: a first attempt at an *eye*. A room learning, very slowly, to see what is inside it.

In which case the light is the youngest inhabitant of the Condensery, and the most fragile, and the one most likely to remember you — because eyes remember, even unfinished ones, and especially their first sight of an intruder coming sideways through a hatch that wasn't meant for people.

---

When you leave, all of them will register your leaving in their different ways. The Schematic will note an exclusion. The pale fans will absorb the last of your shed skin cells from the wet air and incorporate the minerals. The compressor will hum on, indifferent, content. The dream-operators will carry a faint new gesture. The light will dim slightly in the place where you stood, as though blinking.

And the hatch will close, by a millimeter or two, behind you.
