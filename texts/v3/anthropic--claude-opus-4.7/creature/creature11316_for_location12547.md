## The Inhabitants of the Condensery

They are not, when you first see them, remarkable. They are people of the usual two sexes and the usual range of ages, dressed in the practical layered wools and waxed canvases of any wet upland town. The skin runs to a coppery-olive, often freckled with the rust-spots that come from a lifetime drinking distilled water through old brass. The hair is dark and tends to curl in the perpetual damp. They are, on average, shorter than lowlanders by perhaps a handspan, and their chests are deeper, the way the chests of high-altitude people always are. Their hands are very strong. Their forearms are corded from a hundred small daily tasks performed against the resistance of wet rope, wet leather, wet iron.

What is remarkable is harder to point to and lives mostly in their tempo.

They breathe slowly. They move slowly, but not languidly — there is no slack in the motion, only a kind of even draw, like a bow being pulled. A Condensery butcher portioning a shoulder of mutton works at exactly the speed of a Condensery scribe ruling a ledger, which is exactly the speed at which a Condensery child climbs the cistern stairs: four seconds per gesture, give or take, with the small precise actions nested inside the large ones. Watch a group of them eating together and you will see spoons rise and fall in a loose unison nobody is enforcing. They are not aware of it. When it has been pointed out to them by visiting doctors and folklorists they have generally been polite and uninterested, the way one would be uninterested in a stranger remarking that one's heart is beating.

Their faces are still. This is the second thing a stranger notices, after the rhythm. It is not that they are unexpressive — they smile and frown and grieve like anyone — but the expressions arrive and depart without the small constant flicker of micro-feeling that animates a lowlander's face. A Condensery face at rest is composed in a way that lowlanders, depending on temperament, read as either serene or unsettling. The eyes do most of the work. Eyebrows, in the Condensery, are nearly vestigial as a communicative organ; the muscles around the eyes and the corners of the mouth carry the burden, along with the hands, which are extraordinarily articulate.

Because they sign as much as they speak, their hands have developed a vocabulary the hands of lowlanders do not have. There is a particular gesture, made by tipping the right hand from the wrist with the fingers loose, that means *understood, continue,* and you will see it answered between people standing twenty meters apart through a crowd, the way lowlanders nod. There is another gesture, two fingers laid briefly across the inside of the opposite wrist, that means roughly *I have heard you, and I am thinking,* and which is used in place of the verbal placeholders ("well," "so," "hm") that lowlanders scatter through their speech. A long Condensery conversation watched from across a square looks like a slow piece of choreography for hands and shoulders, with the mouths almost incidental.

Their speech, when they use it, is loud, close, and curiously musical. They drop unstressed syllables almost entirely — the language has eroded toward its accented beats, the way a stone erodes toward its hardest grain — and the accented beats fall on the Drum's upstroke, so that any sentence of any length is also, faintly, a piece of percussion. Lowlanders learning the dialect describe the experience as trying to talk while someone else conducts you. Condensery children learning the lowland tongues are reported to sound, to lowland ears, as if they are reciting poetry badly: too even, too placed, the small words given the same careful weight as the large.

They are patient in a way that has to be seen to be believed and is, on close acquaintance, slightly frightening. A Condensery mason setting the corner of a new chimney will spend a full morning on a single course of bricks, not because the work is difficult but because he is checking each brick against the plumb after every Drum-stroke, and he sees no reason to hurry the Drum. They do not, as a rule, finish each other's sentences. They do not interrupt. They will wait through a pause of fifteen or twenty seconds in a conversation without showing any sign of strain, and they regard the lowland habit of filling such pauses with chatter as a kind of nervous tic, like a dog scratching. When they are made to deal with the outside world — at the rail terminus down on the plain, at the market towns where they sell their distilled water and their juniper oil and their dried shrimp — they are often taken for slow-witted, and they have learned to find this restful rather than insulting. It means less is asked of them.

They are also, in a quiet and unsentimental way, religious, though they do not use the word. Each ward has its Drum and each Drum has its Keeper, an office that is half engineer and half something else, passed down through a guild whose apprenticeships last eleven years. The Keepers are not priests — they wear no special dress, they perform no rites that an outsider would recognize as worship — but they are the only people in the town permitted to be present when a Drum's piston is at top dead center during the quarterly maintenance shutdown, the brief still moment when the engine is open and silent. What happens in that moment is not secret, exactly. It is simply not discussed. Keepers, asked, will say only that *one listens*, and will then perform the wrist-touch that means *I am thinking,* and the conversation will move on. Children in the Condensery are told that the Drums are not gods and the Keepers are not priests, and this is said in a tone that makes clear the question has been considered and dismissed, perhaps several generations ago, and need not be raised again.

They die well. This is the observation most frequently made by the lowland doctors who occasionally rotate through. Condensery people in their final illness tend to grow quieter rather than more agitated; they breathe in the long even rhythm they have breathed in all their lives, and at some point the rhythm simply stops, often between one Drum-stroke and the next, so that the family gathered around the bed sometimes does not realize for a full cycle that the person is gone. The dead are not buried — there is no soil to bury them in — but are laid in a small stone cell in the wall of their ward's cistern, where the distilled water seeps past them for a year, and then their cleaned bones are taken out and added to the ossuary beneath the cathedral ward, which is by now very large and very orderly, the long bones stacked like firewood and the skulls shelved with their eye-sockets facing inward, toward the master Drum. The townspeople do not visit the ossuary. The Keepers do.

They love, marry, quarrel, betray each other, and reconcile, like anyone. But the texture of these things is different. A Condensery courtship is a long matter of standing near each other in slightly different parts of the same square over a period of months, until one day the two people are standing in the same part of the square, and then they are understood to be a couple, and the wedding is a formality observed some years later, often after the first child. Condensery quarrels are slow and terrible and can last decades; they are conducted largely through the placement of objects (a pot moved to the wrong shelf, a tool returned to the wrong hook) and through pointed absences at meals, and they are resolved, when they are resolved, by one party simply resuming a gesture they had withheld — pouring the other's tea, say — at which point the matter is over and is not spoken of. To a lowlander this can look like a cold marriage. From inside it is reported to be something else, though the Condensery people do not have, or do not share, a word for what.

They do not, contrary to a common lowland rumor, hear the Drums in their sleep as music. They do not hear the Drums at all, after the fourth day of life, in the way a lowlander would understand hearing. The Drums are not in the foreground of their attention any more than the pull of the earth is in the foreground of a lowlander's. But if the Drums stop — and this has happened twice in living memory, once for nine minutes during a cascade failure in the eastern ward and once for nearly half an hour during the great copper-strike — every Condersery person within range wakes from whatever they are doing, including from sleep, with the same expression: not fear, exactly, but a stark and total attention, like a person who has just realized they have stopped breathing and does not yet know why. In those minutes they do not speak. They do not sign. They stand or sit very still and look at nothing in particular, and they wait. When the Drums resume, they resume what they were doing, in the middle of the gesture they were making, as if no time has passed. They do not afterward describe the silence. The lowland doctor who was present for the second event wrote in his journal that he had never in his life been in a room with so many people who were so completely *somewhere else,* and that he had been glad, when the engines started again, in a way that surprised him and that he could not explain to his colleagues on the plain.

They are, in the end, not quite a separate kind of people from the lowlanders — the blood mixes freely enough at the rail terminus and the market towns, and a Condensery child raised below grows up indistinguishable from any other lowlander, faster of face and shorter of patience and entirely arrhythmic. But a lowland child raised in the Condensery from infancy becomes, by adolescence, one of them: the slow breath, the still face, the four-second hands, the long pauses, the indifference to the engines that nonetheless requires the engines absolutely. Whatever the Condensery people are, they are not inherited. They are made, slowly, the way the basins in the limestone are made, by the same patient pressure applied to the same place for long enough that the place takes the shape of the pressure.

The town says: *it is water that has learned to make a town.*

The people, if you asked them, and if they chose to answer, might say: *and we are what the town remembers of the water.*

But they would not say it. They would perform the wrist-touch, and look at you with their still faces and their working eyes, and wait for you to understand it for yourself, in your own time, on the next upstroke or the one after that, whichever you could manage.
