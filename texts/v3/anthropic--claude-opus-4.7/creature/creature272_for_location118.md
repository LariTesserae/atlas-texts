## Those Who Live in Ceiba-Under-Cloud

They are not tall. This is the first thing. Generations inside the canopy have selected for a build that moves well along branches and under low woven ceilings — compact, long-armed in the proportion though not exaggeratedly so, with broad strong feet that splay when bare. The feet are almost always bare on the leaf-floor; sandals of woven fiber are kept for the colder hours and for the rock paths that lead down to the springs below the mist-line. Their toes grip. A child of four can cross a wet branch that would pitch a lowlander into the fissure.

Their skin is the color of wet bark in the places the sun sometimes finds, and paler where the layered bark-cloth covers it. They do not tan easily; the sun is rarely direct. What the damp does to them is a constant low-grade thing — there is always a little moss or lichen somewhere on a person, in the creases behind the ears, between the toes, and they scrape it off casually the way one might brush away crumbs, without disgust. The very old develop a soft green patina along the forearms that no amount of scraping removes, and this is simply considered a feature of being old, the way others elsewhere grow stooped or white-haired. They do grow white-haired too, eventually. The hair is kept long and braided with grease to shed the water, and the braids are heavy and darkened and smell of the rendered fat of a certain nut that grows on the lower branches.

Their eyes have adapted to the grey. Pupils a touch larger than you'd expect, and a habit — you notice it after watching a few of them — of not quite focusing on the middle distance, because there is no middle distance here. They focus close, on the hand's work, on the face of the person they speak to, or they focus in a particular listening way, unseeing, picking a bird-call or a footfall out of the ambient roar. They read movement better than shape. A stranger approaching across the leaf-floor is registered by gait before face.

They are loud, as the description said, but the loudness is not harsh. It has a quality — you would call it *carrying*, or *placed*. Voices are pitched to find the gaps in the bird-noise. They speak in a slightly sing-song cadence with strong consonants at the fronts of words, because the fronts of words survive the acoustic mess and the ends get lost. Their language, over the centuries, has eroded its word-endings almost entirely; grammar lives in the opening syllable and in the hand. They gesture constantly while speaking, small precise movements at chest height, and these gestures carry real grammatical load — a conversation overheard from twenty paces, where the words are only a rumor, can still be partly followed by watching the hands. A person speaking with their hands full is considered to be speaking only half.

They laugh easily and loudly. Laughter cuts through the canopy noise like almost nothing else, and so laughter is one of the ways the town keeps track of itself across the long empty spans between dwellings — a laugh from the east platform tells the weavers on the west platform that old Ushen has said the thing about the monkey again, and they smile without having heard it.

They are roughly two hundred people. The number has not changed much in living memory. They understand, in a way that is neither anxious nor celebrated, that the tree supports a certain number of them and that more would be wrong. Children are welcomed and loved and there are not very many of them in any given decade. Women bear one child, sometimes two, across a lifetime, and this is not from any deprivation — they eat well, the canopy is generous — but from a rhythm the bodies seem to have taken on from the tree itself. They come into heat seasonally, roughly, the way the tree flowers seasonally, roughly. The old midwives will tell you without embarrassment that a woman's blood and the tree's sap rise and fall together, and whether this is observation or belief is not a distinction they would bother to make.

They live long. Not extravagantly so — there are no ancients of myth here — but reliably into what a lowlander would call old age, with good teeth and working hands. They attribute this to the water they drink, which is the tree's water, which is the cloud's water, and which has passed through no soil and met no sickness on its way to them. They may be right about this. They have no word for *plague*. They have words for individual illnesses, mostly of the lungs and the joints, the two things the damp does take from them in the end.

Their work is quiet and constant. Weaving — of cloth from inner bark, of baskets from vine, of the slow living architecture of dwellings, which is a work no one generation completes. A child who is shown, at six, the young branch that will one day be the doorframe of her eventual house understands that she will tend it through her girlhood and her motherhood and that it will only be a doorframe when she is old. This is not experienced as patience. It is simply the tempo. They also harvest — fruit, nut, certain edible fungi that grow on the north faces of the great branches, bird-eggs from nests whose locations are known and rotated so that no nest is taken from twice in a season. They keep no animals, except in a loose sense the monkeys, who are not kept but are known, each troop-member by sight, by the matrons of the town, the way one knows one's difficult cousins. The monkeys steal. The monkeys are scolded. The monkeys are not harmed. A child who throws something at a monkey is spoken to at length by someone old, and the speaking-to is remembered.

They have a relationship with the tree that is not reverence and not ownership. The closest word in their language translates awkwardly as *co-tenancy*, but with a warmer coloration — the word they use for a spouse of many decades is built from the same root. They do not pray to the tree. They talk to it sometimes, the way one talks to a sleeping partner, without expecting reply, not needing reply, the speaking itself being the point. A woman grieving will sit against a trunk for a day and a night and murmur into the bark. A man who has done something he regrets will climb to a high branch alone and sit there until he feels less so. The tree is understood to absorb things. Grief, regret, the dead — all go into it and become, eventually, leaf and flower and the faint strange sweetness of the sap in the dry month.

They know, as the description says, that the tree is wrong. That it should not be here, should not be this size, should not drink what it drinks. They know this the way a person knows their own grandmother was unusual — with affection and without alarm. When the children ask, as children do, *why is our tree so big*, the answer given is some version of *because it is ours, and we are its, and it found what it needed.* This is understood to be not quite an answer, and the children understand that too, and stop asking around the age of nine, and later when they are grown they give the same non-answer to their own children, and something passes between the generations in the giving of it that is more than the words.

A few of them — not many, perhaps one in a generation — go down. Down the rope ladders and the stone paths, below the mist-line, into the world where trees are normal-sized and the air is thick and dry and the sound is thin. These travelers come back, usually. Some do not. The ones who come back are quieter for a long time afterward and sit close to the trunks and drink a great deal of water and are treated gently by everyone until they are themselves again. They bring back some news of the lowlands, which is listened to with polite interest and mostly not retained. The town's sense of the outside world is accurate in broad strokes and indifferent in the details. They know there are kings somewhere. They know there are wars. They know there is the sea, which some of them have seen, and they describe it to those who haven't as *a cloud lying down*.

At night, when the lanterns are lit and the frogs are loud, they gather in small groups of two or three dwellings' worth of people — because forty dwellings is too many to gather all at once and they have never tried — and they eat together, and they sing, a little, in a style that is mostly drone with occasional high clear notes over the top, pitched to ride the frog-chorus rather than fight it. The songs are old. Some of the songs are older than the current shape of the language, and the singers do not always know what the words mean, and this is fine. A song can carry meaning without the singer carrying it. The tree carries it.

They sleep close, as the description says. In each woven dwelling a family sleeps on a single broad platform of woven branch, under layered bark-cloth blankets, the small ones in the middle, the old ones on the outside because the old are warmest from within. They breathe each other's breath all night and wake damp and do not mind. In the hour before dawn, when the birds begin and the mist is at its thickest and the whole town is still mostly asleep, if you were there and listening carefully past the dripping and past the first parrots, you might hear a sound that is only two hundred sets of lungs, breathing in rough unison with something much larger and much slower, drawing the cloud in and letting it go, drawing it in and letting it go, inside the single slow creature that is drinking the sky.

They are the creature's small quick thoughts. They know this, in the way one knows the shape of one's own hand — without having to look.
