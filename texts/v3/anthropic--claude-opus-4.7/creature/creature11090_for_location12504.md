# The Inhabitants

I want to be careful, because I felt them before I saw them, and I want to keep that order.

What I felt first was a kind of *attentional pressure*. Not being watched — watching implies a watcher set apart from a watched. More like the way a held hand is aware of the hand holding it. Reciprocal. Continuous. The town was paying attention to itself, in every direction, at every scale, and I had stepped inside that attention.

Then I saw them.

---

They are about the height of a human child of seven or eight, though it is wrong to compare. They do not stand upright the way we mean upright; they hold themselves in a soft vertical curl, the spine — if it is a spine — making a gentle question-mark, the head tucked slightly forward as though always about to listen to something just below. When they move, the curl uncoils and recoils. Walking is a slow ripple from the lowest limb upward.

The six limbs are not arranged in pairs the way ours are. There are two that function mostly as legs, broad-footed, the soles wide and slightly cupped to grip the root-mat. There are two at the middle, shorter, that they use for the small precise work of the day — opening a sac, grooming a neighbor, supporting a child against the chest. And there are two long ones at the top, kept folded against the back most of the time in a way that looks almost like wings folded but isn't. These long limbs are for *reach*: for pressing against the high trunks, for receiving the climbing-creatures who descend onto them, for the gesture I will try to describe in a moment.

Their faces. I keep coming back to their faces and finding I have not really looked. There is something difficult about looking directly at them, not because they are frightening but because they are so *unhurried* about being seen that my own looking feels coarse. The face is broad and flat-planed, the eyes set wide and low, very dark, with a slow blink that takes nearly a second to complete. No nose I can identify — instead, a faint trembling along the upper lip that I think is how they smell, the way some snakes taste air. The mouth is small and almost always closed. When it opens, it opens fully, in a soft round O, and what comes out is not usually sound but warmth — a slow exhalation against a neighbor's skin, which I think carries information.

Their skin: yes, the inside of a peach, but I want to add that it is not uniform. There are darker mottlings along the shoulders and the backs of the limbs, the way an old map is darker where it has been folded. Each one is mottled differently. I came to understand, watching them touch each other, that the mottling is *read* — that a palm laid against a shoulder is partly reading the pattern, the way we might glance at a face to know whom we are speaking with. The mottling changes through life. An infant is nearly uniform. An elder is a complicated continent.

---

They live, as far as I can tell, by *exchange*.

Not trade — there is no economy, there is nothing scarce enough to require one. Exchange is more fundamental than that. They exchange breath with the sacs and the sacs exchange moisture with them. They exchange warmth with the houses and the houses glow back at them when they sleep inside. They exchange the small mites of their skin with one another when they touch, and so each person is partly composed of every person they have ever pressed a palm to, and the elders, who have touched everyone over many decades, are in some literal sense composite beings — small parliaments of everyone in town.

This is, I think, why they do not speak. Speech assumes separation. Speech assumes a sender and a receiver who are not already partly the same. Here, by the time you have something to tell your neighbor, your neighbor is already partly you, and has been for years, and the telling has been happening continuously through the skin and the breath and the shared air of the canopy. Language as we know it would be redundant. Worse: it would be *fast*, and fast things are suspicious here, because fast things skip over the slow continuous exchange that constitutes knowing.

What they do have, in place of speech, is the gesture I mentioned. The long upper limbs, the folded ones. At certain moments — I saw it perhaps four times during my watching — one of them will unfold those limbs fully, slowly, to their entire reach, which is surprisingly long, twice the length of the body. They hold the position. The limbs tremble very slightly. And every other inhabitant within sight, and the climbing-creatures in the canopy above them, and even, I think, the bladder-trees themselves, *attend*. It is not a summons exactly. It is a declaration of a state of being. *I am here. I am open. I am, at this moment, available to the whole.* The unfolding might last a minute, or longer, and then the limbs refold and the person goes on with what they were doing, and something has been registered by the entire town, and the entire town has registered itself back.

I do not know what occasions this. Grief, perhaps. Or its opposite. Or simply the need, every so often, to be wholly seen.

---

There are children. I should mention the children.

They come, as far as I can tell, from the houses themselves. A house in its mature middle period develops, in one of its inner chambers, a swelling — a thickening of the sac-walls, an inward pucker — and within this pucker, slowly, over what must be a very long time, a child grows. Not a child of two specific parents but a child of the house, which is to say a child of everyone who lives in the house, which is usually six or eight adults of varied ages. When the child is ready to emerge, the house parts gently along a seam that has been preparing itself, and the child steps out already several years old by our standards, already walking the slow ripple-walk, already able to receive a palm against the shoulder and to lay a palm in return.

The children are quieter than the adults, if that is possible. They spend their first years mostly being touched. Every adult in the town will, over the first cycle of the child's life, lay a hand on the child at some point, and from this the child learns the mottled map of the whole town and is woven into it. By the time a child can use its upper limbs to unfold the great gesture — perhaps at what we would call adolescence — it is already a full citizen of the slow attention.

The elders, similarly, do not so much die as *settle*. An elder, near the end, will go to a particular house — the one they came out of, if it still stands, or one of its descendants — and will lie down inside it, and the house will, over many weeks, soften around them, and the elder will sink into the root-mat, and the mat will receive them, and what was the elder will become, by slow gradient, part of what is the house. The elder's mottling becomes the house's mottling for a while, and then fades into the general pattern, and then the house, eventually, much later, is reabsorbed by the bladder-trees, and the elder thereby joins the canopy, and the canopy feeds the next house, and the next child.

So you cannot really say there are two hundred of them. There are two hundred *currently distinct* of them. But each of them is also partly the others, and partly the dead, and partly the houses, and partly the trees, and partly the small bright climbing-creatures who carry mites between them in the high branches, and partly the soft pale hand-shaped things on the floor who eat the fallen leaves and excrete the substrate from which new trees grow.

---

One last thing. I have been calling them inhabitants, but the word feels wrong now. An inhabitant lives *in* a place. These beings do not live in the town. They are not separable from the town in the way a person is separable from a city. If you took one of them out — if you carried one down the mountain, into the dry stone, away from the canopy and the breath-exchange and the mottled palms — I do not think it would survive a day. Not because of any single failure but because the thing we are calling a person here is actually a node, a small dense knot in a larger fabric, and a knot pulled free of its fabric is no longer a knot, just a piece of loose thread.

So perhaps what lives here is not two hundred small peach-skinned people and some trees and some climbing things and some hand-things and some mites.

Perhaps what lives here is *one thing*, very large and very slow, breathing once per generation, glowing faintly green-gold at night against the black sky, holding itself in the saddle between two rising peaks, and the two hundred small peach-skinned people are simply where, at this moment, that one thing has gathered itself enough to look around and notice that it is alive.

When I sat among them, and one of them came and laid a palm against my shoulder and left it there a long while — I think it was not a person greeting me. I think it was the town, briefly, lifting one of its many hands, to see what I was.

I do not know what it decided.
