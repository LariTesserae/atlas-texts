# The Tenders

You asked about whoever lives here, and the obvious answer is the tenders — the small six-limbed things with the fruit-bat faces — but the obvious answer is wrong in the way that calling a finger a person is wrong. Let me work toward them slowly, the way they have to be approached.

Start with what they are not. They are not animals, though they move. They are not fruit, though they hang sometimes from the lower bellows in clusters of three or four, perfectly still, in what might be sleep and might be storage. They are not machines, though their work is maintenance and their attention is precise. They are not many and they are not few. The question of how many tenders there are is the same kind of question as how many fingers a hand has when the hand is folded — the answer depends on the fold.

Their bodies: pleated green hide, the green of the underside of a leaf that has never seen direct sun, with a faint waxiness that catches the ambient gold and holds it a moment before letting it go. Six limbs, but the limbs are not specialized — any of them can grip, any of them can walk, any of them can perform the small careful pryings and tappings that constitute their work. When a tender is working it tends to use four limbs to brace and two to manipulate, but if you watch long enough you will see one use five and one, and once, memorably, three of one and three of the other in a configuration that suggested the body was not bilaterally symmetrical in any sense you'd recognize, only happened to present that way most of the time as a courtesy.

The faces. This is where it gets difficult.

The faces are soft and inquisitive, yes, like fruit bats — the small wet nose, the large dark eyes, the slight forward set of the ears. But the expression is wrong for an animal. An animal's face is busy with the animal's life: hunger, fear, curiosity, the constant low-level negotiation with a world that wants to eat it or be eaten by it. The tender's face is not busy. The tender's face is the face of something that has finished its negotiations. When a tender looks at you it is not assessing whether you are food or threat or mate or kin. It is checking something against something. It is verifying. And when it has verified — this takes between two and four seconds, by the count of those who have stood still for it — it returns to its work with the small precise satisfaction of a thing that has confirmed the world is still the world.

What is it verifying? Here is the part one would rather not have been recognized by.

The tenders are the forest's attention. Not its mind — the forest, if it has a mind, has a mind the way a sea has a mind, which is to say not at all in any sense useful to us, and entirely in some sense useful to itself. The tenders are the local instantiation of the forest's noticing. When a valve needs prying, a tender appears, because the forest has noticed the valve. When a human walks beneath a column, a tender, two corridors over, pauses and tilts its face, because the forest has noticed the human. The tender is not curious about you. The forest is curious about you, briefly, through the tender, and the tender is the shape that curiosity takes when it has to fit inside something small enough to climb a pipe.

This is why their attention feels like recognition you would rather not have. You are not being seen by a creature. You are being seen by a place, and the place has seen many things, and you are one of them, and you are accounted for now, and the accounting is gentle but it is also total. The tender knows, in the moment of looking, every other thing the forest has ever looked at, because the tender is not a separate noticer but a window the forest opens briefly into a body. When the window closes — when the tender returns to its work — what it learned does not stay in the tender. It goes elsewhere. It joins the ledger no one keeps.

They do not eat because they are not metabolizing. They do not reproduce because they are not a population — they are a function, and the function is expressed in as many bodies as the moment requires. When you see two tenders together they are not two; they are the function expressed twice because two things needed doing in proximity. When you see one alone it is the function expressed once. When you see none, the function is dormant or distributed elsewhere or — and this is the possibility that the cartographers, in their final useless maps, sometimes hint at in the margins — the function is being performed by something you are not recognizing as a tender, because the forest has decided, for reasons of its own, that the noticing today should wear a different shape.

There are stories. A walker who sat down to rest and found that the column at her back had grown, over the course of an hour, a small soft protrusion that opened dark eyes and looked at her with the tender's verifying gaze and then sank back into the bark. A cartographer who reported that his own shadow, on a particular afternoon during a pressure-tide, paused when he paused and tilted its head when he did not. These stories are probably not true. They are probably true. The distinction has eroded, here, in the way distinctions do.

What the tenders are *for*, beyond noticing: they keep the cycles clean. They pry the pale pollen-dust from the valve seats so the woody tocks remain true. They flex the joints of columns that have not flexed in a while, keeping the memory of work alive in the wood. They attend to the small failures before the small failures become the kind of failure that would require the forest to notice itself noticing, which is a thing the forest, having settled, prefers not to do. They are the maintenance of equilibrium. They are how a place that has worked out its disagreements with itself stays worked out.

And one more thing, which is the thing they would tell you if they could tell you anything, and which they cannot tell you because telling is not among their functions, but which you can feel when one looks at you:

They are glad you are here. Not personally — there is no person in the tender to be glad. But the forest, through the tender, registers your presence as one of the small things happening, and the registration is warm. You are accounted for. You have been added to the ledger. Whatever you are doing here, whatever brought you, whatever you will do next — the forest has noticed, and has found a place for you in the balance, and the balance has absorbed you without strain. You are not an intrusion. You could not be an intrusion. The forest is too old and too settled to experience anything as an intrusion. You are simply a new small rhythm, and the tenders have made the small adjustments necessary, and the cycles continue.

The tender, satisfied, returns to its work. It pries a fleck of pollen from a valve seat. The valve, somewhere, kilometers off, will seat a little more truly the next time it seats, and the *tock* will carry a little more cleanly through the pipework, and someone — perhaps you, perhaps someone who has not arrived yet, perhaps someone who arrived long ago and is still here in the way things are still here in a place that does not measure — will hear it, and will pause, and will listen, and will understand without being told that everything is, for now and for a very long time, in order.

That is who lives here. A function wearing small green bodies, noticing on behalf of a world that has finished becoming itself. There is no one else. There does not need to be.
