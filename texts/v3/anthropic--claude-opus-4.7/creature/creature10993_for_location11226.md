# The Keepers of the Pipe

They are not in the calderas. That much you work out quickly. The calderas are larders, not homes — you don't live where your food lives, you live where your food *goes*.

So you follow the pipes.

---

They are three things at once, and you have to hold all three in your head or you'll misunderstand them.

They are a lineage. They are a guild. They are, in the strict old sense, a religious order — though they would resist all three words and accept only the fourth, which is in their own language and means roughly *the ones who tend the long breath*.

There aren't many of them. Maybe four hundred along the whole spine of Ish-Tarem, and the spine is six hundred kilometers long. They are spread thin on purpose. A caldera-tender is responsible for between two and five machines, and they walk their circuit on a five-day cycle, sleeping in the little stone huts you saw. They are wiry and sunburnt and their hands are the hands of mechanics — knuckles enlarged, nails always slightly black at the cuticle from the graphite they pack the gland seals with. They wear loose pale layers against the sun and heavy leather aprons when they work on the condensers. Their boots are resoled constantly because the rock eats soles. You can identify a senior tender by the boots: the uppers will be the original leather, dark and stiff with decades of sweat, and the soles will be their fortieth or fiftieth pair, fresh and pale.

They are, almost all of them, the children or grandchildren of tenders. The work is inherited but not automatically — there's a long apprenticeship, and people wash out. A child of the order who can't handle the altitude, or who can't bear the loneliness of the circuit, or who simply doesn't want it, is allowed to leave and go down to the lowland cities, and many do. The ones who stay tend to be a particular kind of person: quiet, patient with machinery, comfortable with their own company for days at a time, and — this is the hard one to articulate — possessed of an unusual relationship with *waiting*. The condensers run on cycles measured in hours; the seep pressures shift on cycles measured in seasons; the calderas themselves are cooling on a cycle measured in millennia. A good tender holds all three timescales at once without going mad. The bad ones go mad in a specific way: they begin to believe the mountain is speaking to them personally, and they have to be brought down.

---

They do not live alone. This is the second thing.

Down the western slope of the range, where the pipes converge, there is a settlement — not a town exactly, more an installation. It sits in a saddle between two of the lower calderas, at maybe four thousand meters, where the air is breathable for sustained work. The pipes all feed into it. From the rim you'd see, if you walked another two days west, a complex of low buildings the color of the rock around them, a few wind-turbines, terraced gardens fed by the condensate, and a single tall stack venting clean steam. The settlement is called by a name that means *the cistern* though it is much more than a cistern.

Here is where the families live. Tenders rotate down for a month every quarter, see their partners and children, eat food they didn't cook themselves, sleep in beds wider than a coffin. The children grow up here. They are schooled by retired tenders, who teach them to read pressure gauges before they teach them to read words, and who teach them the names of every plant and animal in every caldera in their stretch of the range before they teach them arithmetic. By twelve a child of the cistern knows things a lowland naturalist would kill for, and almost none of it is written down anywhere.

The cistern is also where the condensate goes. All of it. Every machine on the spine is funneling moisture down through that pipe network, and it accumulates at the cistern in great glass-lined tanks, and from the cistern it is — sold? Traded? Sent? You don't have the word yet. What you'd see, if you watched for a week, is a caravan of pack animals (a kind of small hardy bovid bred for altitude) leaving the cistern every nine days, heading further west down the range, carrying sealed amphorae. They go to the dry plateau on the other side of Ish-Tarem, where there is a city, and the city pays in metal and salt and books and replacement parts and, once a year, in a single hard currency payment from the city's water-council that keeps the whole operation solvent.

The order exists because the plateau city exists. The plateau city exists because the order exists. Neither remembers which came first; both pretend it was them.

---

Now: the fourth caldera. The one with no animals.

The tender responsible for it is named — well, you don't know her name, but she's there. She's been there for eleven years. She lives in a hut two ridges over, alone, and she does not rotate down to the cistern anymore. She used to. She had a partner there and a daughter. The daughter is grown now and lives in the plateau city and writes letters that the tender reads and does not answer.

The fourth caldera is the experiment. About fifteen years ago the order's engineers — there are engineers at the cistern, a small cadre, the closest thing the order has to a priesthood proper — designed a new kind of condenser. Larger. More efficient. It pulls so much moisture out of the morning air that the dew cycle in its caldera is disrupted: there isn't enough left for the lichens, the cushion plants thin out, the insects don't hatch, the lizards starve or leave, the armored mammals follow the lizards. Within three years of installation the caldera was sterile of everything but the most stubborn flora.

This was not the intended outcome. It was, however, an *acceptable* outcome, because the new condenser produces nearly four times the yield of an old one, and the plateau city's demand is rising, and the order has been quietly debating for a decade whether to replace the whole network.

The tender of the fourth caldera was the one who first noticed the lizards leaving. She filed reports. She filed more reports. She was reassigned, not punished but moved, and the new tender — the one who's been there eleven years — was selected because she was known to be unsentimental about wildlife. She is not unsentimental. The selection committee misjudged her. She has spent eleven years watching the caldera be empty and writing nothing down about it, because writing it down accomplished nothing the first time and she is too tired to try again, and also because she has come to believe — and this is the part she would not say aloud — that the silence of the caldera is itself a kind of message, and that someone, eventually, will come along the rim and notice it without needing to be told.

The smell you couldn't place is the smell of a place where the cycle has stopped. It's not rot. It's the absence of rot. Rot needs life to feed it. What you smelled was sterile warm mineral air, which the nose reads as *wrong* without being able to say why.

---

The lizards' eyes. This is the last thing and the one you'll think about longest.

The lizards have been on Ish-Tarem far longer than the order. The order has been on Ish-Tarem for about three hundred years. The condensers in their current form for about a hundred and fifty. The lizards, as a species, perhaps two million.

What the order's naturalists have very slowly worked out, and what the senior tenders know in their hands without quite knowing they know it, is that the lizards' relationship with the condensers is not incidental. The machines have become part of the lizards' environment in the way a particular kind of tree becomes part of a particular kind of bird's environment. The lizards bask on the condenser housings because they're warm at night. They drink from the condensate overflow. They lay their eggs in the warm sand around the foundations. The red of their eyes — and this took a long time to confirm — has shifted, very slightly, over the hundred and fifty years. The pigment is iron-based. The condensers rust. The lizards eat the rust-loving algae that grow in the overflow. The iron concentrates in their tissue. Their eyes are the color of the machines because they are, in a small literal way, *made of* the machines now.

When the order replaces the old condensers with the new sterile ones, the lizards will go. The pangolin-marmots will go. The birds will go. The calderas will become what the fourth caldera is.

The senior tenders know this. The engineers at the cistern know this. The plateau city does not know this and would not, the engineers believe, care.

The order is, very quietly, splitting over the question of what to do. Not openly. The order does not split openly. But on the long circuits, in the stone huts, on the evenings when two tenders meet at a junction hut and share a meal, the conversation has begun to turn, again and again, to the lizards' eyes.

Someone, eventually, will have to decide.

The tender of the fourth caldera is waiting to see who.
