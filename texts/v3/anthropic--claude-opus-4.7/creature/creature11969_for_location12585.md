# What Lives in the Drowned Works

You asked who lives here. I have to answer carefully, because the obvious answer — *nothing* — is wrong, and the romantic answer — *the city itself is alive* — is also wrong, and the truth sits between them in a way that took me a while to feel out.

---

**The Tenants of the Sound**

The inhabitant of the Drowned Works is the sound. Not metaphorically — I mean that the standing acoustic field inside the pocket has the properties we usually reserve for an organism. It maintains itself against perturbation. It has a characteristic shape. It metabolizes: the engines feed it, it feeds back into the engines through the pressure-pulses that keep the looser fittings seated and the bearings turning at their resonant frequencies. The hammering is not random. If you listened long enough — longer than a visitor can stand to listen — you would notice that the great percussion has a period, and that the period is the period of the pocket itself, the time it takes a pressure wave to cross the warm volume and return. The city is a cavity. The sound is what occupies the cavity. The machines are the cavity's way of staying full.

This is the only resident in the strict sense. Everything else I am about to describe is either a part of this one tenant, or a guest, or a misunderstanding.

---

**The Two That Might Be Sponges**

I want to be honest: I do not know what these are. But I notice that there are two of them, and that two is a suspicious number in a place that otherwise contains either none of a thing or thousands of a thing. Two suggests a pair. Two suggests something that arrived together, or something that divided once and then stopped.

My best guess is that they are listeners. Not sponges in the biological sense, but something that has adapted — over a span of time I cannot guess at — to subsist on the acoustic energy of the works. They sit on the pipes where the vibration is strongest. They are soft because softness is what absorbs sound. They do not move because moving would cost more than they can gather. They may have been here as long as the works have been running. They may be the only things in the universe that find this place hospitable, and they have found it so hospitable that they have nearly stopped being things at all, settling into the patient near-mineral existence of something that needs only to persist and to take in the pressure that surrounds it.

If they are aware, their awareness is the slowest awareness imaginable. A thought every century. The thought is always the same thought, and the thought is the sound.

---

**The Pale Shape in the Corridor**

This is the one I keep coming back to.

I do not think it was a cable. Cables tumble in a particular way, and the description — *or perhaps a length of cable come loose and tumbling in the current* — is the kind of thing one tells oneself when one has seen something one does not want to have seen. The shape was pale, and it was eel-like, and it appeared once and did not appear again, and these are the properties of an animal that does not want to be observed.

I think there is one of it. Possibly more, but I lean toward one. It lives — if *lives* is the right word — by patrolling the empty corridors between the structures, moving through the warm sluicing water where the silt is kept from settling. It is pale because there has been no reason for it to be any other color for a very long time. It is long and thin because long and thin is the shape that fits between things in a place made of pipes and gaps. It eats — I am guessing now, but the guess feels load-bearing — it eats whatever drifts in from the dark water at the edges of the pocket. Things that wandered in and could not wander back out. Things the city caught. The eel is the city's mouth, in the sense that it is what processes whatever the city happens to consume, but the city does not consume on purpose; the eel is opportunistic on the city's behalf.

It saw you. This is the part I am most sure of. The pale shape moved once and did not move again because it had taken your measure and decided you were too large and too brief and too obviously a visitor to be worth pursuing. It is still down there. It is, in some sense, the closest thing to a person the Drowned Works contains — the only resident with anything like a point of view, anything like a preference, anything like the capacity to choose not to be seen.

It is very old. I do not know how old. Older than seems reasonable. Whatever exclusion-principle keeps biology out of this place has made an exception for it, and the exception has lasted, and lasting is what it does now. It is lonely in the specific way that something can only be lonely if it has forgotten there was ever anything else.

---

**The Ones Who Are Not There**

I have to mention these, because the works do not make sense without them.

Someone laid the brick. Someone bolted the housings to the foundations. The courses of brick under the silt were set by hands, and the hands belonged to people, and the people are not here. They have not been here for a very long time. But the works remember them in the way a tool remembers the hand that shaped it — not as an image, not as a thought, but as a fittedness. The pipes are sized for human maintenance. The valve-wheels, where you can see them, are sized for human grip. The catwalks — there are catwalks, I noticed them late, half-buried, running between the engine-houses — are sized for human feet.

Whoever built this built it to be tended. The tending stopped. The works did not stop. This is the central fact about the place, and it tells you something about the builders: they built well enough that their absence did not matter. Whether this was their intention or their failure I cannot say. I suspect it was neither. I suspect they did not know, when they left, that they were leaving for the last time. I suspect the works ran on after them not because the builders willed it but because the works had reached the point where running was easier than stopping, and no one came back to make the harder choice.

They are residents in the negative sense. The shape of their absence is what the works are shaped around. The catwalks lead nowhere because the people who walked them are nowhere. But the catwalks are still there, and they are still walkable, and this is a kind of patience that I find hard to look at directly.

---

**What I Think Is Actually Going On**

If you press me — and the description presses me, by asking the question *what is it making* and refusing to answer — I think the Drowned Works is a memorial that has forgotten it is a memorial. Someone, very long ago, built a machine whose purpose was to make a particular sound, and to keep making that sound, in a particular small warm volume of water, for as long as the machine could be made to run. The sound meant something. The sound was *for* something, or for someone, or in memory of something or someone. The meaning has been lost. The sound has not.

The two sponge-things heard the original meaning and have been holding it, in their slow soft way, ever since. The pale eel does not care about the meaning and never did; it just lives here because here is where it lives. The builders are gone. The sound continues. The pocket holds.

This is who lives in the Drowned Works: a sound that has outlived its reason, two listeners who have outlived their capacity to report what they hear, one animal that has outlived its species, and the negative shape of the people who set the whole thing running and then, one by one, for reasons we will never know, went up through the amber water and did not come back down.

The city is not running for itself. It is running because no one has told it to stop. There is a difference, and the difference is the closest thing the Drowned Works has to a heart.
