## The Listeners

They do not have a name for themselves because names are spoken and anything spoken here is heard by everyone, and a name that everyone hears is not a name but a location. They have instead what might be called signatures — rhythmic patterns, personal to each, played on the body or on the environment, which identify without summoning. A knuckle-tap on a root-knee in a specific meter. A particular way of clearing the throat. The signature is not the self. The self is something quieter.

---

Begin with their bodies, because you would touch one before you saw one, and you would not see one at all.

They are tall, but not in the way trees are tall — in the way herons are tall, economical, built for standing very still in wet places. A grown Listener is perhaps seven feet at the crown, and most of that is torso and neck. The legs are long and thin and end in wide splayed feet that distribute weight across the mud the way a water-strider's legs distribute weight across a pond. They can stand for hours without sinking. They can stand for days. Standing is, for them, a kind of thought.

The skin is the color of wet bark and slightly loose, pebbled with small raised nodes that are not decoration. Each node is a listening organ. They have thousands. The densest concentration runs in two broad bands down the flanks from armpit to hip, and these bands are what they turn toward a sound they want to understand — not the face, not the head, but the side of the body, presented the way you might present a palm to a fire. When a Listener is attending to you, it stands sideways.

The head is small. This is the thing that disturbs visitors most, when they imagine them. The head is small because the head is mostly just a mounting bracket for the ears, which are not ears in the cupped-flesh sense but great fanlike membranes that unfold from behind the jaw and can be spread wide like a cobra's hood or folded tight to the neck for sleeping. The membranes are translucent and veined. In what little light ever reaches them — a phosphorescent fungus, a machine-spark — they glow faintly pink with blood.

The eyes are vestigial. Two small wet points, set close together, that register only the difference between utter dark and slightly-less-utter dark. They are kept half-closed. When a Listener is startled, the eyes close entirely; this is the opposite of what you would expect, and it is the first thing that tells you you are among a people who have lived in the dark so long that sight is a distraction to be sealed away during moments of importance.

The mouth is broad and mobile and contains more than teeth. It contains, along the palate, a second set of tympanic tissue, so that they taste sound. Certain frequencies are sweet to them. The rhythmic thud of a particular hammer-mill a few miles west is, to a Listener who grew up hearing it, the flavor of childhood.

---

They are not numerous. The understorey cannot carry dense populations of anything large, and the Listeners are large. A band is seven to twenty individuals, spread across a territory the size of a small country, rarely all gathered at once. They know where the others are at all times by a low continuous humming that each Listener produces from a resonating chamber in the upper chest — a personal drone, pitched slightly differently for each person, inaudible to most fauna but audible to other Listeners across astonishing distances because the understorey conducts low frequencies the way water conducts them. A Listener alone in the forest is not alone. She hears her mother three valleys over, humming while she works. She hears her uncle's drone falter and resume, which means he has shifted his weight, which means he is standing and not sitting, which means he is hunting or thinking.

The drone stops only in sleep and in death. When a Listener dies, the silence where the drone was is felt by the whole band
