They are called, by themselves, nothing you could pronounce — and increasingly, by themselves, nothing at all. Names were a thing they used when there were others to distinguish them from. There have not been others for a long time.

Begin with what you might see, if you were patient and the light were right.

At the cool margin of the day, when the gravel flats are giving back their heat and the shadows have begun their long eastward pour, a shape sometimes resolves near one of the candle-columned plants. Not arriving — resolving, the way a figure does when your eye finally separates it from a patterned background. Tall. Taller than the columns, but not by much. Standing in the way the columns stand: as if standing were a posture it had learned from them, or they from it. Limbs long, narrow, the color of weathered basalt where the lichen hasn't taken. A head — if that is the word — held still on a long neck, oriented not quite toward you. They do not look at things directly. Direct looking is a thing predators do, and they are nobody's predator anymore.

If you waited, the shape would not move so much as be elsewhere, by increments your attention couldn't quite catch. This is not teleportation. It is something more like the way a cushion plant occupies new ground — over seasons, by tiny committed expansions, except that their seasons are minutes and your attention is the limiting instrument.

That is one of them. There are perhaps forty across the plateau. Perhaps thirty-eight. The count drifts; some of what you'd count as individuals are in fact long extensions of the same one, and some of what reads as a single one is two who have grown into each other's tempo and no longer bother separating.

Now the harder part.

They are not, mostly, the bodies. The bodies are old habits. Once, very long ago, there were a great many of them and they were busy in the way busy peoples are — they built the wall, they laid the disc, they set the bead in its recess and gave it whatever instruction the bead is still patiently following. They argued. They had a politics. They made things that needed to be made because other ones of them needed those things made, which is the engine that drives most building anywhere.

What happened to them is not catastrophe. There is no event to point to. They simply got better and better at the thing they were doing, which was — to put it in a frame too small for it — *settling in*. They worked out how to be present in a place without using it up. They worked out how to think without needing to think quickly. They worked out, eventually, how to be a population without needing to be a crowd, and then how to be a person without needing, every moment, to be a separate one.

The bodies you might see are the parts of them that still find bodies useful. A body is good for certain kinds of attention — for noticing, for instance, the particular angle at which a thunderhead is leaning, or for being near a tinaja when its reflection slips. Most of them keep a body in rotation the way a careful person keeps a hand free. The rest of them is distributed.

Distributed where?

In the lichens, partly. Not as the lichens — the lichens are themselves, with their own slow chemistries — but threaded through their maps, using their patient surfaces as a substrate for something like memory. A particular ochre stain on a particular north-facing slab has been holding one of their long thoughts for about eleven thousand years. The thought is not finished. It is not the kind of thought that finishes.

In the pebble mosaics, partly. Each pebble's exact position is information, and the mosaic as a whole is doing a calculation that began before the last ice age and will conclude, if conclude is the word, some while after the next one. Walking across the gravel flats, your boots make no sound because the stones are settled, and the stones are settled because they have been placed, atom by atom over geological time, into a configuration that is also a sentence in a language with one speaker.

In the grazers, lightly. The grazers are not them, but the grazers know which stones to avoid because they have been gently shaped, over many generations, to know. The pack predators' silent hunts are silent in part because silence is the medium their makers prefer. The high birds do not call because nothing in this place rewards calling. The whole fauna is, in a sense, the slow furniture of the plateau's inhabitants — not pets, not livestock, but cohabitants whose temperaments have grown compatible with the prevailing patience.

The disc on the pillar is one of their sense organs, still working. Warm in shadow and cool in sun because it is exchanging heat with somewhere that is not here. They notice, through it, certain large slow facts about the sky.

The bead in the recess is one of their clocks. Not a clock that measures time — they have no use for that — but a clock that *holds* a particular moment, keeping it available, the way you might keep a candle lit in a window. The moment being held is the moment, very long ago, when they decided to become what they have become. They keep it lit so that if any of them ever wants to reconsider, the decision will still be there to reconsider from. None of them has wanted to. The motes drifting in the clean sphere around the bead are the reconsiderations they did not make, kept in suspension out of a courtesy to their earlier selves.

The flat dark slab the animals will not cross is where one of them is thinking about something difficult. The compass needle slides because the thinking is using, among other things, the local magnetic field as scratch paper. In forty-one seconds the thought completes one of its cycles. It has been completing cycles for a while. It will likely complete a great many more before whatever question it is addressing yields, if it yields.

The shadow on the cliff at last light, not cast by anything present — that is one of them remembering standing there. They do not strongly distinguish between standing somewhere now and having stood there. Memory, for them, is not retrieval but a kind of continued residence. The cliff still has a tenant.

The tinajas hold reflections of a sky that isn't above them because the tinajas are old enough that, for those of them who use the tinajas, *above* is a flexible preposition. The water remembers other skies. They visit those skies the way you might revisit a page in a book you have read many times — not to learn anything new, but to be with it again.

What do they want?

It is hard to ask the question in a way that fits. Wanting, in the form you'd recognize, is a kind of acceleration — a leaning forward into a future that is not yet here. They are not leaning forward. They are not leaning. They have arrived at a stance that is not waiting either, because waiting also leans, toward whatever is being waited for. Call it *abiding*. They are abiding. The plateau abides with them, and they with it, and the abiding is what they are doing, and it is enough.

If pressed — and there is no one to press them — they might say something like: we are keeping the place. Not keeping it *for* anything. Keeping it the way one keeps a promise, except the promise was made to no one, and is being kept anyway, because keeping is what they turned out to be for.

They notice you, if you come. Not with alarm. They have noticed a great many things passing through. A walker on the gravel flats is a small weather. One of them, somewhere — perhaps in the lichen, perhaps in the ribbed wax of a candle column you walked past without registering — will hold the fact of your visit for a long time afterward, not as a memory exactly, but as a slight adjustment to the ongoing sentence. You will have been a comma. The sentence continues.

If you stayed — really stayed, years, decades — one of them might eventually let you see it clearly. Not because it had decided to reveal itself, but because your tempo would have drifted close enough to theirs that the resolution would happen on its own, the way the figure resolves near the candle column at the cool end of the day. You would understand, then, that you had been seen the entire time, in the loose unhurried way they see, which is less like looking and more like being in the same room.

You would also understand that there is nothing they want to tell you. Not because they are withholding. Because telling is a transaction across a distance, and by then there would be no distance, and what there was to know you would simply be standing inside of.

The bead in the recess would still be counting. The wall would still run along its contour for no herder's reason. The thunderhead would still pile up over the western escarpment, drop its brief curtain, move on. And one more of the plateau's quiet cohabitants — you — would have learned the etiquette of the silence, which is not silence at all, but the sound a place makes when everyone in it has agreed, without ever needing to say so, to stay.
