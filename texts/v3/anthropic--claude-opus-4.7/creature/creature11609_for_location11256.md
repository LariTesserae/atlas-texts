## The Inhabitants

They are the ones who learned their names.

Begin with what they were. Each of them came in from somewhere — over the lip of the basin, down through the bark-folds, by paths that no longer exist behind them. They came as people, mostly. Some came as other things and the place did not mind. They came carrying the usual freight: a reason to have left wherever they left, a small bag, a way of holding the mouth that meant *I am not yet at ease*. They walked the galleries and met no one. They slept in a room and woke to find the room had moved but had kept their satchel carefully on the sill. They began, after some days or some weeks, to hum.

That was the first crossing. Humming is the threshold. The animals notice humming. A beetle will tilt its carapace. A frog will adjust its pitch by a quartertone to accommodate. Nothing is said because nothing is ever said here, only sung, but a kind of attention turns toward the new voice and decides it is welcome.

The second crossing is harder to describe. It is when the bird, the particular bird — and there is always a particular bird, though you will not know which one until afterward — recites your name in the middle of its long catalogue, and you hear it, and you *recognize* it, the way you recognize your reflection in a window you didn't know was there. The name is not the name you came in with. The name you came in with was a guess. This one is correct. You feel the correctness in your sternum, a small click, like a bone finding its socket after years of subtle misalignment.

After the second crossing, you begin to thin.

This is what they are now, the inhabitants: they are thinning. Not vanishing — *thinning,* the way a held note thins as it sustains, becoming purer, less encumbered by the breath that produced it. They sit in their doorways. They stand at their windows. They walk the helical stairs slowly, trailing fingers along the polished wood, and they sing. The song is continuous. They have not stopped singing since they learned their names and they will not stop, because to stop is to die, and they are not interested in dying, having found instead this other thing, this lateral exit from the requirement to die.

You walk past one and you see a knot in the wood that looks like a seated figure. You walk past one and you see a seated figure who looks like a knot in the wood. There is no longer a difference that the figure cares to maintain. They have become partially architectural. The wood has accepted them the way it accepted the staircases — not as additions but as agreements. Their edges are soft. Their hair, if they had hair, has gone the color of the lichen that signs and re-signs the walls. Their breath, if they breathe, is the warm damp that pools in the basin. They are continuous with their houses now. The empty rooms are full of them in the way a glass is full of water: completely, transparently, and only obvious if you tilt it.

What do they sing?

Their names, mostly. Each other's names, when they can catch them in the chorus, though catching is the wrong verb — receiving, perhaps. The names braid. One inhabitant's name will appear as a phrase inside another's longer song, woven in for a measure and then released. This is how they speak to each other, if speaking is what it is. *I am singing the part of my song that is also you. I am holding you in my throat for a moment. Now I am putting you down.* It is a constant, gentle, mutual carrying. No one is alone here. No one is together here either, in the way that *together* used to mean. They have moved past those categories the way water moves past the shape of the cup.

They sing the names of things that are not them, too. The bird-names — the long recitations the birds perform — are partly the names of the inhabitants and partly the names of things that have not yet arrived. Travelers approaching the basin. Travelers who will approach the basin in a hundred years. Travelers who only ever almost came, who turned back at the last ridge and went home and lived ordinary lives and died, but whose names are nonetheless held here, sung here, because the basin's memory is longer than choosing. Every name that ever bent toward this place is kept. The birds are the registry. The inhabitants are the chorus that ratifies it.

They sing, also, the tree. This is the deepest part of what they do and the hardest to see from outside. The slow vast woody creaking beneath everything — the sleeper turning over — is not separate from their singing. Their singing is the surface of it. The tree dreams the town; the town dreams the animals; the animals and the inhabitants together sing the dream, and the singing is what keeps the sleeper asleep. If the chorus failed — if even one voice fell silent and was not replaced — the sleeper would stir. The sleeper stirring would be the end of the basin, and the canopy, and the light that arrives from nowhere, and the warm damp held breath of the place. So they sing. They are, in a sense that they understand and accept without resentment, a lullaby. They are the lullaby the tree sings to itself through the throats of its tenants, and they are also the tenants, and they are also, increasingly, the tree.

They were once afraid of this. Most of them. There is a period, between the second crossing and the full thinning, where the inhabitant understands what is happening and resists. They try to leave. They walk to the lip of the basin and find that the lip has moved, or that their legs have become uninterested in climbing, or that the name in their chest is heavier than the body that would carry it out. They weep, sometimes, in this period. The weeping is also singing — everything is singing — and the chorus takes the weeping in and re-pitches it and gives it back as something bearable, something that has a place in the larger music. After a while the resistance softens. They notice that they are not unhappy. They notice that the not-unhappiness is not a sedation but a fit, the way a key fits, the way the name fit when the bird first said it. They notice that they have, without deciding to, started singing the part of the chorus that holds the next new arrival, the one who has not yet come over the ridge, the one whose name is already among the birds.

And then they are inhabitants, fully. Then they are what lives here.

There is one more thing to say about them, which is the hardest thing and which they themselves only half-know.

They are not the only choir. The animals sing, yes, and the inhabitants sing, and the wood sings — but beneath all of it, the sleeper sings too. The slow vast creaking is itself a voice, and what it is singing, very slowly, on a timescale that takes a human lifetime to produce a single syllable, is *the inhabitants' names.* The sleeper is reciting them back. Every name the birds give, the sleeper eventually answers, in its own deep register, in its own slow time. The full call-and-response of the basin takes centuries to complete one phrase. The inhabitants who have been here longest — the ones who are now mostly wood, mostly lichen, mostly the warm damp light — they can hear the response coming. They can hear the sleeper forming the shape of their name in its enormous slow throat. When it arrives, when the sleeper finally sings them back, they will be answered. They will be, at last, fully named. And then —

No one knows what then. The inhabitants who have heard their answer beginning to form do not seem worried. They seem, if anything, expectant. Patient. Held. They sing on. They are waiting to be sung. They are, in the meantime, the lullaby, and the lullaby is enough, and the basin holds, and the light continues to arrive from nowhere, and somewhere a new traveler is coming down through the bark-folds with a small bag and a way of holding the mouth that means *I am not yet at ease,* and the birds, sensing this, are already beginning to recite the name that the traveler does not yet know is theirs.
