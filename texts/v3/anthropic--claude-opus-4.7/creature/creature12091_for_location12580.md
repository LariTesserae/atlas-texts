## The People of Sefr Pan

They are called — by themselves, when they speak the word at all — the *seyfari*, which means something like "those who keep" or "those who tend," though the verb form implies a reciprocal relationship: the keeping goes both ways. The town keeps them as much as they keep the town.

---

They are tall. This is the first thing you notice, after the quiet. Adult seyfari stand around two meters, most of them, with the long-limbed build that belongs to peoples who have lived for many generations in thin air and reflected light. Their skin runs from a deep copper-bronze to nearly black, with a curious property: it carries fine pale lines where salt has worked itself into the creases over years, so that an old seyfar's hands and face are mapped with bright traceries, like a riverbed seen from above. The young have smooth skin. By forty the lines are visible. By seventy the face has become its own small cartography.

Their eyes are deep-set and heavily lidded — protection against the glare — and they have learned a way of looking that involves very little movement of the head. They will track you across a room with their eyes alone, the rest of them perfectly still. It is not unfriendly. It is the habit of people who have learned that motion startles.

Their hair, when they have it, is kept close to the scalp and oiled flat. Many of the foundry workers shave it entirely. The reason is practical: salt accumulates in hair, and the chemistry of the basin would slowly turn any loose hair into something between fiber and crust.

They wear loose layered garments of an undyed cloth woven from — I think — the long fibers of the fungal mats, processed somehow, beaten and retted and spun. The cloth is the color of weak tea, soft, and breathes well. Over this, in the working hours, they wear aprons and sleeve-guards of the same dark metal as the buildings, hammered very thin and lined with felt. A seyfar in full foundry dress looks like an insect: matte-dark plates over soft pale cloth, with only the hands and face exposed. The plates absorb the radiant heat from the furnaces and conduct it down to a belt of water-tubes that runs around the waist, cooling it. They are walking heat exchangers.

Their shoes are the most carefully made thing they own. Triple-soled, the outer of soft hide, the middle of compressed fungal felt, the inner of woven cloth. A seyfar can run across a tiled floor and make less sound than a falling leaf.

---

They speak softly. Of course. But more than that: their language has evolved around the constraint of quiet.

It is full of sibilants and soft palatal consonants — sounds that carry intimately but die quickly in open air. There are almost no plosives. The harder sounds, when they appear at all, appear at the ends of words and are swallowed rather than released. To an outsider it sounds like a continuous low murmur, almost like the sound of the seeps themselves, which I suspect is not coincidence. The seyfari have been shaped by their soundscape and have shaped their soundscape in turn.

They also gesture. Not in the demonstrative way of peoples who use the hands to emphasize speech, but in a parallel channel — small, precise movements of the fingers and the angle of the wrist that carry meaning alongside the spoken word. A seyfar saying *"the western foundry is running hot"* will speak the sentence in a near-whisper and simultaneously trace, with three fingers of the right hand against the thigh, a sign that means *west* and a sign that means *fever*. The signs are redundant with the words. The redundancy is the point: if the listener missed a syllable, the gesture filled it in. They will use the signs alone, too, across a room, across a foundry floor, across the width of a street where speech would not carry.

Children learn the signs before they learn the words. A seyfari infant is given a soft cloth strip to wear around the wrist, and the parents move the infant's hand through the basic signs — *here, there, mother, water, hot, listen* — long before the child can produce the corresponding sounds. By the time a child speaks, the hands already know how.

---

Their work is metal, and metal is the long thread of their lives.

The foundries produce plates of an alloy — copper-rich, with antimony and something else I do not recognize, possibly tellurium drawn up from the same hot aquifer that feeds the seeps. The plates are exported east, where (I have been told, not shown) they are used in the construction of instruments: gauges, weights, the cores of certain mechanical clocks, the linings of vessels that must resist particular acids. Sefr Pan does not make finished things. It makes the dense and stable raw materials from which finished things, elsewhere, are made.

Every adult seyfar has a foundry role, but the roles rotate. No one spends a whole life pouring metal, or a whole life tending the cooling baths, or a whole life cutting salt-blocks for repair. The rotation is slow — three to five years per role — and is decided by a council of the elders, the *karahin*, who meet at the half-moon and assign positions for the coming season. The reason given, when I asked, was that *"the body forgets, but slowly, and the work must remember more than the body."* I take this to mean: the institutional knowledge must be distributed, so that no single death takes too much with it.

There are roles besides the foundry. There are the *salt-cutters*, who go out at dawn with thin saws and harvest the largest cones, replacing them in the town's walls and floors. There are the *pipe-walkers*, who maintain the network of cooling tubes and the open outlets where the deer-shaped animals drink. There are the *listeners*, who do nothing visible at all — they sit at the edges of the town, in small alcoves built for the purpose, and simply listen, all day, to the cleaners and the seeps and the distant foundries, and report any irregularity. A young seyfar of unusual auditory sensitivity may be marked for listener-training at five or six years old. Listeners are revered and somewhat pitied. The training does something to them. They become, over decades, very quiet themselves, and increasingly difficult to startle, and increasingly far away in their gaze.

And there are the *cleaner-tenders*, who manage the treaty.

---

This is the role I have come to understand least, and to find most moving.

The cleaner-tenders know the small pale crustacean-things individually. Not all of them — there are tens of thousands of cleaners in the basin — but enough of them, the established ones, the long-lived ones who hold territory near the seep-mouths and along the building foundations. Each tender is responsible for a district of perhaps a hundred meters' radius, and within that district they know which cleaners are which, where they nest, when they molt, what disturbs them.

The tenders walk their districts at the cool hours, very slowly, carrying a small clay dish of something I was not allowed to inspect — some kind of supplement, perhaps a paste of ground fungal mat enriched with minerals the cleaners need but cannot easily get. They set tiny portions of this paste at known stations. The cleaners come, or do not come. The tenders watch, and note, and adjust.

When a cleaner dies, the tender finds the body — they always find it, I do not know how — and carries it in a folded cloth to a place at the basin's edge where the bodies are returned to the seeps. There is no ceremony I could see, but there is a pause. The tender stands for some time at the edge before walking back.

When a cleaner is sick, the tender will sometimes carry it inside, into a small humid room kept for the purpose, and tend it there until it recovers or does not. The seyfari do not eat the cleaners. They do not handle them casually. The treaty is older than any living seyfar, older than the present arrangement of the foundries, older perhaps than the town itself. The seyfari speak of it as one speaks of a marriage that has lasted through many generations of both families: not romantic, not even chosen, but constitutive.

---

How they came to this place I do not know, and I do not think they know either, not in the way that other peoples know their origins. There are stories, but the stories are about *the first quiet* — the moment when an ancestor (gender unspecified, name unspecified, only ever called *the one who stopped speaking*) realized that the small pale things would do the work of keeping the seep-mouths clear if they were not driven off. The stories do not say where the ancestor came from. They begin with the ancestor already on the plateau, already among the cones, already learning to be silent.

What I think — and this is only thought, not knowledge — is that the seyfari are an old people who chose this place because it was difficult, and who became, over a long time, the kind of people for whom this place is not difficult but simply home. The salt in their skin-lines. The signs their infants learn before words. The shoes. The treaty. None of these are adaptations to an environment. They are an environment that has been folded into a people, and a people who have been folded into an environment, until you cannot say where one ends.

They do not, I think, consider themselves remarkable. When I tried to express to one of the karahin — through my poor command of their soft language, supplemented by the gestures I had begun to learn — that I found their way of life astonishing, she looked at me for a long time with that motionless tracking gaze, and then signed, very slowly so I could follow: *water comes up. salt forms. we tend. it is not strange. it is only what is.*

And then she struck the brass plate, once, with its felted clapper, and the sound that was not quite a sound moved out across the basin, and somewhere in the salt-cones the cleaners paused for a moment in their patient work, and then resumed.
