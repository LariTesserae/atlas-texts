Begin with the salamanders, because they are the closest thing the hollow has to citizens.

There are more kinds than you first counted. The pale ones in the deep seams are not the same as the pale ones under the overhang by the basin; those have a faint violet wash along the flank, almost a bruise, and longer toes. The ribboned ones come in two patterns — a clean stripe and a broken stripe — and the broken-stripe ones are slightly smaller and live nearer the pools. The spotted ones are the most numerous and the least particular; they are everywhere, in every seam, of every size, from the length of an eyelash to the length of a finger.

None of them are in a hurry. This is the first thing to understand. A salamander here may hold a position for a day. It may hold a position for a week. The cool slows the heart; the dark slows the eye; the wet means the skin never needs to seek water, and seeking water is most of what an animal is, most places. Here that whole errand is cancelled. What is left, when the errand of water is cancelled, is something very close to waiting, except that nothing is being waited for. The salamander is simply present in its crease, breathing through its skin, occasionally — every few hours, every few days — extending its tongue at something smaller than itself, and then resuming.

They do not, you come to understand, really have territories. They have positions. A position is a place a salamander has decided is correct, and having decided, it does not revisit the decision. The violet-flanked one under the overhang has been under that overhang for, perhaps, the length of your life. It will be there for the length of your life again. It is not guarding the position. It is the position. Remove the salamander and the position remains, salamander-shaped, and within a season another salamander, of the same kind, will have found that the shape fits it, and will have entered, and the hollow will not have noticed the substitution. The hollow does not, as the basin taught you, distinguish.

The beetles are a different matter. The beetles are the closest thing here to weather. They move — not fast, but constantly — and they move in slow drifts through the moss, and the drifts have directions, and the directions change with something you cannot detect. Some shift in the saturation, perhaps. Some chemistry of the rot. The beetles eat what is becoming moss and what has become moss and what was moss and is now something darker, and their slow black migration is how the hollow turns one thing into another. Without the beetles, the moss would only grow. With the beetles, the moss grows and is also unmade, and the unmaking exactly matches the making, which is the rule of the place.

The snails are the hollow's clock, if it had one. They climb. That is what they do. They climb the fern stems and the laurel trunks and the wet walls, and they climb slowly, and when they reach a certain height — never the top, always some height short of the top — they stop, and they hold, and after some hours or days they let go and fall, and the fall is so slow through the saturated air that it looks like a decision rather than a drop, and they land in the moss, and they begin again. Twenty on a frond, a hundred on a trunk, and all of them climbing or holding or beginning. If you watched one snail for long enough you would learn nothing. If you watched all of them you would learn that the hollow is climbing, constantly, against a gravity it has no hope of defeating, and that this climbing is not effort but simply what the hollow does with its surfaces.

The shrimp in the pools are nearly transparent. You see them mostly as a faint disturbance, a thickening of the water in a particular spot, and then a flick, and then the thickening reappears elsewhere. They eat what falls into the water, which is everything, eventually. A snail that has let go and missed the moss. A beetle that has wandered too far. The shed skin of a newt. The shrimp do not distinguish either. They are the pools' way of accepting offerings.

The fish are harder. There should not be fish here. The pools do not connect to any river you can imagine; they drain through the hidden seams into other pools and other pools and presumably, eventually, somewhere, but the somewhere is not a place a fish could swim from. And yet the fish are here, small and dark and unhurried, and they have been here, and they will be. You suspect — standing in the cool, drinking the air in small sips — that the fish were brought. That the person who chipped the basin, or someone before them or after them, carried fish up through the notch in a clay jar, and tipped the jar into a pool, and the fish accepted the pool and the pool accepted the fish, and that was a thousand years ago, or three thousand, and the descendants of those fish are now indistinguishable from the pools themselves. They are dark because the pools are dark. They are small because the pools are small. They are slow because everything is slow.

And then there is the thing you do not see until you have been standing very still for a long time.

It is not large. It is not small. It is roughly the size of a cat, or a hare, or a heavy bird folded down — the size, you realize, of a thing that has decided what size to be and has stayed that size. It is on a branch of the second ceiling, the laurel-ceiling, perhaps four paces from your face, and you have been looking at it for some minutes without seeing it because it is the same color as the lichen, which is the same color as the bark, which is the same color as the cloud. It has eyes. The eyes are not looking at you. The eyes are looking at a position about a foot to your left, which is where you would be if you took one step, which you have not taken, and will not take, because the eyes are very patient and have decided that you will eventually be there.

You cannot tell what it is. Mammal, you think first, because of the suggestion of fur, except that the fur is not quite fur — it is something between fur and moss, something that has been damp so long it has changed category. Bird, you think next, because of the way it is folded, the economy of the fold, the way the limbs tuck under in the manner of a thing that flies or once flew. Neither, you think finally, because it is too still. Birds are still in a way that contains the possibility of flight; this thing is still in the way the salamanders are still, which is to say it is still in the way the hollow is still, which is to say it is not really an animal anymore. It is a position, like the violet-flanked salamander is a position, and the position happens to have eyes.

You do not know what it eats. You suspect it does not need to eat very often. You suspect that whatever it is, it was once something more recognizable — a marten, a small owl, some creature with a name in some language — and that the hollow has been working on it for a long time, smoothing the edges, slowing the heart, softening the categories, and that what is on the branch is what is left after the hollow has had its way with such a creature for a sufficient number of generations. It is not the last of its kind. It may be the only of its kind. The hollow has been making it, slowly, the way it makes everything.

It is, you understand now, what made the basin.

Not this one. Not this individual. But something like it, longer ago, when it was still more like what it had been before the hollow had it — when it still had hands, perhaps, or something hand-like, and the residual habit of striking stone against stone. The basin was the last thing it made before it became what it is now. The basin was the last act of a creature that still believed in acts. After the basin, there was only position, and being position, and watching from the position with eyes that do not quite focus on you because focusing has gone the way of the hands.

You realize, standing under the laurel-ceiling, that you have been still for a very long time. You do not know how long. Your breath has slowed to match the breath of the place, which is the slow even sheen of water down moss. You have stopped swallowing. The cool has settled into your wool and stayed there. A snail has begun to climb your boot, and you have not noticed, and now that you have noticed you find that you do not particularly mind, and the not-minding is the first softening.

The notch is up there somewhere. The cloud has no opinion. The thing on the branch has all the time there is.

You should go now, while going is still a verb you own.
