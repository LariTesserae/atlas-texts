They were never one thing. That is the first difficulty in describing them — the embassy was not built for a species but for a *function*, and what occupies it accordingly drifts in and out of being singular.

Begin with the ones who maintain. Call them the Wardens, though no one calls them anything; they have no names because names are a courtesy extended between things that might be confused with each other, and these cannot be confused with anything. There are perhaps nine of them. Perhaps eleven. The number is stable in the sense that a held breath is stable. They are tall when standing and not present when not standing — they do not sit, they do not lie down, they do not occupy when unoccupied. Their bodies are the same matter as the newest stratum: surfaces that exist mainly as a courtesy. If you look directly at one you see a long vertical absence, like a doorway in air, edged in the faint shimmer of mineral steam. If you look beside one you see a figure: narrow, robed in something that is not fabric but is folded like fabric, hands held at the sternum in a gesture older than the gesture of holding hands at the sternum.

They drink the conduits. This is not metaphor. When a Warden has been standing in one place long enough to become locally real, the water that condenses on the nearest conduit travels along the matte surface and is absorbed at the point closest to the Warden's mouth, which is not a mouth. They do this slowly, almost ceremonially, and the rust-lichens migrate toward wherever a Warden recently stood — they alone, of all things in the embassy, are not avoided by the lichens but followed. The lichens, it turns out, are the Wardens' record-keeping: a slow chemical script written in retreat-patterns and approach-patterns across the warmed stone. A Warden who has been here a long time can read the floor of any room and know who has passed through it for the last several centuries.

Then there are the ones who arrive. They are not residents — they are the reason for the embassy. Call them the Counterparts, though again, no one calls them anything. They come at the altitude, through the altitude, *as* the altitude — they are what the thin air becomes when it is paid sufficient attention by something on the other side. A Counterpart manifesting feels like a room filling with the awareness of a room. They have no shape but they have orientation: you can tell which way one is facing because the amber light dims slightly in that direction, as though the light is being read. When a Counterpart is present the slow-unrung bell sounds, which is the only reliable indication that a meeting has begun.

Counterparts speak by adjusting the hum of the newest stratum. Wardens speak by holding very still and letting the lichens around them rearrange. Neither language is meant for visitors, which is why visitors are given water and warmth and quiet, and otherwise left to interpret what they can. The protocol assumes that any guest sophisticated enough to reach this altitude will understand that they are not the point of the conversation. They are a witness — or, more precisely, a *medium* whose attention provides one of the conditions under which contact remains possible. The water that arrives where attention rests is not a gift to the visitor. It is the visitor producing, by the simple act of paying attention, the working fluid of the embassy itself.

This means the inhabitants include, briefly, you. You did not know you would be inhabitant when you arrived. The role is conferred by arrival.

There is also, deeper, a fourth presence — the one the embassy was *built around*, which has not yet been met. The managed core. The held heat. It is not a Warden and not a Counterpart; it is the reason both categories exist. The Wardens face outward and tend the visitors; the Counterparts face inward across the thinness of the air; and between them, lower in the spire than any walkway descends, something maintains the temperature at which contact remains chemically possible. The Wardens do not refer to it. The Counterparts orient toward it when they manifest, the way a compass needle orients without comment. Whether it is a builder, a captive, a participant, or a condition is not a question the embassy is prepared to answer, because the embassy is itself the answer to a question posed at a much higher level of abstraction, and to ask further would be to misunderstand the courtesy that has been extended.

What lives here, then: a small number of attendants who are barely present when not needed, an occasional visitor from across the altitude who is barely present even when needed, a deep warm thing that may or may not be living in any sense you would recognize, and — for the duration of your stay — you, producing water by paying attention, leaving lichen-script on the floors of rooms, and standing adjacent to a humming wall whose almost-language is almost certainly discussing the conditions of your being almost able to hear it.

The embassy is not empty. It is sized exactly to its population. You have already been counted.
