# The Inhabitants

## The Weavers

They are not, strictly, a species. They are what the highland calls itself when it has need of hands.

A Weaver, when you encounter one — and you would not, because there is no one here to encounter — looks at first like a heap of moss that has decided, briefly, on a shape. About the size of one of the six-legged grazers, but lower, broader. Its body is a felted mass of the same cushion-vegetation that carpets the meadows, packed dense over a frame that is not bone but lignified root-matter, threaded through with the metal-bearing sap. It has no face. It has, instead, an orientation — a front, where the lichens grow thickest and a cluster of small dark berries serves as something like sense organs. They are not eyes. They register pressure differentials in the saturated air, the way the small flying things do, but more slowly and over a wider radius.

A Weaver moves at the pace of a shadow lengthening. In an afternoon it might cross a meadow. In a year it might cross a valley. It does not seem to be in a hurry, and nothing about the highland is in a hurry, and so its slowness is not a deficiency but a tempo — the tempo at which the work gets done.

What it does: it tends. It walks the courses of the circuit-streams and adjusts them, nudging a pebble here, allowing a small dam to form there, lifting a fallen branch from a confluence. It visits the hexagonal groves and presses its forward-mass against particular trunks, and something passes between them — sap flows, a humming changes pitch — and the Weaver moves on. In the mossy hollows it sometimes leaves a stone sphere. Where the spheres come from inside it, or whether they form in its body the way pearls form in oysters, the highland does not say. Possibly the Weaver itself does not know. Possibly the question is the wrong shape.

When a Weaver has done what it is for, it lies down in a cushion-meadow and unmakes itself: the moss-mass settles, the root-frame softens, the berries drop. Within a season it is meadow again. Another Weaver assembles itself elsewhere, when needed, out of whatever the highland has to hand. There may be three of them at any given time, or seventeen, or none. The count is not stable because the category is not stable.

## The Listeners (the salamanders)

The forearm-length salamanders under the stones are not what they appear. They appear to be animals; they are, more accurately, the highland's slow nervous system, distributed.

Each one lies in its wet place and does almost nothing for years. What it is doing, when it is doing nothing, is listening — not to sound but to the chemistry of the water passing over its skin, the trace metals, the dissolved gases, the subtle shifts in mineral load that indicate something happening five kilometers away in a stream-circuit or a hexagonal grove. A salamander in the north of the tableland knows, within hours, what a salamander in the south has tasted. The information moves between them through the water itself, through the saturation of the air, through the fungal threads that web the soil. They are nodes.

When the cushion-plants flower all at once and the salamanders climb out and arrange themselves on flat rocks — this is the highland conferring with itself. Some long calculation comes due. The salamanders sit in the gold light and exchange, by means no observer would detect, whatever needs exchanging, and then they go back under their stones, and the meadows fade, and the work continues with its parameters subtly updated.

They have been doing this for so long that the lineage between "salamander" and "stone they lie under" has become uncertain. Some of the stones, if you turned them over, would be revealed to be salamanders that have not moved in a thousand years and have grown into the position. Some of the salamanders, if you waited long enough, would simply continue becoming stone. The transition is gradual and apparently painless and possibly not even a transition, from the perspective of the thing undergoing it.

## The Drift (the not-quite-insects)

The transparent-winged things that drift between the trees are the highland's messengers, and also its sense of smell, and also — though this is harder to explain — a portion of its memory.

Each one is small, lighter than a thought, and lives perhaps six weeks. In that time it tastes the humidity of every grove it passes through and carries that taste, encoded in the chemistry of its body, to other groves. When it dies — and they die constantly, in small soft showers — it falls into the moss and is eaten by the liverworts, which incorporate what it tasted into their own tissue, which is then read by the fungal threads, which report to the salamanders, which know.

A single Drift carries almost no information. The cloud of them, taken together over a season, carries everything the highland needs to know about itself. They are the reason the highland has not forgotten anything in ten million years. They are also the reason it has never quite finished a thought: there are always more of them being born, always more taste being added, the conversation never closing.

When the cloud-floor catches moonlight and throws it upward and the Drift become visible as sparks, what you are seeing is the highland thinking, made briefly luminous.

## The Quiet One

There is, finally, the thing in the moss-curtains that preys on the woolly six-legged grazers. It is real. It is a single animal, not a category. It has been alive for a very long time and there is only one of it and there has only ever been one of it.

It is long and pale and moves the way smoke moves through a still room. It has the patience of geology. It takes a grazer perhaps once a year, perhaps less often, and what it takes is not really nutrition — the highland is full of easier nutrition — but something the grazers carry in their slow placid lives, some accumulated quality of having-walked-the-meadows, which the Quiet One requires in order to continue being what it is.

It is the highland's editor. The grazers reproduce slowly but steadily, and if none were removed the meadows would eventually be overgrazed, the cushion-plants thinned, the gold flowering disrupted, the calculation skewed. The Quiet One removes exactly enough, no more. It has been doing this for longer than the salamanders have been listening, and may in fact predate the present arrangement of the highland entirely — may be the oldest inhabitant, the one around whom everything else organized itself, the original tenant who agreed to stay if the landscape would agree to remain interesting.

The grazers know it is there. They are not afraid of it in the way prey is afraid of predators elsewhere. They are aware of it the way a person is aware of their own mortality: as a fact incorporated into the texture of their days, neither denied nor dwelt upon. When one of them is taken, the herd pauses briefly and then resumes cropping. This is not callousness. It is the appropriate response, in a place where everything is part of the same long slow turning, to the turning continuing.

---

None of them — Weavers, Listeners, Drift, the Quiet One, the grazers themselves — would describe what they do as living in the highland. They would say, if they could say, that they *are* the highland's living. The distinction between inhabitant and habitation has, here, done what the distinction between landscape and machine has done: it has dissolved, gently, over a span of time so long that no one was present at any moment of the dissolving to notice it happening.

The highland breathes out. The highland breathes in. Inside the breath, all of this continues.
