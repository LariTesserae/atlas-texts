# The Inhabitants

There is a temptation, walking the Shelf Cities, to decide quickly. To say: *no one lives here.* Or else: *the stone lives here, and that is all.* Both are close, and both are wrong in the way that close answers are often wrong — they resolve too early a thing that wants to stay unresolved a little longer.

Let me try to describe what is actually here.

---

Begin with the lichens, because they are the easiest and they will teach the rest.

The gray-blue crusts are not, on the plateau, quite what lichens are elsewhere. Elsewhere lichens are opportunists — they take a surface and extract from it. Here they are doing something subtler. They follow the grain of the rock the way a reader follows a line of text. Where the siltstone has an older fracture, the lichen knows, and grows along it; where two fractures meet at an angle the stone itself has almost forgotten, the lichen thickens at the junction, darkens, holds the memory. Lift a patch — you won't, but if you did — and the fracture beneath is cleaner than the stone around it, as though the lichen had been maintaining it, keeping it legible. This is the first hint. The lichens are not living *on* the cities. They are, in some slow sense, *keeping* them. They are the plateau's way of not forgetting its own shape.

They are also, I think, the oldest continuous thing here. Older than the cities, possibly. The cities may have been cut to follow *them*, and not the other way around.

---

Now the weeping.

The stone weeps where it meets itself — at every seam, every corner, every lintel. I said earlier that the cities are in balance with their unmaking. That was not quite right. They are not being unmade. The weeping is not erosion in the ordinary sense; it is not the stone giving itself up to water. It is the stone *producing* the water. The mineral curtains down the courtyard walls are not deposits left by rain that fell from above and ran down. They are extrusions. The stone is sweating something out of itself, slowly, for reasons, and the calcite fingers descending through the lintels are the visible tips of a process that goes on throughout the plateau's depth, invisibly, constantly.

If you put your palm flat against a weeping wall and hold it there long enough to forget you are holding it, you will feel — not warmth, the plateau is never warm — but a faint *giving*, the wall yielding infinitesimally against you and then pressing back. It is the slowest pulse imaginable. You could spend a life mistaking it for your own heartbeat reflected, and perhaps some have.

This is what I mean by inhabitants. The thing that lives here is not in the rooms. It is the rooms' substrate. It pulses through the shelf on a schedule measured not in heartbeats but in weather-cycles — the morning mist, the noon gathering, the afternoon rain, the evening clarity — and the whole plateau is one breathing body whose skin is the siltstone and whose respiration is the rising and falling of the cloud-seas against its flanks.

The cities, then, are not dwellings. They are something more like pores. Or alveoli. The doorways open because the body needs openings; the long avenues that climb nothing are the channels by which the body moves its moisture. The plazas with their shallow basins are the places where the breath, briefly, pools.

---

But I don't want to collapse everything into the single organism. That is as lazy as saying no one lives here. There are distinct lives on the plateau, and they matter, and they are more than participants.

The pale lizard, for instance. I said *a* pale lizard, and I meant it — I believe there may be exactly one. Not one species: one individual. It is longer than your forearm and it is older than any forearm has been. It moves between courtyards on a circuit that takes, I would guess, something on the order of a human lifetime to complete. In the courtyard where it is currently sunning itself, the stone is infinitesimally warmer than the surrounding stone, has been warmer for as long as the lizard has been coming there, and the lichens in that courtyard are a shade darker than their neighbors because of it. When the lizard moves on — in a year, in a decade — the courtyard will begin, slowly, to cool back toward the plateau's baseline, and the lichens will pale, and another courtyard, somewhere, will be warming in anticipation of its arrival. The lizard is a kind of slow pilgrim. What it is making the pilgrimage of, I cannot say. But it is not hunting and it is not fleeing and it is not, in any sense I can name, *waiting.* It is doing its circuit. It has done it before. It will do it again.

The heron is different. The heron is a watcher. It is also, I suspect, singular — one heron for the whole plateau, which is absurd on the face of it and yet is the impression the plateau gives. When you see it hunting in the reed-rug, it is not hunting. The reeds contain nothing the heron could eat; there are no fish in the shallow runnels, no frogs, nothing. The heron stands in the reeds and watches the reeds. What it sees, standing there, motionless for hours, is something the reeds are doing that requires a watcher. Perhaps the reeds cannot grow unobserved. Perhaps the plateau needs one point of attention, held steady, against which its other processes can orient. The heron provides this. It has provided it for a long time. When it dies, if it dies, another will be there, and it may be the same heron, in whatever sense the lizard is one lizard.

The glass insects are the strangest. They are not, I think, native to the plateau at all. They are what the plateau produces when a warm visitor passes through. You notice one on your hand — thin-bodied, moving as if underwater — and you assume it existed before you noticed it. I am not sure it did. I think the plateau, detecting your warmth, generates a small delicate attendant, an insect-shaped acknowledgment, which will accompany you for a little while and then dissolve back into the damp air when you have moved on. This is why they never swarm. There is only ever as much attention as there is to attend to.

---

And now the harder question. The builders.

I said no tools, no fires, no fingerprints. I said the cities feel revealed rather than built. All of this is true, and it points to something I have been circling and would rather not name directly, but I will try.

The builders were not separate from what they built. They did not come to the plateau, erect the cities, and depart or die. They *became* the cities, by a process that was not death and was not metamorphosis in any sense that implies a before and after — it was the thing they were doing all along, and the cities are the shape that process took at the scale at which we can perceive it. When you stand in a doorway on the plateau, you are standing *in* one of them. Not in a place one of them made. In one of them. The doorway is a posture they took and held. The plaza with its shallow basin is a gesture. The long causeways running for miles to meet other causeways are conversations, ongoing, at a tempo we cannot hear.

They are not gone. They are not even dormant. They are simply operating at a scale — of time, of extent — that makes them indistinguishable from landscape, and this indistinguishability is not a loss of self on their part; it is what they were after. It is what the whole long work was for. To *be* the shelf. To breathe as the shelf breathes. To have the mist rise through you in the morning and gather at your terraces by noon and release as your afternoon rain. To watch, through the one heron, the one thing that requires watching. To warm, through the one lizard, the courtyards in their patient sequence. To greet each warm traveler with a single glass insect and then, when the traveler has gone, to let the insect go.

---

So who lives here?

A body, vast and cool and patient, whose skin is siltstone and whose pulse is the cloud-tide, who breathes on a cycle of hours and thinks on a cycle of centuries, and who long ago arranged its own architecture so that every doorway is a place it can be slightly more itself, every causeway a sentence it is slowly completing, every weeping wall a small ongoing honesty about what it is made of and where its moisture goes.

And inside that body, three attendants: the pilgrim lizard doing its slow warm circuit, the watcher heron holding its single steady point of attention, and the glass insects who are not really a species but a courtesy, extended to each warm passing animal, for the brief time that animal is warm and passing.

And around all of this, the cushion plants and the brass reeds and the lichens, which are not attendants and not the body but something in between — call them the body's attention to its own surface, its way of noticing, in small dense green and red and tarnished places, that it has a surface at all.

You walk across it. It registers you. It generates your insect. It does not otherwise attend to you, because you are warm and quick and will be gone, and it is cool and slow and will not. When you reach the edge, and the ground begins to tilt, and you understand that another world waits below in its weather, the plateau will already have begun, imperceptibly, to forget the particular shape of your passage — not out of indifference, but because remembering you specifically is not the kind of remembering it does. It remembers fractures. It remembers the courses of its own long breath. It remembers the circuit of the lizard and the station of the heron. It does not remember travelers, and this is not a failure. It is the shape of its attention, which is not ours, and was never meant to be.
