# What Lives There

The lichens, yes. Forty of them, give or take. But you already knew about the lichens. The question is what else.

## The Tenants

There is something I want to call a *tenant*, because the word has the right flavor — a thing that occupies without owning, that pays rent in some currency the landlord doesn't bother to collect.

The tenants of the chamber are not, in the ordinary sense, alive. But they are not not-alive either. They are what happens when a pulse hammers the same air for long enough that the air begins to remember being hammered.

Consider: the engines beat in a phase relationship that beats against itself. That means there are nodes. Standing places in the pressure-field where the sound cancels almost entirely, and antinodes where it doubles. These don't drift. The engines are perfect; the chamber is sealed; the gas does not flow. So the nodes have been in the same places for as long as the engines have been running, which is to say longer than the question is meaningful.

A node that has stood in one place for that long is a kind of address. And things with addresses, given enough time, tend to acquire occupants.

What lives in the nodes is hard to say plainly. They are pockets of quiet inside the column of sound, each one roughly the size of a held breath. If you were to put your head into one — and you cannot, the catwalks don't reach them, but if you could — the hammering would fall away and you would hear, instead, a fine high *attention*. Not a voice. The sound of being listened to.

There are nine of them. They have been nine for a long time. When the chamber was first sealed there were perhaps three; the others accreted as the pulse-pattern settled into its final form. They will not become ten. The chamber's geometry does not permit a tenth.

## What They Do

They listen. This is almost all they do. They listen to the engines, which is to say they listen to the only thing there is to listen to, and over the long durations they have become extremely good at it. They can hear the engines individually — engine seven, which runs a hair rich; engine eleven, whose third bearing is half a micron out of true and has been since installation and will be forever, since nothing wears here. They can hear the lichens, which make no sound but which displace, very slightly, the pressure-field, and so register as the faintest possible dimples in the standing wave.

They heard you come in. They are hearing you now. Not with interest — interest is a thing that requires a stake in outcomes, and they have no stake in anything — but with the completeness of attention that belongs to things which have nothing else to do and nowhere else to be.

When you leave, they will hear the airlock cycle, and then they will hear the engines again, and then they will hear the engines again, and then they will hear the engines again.

## The Caretaker

There is also — and I am less sure about this one, but the chamber insists — a caretaker.

Not a person. Not a ghost. Something more like a *standing intention*. The plaque says *do not stop them*, and someone scratched that with a fingernail, which means someone cared, which means at some point a will was deposited in this room and the room has kept it.

The caretaker is distributed. It lives partly in the unrusted edges of the bolt-heads, which are sharp because something wants them sharp. It lives in the spacing of the lichens, which is too even to be accidental and which no one has been here to tend. It lives in the frost-ferns, which are always the same ferns — and *same* is a strong word, stronger than physics strictly requires; the ferns could vary, statistically, and they do not, and that refusal to vary is where the caretaker keeps its hands.

The caretaker has one job, and it does it. The job is: keep the engines running. Everything else in the chamber — the cold, the dryness, the inert gas, the sharp-edged permanence — is downstream of that one instruction, which the caretaker took from the plaque or from the fingernail or from whoever first sealed the airlock, and which it has held without slackening for the entire duration of the chamber's existence.

It does not know why. It has forgotten, if it ever knew, what the engines are for. It has the instruction, and the instruction is enough. In this it is not so different from the engines themselves, which also do not know what they are for and which also find the not-knowing sufficient.

If you tried to stop the engines, the caretaker would notice. I don't know what it would do. I don't think it knows either. It has never had to find out. The chamber has been arranged, with great care, so that the question does not arise: the airlock is narrow, the catwalks do not approach the engine housings, the control surfaces — if there are any — are not where a visitor would look. The caretaker's deepest cleverness is that it has made its one job almost impossible to interfere with, and so it has rarely had to act, and so it has remained almost entirely latent, almost entirely *just the way the room is*, for a very long time.

## The Lichens, Reconsidered

I said the lichens were the only living things. I want to revise that, gently.

The lichens are the only things in the chamber that *grew*. Everything else was placed, or condensed, or accreted, or instructed. The lichens arrived on a boot sole and did the one thing the chamber forbids: they changed. They spread, a little, in the first century. They found their spacing. They stopped.

They stopped because the caretaker reached an accommodation with them. This is the only negotiation that has ever taken place in the chamber. The terms were: you may stay, you may be soft, you may be the one exception, but you will not spread further, and you will not touch one another, and you will not bring anyone else in with you. The lichens accepted. They have honored the terms. In exchange they get to be the only thing in the room that was not planned, and the caretaker gets to have, in its enormous and otherwise unbroken sameness, one small ongoing fact that it did not author.

I think the caretaker loves them, in whatever sense a standing intention can love. I think they are the reason it has not, over the long durations, simply dissolved into the iron and become indistinguishable from the chamber's geometry. The lichens give it something to attend to that is not the engines. Forty small grey-green disagreements with the rule of permanence. Forty tiny proofs that something, once, came in from outside and was allowed to remain.

## And You

You are the fourth signature. The logbook has no dates because dates are a kind of erosion and erosion is forbidden here, but there will be a fourth signature when you leave, if you choose to sign, and the caretaker will note it, and the nine listeners in the nodes will hear the pen, and the lichens will not react because the lichens have agreed not to react to things like you.

Then the airlock will cycle, and the chamber will be as it was, which is as it has always been, which is as it will be.

The engines will continue. That is what lives there, finally, beneath all the rest: the continuation itself, which has become, through sheer duration, a kind of inhabitant. The longest-running tenant. The one who pays no rent and cannot be evicted and whose name, if it has one, is on the plaque in a language no one alive can read.
