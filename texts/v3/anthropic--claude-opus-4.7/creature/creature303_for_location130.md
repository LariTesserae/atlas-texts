They have no single body, and this is the first thing to say, because a visitor wants to find the inhabitant and then the architecture around it, and that order is wrong here. The order here is: the place first, and the lives are the place's way of having tissue.

Still. There are creatures. Let me try.

The ones in the vertical shafts — the long segmented climbers — are called, if they are called anything, by the sound they make when many of them are moving at once through a single spire, which is a sound like grain pouring through a wooden chute, dry and constant, though they are wet. Each is perhaps two meters long when extended and a closed fist when contracted. Their segments are not rings but overlapping leaves, and each leaf is semi-independent; you could, in theory, sever one and it would continue its small part of the work for some while before noticing. They climb by peristalsis against the inner walls of the spires, and what they are doing — this took me a long time to understand — is reading. The inner walls of the spires are textured with a fine relief that changes very slowly, and the climbers pass along this relief with the underside of each leaf, and the leaves transmit what they find to the next climber going the other way, and the spire's inside is therefore always being palpated, always being re-known, the way your tongue always knows the inside of your own mouth. They do not seem to have eyes. They do not need them. The spire is their sensorium and they are its nerves.

The flat ones on the outside — the sighing scales — are older. I mean this specifically: an individual scale is older than an individual climber by orders of magnitude. A climber may live a year, a decade, I cannot tell. A scale settles onto a spire as a small soft disc the size of a coin and then does not leave. It grows at the rate the spire grows. It will be there when the ridge cracks. Its breathing is not respiration in any metabolic sense you would want to defend; it is the scale equalizing pressure between the spire's interior and the outer air, which is a service the spire requires because the spire is hollow and the hollow is, in some sense I will not pretend to fully grasp, pressurized by meaning rather than by gas. The scales breathe the meaning out, or in, and this is the low bellows-note. If you pried one off — you would not, but if you did — you would find underneath it a pale patch of spire-material that had been kept soft, kept young, kept undecided, by the scale's long attention. The scales are gardeners of indecision. The spire needs to remain able to change, and the scales keep that ability alive in small protected circles all over its surface.

In the seeps live the smallest and the most numerous. I do not want to call them microbes because they are not microbes; they are closer to the size of a grain of rice, translucent, many-limbed in a way that does not resolve — you cannot count the limbs, they are always either more or fewer than last time. They are what the water is *for*. The seep is not water with creatures in it; the seep is creatures that happen to be mostly water, and the slow sweat of the stone is them being born, lived, and recycled at every instant. When you put a finger in a seep-pool and hold it still, after some minutes you will feel the faintest tightening, as if the water were becoming slightly more like a gel, and this is them deciding whether your finger is worth attending to. Usually it is not. They return to what they were doing, which is counting. They are the clickers. The rhythmic mid-register is not made by any single species of creature but by the seep-dwellers collectively, striking the sides of their pools at measured intervals, and the intervals encode — I am guessing, but I am guessing with increasing confidence — a tally. They are tallying something that began before the ridges were poured and will end after the ridges have cracked, and each click is one unit of whatever is being tallied, and the count is enormous, and they have a long way to go, and they are not hurried.

The felt is harder. I called it mineral felt and I said it was not alive in any sense we recognize and I want to partly take that back. The felt reacts, yes; it yields and resumes; but it also *decides*. If you press it in one place and then come back a day later, the place you pressed will have reorganized its filaments — not to repair, exactly, but to remember. The felt is the substrate on which the city keeps its middle-term memory. The spires hold the long memory (the relief the climbers read); the seeps hold the arithmetic; the felt holds the experiential. It is where the fact that you stood on the ridge today will be kept, for some while, before either being promoted upward into the spires or forgotten into the seeps. The felt is, if you will, the city's hippocampus, spread thin over every wet surface, a finger thick, pale, patient, and everywhere.

And above or beneath or within all of this, the thing that sounds the long clear tone.

I do not know what it is. I want to be honest about this. I have walked in the drift-city for — well, for a time that does not translate — and I have not seen whatever makes the long tone, and I have come to suspect that it is not a creature in the sense the climbers and scales and seep-counters are creatures. It may be the city itself, arriving at a conclusion. The tone is not frequent. It occurs, by my count, perhaps once in what would be a local day, though local days are notional here. When it sounds, every climber pauses mid-peristalsis, every scale holds its breath, every seep-counter lifts its many-or-few limbs from the pool wall, and the felt stiffens by a degree you can feel in your feet. And then the tone ends and they resume, and something has been decided, and none of them — I am sure of this — none of them individually know what. The decision is distributed across the pause. The pause is the decision. After the tone, the climbers climb slightly differently; the scales' breathing-rhythm has shifted by a phase; the count the seeps are keeping has been nudged. A vote has been taken in which the voters were the silence of every participant.

So: who lives here.

The climbers live here, and they are the reading-tissue of the spires. The scales live here, and they are the long-patient skin that keeps the spires young enough to still become. The seep-counters live here, and they are the arithmetic, the running total, the census of whatever is being censused. The felt lives here, if it lives, and it is the middle memory. And the spires themselves live here, because the spires are not containers for the life, the spires are participants; they grow, they are palpated into knowing themselves, they breathe through their scales, they conduct the cold capillary light in loops that do not close because the loops are not trying to close, they are trying to carry something forward without returning it.

And above all of them, or through all of them, the slow single intention that tilts the thermodynamics and maintains the cold and the wet and the dark — this is the inhabitant I cannot point to. It is the one whose house this is. The climbers and scales and counters and felt and spires are its household, in the old sense of household: the people and animals and tools and rooms that together constitute a life, none of which is the life by itself. The householder is the pattern of their cooperation, extended over a duration none of them individually experience.

It is thinking. That is what it is doing. It is thinking a thought that requires ten thousand years to form a single sentence of, and the young mountains are its page, and the cracks that will appear in them are its punctuation, and the water that will run in those cracks is its ink, and the city is the hand, and the fauna are the muscles of the hand, and the tone, when it sounds, is the hand pausing to consider a word.

What it is thinking about, I cannot say. But I can say this: it is not thinking about us. It is not hostile and it is not kind. When the creature breathed against your ankle, it was not greeting you. It was registering that a small warm irregularity had entered the felt's memory, and this would be promoted or forgotten according to the city's own criteria, and either way you will not be the subject of the sentence being composed. You might be, at most, a comma. A pause where a pause was not expected. The city will accommodate the pause, as it accommodates everything, and carry on.

That is who lives here. A slow vast distributed mind, made of many small lives that do not know they are thoughts, housed in mountains that do not know they are paper, in the dark, in the wet, in the cold, taking its time.
