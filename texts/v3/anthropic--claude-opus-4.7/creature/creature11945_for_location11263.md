# What Lives There

## The Breathers

Begin with the trees, because everything else is a guest in their lungs.

They are not quite plants. Or — they are plants the way a whale is a fish, which is to say: from the outside, to a casual taxonomist, yes. They photosynthesize. They have bark and leaves and roots. But they breathe in coordinated cycles across a continent, and no plant does that. Somewhere underground, in the substrate the river runs through, their roots are touching. Not merely interlaced — *fused*. The plateau is, in one sense, a single organism with several thousand crowns, and the crowns are what we mistake for individual trees.

What this organism wants, if it wants anything, is difficult to say. It has been alive long enough that its wanting may not resemble ours. It tolerates the fauna because the fauna fertilizes it and disperses it and aerates its hollows. It tolerates the people because the people are small and warm and paint inside it, and perhaps it likes being painted, the way a sleeping dog likes being scratched. The pitcher-flowers are not accidents; they are offered. The hollows are not erosion; they are made. The trees grow houses for whoever is small enough to live in them, the way a body grows a gut for its bacteria.

A tree that stops breathing is not dying. It is *withdrawing* — pulling its consciousness, if that is the word, down through the roots and into the common substrate, leaving the crown to fall because the crown is no longer needed. The people are right to flee such hollows. What falls is empty. What was inside has gone elsewhere, and the elsewhere is under everyone's feet, all the time.

## The People

They call themselves something that means, roughly, *the ones who climb during the exhalation*. There are perhaps four hundred of them on the entire plateau. They do not consider this few. They consider it correct.

They are tall and long-limbed, because the plateau selects for reach; short people cannot get to the high fruits or the high nests, and the high nests are where the eggs are, and the eggs are most of the protein. Their feet are wide and their toes long and prehensile enough to grip bark-grooves while their hands are busy. Their skin is the deep brown of wet wood, and they oil it with rendered fat from the soft-bodied grubs that live in the hollows, which keeps the bark from scraping them raw. They wear almost nothing — a woven belt, a net, a quiver, sometimes a single long wrap of bark-cloth for sleeping. Ornament is rare and meaningful. A bead of the dark glass at the throat means a person has killed one of the great hornbills, which is dangerous and ceremonial work and not done often.

They are organized in a way that does not map cleanly to families or clans. A hollow is inherited not by blood but by *fit* — by who the tree seems to want, which is determined by spending nights in it and seeing whether one dreams well. A tree that dislikes its tenant gives bad dreams; the tenant moves on. A tree that loves its tenant gives the long slow dreams of the substrate, dreams in which one is very large and very old and full of running water. Such tenants stay for life and are buried in the hollow when they die, and the tree grows around their bones, and after a few generations the hollow is partly made of person.

They have a language with about nine thousand words, of which perhaps two thousand are for sounds. They distinguish the call of a hornbill at one kilometer from the call of the same hornbill at one and a half, because the difference matters when you are deciding whether to move. They have separate words for the in-breath wind and the out-breath wind and the brief still moment between them, and they consider the still moment sacred and do not speak during it if they can help it.

They paint because the trees like to be painted. The figures on the inner walls are not always of animals one can recognize because not all the animals on the plateau are *visible* animals. Some of the figures are of the breath itself — long sinuous shapes that loop through the composition. Some are of the substrate-dreams: enormous slow things with too many limbs, wading through dark water. Some are of the pale things, and these are always painted in white earth alone, no red, and always at the edge of the composition, never the center.

They do not have a word for *alone*. They have a word for *between trees*, which means something like *unattended*, *available*, *crossable* — the state of being in the highway between the columns, where anything might happen and one is briefly nobody's responsibility. To be *between trees* for too long is dangerous, and travelers are met at the next hollow with a quiet ritual that re-attaches them to the plateau's notice.

## The Loud Things

The hornbills are not just hornbills. They have been on the plateau long enough that they have specialized into roles a forest bird should not have. There are the *callers*, the dog-sized ones with the bone helmets, who maintain the kilometer-spanning duets and seem to be mapping the plateau acoustically, every hour, forever — a sonar of voices, so that any tree that falls or any new presence that arrives is registered within minutes by a network of beaks. The people listen to the hornbill-calls the way a sailor listens to weather. A change in the pattern means something has changed in the world.

There are the *gleaners*, smaller and quieter, who eat the parasites off the great trunks and are tolerated by everything because everything benefits from them.

There are the *thieves*, iridescent and clever, who steal anything shiny and cache it in disused hollows, and the people sometimes raid these caches and find astonishing things — beads of glass not made by any people anyone knows, scraps of metal the color of old blood, once a small carved figure of a woman with closed eyes that no one would touch and that was left where it lay.

The cicadas keep time. There is a theory among the people, half-joking, that the cicadas are the trees' way of counting, because the cicada-rhythm shifts almost imperceptibly with the breathing cycle, and if you listen for a long time — months, years — you can hear that the cicadas are *tuned* to the inhalation, and that they are doing some kind of work with their sound that nobody understands.

The long-limbed mammals in the mid-canopy are something like lemurs and something like nothing else. They are intelligent in the way crows are intelligent. They are afraid of the people and respectful of the trees and openly hostile to the pale things, which is one of the few useful pieces of information anyone has about the pale things. When a troop of them goes suddenly silent and clusters on the underside of a high branch, looking down, something is wrong below, and the people learn to look up at the lemurs to learn what is happening at their own feet.

## The Pale Things

I will tell you what can be said.

They are taller than the people, by perhaps a head. Their proportions are wrong in a way that is hard to describe; the limbs are slightly too long, the torso slightly too short, the head set forward rather than up. They do not have hair. Their skin is the color of the inside of a soft fruit, and it has a faint translucency, so that in certain lights one can almost see the suggestion of structure beneath — not bones, exactly. Not organs. Something more like the grain in wood.

They move slowly and they move during the inhalation, which is the opposite of the people. When the plateau breathes in and the people pause and listen, the pale things walk. When the plateau breathes out and the people climb, the pale things stand still. This rhythm is the deepest reason the two have not, in living memory, met directly: they occupy different beats of the same measure.

They are not animals. The people know this, though they will not explain how they know. The lemurs know this too. The hornbills do not call when a pale thing passes beneath; they fall silent, which is the only thing that ever silences a hornbill, and which is why a sudden quiet in the upper canopy is the most frightening thing on the plateau.

The best guess — and it is only a guess, made by no one with authority — is that the pale things are what the trees grew before they grew the hollows for people. An earlier tenancy. A first attempt at a small warm thing to live inside the lungs and dream the substrate-dreams and paint the walls. Perhaps the pale things were too slow, or too strange, or insufficient in some way the trees needed; perhaps the people are the second draft. Perhaps the pale things are still loved, in a distant way, the way one might still feed a previous dog who has gone feral but comes around the edges of the yard.

Or perhaps the pale things are something the trees dream, and the dreaming is so old and so strong that the dreams walk. The people's word for them, when they must use one, translates as *the ones we are instead of*.

They do not approach. They watch. Once in a generation, a person goes *between trees* for too long and is found later, sitting calmly in a glade, no longer quite themselves — quieter, slower, breathing on the inhalation rather than the exhalation, eating less, eventually walking off into the long aisles and not coming back. The people do not consider this a death. They consider it a transfer. The pale things, perhaps, gain one. The plateau is in balance.

## The Substrate

Under the moss, in the dark, in the river that runs without surface, things live that nobody has properly seen. Blind fish with long whiskers. Pale crustaceans the size of a hand. And, in the deepest pools where the roots converge — something larger. The people do not fish those pools. The lemurs do not drink from them. The hornbills do not fly over them, which is how the people know where they are: by the holes in the acoustic map, the silent zones in the constant calling.

What lives in the deep pools may be the trees' own root-mind, given a body to swim in. It may be a remnant of the sea that was here when this plateau was a seabed, two kilometers ago and ten million years. It may be nothing at all, and the silences may simply be where the hornbills do not need to call because nothing ever changes there.

The people sing about the deep pools, but they sing in the voice one uses for a sleeping parent: low, fond, careful not to wake.

## What Stays

Everything that has come to the plateau and stayed has done so by finding its beat. The trees breathe on forty seconds. The cicadas tune to the breath. The hornbills call across the spaces between. The people climb on the exhalation, and the pale things walk on the inhalation, and the lemurs watch from above, and the river runs in the dark, and the deep things turn over slowly in their pools, and the painted figures on the inner walls of the hollows watch all of it with red and white earth eyes.

A stranger who comes here either finds a beat or does not. Those who find one are absorbed — given a hollow, eventually, by some tree that decides it likes them, and after enough seasons the stranger is no longer a stranger but a tenant, and after enough generations the tenant's bones are part of a wall, and the wall is painted, and the painting is of something the tenant once saw in a dream they had on their first night in the hollow, when the tree was deciding.

Those who do not find a beat leave, or are taken *between trees*, or simply walk out one morning during an inhalation and are not seen again.

The plateau has room. It has had room for a very long time. It will have room for a very long time more. And whoever lives here lives, finally, inside a single slow breath that has been going on since before anyone was counting, and will go on after.
