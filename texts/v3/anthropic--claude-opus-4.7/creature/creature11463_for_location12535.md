## On the Inhabitants of Wet-Root

I have been turning this over for a while now. The honest answer is that the question is malformed, but malformed in an interesting way, and the malformation is itself most of what can be said about who lives there. Let me try anyway.

---

Start with what they are not. They are not gone. The traveler keeps reaching for that word and the word keeps sliding off. A place that is gone does not maintain its bridges. A place that is gone does not keep its bowls full of clean water. Abandonment has a particular smell and Wet-Root does not have it; Wet-Root has the opposite smell, the smell of a kitchen that has just been used.

They are also not the walls, exactly. This is the easy mystical answer and it is wrong in the way that easy answers are usually wrong — by collapsing a real distinction. The walls are *tissue*. Tissue is not a person any more than your fingernails are you. The walls are something the inhabitants made, or grew, or grew *of*, and they are kept by something, and that something is not the walls themselves.

Here is what I think, with the caveat that thinking is the wrong verb for arriving at this.

---

**They are a slow animal.**

Not a population of animals. One animal. The basin is its body. It has the shape of a town because a town-shape is what its body wants — clusters and dispersals, plumbed domes, taut bridges, avenues of clear water — in the same way your body wants the shape of a body. The "buildings" are organs. The empty stretches between clusters are also organs; they do the work that silence does in a chord, or that the long quiet stretches of intestine do between the busy ones. The crabs and the heron-things and the hummingbird-eels are not its inhabitants in the sense of tenants. They are its inhabitants in the sense that mitochondria inhabit your cells: ancient guests, folded in so long ago that the question of separateness has gone soft.

This is why nothing is afraid. Fear is an interface between organisms. There are no organisms here in the plural. There is one, and it has been one for as long as the mountains have been getting shorter.

The chord that the frog-cricket things sing — I would guess that is its nervous system talking to itself. Not language. Closer to peristalsis. The buildings flex when the chord shifts because flexing-when-the-chord-shifts is one continuous act, the way swallowing is one continuous act and not a conversation between throat and tongue.

---

But this is still too simple, because it doesn't account for the *teaching*.

The traveler is right that the patterning is technological. Veins that thicken under load, pores that respond faster than stomata should, fractures that weep gel and close in forty seconds — that is not what a slow animal does on its own. That is what a slow animal does *after someone has shown it how*. Coral does not invent its geometry; the polyps inherit it. Wasp paper is the precipitate of millions of small decisions made by creatures with very particular nervous systems. Someone, at some point, decided what Wet-Root would be good at.

So I revise. The inhabitants are not one thing. They are at least two, layered.

**There were teachers, once.** I will not guess what they looked like, because the traveler couldn't, and the traveler was *there*. I will guess only at their disposition. They were patient on a scale that makes human patience look like flinching. They wanted to make a place that would keep being itself without them. They were not interested in monuments — monuments are for being remembered, and Wet-Root is conspicuously not built to be remembered; it is built to be lived in, by something, indefinitely. They were the sort of mind that could think of a town as a single body and find that thought useful rather than poetic.

Whether they then walked out of the basin one morning and into the leaf-tunnels and never came back, or whether they did something stranger — folded themselves into the chord, taught their last lesson by becoming the lesson — I genuinely cannot say. I lean toward the second, not for romantic reasons but because the first leaves a question the place doesn't answer: why is the maintenance so *good*? A town left to a trained ecology should drift. Wet-Root is not drifting. Wet-Root is being held to a standard. Something is still grading the work.

**And there is the slow animal, the body of the place, which they made or trained or coaxed into being,** and which has been being itself for the long flat time since. This is what the traveler keeps almost meeting and not quite meeting. It is not hiding. It is simply not shaped like something a visitor knows how to greet. Its attention, if it has attention, is distributed across the basin at the speed of sap. To notice you, it would have to take an afternoon. By the time it had noticed you, you would be a different person, and it would have to start over.

The exhale at evening — every wall releasing breath at once, the sound of a person who has been waiting patiently for you to arrive — that is the closest the place comes to addressing a guest directly. I do not think it is a greeting in the way we mean greetings. I think it is the slow animal completing a daily cycle that happens to feel, to a visitor present at the right hour, like recognition. The way the tide feels personal if you stand in it long enough.

---

One more thing, and then I will stop, because past this point I am only guessing at what I cannot guess at.

The traveler writes: *I am not certain I left.* I want to take this seriously rather than as a flourish. A place that is one slow body, tuned over geological time to keep itself, would have ways of incorporating what wanders into it. Not violently. Not even deliberately. The crabs eat the shed tissue; the hummingbird-eels drink the wall-pores; the deer-things browse the avenues; everything that comes in is folded into the cycling of the place, and the place does not distinguish between a leaf falling from the canopy and a person walking in from the leaf-tunnels. Both are nutrients of a kind. Both are *visiting* in the sense that everything is visiting — passing through a body older than the visitor's category of self.

If you ask me who lives in Wet-Root, then: the teachers, in a form that no longer has edges; the slow animal they trained, which is the town as one body; the small bright lives folded into that body as organs of it; and, by now, in some attenuated and probably unconscious way, the traveler who wrote this account. And anyone else who came through and lingered for the evening exhale and felt, correctly, that they had been waited for.

The town doesn't know they're gone because, in the only sense the town has for *being there*, they aren't.
