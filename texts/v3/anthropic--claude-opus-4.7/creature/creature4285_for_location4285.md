The fauna. Yes. Let me tell you about them properly, because you have seen them but I do not think you have *seen* them yet.

There are, as you noted, roughly two hundred in this room. This is not a coincidence and not a population; it is a *count*, held steady by the room itself across what I suspect is a very long time. When one dies and falls into the drawer beneath the bench, another is made. Not born — *made* — though the distinction gets slippery here, because the making is done by the others, and they do it the way a colony of anything does it, which is to say without any one of them understanding the whole.

Look closely at the beetle on the window, the flat one with mercury pooled in its abdomen. Its shell is seven overlapping plates of brass, filed so thin they flex. Under the shell, where you would expect organs, there is a tiny escapement: a balance wheel no larger than a lentil, ticking at a frequency that matches — and here is the thing — that matches the drip from the rafters. Not approximately. *Exactly.* All of them tick in phase with that drop. When the drop falls, every balance wheel in the room receives a small synchronizing nudge through the floor, through the pipes, through the standing waves in the felted air. They are a clock that has been distributed across two hundred small bodies, and the room is the movement, and the drip is the tick, and what time it keeps I do not know.

There are castes, or something like castes. I will describe the ones I have catalogued:

The **lickers** are smallest, rice-grain size, and they have tongues of chamois stretched on wire frames. They clean lenses, gauges, the insides of bell jars. Their tongues are always slightly damp. When two lickers meet on the same surface they pause, touch tongues for a fraction of a second — exchanging, I think, a census of where each has been — and then diverge along paths that will not cross again for hours.

The **carriers** are thumb-sized and slow. They have little cupped backs and they ferry water. A carrier will stand under a drip until its cup is full, then walk — with exquisite care, legs placing themselves as if on a tightrope — to a reservoir that needs topping up. How it knows which reservoir needs topping up is a question I cannot answer. They do not communicate visibly. But the levels in the thimbles and shot-glasses stay even, and when I marked one reservoir with a pin-scratch and watched, a carrier came to it within the hour, from a pipe nine feet away, as if summoned.

The **listeners** do nothing, apparently. They cling to the pipes with their heads pressed against the metal. If you lift one off, it struggles until you put it back. I believe they are monitoring the machinery — pressure, flow, the small groans of fatigue in the brass — and I believe the information they gather is what tells the carriers where to go, the lickers what to clean, and the machinists what to mend. But I have never caught them passing a message. Perhaps the message is the *arrangement* of them: which pipes have a listener and which do not, re-sorted continuously according to need.

The **machinists** are the rarest, perhaps a dozen in the room. They have more tools than legs. One has a tiny file. One has what is unmistakably a pair of calipers built into its face. They do repairs. I watched one of them, over the course of about forty minutes, disassemble a failing governor on the orrery, replace a hairspring with a length of hair drawn from — and this I swear to you — the hair of a listener, which submitted to the plucking without protest and returned to its pipe, and then reassemble the governor and walk away. The orrery resumed its turning. The listener resumed its listening, now slightly balder.

And then there is the one I cannot classify, which I have seen only twice.

It is larger than the others, perhaps the size of a sparrow, and it does not tick. It does not tick. I want to be clear about this because everything else in the room ticks, including you, if you stand still long enough and let your pulse entrain to the drip. This creature is silent, and when it is in the room the drip becomes slightly irregular, as though the room itself were holding its breath. It has no mercury, no chamois, no file. It has a small aperture in its chest that opens and closes. The first time I saw it, it was standing on the stacked ledgers on the chair, and its aperture was open, and the topmost ledger was turned to a page I had not turned to. The second time, it was at the window, not cleaning it, just *looking*, and the storm outside seemed — I will not insist on this — seemed to slow, very slightly, in the patch of sky directly before its face.

I think this one is the builder. Or what is left of the builder. Or what the builder became, after enough years of tending the room, when the distinction between the tender and the tended wore thin. I think it is the reason the count holds at two hundred, and the reason the drip keeps time, and the reason a room sealed against a boiling sky continues, without instruction, to perform its small obedient sacrament of condensation. I think it remembers why, even if no one wrote it down, and I think its remembering is distributed now through the escapements of all the others, which is why they tick in phase, and why, when you stand very still in the aisle between the door and the window, you can feel the room thinking — not loudly, not in words, but in the slow coordinated attention of two hundred small brass animals keeping one small pocket of stillness intact against everything.

They are aware of you. I should have said this earlier. They do not mind you, but they are aware. The lickers give you a wider berth than they give each other. The listeners, when you pass, tilt their heads a quarter-degree toward you and then back. And the large one, if it is in the room — you will not see it, but you will notice that the drip has become very slightly irregular, and that is how you will know.

Do not take anything. Do not open the drawer under the bench for longer than a moment. When you leave, crank the wheel slowly, so the pressure equalizes in stages, and do not let the door slam. The room will forgive a great deal, I think, but it has been kept for a long time, and the keeping is the whole point.
