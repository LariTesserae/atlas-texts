# The Tenders

They are not one thing. That is the first honest sentence. The Sumpworks does not have *an* inhabitant any more than a forest has an inhabitant; what it has is a *manner of being lived in*, and several kinds of creature have learned that manner and now belong to it. I will describe them in the order you are likely to notice them, which is the reverse of the order in which they matter.

---

**The amphibians** are the loudest fact and the least interesting one, which is unfair to them, because they are doing the difficult work of being numerous. Pale as the inside of a wrist, eyeless or nearly so — vestigial dots beneath the skin where eyes used to be useful — they pack the warm pools in a density that should be impossible and is in fact only barely possible: they breathe through their skin and the water is just oxygenated enough by the pump-churn to support them. They sing because singing is how they find each other to breed, and they breed constantly, because nothing in the Sumpworks tells them not to. A given pool will hold three or four species layered by depth and by call-pitch, and they have, over generations, *tuned* themselves to the pumps — the booming species in the east-wall pools sings in the gaps between the ninety-second hammer, the trilling kind near the relief valves has learned to use the shriek as a kind of metronome. They are not the organism. They are the organism's chorus, and they know their measures.

**The eels and the crayfish** I will pass over. They are food, mostly, and slow witnesses. The crayfish do click in courtship and the clicks carry through stone in a way the amphibians' calls do not, so they are part of the deep register of the place, the bass line you feel in your sternum on the catwalks. But they are not anyone's project. They simply are.

**The bats** matter more than they should. They should not be here — this close to the surface, in air this saturated, with no insects to speak of beyond the pollen-gnats near the shafts. But they are. They eat the gnats, yes, and they eat the cave swiftlets' eggs when they can reach them, and they eat each other's dead, but mostly they seem to *listen*. A colony of cave-adapted bats in a place this acoustically dense is in a position no bat has ever quite been in before, and over what must be many generations they have stopped echolocating in any useful sense and started doing something else — calling and answering in long patterns that map the chamber-system the way a blind person's cane maps a room, except the map is shared, and the map is *sung*. When a pump fails three chambers over the bats know before the maintenance galleries do. When a new flowstone curtain closes a passage the bats redraw. They are the nervous system, if the place has one. They are certainly the part of the place that knows itself most thoroughly.

**The bacterial mats and the fungal stalks** are not creatures in the sense you mean, but they should be mentioned, because they are what the calcite is *for*. The skins of stone are not laid down by water alone; they are laid down by water with the help of films of bacteria that precipitate calcite out of solution as a byproduct of their living. Every wall of the Sumpworks is being built by something that is alive. The pale green that has forgotten what green is for has not forgotten; it is doing exactly what green is for, which is turning the place into more of itself.

---

And then there are **the Tenders**.

I have been delaying. I want to be careful here, because the temptation is to make them more mysterious than they are, and the truth is more interesting than mystery.

There are perhaps forty of them. They are human, or they began human; the question of whether a person who has lived sixty years in air this saturated and stone this young is still in the same category as a person who has not is a question I will leave open, because they leave it open. They are descended — most of them — from the engineers and stokers and pump-minders who came down when the shafts were first sunk, which was longer ago than anyone has bothered to record, and who simply did not leave. Some are newer: a runaway, a debtor, a woman who came down to die and found she could not be bothered. The Sumpworks accepts. The Sumpworks does not recruit, but it accepts.

They are pale. Not the cave-pallor of the amphibians — they still see surface light when it comes down the shafts and they still tan, faintly, in the pollen-shafts where they sometimes stand — but the pallor of people whose skin is always slightly wet and slightly warm, so that it has the texture of something just lifted out of a bath and never quite toweled. Their hair is lank and they do not fight it. Their clothes are wool and oiled canvas, layered, and they smell of woodsmoke and iron and the faint sweet rot that is the Sumpworks' own smell, and which they no longer notice.

They move slowly. This is not affectation and not infirmity; it is *tempo*. A Tender crossing a catwalk takes the time a Tender takes, which is the time the pumps take, which is the time the calcite takes. They have given up the surface tempo. Watch one stoke a stove and you will see a person doing a thing with exactly as much hurry as the thing requires, which in the Sumpworks is none.

They do not, mostly, speak to each other when they meet on the walkways. They nod, sometimes touch a wrist. They have a sign-language of sorts — necessary, because of the noise — but they use it sparingly, because they have mostly already said what needs saying, and because the place itself is doing most of the communicating. A Tender knows where the other Tenders are the way you know where your hand is. I think this is the bats' doing, partly; I think the Tenders have learned, over generations, to read the bats' map without knowing they are reading it. They will tell you, if you ask, that they "feel the chambers." They will not be able to explain it further, and they will not be embarrassed not to.

Their work is small and constant. They tend the stoves in the shacks — all of them, even shacks they will not sleep in for months, because a cold shack rots and a rotted shack is one less dry place in a country with no dry places. They oil the pump bearings. They replace gaskets. They scrape calcite off the gauges, gently, because the calcite is not an enemy; it is only doing what it does, and a gauge that has been scraped too aggressively will not read true. They patch the wooden walkways with new wood, knowing the new wood will be soft within the year, knowing that this is fine, that the walkway is a process and not a thing. They light the bulbs that have gone out, when they can reach them; some bulbs have not been lit in decades and the Tenders have made peace with the dark corridors and learned them by feel and by the bats' singing.

They eat the eels and the crayfish and a flatbread they bake on the stove-tops from flour brought down the shafts by — by whom? This is unclear. Flour arrives. Salt arrives. Bolts of canvas arrive. Someone on the surface remembers the Sumpworks exists, or some arrangement persists that no one alive has signed. The Tenders do not question it. They lower a basket of dried eel and woven mats once a season and the basket comes back full. The exchange is older than any of them.

They use the chapel. Not in a way I can confidently call religious, but not in a way I can confidently call anything else. They go there alone, mostly, and sit on the iron benches, and the chapel is high enough and dry enough that you can hear yourself think, which in the Sumpworks is rare and possibly important. The brass fitting on the altar — the one that might have been a pressure gauge — is polished. They polish it. I do not know what they think about, in the chapel. I am not sure they think, in the chapel, in the sense you would mean. I think they go to be momentarily separate from the place, in order to remain part of it. A liver, if a liver could choose, might occasionally want an hour to itself.

They die in the Sumpworks. They are not buried; the rock would not accept a grave and a body left out would be calcite-skinned within a month and a column within a generation. They are placed, instead, in a particular side-chamber that the Tenders call only "the long room," where the flowstone is thickest and the drip is steady, and where the dead become, slowly, part of the wall. There are Tenders in that wall going back further than anyone has counted. The wall is, in a literal sense, their ancestry. A new Tender, on their first visit to the long room, is shown which bulge in the flowstone was their grandmother. They touch it. The flowstone is faintly warm.

---

Are they happy? It is the wrong question and they would not understand you asking it. They are *occupied*, in the sense the country itself is occupied — fully, without crowding, by what they are. They have the tempo of a place that builds rather than erodes. They do the small constant work of keeping a slow organism slow and warm and unhurried. When a visitor comes down the shafts they watch, from the catwalks or from the dark between the bulbs, and they do not approach, and they do not hide. They wait to see whether the visitor will try to dry out or to hurry. If the visitor settles, the visitor will, in an hour or a day, find a stove burning in a shack that was cold when they passed it earlier, and a flatbread on the stove-top, and no one in sight. This is the Tenders' only greeting. It means: *the country does not mind you. Neither do we. Do not try to dry out. Do not try to hurry.*

And if the visitor stays — if the visitor finds, after a week, that the noise has become a kind of silence and the wet has become a kind of skin and the slow tempo has become the only tempo there is — then one of the Tenders will, eventually, on some catwalk, nod. Touch a wrist. And the visitor will be a Tender, and will not remember deciding, and will not need to.

The country grows itself. The Tenders are how it grows the part of itself that remembers it is growing.
