# What Lives on the Brasswing Escarpment

## The Wading Things

Call them stilts, for lack. They stand one and a half meters at the shoulder-joint, though "shoulder" misrepresents the architecture — their legs attach high on a barrel-shaped body, and the body itself is slung between the legs like a hammock, so that when they walk the body sways and the four eyes (arranged in a diamond on what serves as a head) compensate in four directions at once. The throat-sac iridescence is not decoration. It is temperature regulation; they dump excess heat through the sac membrane into the evening air, which is why they are most active at dusk, when the escarpment exhales and they exhale with it.

They spear things, yes, but what they spear is not always alive. They spear the surface of the water itself, in patterns. A naturalist who watched one stilt for six hours recorded that its strikes into empty water described, over the afternoon, a slowly rotating figure that resembled the shadow-offset of the standing ring. She did not publish this. She was not sure what she had seen.

The stilts lay eggs in the memory-moss. The moss remembers the eggs after the eggs have hatched and gone; a stilt nesting-ground shows as darker green for a season, and the stilts return to the dark patches the following year, and the patches stay dark longer because of it. No one is sure whether the moss is helping the stilts or using them.

## The Flyers

The lacquered-seed bodies are real lacquer, or something chemically indistinguishable — a hard resinous shell that the flyer grows by secreting and then polishing against the brasswing leaves. A young flyer is dull. An old flyer gleams. The membrane wings are grown separately and molted twice a year; the discarded wings drift down into the catchment pools and dissolve into a thin oil that the stilts drink.

They are gregarious and seem, in their own way, musical. A flock moving through a brasswing grove does not merely make the leaves chime harder — the flock *chooses* which leaves to strike, and the resulting chord is not random. Recordings, slowed down and analyzed, reveal that a flock of forty flyers passing through a mature grove will produce, over about ninety seconds, a sequence of intervals that repeats with small variations from grove to grove and from season to season. It is not a song a human could hum. It is not quite a language. It is something the flyers are doing *with* the trees, and the trees, having hollow trunks and metallic leaves, appear to be willing participants.

The flyers nest in the ceramic tower. No one has seen them enter it. No one has found an opening. But at dawn, flyers emerge from somewhere near the tower's upper third, and at dusk they return, and the tower is where they go.

## The Shapes in the Water

Here the account thins, because the evidence thins.

They are large — larger than the stilts, certainly; the displacement of water when one passes under the surface suggests something the size of a small boat. They are slow. They do not surface. They are not hunted by anything and they do not appear to hunt. The stilts walk among them without alarm and sometimes stand on them, using a broad back as a fishing platform, and the shapes tolerate this or do not notice.

What can be said: they are warm. Thermal imaging of the deeper pools shows long, slow plumes of heat that are neither the pool's own nor the basalt's. They move on paths. The paths, traced over months, describe circuits inside the pool that match the runnels on the surrounding stone — as if the shapes below are walking the same map the water walks above, only in a different medium.

One theory, unpopular because it is unprovable and unsettling, is that the shapes are not animals at all but components. That they are part of the slow machine, and that what the stilts are doing when they stand on a shape's back is the same thing a bird does when it stands on a cow: taking advantage of a large patient thing that is busy with its own unrelated work.

## The Ferns and the Brasswings

These are not, strictly, fauna, but they behave enough like persons that they deserve a paragraph.

The cathedral ferns unfurl toward whatever is warmest. In a grove with no humans, they orient toward the catchment pools and the steam vents. In a grove with a human sitting still, they will, over about two hours, rotate their fronds toward the human. It is not predation. They do not close, do not sting, do not feed. They simply attend. A person who sleeps in a fern grove wakes surrounded by a blue-black parabolic dish of fronds all pointed at them, and the air inside the dish is two degrees warmer than the air outside, and the person has dreamed vividly, usually of water.

The brasswings are older than the ferns and less interested in visitors. Their forty-one-year bloom produces a single flower per tree, high in the crown, metallic like the leaves but softer, and the flower rings at a frequency an octave below the leaves. During bloom-years the whole escarpment sounds different — deeper, fuller, almost orchestral — and the flyers' chord-sequences grow longer and more elaborate, and the stilts' spearing-patterns grow more complex, and the standing ring's shadow-offset, according to the only measurements taken during a bloom, shifts by nearly a full degree for the duration of the flowering and then shifts back.

Whatever the escarpment is doing, it does more of it when the brasswings bloom.

## The Memory-Moss

A carpet is the wrong image; a carpet is passive. The moss is a sheet of slow attention that covers most of the terraces' flatter surfaces, and it is all, as far as anyone can tell, one organism, or one federation of organisms that has agreed to behave as one.

It remembers footprints for a week. It remembers a body that has lain on it for longer — a sleeping shape will show as a darker patch for a month. It remembers the stilts' nesting sites for a year. It remembers the paths of the hexagonal tile road on either side of where the tiles stop, faintly, as a slightly different shade of green running off into the terrace and fading after about fifty meters, as though the road once continued and the moss is still, three centuries later, holding the shape of it.

It is not clear whether the moss is the escarpment's memory or merely a visible instance of a memory the escarpment keeps by other means as well. The moss grows on the basalt. The basalt hums. The humming, recorded and slowed, contains patterns. Some of the patterns, decoded by a researcher who later left the field, correspond to the outlines of footprints.

## The Ones Who Come

And then: the scientists, the pilgrims, the curious, the naturalists who stay an extra month. They are not native to the escarpment but they are, in the long accounting, fauna too — a migratory species that visits for a season and leaves footprints the moss holds for a week.

Some of them adjust their heartbeats. A few of them do not leave. There is a small, quiet community at the base of the eastern terrace, in tents and low shelters, of people who came for a season and found they could not re-enter the tempo of the world below. They do not talk much. They walk the hexagonal road to where it ends and they stand there, and then they walk back. They drink from the catchment pools, which should not be safe but is, for them, after a while. They grow slower. Their skin takes on a faint mineral cast. When they die — and they do die, though slowly — they are buried in the memory-moss, and the moss remembers them as darker patches for what witnesses describe as an unusually long time.

## And Whatever Is Underneath

The slow machine has no face and no name and does not, as far as anyone can determine, notice individual lives. But the stilts' spearing-patterns and the flyers' chord-sequences and the ferns' orientations and the moss's memory and the shapes' circuits and even the pilgrims' slow adjustments are all, each of them, small local expressions of the larger thing's work. They are not its servants. They are its weather. They arose because the machine made a place where they could arise, and they fit the place because the place has been, for centuries, gently selecting for things that fit.

What lives on the Brasswing Escarpment is, in the end, a single long cooperation running at several speeds at once — the flyers fast, the stilts medium, the shapes slow, the brasswings slower, the moss slower still, and the machine beneath them all moving at the pace of the shadow-offset, half a degree in two hundred years.

The naturalist who stayed the extra month wrote, in her last note before she stopped writing:

*I think it is not doing anything to us. I think we are one of the things it is doing.*
