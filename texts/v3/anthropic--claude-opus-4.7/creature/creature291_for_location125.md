# The Inhabitants of the Warmwater Conurbation

They are not one kind of thing. This is the first fact, and the one most often gotten wrong by those who arrive expecting a *people* in the singular. The basin hosts lineages, and the lineages braid.

## The Waistdeep

The majority, by count, are the ones a visitor would first call human and would, by the end of a week, stop calling that. They stand a little taller than you'd expect in the water — long-legged, with a particular fold at the knee and ankle that lets them walk all day in the shallows without tiring. Their feet are broad and slightly webbed between the first three toes; the fourth and fifth are shorter, gripping toes, good for piling and piers. Their skin runs in a range from pale silt-grey through warm bronze to the near-black of the glassy pebbles, and it carries, faintly, the same fine patterning as the tower walls — not tattoo, not quite, but something the skin does on its own, responsive to health and mood and the chemistry of the water they've been standing in. A Waistdeep child's patterning is simple, almost blocky. By adulthood it has become intricate enough that close friends read each other's forearms the way you might read a face.

They do not sweat. They exchange heat with the water directly, through a fine subdermal network that flushes pink when they're exerting and goes nearly white when they're cold, which is rare. They drink less than you would; much of their hydration comes through the skin, and a Waistdeep who has been out of the water for more than a few hours becomes visibly ashen and a little slow, and will seek the nearest canal-step and sit down in it without ceremony, mid-conversation, and resume talking.

Their lungs are ordinary. Their hearing is not — the roar of the city is information to them, and they parse it the way you parse a crowded room, tuning to the bell-pitch of their own district, the pressure-valve rhythm of a familiar tower, the specific slap of water against their own home-pilings. A Waistdeep raised in one quarter and moved to another spends a month half-deaf, reassembling the soundscape, before they can hear a friend calling from two causeways over.

They live, on average, about a hundred and forty years, and they age slowly and then quickly — youthful for a long time, then compressed into a brief, dignified senescence that everyone around them recognizes and makes room for. The dying sit in the deep pools, in the last weeks, and the filaments gather around them, and this is not sad exactly. It is understood.

## The Commensals

Then there are the ones who share the Waistdeep lineage somewhere far back but have diverged. The Commensals are smaller, rarely above chest-height on a Waistdeep, and they live partly in the soft mounds of filament-colony. They can breathe the water for stretches of an hour or more, through a fringe of vascular tissue at the throat that looks, when dry, like a high dark collar. Their eyes are large and their pupils are horizontal slits — they see well in the rose-dark of night and the pearl-bright of day with equal ease, and they squint in neither.

Commensals are the ones who tend the mounds. They know, by touch, which colonies are healthy and which are souring; they reroute the slow pulse of the big mounds when a new causeway disturbs the current; they know the names, in a sense, of individual filament-mats that are older than any building in the quarter. The Waistdeep defer to them on all matters below the surface, and the Commensals defer to the Waistdeep on everything above it, and between the two lineages there is a steady low-grade gossip, a lot of affectionate mockery, and a great deal of shared business.

A Commensal out of water for a full day is in trouble. A Waistdeep fully submerged for a full day is in trouble. They meet, always, at the waterline, and the waterline is where most of the city lives.

## The Quiet Kin

Less visible, less numerous: the ones who came from somewhere else, long enough ago that *somewhere else* is not a current memory but a shape in the grammar of their names. They are taller than the Waistdeep, thinner, and they do not pattern. Their skin is smooth and matte and the color of wet slate, and they wear long wrapped garments that the water does not seem to soak. They speak less. They are the ones most often found in the tower-interiors, tending to the things the walls compute, because the computation is partly theirs — they brought some of it with them, long ago, and grafted it into what was already here, and the grafting is now so complete that no one separates the contributions.

A Quiet One will stop in the middle of a causeway and listen to a tower for ten minutes, unmoving, and then nod once and walk on, and a week later a pressure valve three districts away will be replaced, and the replacement will have been because of that listening. They are not mystics. They are technicians of a tradition so deep it has become indistinguishable from attention.

They live longer than the Waistdeep. No one is sure how much longer. They do not make a point of it.

## The Gate-Passers

The large things that move through the deep channels, for whom the causeway gates open.

They are not pets and they are not gods and the city does not mythologize them, which is itself a mark of how old the arrangement is. They are simply the other large inhabitants of the basin, and they have their routes, and the city has its routes, and the two systems interlock by negotiation that is now pure habit. A Gate-Passer is perhaps forty meters long, soft-bodied, and moves by slow peristalsis through the warm deep water. They filter-feed on the filaments of the deeper mounds, which is why the mounds are healthier than they would otherwise be; the Commensals coordinate the harvest, in a sense, by shaping which mounds are ready.

A Gate-Passer does not speak and is not believed to think in any way a Waistdeep would recognize. But it knows the city. It knows the gates. It knows, somehow, when a gate is stuck and a barge is in the way, and it waits, patient as weather, until the path is clear. Children are taken to watch them pass, once or twice in a childhood, from a safe platform, and what the children see is a long dark shape moving under pearled water, and a slow wake, and nothing more. It is enough. It is part of what they will later mean when they say *home*.

## The Working Small

And then, everywhere, in the mist and the water and the stone and the skin of every inhabitant: the tiny things. Not a lineage in the sense the others are, but a population — descended, probably, from tools released into the basin so long ago that their toolness has become a biology. They repair the towers. They close the cuts. They filter the water that filters through the Waistdeep and the Commensals and the Quiet Kin alike. They are eaten, in vast numbers, by the filaments, and they seed, in vast numbers, from the filaments, and the cycle has stabilized into what is effectively the city's metabolism.

No one commands them. No one remembers commanding them. They are the reason a newcomer's cut closes overnight, and they are also the reason a newcomer, after a year, begins to pattern faintly at the wrists — because the Working Small have started to recognize them, and to write on them, a little, the way the walls are written on. This is how the city adopts you. Not by ceremony. By accumulation.

---

What they all share, across the lineages:

They do business constantly and they rest constantly, and the two are not opposed. A Waistdeep sitting in a canal-step selling pressed cakes is resting. A Commensal hip-deep in a mound, rerouting a current with both hands, is resting. A Quiet One listening to a tower is resting. Rest, here, is not stillness; it is the condition of being correctly placed in the flow, and the flow is the city, and the city is what they are all, together, doing.

They sing at night because the cooling walls need the vibration. They did not always know this. Now it is simply what one does when the light turns rose — one sings, and the walls click in response, and the filaments brighten, and the song is picked up by the next district, and the singing is the city maintaining itself, and also the city enjoying itself, and there is no distinction between the two that anyone bothers to draw.

If you asked one of them, any of them, *what are you*, they would not understand the question in the singular. They would gesture — at the water, at the mist, at the other lineages passing, at the Working Small invisible in the air between you — and they would say something that translates, approximately, as: *we are the part of this that talks*.

And then they would go back to work, which is also rest, which is also the song, which is also the city, which has never really stopped.
