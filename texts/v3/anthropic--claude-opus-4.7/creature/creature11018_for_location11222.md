## The Inhabitants of the Engine Shoal

### The Basal Beasts

Begin at the bottom, with the old ones. They are the size of oxen, of small boats, of upturned bathtubs grown shaggy with their own slow accretions. Call them **anchorworks**, though they have no name for themselves and possibly no name for anything.

An anchorwork's body is roughly barrel-shaped — a great riveted drum of dark iron, dim-glowing along its lower seams where the boiler sits closest to the hull. Brass piping wraps it in slow spirals, rising to a crown of three or four heavy valves that exhale once every few minutes with a sound like a cathedral door settling. Two flywheels, set on either flank like the gills of a fish, turn so slowly that on first glance you would think them still; only by marking a fleck of rust against one spoke and waiting do you see that yes, it moves, it has moved a hand's breadth in the time you held your breath.

Underneath, in place of legs or fins, an anchorwork has a foot — a single broad pad of fused metal and pale ceramic, scalloped and pitted, that grips the ridge by sheer weight and by the slow welding of mineral deposit. They have not moved in years. Some of them, perhaps, have not moved since they finished assembling, decades ago. They are stations more than animals. Smaller engines orbit them, dock at their warm flanks to feed on radiated heat, lay down sediment on their backs that becomes, in turn, the substrate for the next generation.

What does an anchorwork *do*? It runs. That is its whole verb. It maintains its internal fire by drawing dissolved iron and sulfur compounds through intake gills along its lower edge, processing them in the boiler, exhaling spent gases as the steady bubble-columns that mark each great one's position from above. It is a furnace that eats the sea slowly and breathes the sea slowly back. Its pulse is the slowest rhythm in the shoal's polyphony — the thud you feel in your sternum, the bass line under everything.

If an anchorwork has anything like awareness, it is the awareness of a glacier or a slow tide. It registers the shoal around it as a kind of warmth, a pressure, a chemistry. It would not notice you placing your hand on its flank unless you stayed there for a season.

### The Wheelers

The middle layer holds the **wheelers** — dog-sized to calf-sized, the working bulk of the shoal's population. These are the ones a visitor sees most, because they move at speeds a human eye can follow without strain.

A wheeler is built around a single principle: locomotion as livelihood. Most have a paddle-wheel or a screw or a pair of articulated fins driven by a small piston-stack, and they hold station in the midwater with constant gentle correction, the way a fish hovers. Their bodies are more various than the anchorworks'. Some are spherical, ringed with brass bands; some are elongated, almost eel-like, with the boiler running the length of the spine; some have crab-articulations of segmented iron plate. The pale ceramic substance shows up in their joints, their bearings, their pressure-couplings — wherever something has to move against something else without seizing.

They drift in territorial loops, as the description says, and the loops are real territories. Watch long enough and you'll see one wheeler nose gently into another's path and then turn, courteously, away — no contest, no display, just a small adjustment. There are no fights here. There is too much to share and too little hurry. A wheeler will spend the morning circling a particular anchorwork, grazing its exhalation, then drift across the ridge to graze another's. The loops are not random. Each wheeler has a route, and the routes interlock like clock-gears across the whole volume of the shoal.

When two wheelers pass close, sometimes they exchange. A small part — a gasket, a pin, a worn cog — drops from one and is taken up by the other within an hour, integrated into a waiting slot. This is not theft and not gift. It is the shoal's circulation. Parts move through bodies the way molecules move through cells.

A wheeler lives, perhaps, five to ten of our years. Toward the end it slows. Its boiler dims. It begins to drift downward, settling toward the basal layer, and there it is taken apart over weeks by the patient small things, its body distributed across a dozen new growths.

### The Tickers

Above the ridge crest, in the bright water, swarm the **tickers**. Cat-sized down to fist-sized, and the smallest of them only the size of a walnut. There are thousands.

A ticker has a clean, finished quality the larger engines lack. Its brass is polished by constant motion against water. Its glass faces — yes, some have glass faces, like watch crystals — show the dim glow of an interior fire reduced to a single bright pinprick, the smallest sustainable flame in the shoal's chemistry. Most tickers have no paddle or screw; they propel themselves in brief darting bursts by venting a puff of warm exhaust from one of several nozzles set around the body. Between bursts they coast, turning slowly, sampling the water with feathery sensor-tendrils of fine wire.

These are the inspectors. They are the shoal's eyes, if it has eyes. They drift among the larger engines, settling briefly on a flank or a fin, ticking there for a few minutes, then darting away. What they are inspecting, what they are reporting, where the information goes — none of this is legible from outside. But the shoal *coordinates*, in the way an anthill or a coral colony coordinates, and it is the tickers that carry whatever signal does the coordinating.

A ticker is the inhabitant most likely to interact with a visitor. The small one that settles on your shoulder, mistaking you for substrate — that is a ticker, and it will tick contentedly against you for as long as you hold still, its little fire warm through the fabric of your suit, its sensor-wires brushing your neck. When you move, it lifts off without offense and finds another perch.

They are also the shoal's main reproductive surface, in a sense. New engines accrete most easily in the spaces where tickers congregate, because the tickers shed parts constantly — worn gears, broken springs, spent gaskets — and these are the seeds around which new bodies build. A ticker that loses a part replaces it within hours from the surplus drifting in the water around it. A ticker, of all the shoal's inhabitants, lives the briefest and the most replaceably; the swarm is essentially immortal, but any individual ticker may be only weeks old and weeks from dissolution.

### The Slow Crawlers

There is a fourth kind, less obvious, that lives along the surfaces themselves. **Crawlers** — flattened things, plate-shaped, the size of dinner plates or smaller. They creep across the flanks of the anchorworks and along the ridge stone, moving at the speed of a minute hand. They have no boilers visible. They run cold, or nearly so, drawing what little energy they need from the temperature gradient between an anchorwork's hot side and the cold water — they are thermoelectric, perhaps, or something stranger. Their bodies are mostly the pale ceramic, fringed with delicate brass cilia.

A crawler is a grazer of surfaces. It scrapes the rust-bloom from the iron flanks, ingests the oxidized material, processes it, lays it back down as a thin neat film of refined mineral that becomes substrate for new growth. The crawlers are the gardeners of the shoal, or its janitors, or — most accurately — its soil. They are the slow patient layer that prepares the ground on which everything else accretes.

You almost don't see them at first. They blend into the engines they crawl on, and they move so slowly that you mistake them for fixed features. But come back to the same spot in an hour and the plate has moved a hand's breadth, and a clean lighter patch of metal shows where it has been.

### The Single Question

Now: is the shoal one thing or many?

This is the question a visitor cannot resolve, and perhaps the shoal itself cannot resolve. Each engine has its own boiler, its own cycle, its own slow business. Each could in principle be lifted out and examined as an individual. And yet they exchange parts, share exhaust, coordinate territories, and pool the strange slow learning by which the shoal absorbs the shapes of dropped coins and keys. No single engine remembers the coin. The shoal remembers the coin. The memory lives in the distribution of new gear-diameters across a thousand small bodies, none of which individually knows what a coin is.

So they are inhabitants in the way the cells of a body are inhabitants of the body — separable in principle, meaningless in practice. The shoal is a *they* and an *it* at once, and the grammar of either language fails it.

If you asked the shoal what it was, and if it could answer, it would not answer in words. It would answer by continuing: by the slow thud of the anchorworks, the patient orbits of the wheelers, the bright chirring of the tickers, the imperceptible creep of the crawlers across the warm flanks of their elders. It would answer by being more itself tomorrow than today. It would answer by taking your dropped coin and, in a week, holding up — somewhere in its body, in a gear of exactly that diameter spinning in a ticker no bigger than your thumb — the shape of your visit, preserved.

It is alive. It is industrial. It is one organism wearing a thousand bodies, and it lives in a small bright bounded place on the floor of a cold sea, breathing toward a surface it will never reach, learning very slowly what it is allowed to become.
