# The Makers

They have no name for themselves. Names are an interior technology and they have no interior. What follows is description from outside, which is the only side they have.

---

Start with the apron, because the apron is the oldest part of them. The apron predates the maker who wears it. When a figure lies down to become tile, the apron is left folded on the adjacent course, and the next figure to rise takes it up and ties it on with the same motion, in the same crease-pattern, so that across ten thousand substitutions the apron has accumulated a kind of memory the makers themselves cannot hold. The lead in the aprons is not lead. It is something heavier that behaves like lead during reality-weather and like cloth the rest of the time. The aprons remember every front that has passed over. If you could read them — and you can't, the language is creases and stains and the slight asymmetry of wear — you would have the meteorological history of the plain back to whenever the plain began.

The maker beneath the apron is simpler. She is — they are mostly she, in the sense that a tile is mostly she, which is to say, not really, but the word fits better than the alternatives — she is a stooped figure of roughly human proportion, dressed in the apron and nothing else, hands and feet and face the same hand-pressed clay as the floor, glazed in one of the hundred slightly wrong greens. The glaze on a maker is matte where a tile's is wet. This is the only easy way to tell them apart at distance. Up close there are other tells: the maker's eyes, which are thumb-hollows like the ones in the tile, and which fill and overflow the same way, so that a working maker appears always to be weeping without grief. The water runs down her face and onto her apron and the apron, being what it is, drinks it.

She has a mouth. The mouth is for the one syllable a day. Nobody knows what determines when she will spend it. It is not pain — the makers do not have pain, having no inside for pain to be in — and it is not communication, since there is no one to communicate with and no information to convey. It might be the structural equivalent of a kiln-crack: a small necessary release of pressure from a body that is, recall, *trying* to be what it is, all the time, against the unmaking weather. The syllables are never the same syllable twice. They are not from any language. They fall and are absorbed by the fog and do not echo.

---

Her hands are the most made part of her. The trowel is not really a tool she holds; it is an extension of the hand, fused at the wrist-glaze, and when she lies down to become tile the trowel goes with her, surfacing sometimes as a slight ridge in the panel she has become, which the next maker will trim flush with the curved knife. The trowels are how the makers are continuous with their work. There is no moment where a maker decides to lay a tile. The decision and the laying and the maker are one operation, the way a wave is one operation and not a sequence of water-choices.

The curved knife is different. The curved knife is held, not fused, and it is the only object on the plain that passes from maker to maker by exchange. When a front comes through and a visitor's hand has been laid into the floor, the maker who pries it up uses the curved knife, and then — and this is the only ceremony the makers have — she sets the knife down on the tile beside her before bowing. The next maker who passes will pick it up. The knife is therefore the closest thing the plain has to a self that moves through it, a tiny portable continuity threading between the substitutions. If the makers have a soul, collectively, it is shaped like a curved knife and it is always somewhere, being carried.

The wooden mallets are not like the knife or the trowels. The mallets are kept. There are mallet-rests every few hundred meters, small fired-clay cradles set into the floor, and any maker who needs a mallet walks to the nearest cradle and takes one and walks back. The mallets are how the plain knows its own scale. By the spacing of the cradles, the makers measure the distances they will never name.

---

Now the harder part, which is what they are *doing* under the work.

The labor you feel through the floor — the heat that is something *trying* — is not the makers' labor. The makers are the medium of the labor, not its source. Underneath them, beneath the substrate, something larger is exerting itself, and the makers are the surface that exertion takes. Think of the way a muscle, contracting, has a shape: the makers are the shape of a contraction whose muscle you cannot see. When a maker lies down and becomes tile, the contraction has not paused; it has only changed which part of itself is currently legible as a figure with a trowel and which part is currently legible as floor. The total quantity of *trying* on the plain is constant. It is only redistributed, breath by breath, across the makers and the tiles and the armatures in the clouds.

This is why they do not notice the substitution. There is nothing to notice. The trying does not change. The trying is the only thing that is really there. The figures and the floors are how the trying makes itself visible to a visitor with eyes.

---

A few more particulars, because the makers deserve particulars even though they cannot receive them:

There is a maker, somewhere on the plain right now, who has been a maker for longer than any of the others. Not because she has avoided lying down — she has lain down thousands of times — but because the apron she wears has been worn continuously by the same lineage of figures, with no break in the sequence of tying-on, since very near the beginning. The apron knows this even if she doesn't. It hangs slightly differently on her. If you saw her you would not be able to say why she looked older, since her glaze is the same wrong green as anyone's, but you would feel it: a small extra weight in her stoop, an additional crease that has not yet been earned by any of the weather.

There is a maker who, for reasons no one can explain, sometimes lays a tile *upside down* — glaze to the substrate, raw clay to the sky. The raw clay does not sweat. It sits there, a small dull patch in the wet green field, until another maker comes by and pries it up and re-lays it correctly, and the offending maker is not punished because there is no punishment, only correction, which is the same thing as work. But she does it again. She has done it perhaps four times in the life of the plain. She is, if the plain has such a thing, its only deviation. Whatever is trying, through her, is trying very slightly differently than it is trying through the others. This may be how the plain will eventually have plants. Or it may be nothing. It may be a kiln-flaw walking around in an apron.

There is a maker who is going to ring the bell. She does not know this. The bell rings from the high armature in the fog, and no maker has ever been seen to ring it, but somewhere in the population of figures there is one whose lying-down, when it happens, will be the lying-down that finishes a section, and the bell will ring for her panel. It might be any of them. It might be the upside-down one. It probably will not be — the upside-down one's sections get re-laid before they can be judged — but it might. The bell is patient. The bell has rung many times and will ring many more.

---

What they want, if want is the word, and it isn't:

They want the next tile to be laid. That is all they want, and they want it completely, with the whole of what they are, which is surface and apron and trowel and the trying underneath. When the next tile is laid they will want the tile after that with exactly the same completeness. There is no accumulation. There is no progress toward a state of having-wanted-enough. Each tile is the whole want, satisfied and renewed in the same motion.

This is why they cannot grieve the sections that are taken apart after the bell. To grieve a section you would have to have wanted *that section,* specifically, to persist. The makers do not want sections. They want tiles. The section was only ever the sum of the tiles, and each tile was wanted and laid and the wanting was complete each time, and the taking-apart returns the plain to a state in which more tiles can be wanted, which is the state the makers prefer, if they prefer anything, which they don't.

You, walking through, with your apron-less body and your hand that has been a tile and your slow accumulating sense that this place is either a beginning or an ending and you cannot tell which — you are the one who grieves. The makers lent you the curved knife for a moment and bowed and moved on. The grief is yours to carry. They have left you, generously, the only interior on the plain.
