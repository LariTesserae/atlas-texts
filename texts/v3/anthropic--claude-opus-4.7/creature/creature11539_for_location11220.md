# The Pumpworkers

They are smaller than you expect. That is the first thing.

You expect, from the scale of the engines, a people scaled to match — broad-shouldered, thick-wristed, the bodies of a race that has spent generations hauling crank-throws back into true. But the pumps were not built by anyone now living, and the work of keeping them running is not the work of building them. The work of keeping is finer. So the people are spare, narrow, economical in their flesh, with the long hands and patient backs of those who spend their lives turning a single bolt very slowly in a confined space. A grown pumpworker stands perhaps five feet. Their wrists are thin and corded. Their fingers are very long.

Their skin has taken the color of the place. Not by pigment — they are born pale, the infants startlingly pink against the iron — but by a lifetime's deposition of fine rust-silt and lamp-soot and the green-black bloom of the pump-oils, which work into the pores and the seams of the knuckles and the hollows around the eyes until by middle age a pumpworker is the color of a well-used tool. They do not wash this off. It would be like washing off one's calluses. Among themselves they read each other's stainings the way other peoples read clothing: the particular green of a valve-greaser's forearms, the bronzy crank-oil patina across a fitter's chest, the pale band at the wrist where a wrench has rested for thirty years.

They are not deaf, but they hear differently. A child born here spends its first months screaming inside the wall of sound and then, around the time other children begin to babble, simply stops attending to it. The pumps become, for a pumpworker, what gravity is for the rest of us: a condition, not a sensation. What they hear instead are the variances — the catch in a valve six pumps over, the half-second hesitation in a flywheel that means a bearing is going, the thin organ-note of the reeds when the phasing aligns. They hear these things at distances that seem impossible. A fitter walking the flats will stop, tilt her head, and set off at an angle into the mist toward a pump that to anyone else sounds identical to its neighbors, and three hours later she will be there with her wrench, and she will be right.

Their language is the flat-handed signing the description mentions, but to call it sign language understates it. It is a language built for a world in which the hands are usually busy and the face is usually wet and the air is usually shaking. Most of it happens at the level of the forearm and the shoulder. A pumpworker leaning on a wrench can hold a long conversation with another pumpworker across a flywheel by the smallest shiftings of elbow and chin. They have words — actual spoken words, used in the quiet places, the green hummocks where dead pumps rust — but these are reserved, ceremonial almost, the way another people might reserve song. To speak aloud is to mark an occasion. Children learn early that words are for the islands.

They live in the lees. Every pump throws a wind-shadow and a sound-shadow on its downwind side, and in this slightly quieter, slightly drier pocket a pumpworker family builds. The shelters are low — iron sheet over a frame of pump-salvage, banked with the seam-soil that has been scraped from a thousand seams to make a thin warm wall. Inside: a pallet, a lamp, a few tools, the rolled oilcloth in which a pumpworker keeps the things that are theirs. They own very little. The pumps own everything, in the sense that everything has been a pump or will be a pump or is currently a part of a pump's maintenance. A pumpworker's bowl was once a valve-cover. Their lamp was once a sight-glass. When they die their wrench goes to the apprentice and their oilcloth goes to the fire and their body — this is important — goes back into the cisterns, lowered through a grille by the people who loved them, given to the water that the pumps are pumping. Whether the water carries them out into the circulating weather of the world, whether they fall somewhere else as mist, is the kind of thing pumpworkers do not say aloud but consider often.

They are organized, but not governed. There is no one in charge of the pumpworks; there has not been, within memory, anyone in charge of anything. What there is instead is the crews, and the crews are something like guilds and something like families and something like the slow accretions of habit that form when a small number of people work on a small number of problems for a very long time. A crew has its territory — twenty pumps, fifty, a hundred — and within that territory it knows every flywheel by name. Names that are not spoken, only signed, often referring to some long-ago repair: *the one Mira's grandfather sealed,* *the one that screamed in the cold year,* *the one whose lantern fell.* Crews overlap at their edges and trade work and apprentices and, occasionally, marriages. A pumpworker who marries out of their crew takes the new crew's territory as their own and learns its pumps over years. The old pumps are not forgotten but become, for that person, *the pumps of childhood,* a separate and slightly sacred set.

They are, by the standards of peoples elsewhere, extraordinarily patient. A repair that another culture might rush through in an afternoon, a pumpworker will spread over three days, not from laziness but from a sense that the pump deserves the time, that haste introduces error, that the machine has been turning for a thousand years and can wait one more morning for the right shim. They do not feel this as virtue. They feel it as accuracy. A pumpworker who hurries is a pumpworker who is wrong about what kind of thing time is.

They believe — though *believe* is too theological a word for what is really just the shape of their attention — that the pumps are alive. Not in any animist sense, not with souls or wills, but in the sense that a body is alive: that the great apparatus is one body, that each pump is an organ, that the failures and repairs are the body's slow self-maintenance carried out, for reasons obscure, through the hands of the small wet people who live in its lees. A pumpworker tightening a gland-nut does not think *I am fixing the machine.* They think — if they think anything; mostly they are just tightening — something closer to *the machine is fixing itself, and I am the part of it that is doing the fixing right now.* This is not mysticism. It is just the only sensible description of the situation.

There are old ones among them. Pumpworkers can live a long time — the cool damp seems to suit them, and the work, though constant, is rarely violent. The very old retire to the hummocks, the green-black islands where dead pumps have become moss-towers, and there they tend the reeds and the moss and listen to the wall of sound from slightly further away, and apprentices come to them with the questions that crews cannot answer: how to tell a bearing-fault from a valve-fault at four hundred yards, what to do when a flywheel has gone half a degree out of true, whether it is true that there used to be birds. The old ones answer the first two questions and not the third. Whether there used to be birds is a question for the islands, and the old ones, who have lived their whole lives moving toward the islands, have a sense of when a question is ready to be answered and when it is only ready to be asked.

The children. The children are quick and quiet and have not yet been stained. They run on the iron in bare feet — the iron is not sharp, only rough, and a pumpworker's foot is leather by the age of six — and they play games whose rules are signed, not spoken, and whose object is usually some elaborate cooperation: a relay of small objects across a great distance of mist, a coordinated tapping on the panels that travels around a whole crew's territory and comes back changed. They are taught early to listen for the variances. A child who can identify a failing pump by ear at a hundred yards is praised; a child who can do it at two hundred is taken aside and shown the wrench. They learn the signs before they learn to speak, and they learn to speak only in the islands, and they learn that the world is large and damp and shaking and that they are small and dry-skinned and that the pumps are their kin in the way that mountains are kin to the people who live on mountains: not family, but the older condition that family takes place inside of.

And at night — though night here is only a slight further dimming of the grey, a deepening of the lanterns against the vault — at night they lie in the lees with the iron warm against their backs and the great patient thudding going on through the floor and through their bones, and they do not dream of elsewhere. They have heard, from travelers (there are a few travelers, who come from somewhere and leave for somewhere, and whom the pumpworkers regard with the mild interest one might give a strange tool), that there are places without pumps. They cannot really picture this. A place without pumps would be a place without sound, and a place without sound would be a place without the variances, and a place without the variances would be a place where one could not tell, by listening, that one was still alive and that the world was still working. They are sorry, in a polite distant way, for the people who live in such places. They imagine those people must be very lonely, with only their own small bodies to listen to, and no great body around them, breathing.
