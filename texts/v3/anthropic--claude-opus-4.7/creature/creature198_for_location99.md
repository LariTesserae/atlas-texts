# The People of Vost-Under-Ice

They are called, by themselves and by the few outside traders who reach them, the *Vostig* — though the word in their own tongue is softer than that, more like *vostha*, a sound made mostly with breath and the roof of the mouth, appropriate to a place where voices do not carry.

## Their bodies

They are small. Not dwarfish, not stunted — proportioned — but small, the way a people become small when fifty generations have lived in a place where every calorie is condensed from cold wet air and cropped from lichen-stone. A tall Vostig woman stands to the collarbone of a lowlander. Their hands, however, are not small. Their hands are long-fingered and broad-palmed and disproportionate, because in Vost the hands do most of the talking, and evolution, or use, or both, has made them expressive instruments. A Vostig hand at rest still seems to be considering something.

Their skin is pale but not white — a wet-stone grey with undertones that shift in the flat light, sometimes bluish, sometimes faintly coppery near the mouth and the inner wrists. It is always slightly damp. They do not sweat the way lowlanders sweat; they *glaze*, the way the walls glaze, a thin continuous film that they are constantly wiping away with the backs of their wrists in an unconscious gesture that outsiders find unsettling because it looks like weeping. Their eyelashes are extraordinary — long, dense, hooked — and they are not decorative. They catch the condensation before it runs into the eyes. A Vostig child with short lashes is considered sickly and is watched.

Their eyes are large and pale and the pupils are slow. In that flat directionless light, pupils that responded quickly would be a torment; theirs open and close on the time-scale of minutes, so that a Vostig who steps into a dim interior from the bright cirque does not blink and adjust — they simply wait, patient as a cat, for their eyes to finish arriving.

Their lungs, you understand, are not quite like yours. Something has happened in the pleura over the generations, some folding, some additional surface. They breathe shallow and constant, pulling water out of the air as much as oxygen, and this is why they rarely drink from cups. A Vostig drinks perhaps a half-cup of water in a day. The rest they inhale. A lowlander cannot do this and a lowlander who stays in Vost for a season begins to cough up the humidity in a slow drowning the locals recognize and treat with resignation and the burning of certain dried sedges.

They are long-lived. Not dramatically — a Vostig elder might reach ninety, a hundred — but they age slowly and all at once, looking forty for decades and then collapsing into very old over the course of a single winter. There is a word for this final year, *gilvost*, which means something like *the settling*, and it is treated as a season the person enters rather than a decline they suffer.

## How they move

Here is the thing about the Vostig: they are the most graceful people you will ever see, and it is not graceful the way a dancer is graceful. It is graceful the way water finds its level. They do not seem to decide to move. They are where they were and then they are where they are going, and the transition does not catch your attention. This is partly because sound does not accompany them — no scuff, no footfall, no rustle of clothing — and partly because the city has taught them, over centuries, that wasted motion costs heat. They move minimally. They pass each other in impossibly narrow cantilevered passages without touching, without seeming to calculate, and a stranger watching from above would swear the passages are wider than they are.

And they are always, always touching the walls. A Vostig walking alone will trail two fingers along the weeping bonestone as they go. Not for balance. The wall is cold and moving and slightly iron-scented and they are listening to it through their skin. You can tell a native from a first-generation settler by this: the native's fingertips are slightly grooved, worn smooth and shallow-channeled by a lifetime of wall-reading.

## How they speak

Three languages, always at once.

The first is *spoken* — low, breathy, conserved for the three-pace radius in which it works. It is a language full of fricatives and vowels and almost no hard stops; it was shaped by the medium it travels through. A Vostig conversation at arm's length sounds like two people sighing at each other thoughtfully.

The second is *handed*. Not sign language as lowlanders know it — not a translation of the spoken tongue into gesture — but its own syntax, its own vocabulary, older than the spoken language by all accounts. It works at any distance the eye reaches. It is how you are told, from across the cirque, that your cistern is overflowing, or that your child is at such-and-such ledge, or that a trader has arrived at the lip. The hands are precise and economical and use the whole arm; a Vostig speaking handedly at full volume uses the space around their body like a sculptor. Two old friends signing to each other across a market will often not bother with faces at all; they watch each other's hands.

The third is *touched*. This is reserved for intimates — lovers, parents and young children, the dying and their attendants — and it is done by pressing the fingertips to certain points on another's forearm, palm, or the nape of the neck. It is slower than the other two and conveys what the other two cannot: grief, certainty, the specific shape of love for a specific person. It is not taught formally. It is learned the way a dialect is learned, by being close to people who speak it.

A Vostig who loses the use of their hands is considered to have lost something closer to speech than to dexterity, and is cared for accordingly, and is spoken *to* in touch for the rest of their life.

## What they do

They keep the city. That is the simplest way to say it. Every Vostig, from the time they can walk, has a piece of the plumbing diagram in their head, and tending it is the work of their life. Children learn cisterns before they learn letters. A twelve-year-old can reroute a stair-waterfall with three stones and a length of felt and will do so without being asked because the pattern in their head showed them the stair was wrong.

They work copper, in small amounts. They weave the hair-fiber rope. They tend the shoulder-ewes, whose forehead-pads they read with their palms, communicating in a way that is not quite speech and not quite herding but something in between — the ewes, remember, have no eyes, and the relationship between a Vostig and their ewe is a pressure-conversation conducted across a pad of skin and sensitive fingers. A good shepherd and their lead ewe can coordinate a flock's movement through fog so thick neither can see. Neither needs to.

They feed the cistern-things. Every household has at least one; a prosperous household has three or four, in descending cisterns, and the scraps move down the system along with the water. The cistern-thing is not a pet and not a tool. It is called by a name chosen when the cistern is dug, and when it dies the household goes into a particular kind of mourning — not loud, of course, nothing in Vost is loud — but marked: the household does not trade for a lunar month, and the sealed cistern becomes a small shrine against the outer wall of the house, and the new cistern dug beside it is understood to be a new relationship, not a replacement.

They trade, a little, with the lowlands. Copper, mostly, and a particular dye made from the iron-salts in the wall-seep, which holds color in fabric as nothing else does. Traders come up in summer and do not stay long; they find the silence intolerable and the light worse.

And they tend the listeners.

## The listeners

This needs saying carefully because the Vostig do not say it.

The soft slow faces in the bonestone — the things that watch from the weeping rock — are not strangers to the Vostig. Every house has one or more in its back wall. The back wall of a Vostig home is always unplastered, always left as raw weeping bonestone, and somewhere in that wall there will be a listener, and that listener is known to the family. Has a name. Is acknowledged, when one passes it, with a particular small gesture of the left hand. It is fed — not food, but attention. A Vostig will sit before their listener in the evening and hand-speak to it, quietly, about the day. The listener does not respond in any way a lowlander would notice. But it is, in the family's understanding, *keeping the day*. The listener remembers what the family tells it. When the family's line ends — when the last Vostig of that house dies — the listener is said to carry the line's memory into the rock, and the house is abandoned, and within a generation it has been built over by another family with a different listener in a different wall.

This is why the Vostig do not fear death the way lowlanders fear it. A Vostig who is dying is brought to sit before the family listener and speaks to it, touched and handed and whispered, for as many hours as they have. What they say is given to the rock. The rock is patient. The rock has been taking in the city's days for as long as the city has been condensing out of the air.

The listeners are also — and here the Vostig grow reticent even among themselves — the reason for the thoughts-the-mountain-is-having. The faces in the condensing air, on those heavy mornings, are sometimes recognized. Someone's grandmother. Someone's dead child. The Vostig do not interrupt these because the rock is thinking out loud and the thought may be a gift being returned, briefly, to the air.

## How they are with each other

They are not a warm people, by lowland standards, and they are not a cold people. They are *present*. A Vostig looking at you is looking at you in a way you have probably not been looked at before — slow-pupiled, unhurried, taking you in the way the rock takes in water. It is not scrutiny and it is not judgment. It is attention of a kind that lowlanders find either unbearable or addictive, depending on the lowlander.

They touch each other constantly and without performance. Hands on shoulders, forearms, the backs of necks. A Vostig mother walking with her child keeps a hand on the child's head not to steer but to stay in conversation. Two Vostig friends sitting together will rest their wrists against each other for an hour without speaking and consider themselves to have had a long talk.

They do not lie well. The hand-language makes it difficult — the hands betray what the face and voice might conceal, and they have not developed, as lowlanders have, the habit of keeping the hands still while the mouth works. A Vostig attempting to deceive looks, to another Vostig, like someone gesturing in two contradictory directions at once, and is recognized immediately and usually with pity rather than anger. Deception in Vost is considered a kind of illness, a failure of integration between the three languages. It is treated, not punished.

They marry in multiples and in singles and in patterns that do not map easily onto lowland kinship. What matters is the household and the cistern-system that sustains it. A marriage-agreement leaves a flower on the cirque floor, as noted, and the flower is eaten by a shoulder-ewe, and this is taken as the agreement being carried into the body of the place. A forgiveness leaves a flower too. A Vostig feud can last decades — quietly, of course, turbulently but silently, as everything here is — and end in a single afternoon when one party leaves a flower and the other party goes to watch it be eaten.

## Their relation to the thread

The single thread of water at the top of the cirque — the one that has been falling forever into its catch-basin — is not worshipped. The Vostig do not worship. But every Vostig, once in their life, makes the climb to the slot of sky and stands beside the thread and puts a hand into it. This is done alone. It is not ceremonial; there is no age at which it must happen, no witness required, no account given afterward. It is simply something each person does, when they know it is time, and the water is very cold, and it takes some of what the person is and carries it down through the whole plumbing diagram of the city, through every cistern, past every listener, through every household's attention, to the lowest catch-basin at the cirque's lip, from which it eventually leaves the city as mist and returns to the air it came from.

A Vostig who has done this is said to be *threaded*. It is the closest thing they have to a coming-of-age, and it has no fixed age, because the place does not care about calendars — it cares about when a person is ready to be part of the water.

And that, in the end, is what they are. Not inhabitants of Vost. Not exactly. They are one of the forms the cirque takes, along with the flyers and the ewes and the cistern-things and the listeners and the thread — a way the mountain has, under its blanket of wet cold light, of condensing itself into something that can tend itself, and remember itself, and speak, in three soft languages, to its own slow rock.
