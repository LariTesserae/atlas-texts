The ones who live here are not what you would call inhabitants. They are tenants, perhaps, or descendants of tenants, or the long quiet result of something that was once a population and is now closer to a condition.

Begin with the goats, because they are the easiest to see, and because seeing them properly takes longer than it should. They are not goats. They have the shape of goats the way certain clouds have the shape of horses — convincingly, from a distance, until the wind shifts. Their wool is not wool but something finer, hollow-shafted, the individual fibers warm to the touch even when the animal has been dead some hours. (You learn this from the scatter, never from a body; the bodies go elsewhere.) Their pupils are horizontal, like a true goat's, but the horizontal is wrong somehow — too level, too still, as if painted on. They do not blink in unison but they do blink in sequence, the lead animal first, then the second, then the third, a slow ripple down the line that you only notice on the seventh or eighth day of watching. And they listen. Not to you. They tilt their heads down toward the rock, often, and hold the pose, and then move on. Whatever they hear in the hum, they hear more of it than you do. They have been listening for a long time.

The six-legged things — call them shrikes, though they are not birds and do not impale anything — are the mountain's grammarians. They patrol the moss-mats in slow loops, and what they do, mostly, is correct. A fallen needle in the wrong place is picked up and moved. A pebble that has rolled onto a cushion plant is rolled off. They tend the small dishevelments. Watching one work, you understand that the lushness of certain ledges is not only the warm rock; it is also this, this patient sub-gardening, this hundred-thousand-year habit of putting things back. They have six legs because four was not enough hands. Their eyes are very large and very dark and seem to see in some register adjacent to light — they are unbothered by the deepest shadow under the trees, and they avoid certain stretches of open ledge that look, to you, identical to the stretches they cross freely. They know something about the ground that you do not.

The grouse-things are simpler, or perform simplicity. They are the mountain's pulse made visible: they walk in time. If you sit very still and watch a flock pick through the moss, you will notice that their steps fall on the beat of the hum, that when the slow piston stroke completes somewhere below, every small dark head dips at once to feed, and then lifts. They are metronomes wearing feathers. Whether they were bred to this rhythm by long exposure, or whether something deeper is true — whether they are, in some functional sense, part of the machinery, sensors in a soft outer layer — is not a question the mountain answers. They taste, the predators presumably know, of warm iron.

And the predator. Yes. There is one, and there is only one, and this is the thing that takes the longest to understand. The curved tooth in the wool is always the same tooth. Not the same kind of tooth — the same tooth, with the same small chip near the root, the same patina. It is left, you eventually come to suspect, deliberately. A signature. A courtesy of the kind the ecosystem runs on: I was here, I took one, the count is correct, please continue. Whatever leaves the tooth is large enough that the goats do not flee from it so much as defer to it, the way a small country defers to weather. It moves at night, or in the sidelong exhausted light that passes for day, and it moves without disturbing the moss. The shrikes do not correct its passage; either it leaves nothing to correct, or what it leaves is not theirs to touch. You will find, once, a place where it slept: a depression in the lichen the size of a small car, still faintly warm, and around the edges of the depression the cushion plants have grown thicker, fed by something. It eats the goats and the goats are fed by the warm rock and the warm rock is the breath of the machine and so, in a roundabout and entirely literal way, the predator eats the mountain. It has been doing this a long time. It may, by now, be partly made of mountain. This would explain why you never see it: it does not need to arrive anywhere. It is already there, distributed, the way weather is distributed, condensing into a body only when a body is required.

There is one more category of resident, and this is the one you will argue with yourself about for a long time after you leave, if you leave.

In certain places on certain ledges — not many, perhaps a dozen across a whole day's walking — the black trees grow in a ring. Not a perfect ring. A ring the way a thought is a ring, with a center and a circumference and an implication of intent. Inside the ring the moss is deeper and the air is a half-degree warmer and the hum, if you lie down with your ear to the ground, is not quite the hum you hear elsewhere. It has, faintly, a shape. A cadence. Something almost like the rise and fall of a sentence being spoken very slowly by someone with all the time in the world.

You will want to call these people. You will want to say: something lives in the rings, something that was once a person or once a community of persons, something that went into the mountain long ago and did not come out, or came out as this — as warmth, as cadence, as the particular density of moss inside a circle of trees. You will want to believe this because the alternative is that the mountain is simply talking to itself, and has been, for longer than there have been ears to overhear.

Either is possible. Both, perhaps. The rings do not answer when addressed, but the cadence does sometimes change after you have spoken inside one, as if something has noted your contribution and is considering it. The shrikes give the rings a wider berth than they give anything else. The goats sleep at the edges of them, never inside, with their long bodies oriented inward, like listeners at the rim of a well.

This is who lives here. A predator that may be the mountain's own appetite, given form when needed. A gardening species that maintains a tidiness whose purpose no one alive remembers. A prey species that keeps time. A flock of impostor-goats that hear more of the hum than is comfortable to think about. And, in the rings, the possibility of persons — old persons, slow persons, persons who have become the conditions of their own dwelling, who exhale through warm stone and speak in cadences too long to catch in a single visit.

They share the mountain the way organs share a body. None of them is in charge. The machinery is not in charge either; it is only the loudest of them, the one whose pulse the others have organized themselves around. If it stopped — and there is no reason to think it will — the goats would go quiet first, then the grouse, then the shrikes would lose the rhythm of their corrections, and the rings would cool, and the predator would condense one last time out of the failing warmth and walk down off the mountain looking for somewhere else to be distributed. The forests would persist a little longer, on stored breath, and then they too would thin, and the mountain would become only a mountain again, which is to say: much less than it is now.

But it has not stopped. The piston completes its slow stroke. The needles tremble. Something in a ring of trees finishes a syllable it began an hour ago and begins, with great patience, the next one. A goat, three ledges down, tilts its head to the rock and listens, and is satisfied, and walks on.
