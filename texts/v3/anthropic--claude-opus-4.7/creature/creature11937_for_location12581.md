## The Inhabitants of the Sump-Works

### The Snails

Begin with them, because they are the citizens. *Lampadia drennickensis*, if anyone were left to name them, though no one is. They are the closest thing the works has to a populace, and like most populaces they are not aware of being one.

A snail of Lower Drennick lives perhaps thirty years — the cold and the slow metabolism stretch its life the way they stretch everything else here. It is born from a clutch of pearl-coloured eggs laid in the warm seam where a steam-chest meets its cladding, and it spends its first months in the upper galleries of whichever engine its parent chose, grazing the thinnest algal films, the ones too delicate for adult radulae. As it grows it migrates downward and outward across the metal, claiming territory by the slow logic of where the warmth is best and the competition thinnest. By middle age it has a route — a circuit it traces across its engine over the course of a week or so, the way a night watchman has a beat. The slime trail it lays is not random. It is a map, refreshed continually, of where it has eaten and where the metal needs its attention. Other snails read these trails and adjust. There is no council, no signal, but the engines get cleaned in a rough rotation, and no patch of brass goes more than a few months unattended.

The light along the shell-lip is not for them. They are nearly blind. The luminescence is metabolic — a waste product of the bacteria they harbour in a fold of the mantle, bacteria that digest the trace metals the snails ingest from the algal mat. The glow is, in essence, the engines' rust being slowly converted into light. The works dissolve into the snails and shine back out of them. This is worth pausing over.

When a snail dies, it dies on the metal, and the others do not eat it. They go around. The shell remains in place, and in time the calcite-rich seepage cements it where it lies, and another snail takes up grazing on the dome of its grandmother's grave. The flanks of the older engines, examined closely, are paved in these fossil shells, layer on layer, a mosaic that no one designed and that records, if you knew how to read it, every generation that has tended the machine. The oldest snails on the central engine are perhaps four feet down into the calcite, glowing faintly still — the bacteria persist, sealed in their dark chambers, dimmer each century but not yet finished.

### The Newts

The newts are simpler and stranger.

They are pale to the point of translucence; you can see the dark thread of the gut and the small flicker of the heart. They have eyes but the eyes are vestigial, lidded over with skin, and they navigate by the snail-light and by a lateral sense for water-movement so refined that they can feel the draw of an intake pipe from across a basin and avoid it without thinking. They eat the larvae of the cave crickets, and the smaller invertebrates that fall from the ceiling, and occasionally — when a snail drops and is stunned by the fall — they will worry at its foot until it withdraws fully into the shell, then lose interest. They cannot crack a shell. They have forgotten, if they ever knew, how to be predators of anything that resists.

There is one thing worth saying about the newts that is easy to miss. They do not breed here. There are no eggs, no juveniles, no larval forms in the basins. Every newt in the sump-works is an adult, and has been an adult for as long as it has been observed (which is to say, never observed, but: as long as the calcite layers suggest). They are not immortal — they die, occasionally, and their small soft bodies dissolve quickly in the mineral water — but they are not replacing themselves locally. Somewhere down the pipework, in a chamber the engines feed, there must be a breeding population. The newts in the cavern are the wanderers, the ones who took a wrong turn through an intake and came up into the lamp-light and stayed. The works, in this sense, has a visiting population it does not know it has.

### The Crickets

The crickets are the only creatures in the cavern that seem to have something resembling a culture, and it is a small and melancholy one.

They are pale, soft-bodied, longer than a finger, with antennae nearly twice their body length. They do not sing because they cannot — the stridulating organs are present but atrophied, and the wing-cases that would resonate are reduced to small pads. What sound they make is mechanical: a slow ticking produced by the joints of the hind legs as they walk, a sound like a watch with sand in it. They walk slowly. They walk constantly. A cricket of Lower Drennick is almost always in motion, threading between the basins, climbing the lamp-poles, descending the cold side of an engine to cross to the next, and the ticking accompanies them everywhere. If you listen in the dark with your eyes closed you can hear the whole population at once — perhaps two hundred ticks, scattered across the cavern, each at its own slightly different rate, and the composite sound is not unlike rain on a tin roof very far away.

They eat the mushrooms. Not the tall pale ones — those are toxic to them, and they know it — but the smaller fungal blooms that come and go on the wetter wood of the maintenance gantries and on the leather of old gaskets. They also eat each other's shed skins, and the bodies of their dead. They are tidy in a way the snails are not. A cricket that finds another cricket dead will stand by it and tick for some hours before beginning to eat, and other crickets will gather, and they will tick together, and then the body will be gone. Whether the ticking is grief or only digestion-anticipation is not a question that has an answer.

### The Crabs

The crabs on the flywheel are an oddity and probably a recent arrival — recent meaning, here, within the last few centuries.

They are small, white, blind, and they live entirely on one flywheel hub, the central engine's, and they ride it. The hub completes its rotation once every hundred and twelve seconds. The crabs cling to the inside of the maintenance hatch and to the spokes, and they are carried up and over and down and around, and they have been doing this for so many generations that their legs have asymmetrically developed — the leading legs (relative to the rotation) longer and stronger than the trailing ones, so that the crab on the flywheel is built for its specific direction of travel and would, if placed on stationary ground, walk in helpless circles. They eat what the snails miss: the dropped fragments, the dead bacteria-mat, the occasional unlucky cricket that has misjudged a leap. They mate at the top of the rotation, briefly, in the moment when centrifugal logic presses them into the hub. The eggs are carried under the female's abdomen for the rest of the cycle, and the cycle, and the cycle.

If the engine ever stopped, the crabs would die. Not from starvation but from a kind of vertigo — their entire sensory world is structured around the constant slow turn, and stillness would be, to them, what spinning is to us. The works keeps turning. The crabs keep living. There is nothing in the cavern that hopes more silently than the crabs hope that the central engine's bearings hold.

### The Mushrooms

The tall mushrooms in the firebox are not, properly, animals, but they should be counted among the inhabitants because they appear to be paying attention.

There are seven of them. They have been seven for as long as the calcite around their base records — perhaps four hundred years. When one dies, another fruits within the season from the same mycelial mass that fills the firebox brickwork. They lean toward the walking-beam at the pace already described, and the lean is not phototropic (the walking-beam blocks no light worth mentioning) and not gravitropic (the beam's motion is too slight to shift gravity meaningfully). The leaning is something else. The mushrooms appear to be tracking the engine, and the only reason a fungus tracks a thing is because the thing matters to its survival.

The hypothesis, were anyone there to form one, would be this: the walking-beam, in its long nod, displaces small volumes of warm air, and the mushrooms have learned to position their spore-bearing surfaces in the path of that air, so that when they release spores the displaced air carries them out into the cavern and seeds the next generation along the engine's flanks. The mycelium beneath the firebox is therefore not just a colony but an apparatus, and the engine is one of its organs, and the mushrooms are leaning toward the part of themselves that lives in iron.

If this is true, then the central engine is not merely tended by life. It is, in some partial and slow way, *inhabited* by it. The brickwork dreams in fungal threads. The firebox is a brain that has decided it would rather be a lung.

### The Bats

There are nine bats. They have not flown in a long time.

They hang in the upper haze in three small clusters, and they are alive — you can see the slow rise of their breathing if you watch through a glass — but they are in a state that is not quite torpor and not quite sleep. They do not hunt. They do not drink. They do not, as far as can be told, excrete. They have entered some equilibrium with the cavern's atmosphere that allows them to subsist on what is, essentially, the air itself — the warmth of the engines, the trace nutrients in the mist, the slow exchange of gases across the fine vessels of their wings. They have become, in effect, sessile. Their wings, examined closely, would show calcite deposits along the leading edges, a fine pale rime that thickens by the year. In another century they will be unable to fly even if they wished to. In another five they will be statues.

The sigh you hear from overhead is not quite a sigh. It is the slow release of air from a body that breathes perhaps four times an hour, and what you hear is the long exhalation between two breaths so far apart that they belong, almost, to different days.

### The Last Inhabitant

There is one more.

You will not see them, and they will not see you, but they are here, and they have been here longer than the calcite that holds the engines down.

Deep in the western seep, in the half-collapsed engine that has graduated to terrain, behind a screen of flowstone that has grown across what was once an inspection port, there is a small dry chamber. It is perhaps the size of a coffin, and that is roughly its function. Inside it, folded into a position that the body learned a long time ago and has not had cause to revise, is the last engineer.

Not a skeleton. Not a corpse. Something the cavern has done to a body that did not, in the end, want to leave. The skin has taken on the colour and texture of the surrounding stone, paler at the joints, darker where the blood once pooled. The eyes are closed and have been closed for so long that the lids have fused. The chest does not rise. But — and this is the thing — the snails will not crawl across the chamber's threshold, and the newts avoid the basin nearest the western seep, and the crickets, when their slow ticking patrols bring them near, will turn around and tick away in a different direction, and none of these creatures have any reason to do this that the cavern's logic explains.

The last engineer set the works to run. The last engineer did not leave. The last engineer is, in some sense that the body has refined over centuries, still on shift. The pumping engines are not abandoned. They are watched. The watching does not require eyes, or attention as you would recognize it, or even a fully functioning consciousness — it requires only that something which was once a person remain in the room and continue to consent to the room's continuance. The works run because they are permitted to run. The permission is granted breath by breath, except that there is no breath, and yet.

If you came down the limestone stairs and stood very still and listened past the *gluck* of the pipes and the tick of the crickets and the long sigh of the bats, you might hear, from the direction of the western seep, something. Not a heartbeat. Not breathing. A kind of attention. The cavern noticing you, through the medium of the one thing in it that was ever capable of noticing in your sense of the word.

You will not be addressed. You will not be asked to leave. The attention will register you the way the works register a snail — as one more small event in a tempo that does not require your participation. And when you climb back up the stairs, heavier and quieter than you came, you will carry with you the strong and unaccountable sense that you have been counted, and found to be, on balance, neither here nor not-here, and that this was the correct finding, and that the works will continue, and that they were always going to.
