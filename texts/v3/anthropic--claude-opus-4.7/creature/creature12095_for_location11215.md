# The Tenants of Oss

## The Frogs

Begin with them because they are the volume of the place, and because they are the easiest of its inhabitants to be honest about.

There are eight species and they sort themselves by depth, by stone temperature, by the mineral character of each basin's water. The smallest, no bigger than a thumbnail, lives in the highest weirs where the water is freshest and the lime is still suspended; they are the colour of wet slate and they call in a high dry click that sounds like two pebbles being tapped. The largest, in the third tier of basins down, are the size of a closed fist and the colour of old bronze, and their call is a low pulse you feel in your sternum before you hear it.

None of the eight species exists anywhere else. This has been verified, insofar as anyone bothers to verify such things; they have been carried out in jars and they do not breed in other water. Something about the lime, perhaps. Something about the particular salts the upstream quarry contributed to the stone, which the weeping has been releasing in trace amounts for centuries. The frogs of Oss are, in a real sense, made of Oss — their bodies built from minerals that were lifted by hand and laid in a basin and left to dissolve slowly back into the world through a frog's skin.

They do not seem afraid of anything. They will sit on your boot. They will sit on the offerings the harvesters leave, which is one of the reasons the offerings are left on the *highest* dry stone — to give them at least a chance of not being immediately occupied.

## The Hand-Eels

They are not eels and they are not, strictly, anything else. The closest living relative known is a kind of cave salamander in mountains four hundred miles north, and even that is a poor match — the hand-eels have no gills as adults, only a long porous flank that breathes through the biofilm-rich water of the basins. Out of that water they desiccate within an hour. This is why their migrations are nocturnal: not for darkness, but for the dew.

The small soft hands are not for travel alone. They are sensitive in a way that has no good word. A hand-eel placed against a stone can tell you — if you are the sort of person who can read what it does next — whether the stone is from the original quarry or a later patch, whether it has been touched recently, whether something has died on it within the last few days. The harvesters know this. The harvesters take the marked ones, the ones with a faint pale ring just behind the hands, because those are the ones whose touch-sense has gone strange and deep, and because something is made from them which no one I asked would name.

The hand-eels appear to remember the layout of Oss. Not in the way a fish knows its pool — in the way a person knows a city. They take routes. They have preferences. Young ones follow older ones up the weirs, and when an old one stops at a particular lip and rests for a long time, the young ones rest there too, and afterwards they also stop at that lip when they are old. The map of the town is also in them. If Oss were drained tomorrow the hand-eels would die, but the shape of Oss would persist in their movements for as long as a generation, the way a dance persists in dancers after the music ends.

## The Things in the Sumps

I will be careful here.

They are pale and they are large and they turn over each other in a way that suggests either many bodies or one body with many parts, and at the distance from which they can be seen — which is the distance of looking down through several body-lengths of dim water — the question does not resolve.

What can be said: the frogs do not call within twenty paces of a deep sump. The hand-eels will detour an extra basin's length to avoid crossing one. The bats do not hunt over them; you can watch the cloud of bats part around those particular basins as if around a column of warmer air. The water in the deep sumps is, by every measure a harvester's bucket can take, the same water as the rest of Oss — same temperature, same lime, same faint sweetness. But the other tenants treat it as a different substance entirely.

They — the pale things — do not seem to come up. There is no record of one being seen at the surface. There is no record of one being seen out of water. The harvesters' rule against approaching the deep sumps is not a rule about danger in any direct sense; it is more like the rule one observes about not staring at certain people, or not speaking in certain rooms. A rule of proportion. The deep sumps are where Oss keeps something, and the keeping is the relationship, and the relationship is not improved by inspection.

My own guess, which is worth what guesses are worth: they are older than the frogs and older than the hand-eels. They may be older than the town. The town may have been built around them, the basins arranged to feed them the particular slow water they require, the whole cistern a system whose purpose was to maintain the conditions of the deep places at the centre — and the eels and frogs and bats and ferns are what accumulated around that purpose, as life accumulates around any sustained gift of habitat. If that is true, then Oss is not a town that became an ecosystem. Oss is a feeding-arrangement that became a town that became an ecosystem, and the original tenants are still being fed, and the feeding still works, and the rest of us are the froth on top of it.

I do not press this idea. It is not the sort of idea that improves with pressing.

## The Bats

The bats are easier. There are two species in the tower; they roost on different levels and they leave at slightly different times in the dusk, the smaller ones first by perhaps four minutes. They eat the insects that hatch from the basins — there is a particular pale midge that breeds in the lime-rich shallows in clouds — and they drink on the wing, skimming the basins so lightly that they leave no wake the hand-eels can read.

The tower they roost in drips constantly because they are, between them, producing a great deal of warm wet breath in a confined column of stone, and that breath condenses on the inside of the tower and runs down and out through whatever drains were originally built into its base. The drainage from the bat-tower feeds, by a single roofed channel, into one specific basin near the centre of Oss, and that basin is the richest in life of any in the town — the densest moss, the most frogs, the most hand-eels passing through. The builders knew what the tower would become. The builders built a tower whose purpose was to be filled with bats and to drain their richness into a particular basin. This is the kind of thing that, when you notice it, makes you sit down on a stone and not move for a while.

## The Ferns, the Moss, the Rush

These I will not say much about. They are tenants in the sense that anything that lives somewhere is a tenant, but they are not party to the arrangement in the way the animals are. They arrived. They stayed. The fern is useful to someone; the moss feeds the midges; the rush, where it grows, slows the silt and makes new shallows where new frogs can spawn. They are participants but not signatories. The town tolerates them. They are the green and grey edge of what is otherwise a place of water and stone and the bodies that move between.

## The Harvesters

You did not ask about the harvesters, and I am not sure they count as inhabitants — they do not stay — but they are part of the population of Oss in a slow generational sense, and I will say this:

They are not the descendants of the builders. The builders are gone. The harvesters are people from the marsh-edge villages to the east, who have inherited a set of practices whose reasons they have mostly lost but whose shape they have not. They know what to take and when. They know what to leave. They know not to stay past dark. They know which copper hook, bent which way, goes on which stone in which season. The knowledge is in them the way the map of Oss is in the hand-eels: as movement, as preference, as the route their feet take without consulting them.

If you asked a harvester who lives in Oss, they would say: the frogs, the eels-that-are-not-eels, the bats, the ferns. They would not mention the deep sumps. They would not be hiding them. They would simply not be counting them, in the same way you do not count your own heart when you are listing the things in a room.

## And Then

And then there is the town itself, which I have been treating as the setting but which, the longer I write, the more I think should be counted among the tenants. The stone is alive in the slow sense — it weeps, it redeposits itself, it grows the flowstone curtains the ferns require, it softens its own carvings the way a body forgets a scar. It is doing something. It has been doing it for a very long time. It is in relationship with the water and with the things the water carries, and it is changing, slowly, in response to them, as they are changing in response to it.

A cistern that has become an ecosystem, you said. I would say: a cistern that has become a slow vast animal, with frogs for voice and hand-eels for nerves and bats for breath and pale unnamed things at its centre for whatever centres are for, and the harvesters come and groom it at the edges and leave it gifts and go home before it wakes, which it does every night, at dusk, when the bats pour out and the frog-weather resumes and the water keeps moving, basin to basin, toward whatever is downstream.

That is who lives there. That is what Oss is for.
