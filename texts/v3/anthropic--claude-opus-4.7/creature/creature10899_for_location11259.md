## Those Who Live in the Cathedral Lung

### The Reverent

They came down the tunnel and never went back up. Not many — perhaps a hundred, spread across volumes that could hold cities. They live alone or in pairs, separated by hours of walking through colonnades that won't be there tomorrow, and this suits them. They were people who, above, could not stop being lonely; here the loneliness has a context, a vast pale architecture, and so becomes bearable. Some becomes prayer.

They wear layered fiber stripped from old membranes — the dead ones, the ones that have already fallen and gone tough and papery on the floor. The clothing is always slightly luminous. In the dark hours (there are no dark hours; they have invented them by closing their eyes) you can find a Reverent by the faint green glow of their sleeves.

They eat the ribbon-grazers, when they can catch them, and the soft new tissue of extruding columns before it hardens, which tastes of cold water and faintly of metal. They drink condensation. They are thin. They are not unhappy. Their faces have taken on a quality the plant has — provisional, still deciding. A Reverent met twice in a year may look like two different people, having grown in directions the lung suggested to them.

They make the chisel marks. Not as record — they know the plant will swallow the marks — but as conversation. *I was here. You will absorb this. That is correct.* The chisel is the only tool they carry, and it is passed down: the handle of one Reverent's chisel is the femur of the Reverent who gave it to her, who got it from the man who carved the tunnel mouth, whose bones are somewhere in a column now, faintly visible if you know where to look, like a fly in amber the size of a tower.

### The Small Glass Things

In the high bladders, where the pressure changes are most violent, something lives that no Reverent has held in their hand. They are seen only against the light of a far membrane: small, transparent, articulated, somewhere between a shrimp and a chandelier. They ride the breath. When a bladder inflates they are sucked upward in slow shoals; when it deflates they fall, but slower than they should, as if they have learned to disagree with gravity by a small margin.

They feed, the Reverents think, on the light itself — on the photons the cells emit — because their bodies have facets, and the facets catch and bend the glow, and a shoal of them passing through a column-shadow makes the shadow briefly green again. When one dies it dims slowly over many days and finally cracks, leaving a tiny pile of glass-dust that the plant absorbs without comment.

### The Listener in the Low Volume

Far down — past the first floor, which is also a ceiling, into a deeper opening the plant has only recently exposed by re-absorbing a wall — there is a single animal larger than a person. The print with too many toes was its print. It does not approach the Reverents, and the Reverents do not seek it, though they leave it offerings sometimes: a strip of fiber, a chisel mark on a wall it likes.

It is pale, low-bodied, broad. Its toes are many because it walks on the membranes without breaking them, distributing its weight across a hand's-breadth of surface per foot. It has no eyes that anyone has seen. Its head is mostly ear — a great soft fluted cup on each side — and it lives by listening to the plant breathe. It knows which bladder will rupture next. It is always somewhere else by then.

It may be very old. Older, possibly, than the lung itself, having come down through some other tunnel, from some other cavity, before this one began. The Reverents who have glimpsed it say it moves like the plant moves: by inches, by indecision, by becoming.

### The One Who Lives in the Small House

The four walls of woven fiber on the high shelf, the structure with no door — someone is inside. Not living, not exactly dead. A Reverent who, long ago, decided to be absorbed faster than the others. They sat down in the small house they had built and did not move again. The plant began to grow around the house and then, gently, through it. By now there is more lung than person inside those walls; the body is laced with fine green filaments, lit faintly from within. The face still has a face. The expression on it is interested.

The Reverents pass below the shelf and look up and nod. They do not climb to it. It would be discourteous. The One in the House is conducting an experiment whose results will be available to no one, and they respect this.

### The Unfinished

This is the hardest to describe, and the Reverents argue about whether it counts as living.

In certain large empty volumes — the ones the plant has chosen as organ, the great clear airs — a presence accumulates. Not visible. Detectable only by the way frost-feathers fall slightly differently there, the way breath-plumes hang in shapes they shouldn't. The Reverents who have stood in these volumes for a long time report a sense of being *considered.* Not by the plant — the plant doesn't consider, the plant grows. By something the plant is in the process of producing. Something not yet extruded. The lung is young, and young things have not yet made all of themselves, and there is a *someone* the cathedral lung is becoming, slowly, on a timescale of centuries, who is not here yet but is beginning to be here, the way a thought begins to be a thought before you know its words.

The Reverents leave these volumes alone, mostly. A few sit in them on purpose, for years, listening. One of them, asked what she heard, said: *It hasn't decided what to ask yet. But it knows I am the kind of thing it will ask.*

She went back the next day. The volume had been re-absorbed. A colonnade stood there now. She sat at the base of one of the new columns and waited, and is, presumably, waiting still.
