# The Inhabitants of the Sumpworks

They are people, or began as people. This needs to be said first, because everything else will complicate it.

There are perhaps two hundred of them. The number is hard to fix because they do not gather, and because some of them work shifts that mean you might count the same person twice in a day and miss another entirely. They are thin without being gaunt, the way deep-water fish are thin — built for a medium that does not demand bulk. Their skin has taken on the faint translucency of the climbing plant's leaves; under the yellow bulbs you can sometimes see, in the older ones, the dark suggestion of the vessel beneath the wrist. This is not illness. They eat what comes down in the freight cage and they eat the glassy beaded moss, which they have learned to harvest with small bone tools, and they are not sick. They are simply being slowly revised by the room.

Their eyes have grown. Not grotesquely — you would not notice if you met one alone, on a surface street, in daylight you had not forgotten existed. But side by side with a visitor the difference is plain: the pupils are larger, the iris a thinner ring, and the whites have yellowed to match the bulbs, so that looking at them is like looking at two more small lamps strung on the cord of a face. They blink rarely. They do not need to. The air is wet enough.

They speak, but sparingly, and what they speak is a register of the language that has shed most of its verbs. *The press. The waxed cloth. The third drip past the cistern.* They name objects and locations and conditions; they do not often say what is to be done with these, because what is to be done with these is already understood by everyone. A child born here — there are four children, that I can find — learns the dense small nouns of the cavity first, and only later, from the older arrivals, learns the verbs that describe motion across distances longer than the town itself. The children do not believe in those verbs the way they believe in *brick* and *belt* and *drip*.

They sleep in shifts that are not quite shifts, because the work does not stop and is not hurried. A worker leaves the loom, walks the four damp meters to a room with a mat, lies down, and the next worker has already arrived at the loom, having been at the mat the worker is now lying down on. The mats are warm from the previous body. This is considered comfortable. There is no privacy here and the concept has gone soft from disuse; intimacy and labor and rest have braided into a single fiber that they pass between themselves like the shuttle of the loom they tend.

They have a relationship with the cats that resembles diplomacy. The cats — one gray, one the color of weak tea, matching the fungus — are not pets. They are consulted. When a cat sits for a long time at a particular brick, the masons come and look at that brick, because the cats have been right, so far, about which walls the cavity is preparing to press inward. No one taught the cats to do this. No one taught the people to ask.

Their hands are extraordinary. This is where you see what they are for. The fingers are long and the joints have a kind of considered quietness, as if each knuckle has decided independently to move only when necessary. They can place a component into another component with a precision that does not require looking. Many of them work with their eyes half-closed, or open and pointed at nothing, while their hands continue the assembly. The components they make are, I think, very small, and require a stillness that ordinary human hands — hurried, distracted, fed on noise — could not provide. The cavity has made them still. Or they have made themselves still, in answer to the cavity, which is the same thing said from the other direction.

They do not, as far as I can tell, remember the surface clearly. The older ones can produce the word *sky* but seem uncertain what to do with it; they offer it the way you might offer a stranger a coin from a country you have not visited. The younger ones — the ones born here, and the ones who arrived very young — do not produce the word at all. They have a word, instead, for the ceiling: *the lid*. They speak of *the lid* with a quiet attention that is not quite reverence and not quite fear. The lid sweats. The lid presses. The lid is making room.

For what? They have the same agreement about this that they have about the cavity's growth, which is to say no agreement, only a shared posture. If you ask them directly — I have tried — they look at you with their yellowed eyes and say a noun: *the cage*, perhaps, or *the wax*, or *the next*. They are not being evasive. They are telling you, as plainly as they can, that the answer is the work, and the work is the answer, and that the thing being prepared for will know itself when it arrives, by the components it finds waiting, wrapped, labeled, stacked in courtyards.

One of them, an older woman who tends the bird, told me something I have been turning over. She said — and this is a paraphrase, because she said it in nouns and I am rendering it in sentences — that the bird was not brought down. The bird, she said, was *found*, in the second factory, on the morning after the cavity first opened, sitting in the rafters as though it had been there always, clicking. She said the bird is the oldest inhabitant. She said the bird is here for the same reason they are here, but the bird knew first. She said this without inflection, the way she might have said *the press descends*.

I do not know if I believe her. I do not know if she believes herself, in the sense that *believe* means anything here. But I noticed, as she spoke, that her hands were moving — folding a small square of waxed cloth into smaller and smaller squares, with the precision of someone assembling a component — and that her eyes, the yellow lamps of them, were fixed on the rafters above us, where the bird was, and that the bird, just then, clicked once, as though something had been wound one notch tighter.

They are waiting. That is what lives in the Sumpworks. Not patient, exactly, because patience implies a horizon. They are the shape that waiting takes when it has forgotten there was ever anything else to be, and when the room around it has begun, very slowly, to wait back.
