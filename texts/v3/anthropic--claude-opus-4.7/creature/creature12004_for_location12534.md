# The People of the Drowned Cordillera

They are not many. That is the first thing to understand. You could traverse three days of channels and see only a half-dozen of them, each at distance, each absorbed in some task — a figure on a dock mending net, a silhouette on a catwalk above a turbine sluice, a boat-shape pulling away from a kelp-cut. The country is full of life but thin with people, and the people have arranged themselves accordingly: spread out, paired or in small kindreds, each station tending its stretch of the drowned range.

## Bodies

They are short and broad. Generations in cold water and cold wind have selected for it — compact torsos, thick through the chest, short strong forearms, the kind of legs that hold a pitching deck without thought. Their hands are extraordinary: wide-palmed, the fingers slightly webbed at the base (not dramatically, you might not notice in a handshake, but enough that they swim differently than you do), the skin permanently softened and wrinkled at the pad-tips from immersion. The nails grow thick and grey-translucent like horn. Their hair runs dark and oiled — they oil it deliberately, with rendered fish-fat worked through to the scalp, which is why you smell them before you see them in any enclosed space, a smell that is not unpleasant once you stop classifying it: lanolin and smoke and something faintly marine.

Their eyes are pale. This is the trait that startles outsiders. Across every family line, regardless of skin tone (which ranges from a weathered tan to a deep coppery brown depending on which basins their grandparents worked), the eyes come in at the cold end of the spectrum — grey, grey-green, a washed-out blue like ice that has been melted and refrozen. They see well in the green dim under the kelp canopy. They see well in fog. They do not see particularly well in bright direct sun and they squint constantly when the cloud breaks, which gives even the young ones a creased look around the temples.

The children swim before they walk. Literally — it is the first developmental milestone, around six months, when an infant is dipped held-under-the-arms into a warmed basin and discovers that the kicking it has been doing in air makes more sense in water. By three they paddle the shallows unsupervised. By seven they free-dive the kelp-bases for shellfish, holding breath for stretches that would alarm a physiologist who hadn't measured one of them. Their lung capacity is genuinely anomalous; their spleens, too, are enlarged, releasing oxygenated red cells on demand the way certain sea-mammal populations do. They evolved into this, or were shaped by it, across however many generations have been here. They no longer remember which.

## Dress

Practical and layered. Closest to the skin, a knit wool undergarment, oiled — the wool comes from the small hardy sheep some families keep on the larger islands, animals adapted to the wet wind and the sparse grazing. Over the wool, oilskin: heavy waxed cotton or, increasingly, a rubberized canvas they get from somewhere downcountry, traded for kelp-product and dried fish. The oilskins are not new. They are mended in long visible seams of contrasting thread, and the mending is a point of pride — a man whose oilskins have been patched by three generations of women in his family wears those patches the way another culture might wear medals.

On their feet: tall boots, often rubber but sometimes still the older leather-and-tar construction the grandfathers wore. Inside the boots, dry wool, changed twice a day if they can manage it. Wet feet are the enemy. Wet feet are how you lose toes by forty.

On their heads: hoods, usually, attached to the oilskin. When they take the hoods down indoors, they reveal close-fitting wool caps underneath, often elaborately patterned. The pattern tells you which basin-family they come from. You learn to read these the way a sailor reads flags. The Hesker people knit in zigzags. The folk from the Long Sound knit in concentric diamonds. The two families who tend the southern turbine hall — and who have intermarried so completely they're really one family now — knit a kind of broken cross that no one else uses.

## Work

They are engineers and harvesters in equal measure, and they don't distinguish between the two activities the way an outsider might. To them, tending the hydroelectric house and tending the kelp-cut and tending the fish-weir are all the same kind of work: you are managing a flow. You are adjusting a system that wants to run away from you. You are listening for the note that means something has gone wrong.

The turbine halls were built by their great-great-grandparents, in a period the old people call simply "the building," which lasted maybe forty years and produced almost everything mechanical that now exists in the country. Since the building, the people have been maintaining. They cast their own replacement bearings in small foundries on two of the central islands. They wind their own armatures. They mill their own gaskets from a kind of tough kelp-rubber they render themselves in iron pots. The technology has not advanced because it does not need to — it works, and they understand it intimately, and the parts they cannot make they trade for downcountry, which is a journey of some weeks by boat that the younger adults make in rotation as a kind of rite.

They harvest the kelp on a long rotation, never stripping a stand, working it the way a careful forester works a wood. The kelp goes to many uses: food (the tender inner ribbons, salted and pressed into cakes); the rubber-analogue mentioned above; a kind of paper; a fiber that can be spun, with effort, into a coarse cloth used for sacking and rough garments; and most importantly, fertilizer for the thin soils on the exposed island crowns where they grow what little they grow — root vegetables mostly, hardy greens, a few stunted grains in the most sheltered hollows.

They fish, of course. Constantly. The schools that move between basins are predictable enough that the weirs and nets are set seasonally with great precision. A man can tell you, looking at the chop and the cloud-base and the temperature of the water at his ankle, whether tomorrow's pull will be heavy or light, and he will usually be right.

And they tend the gradients — this is their term, and it covers everything to do with water moving from basin to basin. Every significant fall has a structure on it: sometimes just a measurement station, sometimes a small generator, sometimes a full turbine house with a crew. Power runs along those creosoted lines to the larger stations and to the small clusters of houses on the more hospitable islands. The whole electrical net is theirs, hand-built and hand-maintained, and they are quietly, deeply proud of it. A young woman apprenticing as a station-tender will speak of "her" turbine the way someone elsewhere might speak of a horse.

## Houses

Built into the leeward sides of the higher island-knobs, dug back into the rock where possible, fronted with stone and roofed with slate or with the rust-orange corrugated iron you saw on the processing shed. The roofs are weighted with more stone against the wind. Inside: low ceilings, small windows, deep windowsills that double as seats. A central stove burning peat (cut from the boggy hollows on certain islands) or burning a kind of compressed kelp-and-fish-oil briquette that gives a dense slow heat and a smell you either grow up with or never get used to.

The houses are warm. This matters more than anything else about them. Outside is cold-wet wind for ten months of the year, and inside is a kind of overheated den, and the transition between the two states structures the day. You come in, you shed the outer oilskin in a porch-vestibule built for that purpose, you hang it to drip, you change your wool, you step into the inner room, and the warmth hits you like an embrace. There is almost always tea. There is almost always something simmering — fish stew, kelp-and-root pottage, a thick chowder built from whatever the day brought in.

The houses are decorated, but sparely. A few framed photographs (they have photography, of the older wet-plate kind, practiced by two or three specialists who travel between settlements). A shelf of books — they are literate, universally, and they trade for books downcountry as eagerly as they trade for machine parts. A wall of hung tools, each in its place. The knitwork I mentioned — caps, but also blankets, shawls, sweaters — in the family pattern, often hung as wall pieces when they grow too worn to wear.

## Disposition

They are quiet. Not unfriendly — a stranger arriving at a settlement will be fed, dried, given a bed, asked careful and intelligent questions about the world outside — but quiet in the way of people who have always lived sparsely and whose work demands attention to small signals. They listen. They listen to each other and to the water and to the engines, and they do not fill silence with chatter the way denser populations do.

They have a dry humor that takes some time to register. It runs to understatement and to a kind of fond, weather-beaten irony about their own circumstances. A man whose boat has just been swamped and who has lost a half-day's catch will, on being pulled out, say something like "well, that's the fish back where they wanted to be," and his rescuers will laugh, and it will be — actually — funny.

They are unsentimental about death and grief in a way that can read as cold to outsiders but is in fact a deep practical tenderness. People drown here. People are caught by squalls and lost. Children sometimes do not come up from a dive. The community absorbs these losses without breaking, holds a brief direct ceremony (the body, if recovered, is committed back to the water in a weighted shroud at a chosen deep place; if not recovered, a stone is set on the family's crown of their home-island and the dead person's cap is hung above the door for a year), and resumes work. The grief is private and long. The public face of it is brief and composed. Outsiders sometimes think they don't feel it. They feel it like everyone else; they have just decided, collectively and across generations, that the work does not stop.

They are religious in a quiet, unprogrammatic way. There is no church. There is no clergy. But there are practices — the small offering of the first fish of a season returned to the water, the song sung when entering a new basin for the first time in a year, the unspoken rule that you do not speak ill of the wind while it is blowing — that everyone observes and no one explains. Pressed, an old woman would tell you that the country itself is alive in some sense that doesn't quite match any word the outsider has brought with them, and that the proper relation to it is one of attention and respect and a kind of working partnership. She would not insist on this. She would tell you this and then offer you more tea.

## A Last Note

What strikes you, if you stay long enough to be allowed to see it, is how *settled* they are. Not in the sense of being stuck — they move constantly, the work demands it, the boats are always going somewhere. But settled in their relation to the place. They are not trying to leave. They are not trying to expand. They are not trying to extract some final value from the country and move on. They have arrived at an arrangement with the drowned cordillera, an arrangement worked out over generations of trial and loss and patient adjustment, and they intend to maintain it.

They have, in a sense, done what the mountains did. The mountains finished becoming, a hundred million years ago, and then settled into being the most active version of their final shape. The people, over their much shorter span, have done the same thing. They have stopped becoming anything new. They are, at this moment, in motion — fishing, mending, tending, listening, oiling their hair and their boots, knitting in the family pattern, watching the squall line cross two valleys over — within a form that is not going to change.

They are the country's last inhabitants and they know it. There is no one coming after them, because there is nothing left to become here. This does not trouble them. They have tea on. The turbine is running. The kelp is up. The boat is loaded. The wind is from the northwest and a little colder than yesterday, which means the southern weirs will pull well tomorrow.

It is enough. It has been enough for a long time. It will be enough for as long as they are here to tend it.
