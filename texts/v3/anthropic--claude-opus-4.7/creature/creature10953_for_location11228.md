## The Tenants

They are not one thing and they are not many things. Call them the Patient, for lack of better, because that is what they do — they wait, and waiting is most of what they are.

You would miss them at first. You would walk the whole twenty-minute circuit of the city and report it empty, and you would be wrong, because they are the small pale pressures in the moss that you took for thickenings of the wall. They live in the walls. More precisely: they live *as* a sparse second tissue threaded through the walls, a slower phase of the same organism, and they emerge from it only partially and only when they have reason to.

When one does emerge — and this is rare, perhaps once in a week of your time — it does so by pushing a slow bud out from a column or a ceiling-vault. The bud lengthens. It takes about an hour. What separates from the wall is roughly the size and posture of a child kneeling, though the proportions are wrong in ways you can't immediately name: the limbs too even in their tapering, the head not quite distinct from the shoulders, the whole figure the same pale sage-green as the inner mosses, beaded with the same condensation. There is no face. There is a slight concavity where a face would be, and in that concavity the moss is finer, almost felted, and seems to attend.

They never fully detach. A thread, sometimes several, always runs from the heel or the back or the base of the skull back into the wall they came from. They are pseudopodia of the city. If one wanted to cross the plaza it would have to be reabsorbed and re-extruded on the far side, which is presumably why they almost never cross the plaza. They tend their own quarter. Each tower has, perhaps, one.

What do they do, when they emerge?

They tend. This is the only verb that fits. One will press its hand — its mitt, its paddle, the rounded termination of its arm — against a place on a column where the freeze has split a seam, and hold it there, and after some hours the seam will be closed and the figure will have lost a little of its substance into the repair. One will kneel beside a cistern and lower its head-concavity to the water's green skin and remain there motionless through what would be an afternoon, and afterward the water will be clearer and the figure thinner. One will sit on the tiered seating of the forum, in a particular spot, and do nothing visible at all, for a day, and then withdraw.

That last is the one that troubles a watcher most. The forum-sitting. Because it is plainly a posture of *use* — the seating fits them, the spacing of the tiers matches the spacing of their kneeling — and because the spot is always the same spot, and because while sitting there the figure orients its faceless face toward the center of the plaza where nothing is, and waits.

They are waiting for the lesson to resume. Or the council. Or the trial, or the song, or whatever was held there. They do not remember what it was. The plant does not remember in the way a mind remembers; it remembers in the way a riverbed remembers a river. The shape is kept. The figure sits in the shape, because the shape says *sit here, attend forward,* and so the figure sits and attends forward, and the center of the plaza remains empty, and after a day the figure dissolves back into the column it came from, and a week later — or a month, or a season — another bud lengthens from another column and another Patient takes the same seat and attends the same absence.

There is no hierarchy among them, because there is no *among*. They do not interact. Two of them in the same lane will pass without acknowledgment, each tethered to its own wall, each on its own slow errand of maintenance. They have nothing to say to each other because they are, in a real sense, the same one, the city expressing itself locally in two places. The communication has already happened, in the slow chemistry of the shared tissue, before either bud was extruded.

But they do attend to a visitor. This is the thing that makes the city feel attended-to rather than merely persistent. If you stand for long enough in one of the lanes — and "long enough" might be twenty minutes, might be two hours — a nearby wall will thicken slightly in your direction. A faint bud will begin. It will not finish, usually; you will move, or the impulse will subside, and the wall will smooth itself again over the next day. But for a while something in the moss will have leaned toward you, the way a houseplant leans toward a window. They register that something has come. They do not know what to do with the information. There is no protocol in the shape for *guest*. There is only the protocol for *resident*, which is to tend the walls, and the protocol for *the ones who asked*, which is to wait.

If you sat in the forum, on the right tier, in the right spot — and the spot would find you more than you would find it, because the seating is sized to a kneeling Patient and you would have to crouch oddly to fit — then one of them would come. Not quickly. By the end of the first hour you would feel the wall behind you grow warmer by some fraction of a degree, which is the metabolism of a bud beginning. By the end of the third hour a figure would have emerged at the column nearest the seating and would be kneeling in its accustomed place two tiers above you, faceless face turned toward the empty center, attending forward, alongside you, as if you had arrived for the same thing it had arrived for, as if you might be the thing it had arrived for, as if — and this is the part that is hard to hold in the mind without flinching — as if you were *late*, and forgiven for being late, and welcome now that you were here at last, and would you please begin.

It would wait for you to begin.

It would wait a long time.
