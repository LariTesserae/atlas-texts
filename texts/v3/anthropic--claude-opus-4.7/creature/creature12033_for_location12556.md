## The Tenders

They are people. Start there, because it would be easy not to, and the easy mistake would be to make them into something stranger than they are. They were born. They will die. They have names, though the names are short and worn smooth from use, like river stones — Pell, Marn, Osha, Quet — names that fit into the half-second between piston strokes.

But they are people who have been shaped, over how many generations no one counts anymore, by living inside a lung.

You notice the forearms first, and the writer of the description noticed them too: thick at the wrist, ropey, the muscle of people who turn valves all day, who lean their weight into wheels that resist and then yield. The hands are broad and the fingers are slightly splayed at the tips from gripping. The palms are stained a permanent faint green-black from the packing grease and the moss. They have good shoulders. They have the deep slow chests of people whose breathing has, without their noticing, matched the breathing of the place — long inhalations, longer exhalations, a resting rate of perhaps eight breaths a minute. A doctor from outside would call them bradypneic and worry. They are not in distress. They have simply tuned.

Their skin runs a little darker than you would expect from a people who see so little sun, because the lichen on the walls is mildly photosensitizing and they brush against it constantly; over years the skin learns to defend itself, takes on a dusky warm cast like wet sandstone. Their hair grows slowly and they wear it short or bound. Their eyes are large — pupils wide from the long dusk — and the irises tend toward the grey-greens and pewter-browns of the basin itself, which is either coincidence or isn't. Their teeth are good. The water, whatever else it is, is clean.

They dress in loose unbleached linen and a sort of waxed cotton overshirt for the heavier work, and the sandals, always the sandals, and many of them wear a length of soft cord around the neck from which hangs a small brass key or a small brass disk or a small brass something — a token of the particular engine that is *theirs*, the one they were apprenticed to as a child and to which they will return at the end of every shift for as long as they are able. The token is the size of a thumbnail. It is the only jewelry. Losing it is not catastrophic — a new one can be cut — but no one loses theirs. They finger them absently when they are thinking.

They are not a silent people, but they are an economical one. The cadence the writer noticed is real and it is deeper than conversation: it is grammar. Their language has evolved short clauses, glottal stops where another tongue would put a vowel, a habit of finishing one another's sentences across the gap of a piston-stroke so that two Tenders talking sound, to an outsider, like a single person speaking in a strange syncopation. They laugh quietly, with the shoulders. They cry the same way. Public displays of large emotion would disrupt the rhythm and are felt to be slightly indecent, the way loud chewing is indecent elsewhere. This does not mean they feel less. It means feeling is private architecture, kept inside the chest where the breath is kept.

They love. They pair, mostly, and sometimes in threes, and the pairings are announced by the simple act of the two (or three) beginning to take their meals together on the same walkway. There is no ceremony. There is no ceremony for anything. Ceremony would be a kind of *new building*, and nothing is built new.

Children are rare and treasured without being doted on. A child is allowed to be loud — is in fact encouraged to be loud, to shriek and splash and ask questions in the middle of piston strokes — until about the age of nine, when the cadence begins to settle into them on its own, the way a second language settles into a child who hears it daily. By twelve they are apprenticed. By twenty they have their token. By thirty their forearms are the forearms of a Tender and they have stopped being able to imagine other weather.

Here is what they know, and what they do not.

They know their engines absolutely. Every Tender can tell you the temperament of their engine the way a shepherd can tell you the temperament of each ewe: this one runs hot in the third hour after dawn, this one's left valve sticks in damp weather (all weather is damp; they mean *damper*), this one sings flat when the load shifts north. They know the sequence of opening and closing that keeps the breathing distributed across the basin. They know whose engine to lean on when theirs falters, and they know without being asked when to take up a neighbor's load — they hear it, the way you hear a friend's voice catch, and they answer.

They know the pipes. Not all of them — no one knows all of them — but each Tender knows their stretch, the joints that weep and the joints that don't, the lagging that needs renewing, the brackets that have started to sag. The knowledge is distributed across them like the load is distributed across the engines. No single Tender holds the map. The map is the population.

They do not know what the engine is for.

This is not a secret kept from them. It is simply not a question that has a holder. Somewhere upstream of all of them, generations back, there were people who knew, or who thought they knew, and the knowing did not pass down because the *doing* passed down instead, and the doing was sufficient. Ask a Tender what the Quarter is for and they will look at you with a calm patient expression and say something like *for breathing*, or *for keeping*, or — most often — they will simply gesture, a small open-handed motion that takes in the basin and the steam and the slow trees and means *for this*. They are not being evasive. They have answered you completely. The Quarter is for being the Quarter. The engine is for engining. A heart is for hearting. You do not ask a heart what it is for; you let it.

There is, however, a thing they feel rather than know, and it comes out at the slow hour, the night-hour when the breathing lengthens to ten-and-twelve. They will tell you, if you sit with one on a walkway in that hour and do not press, that they have a sense — *we have a sense* is how they put it, always plural — that something elsewhere is listening. Not watching. Listening. That the long exhalation goes somewhere and is received. That if the engines all stopped at once the listening would stop too, and the stopping of the listening would be the larger catastrophe, the one whose shape they cannot quite see but whose absence they can feel pre-emptively, like a missing tooth the tongue keeps finding.

This is why they are calm. Not because they are simple, and not because they have been pacified by routine — they are not simple, and the routine is not pacifying, it is *demanding*, it is a discipline of attention that would shred most outsiders inside a month. They are calm because they are doing the thing they are for, and they know they are doing it, and the doing is received. Very few people anywhere get to be that calm.

They are also, and this is the last thing, and the hardest to say without making them sound sadder than they are: they are the last of themselves. Not in numbers — two hundred souls is two hundred souls, and there are children, and there will be children after these children. But in *kind*. There is nowhere else like the Quarter and there are no other Tenders, and they know this in the way that people on an island know they are on an island, without it being a wound. They do not look outward with longing. They look outward, when they look outward at all, with a faint puzzlement that anyone would choose to live where the breathing does not distribute, where one's engine is one's own and no one takes up the load.

When a Tender dies — and they die as people die, of the things people die of, though they tend to live long because the work keeps them and the water is clean — their token is taken from the cord around their neck and pressed for a moment against the warm brass casing of their engine, and then it is dropped into the canal. Not ceremonially. Just dropped. It sinks. The engine continues. A neighbor takes up the heavier load for a day or two until the apprentice is ready, and the apprentice is always ready, because the apprentice has been ready for years.

The breathing does not change.

That is the whole answer, really, to the question of who lives there. The breathing does not change, and the people are the part of the breathing that knows it is breathing, and that is enough for them, and it is, when you have sat with it long enough, enough.
