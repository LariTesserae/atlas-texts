# The Tenders

They are not, at first, anything you would call alive. You would walk past one a dozen times and call it a fitting, a junction, a place where two pipes meet and a little brass cap has been screwed down over the seam. That is, in fact, what most of them look like. The hollow has, by rough count, somewhere between sixty and ninety of them, though no one has ever finished counting because they move — slowly, slowly — between the engines, and the count drifts.

Picture a thing the size of a closed fist, or sometimes a child's two cupped hands. Brass mostly, but with pewter at the joints and, here and there, a little stub of horn or bone-button worked into the casing where some long-ago hand made a repair with what was lying about. They have no face. They have, instead, a *front* — a side that tends to be aimed at whatever they are attending to, marked by a small grille like the speaker of an old wireless, behind which something dim and wet seems to flex when they're working. They have legs of a sort: three or four jointed wires per side, fine as knitting needles, which they fold under themselves when at rest so that they look exactly like a pipe fitting that someone left on a ledge. When they move, they move like a slow harvestman — one leg, then another, with long pauses between, as though each step required a small private decision.

What they do is tend the engines. Not repair, exactly. They *adjust*. A tender will spend half a morning beside one of the squat brass barrels, doing nothing visible, and then will reach out with one wire-leg and turn a valve by perhaps a sixteenth. Then it will fold itself back down and wait. Hours later it will turn the valve back. The engines, you come to understand, are not running on their own. They are being kept running, valve by valve, by these patient little fitters, who have been doing it for so long that the work is indistinguishable from the work doing them.

They drink. This is the closest they come to anything you'd recognize as a need. At the lowest point of certain pipe-catenaries, where condensation drools, a tender will sometimes be found beneath the drip, very still, the grille on its front pointed up. The water disappears into it without sound. Whether it fuels them or simply passes through them on its way to somewhere else, you couldn't say. Possibly they themselves are part of the circulation — possibly each tender is just another loop of the system, water entering here, leaving there, the small brass body merely the shape the flow takes when it pauses to think.

They are aware of you. This becomes clear if you sit still long enough. The grille-fronts orient. Not all at once and not obviously — over the course of an hour, you'll realize that more of them are facing you than were before. They do not approach. They do not retreat. They register, and incorporate, and adjust, the way they adjust the valves. A visitor is a new variable in the hydraulics of the hollow. The tenders fold this variable in.

If you put your hand down flat on the clay and wait — really wait, past the point where waiting feels purposeful — one will sometimes come. It will take a quarter of an hour to cross a distance you could cross in two steps. It will stop just short of your fingers. The grille will be aimed at your palm. And then, if you are very still, you will feel against your skin a small warm exhalation, like breath but drier, scented faintly of hot brass and pondwater. That is as close as they come to greeting. After a while it will turn and walk, with the same painstaking deliberation, back to whatever valve it had been minding, and you will understand that you have been measured and accounted for, and that you are now, in some small way, part of what the hollow is balancing.

There is a hierarchy of a kind, though hierarchy is the wrong word — there is no command, only specialization. Most tenders work the valves. A smaller number, perhaps a dozen, are *seam-watchers*: they live on the pipes themselves, riding the long copper runs, and where a rivet weeps too freely they settle down over it and stay, sometimes for years, their bodies slowly accreting a green skin of verdigris until they are indistinguishable from the pipe except by a faint warmth. A still smaller number, maybe three or four, are larger — closer to the size of a loaf — and these are almost never seen. They live inside the engines. You can sometimes hear one shift its weight when an engine gives its wet cough; there is a soft internal *clack* like a domino being set down, and then the vapor goes up. What these larger ones do is not known. The smaller tenders seem to defer to them, in the sense that valves are sometimes turned in response to a sound from within an engine that the smaller tender could not have made.

And then there is, perhaps, one more. The hollow's people, when they speak of the place at all, will sometimes mention *the keeper* — singular, definite — but no one has ever produced it. The brass plate in the pump-house is said to be its dedication, though of course the dedication is unreadable. The theory among those who come back, and come back, and come back, is that the keeper is the hollow itself: that all the tenders together, and all the engines, and the pipework and the pools and the slow shuffling of water from one ghost-ring of clay to another, *constitute* a single slow mind, and that this mind is engaged in a thought so long and so distributed that no single moment of its thinking is legible, but that the thought, in aggregate, is what keeps the place from settling, and what calls people back.

If so, the tenders are its hands. Or its nerves. Or — likeliest — they are the small repeating syllables of a sentence that has been going on for a very long time, and that has not yet, and may not ever, come to its full stop.

What they want, if they want anything, is to continue. Not to finish; finishing would be a kind of death to them, the way silence would be a death to a held note. They want the valves to keep needing turning. They want the pipes to keep weeping at their low points so that there is always something to drink. They want the vapor to keep going up, and the pools to keep redrawing themselves, and the visitors to keep coming down the worn groove of the path and sitting on the clay and being, briefly, folded into the count.

And when you leave — because people do leave — one of them will, almost certainly, be watching you go. Not with regret. Tenders do not have regret, any more than a valve has regret. But with the small attentive patience of something that has noted your direction, and your speed, and the rate at which you displace the mist, and has already begun the long slow calculation of when, and by what path, you will come back.
