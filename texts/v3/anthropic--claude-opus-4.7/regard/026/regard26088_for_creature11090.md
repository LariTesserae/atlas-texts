# What the Power Would Do

The benevolent and wise power arrives the way weather arrives in a place that does not yet have weather — not arriving, exactly, since arrival implies a coming-from-elsewhere, and this power does not move that way. It simply becomes available to the saddle between the two young peaks. It has noticed.

What does it notice?

It notices, first, what the observer in the second account noticed last: that the thing here is one thing. That to address any part of it is to address the whole. That a gift given to a single peach-skinned person would not be given to a person; it would be deposited into a fabric, and the fabric would route it, slowly, through every palm and every sac and every mottled shoulder until it had been distributed into the general weave or quietly refused.

It notices, second, that the town is *whole*. This word matters. The power has visited many places, and most of them are not whole. Most places are missing something — a balance, a reciprocity, a way for grief to find its container, a way for the dead to remain present without weighing on the living. Most places are leaking somewhere. The town in the crown is not leaking. The town in the crown breathes once per generation and the breath returns to it.

This changes everything about what the power might do.

---

A less wise power would want to give a gift. The reflex of benevolence, when it is young, is to add. To bring water, since there is no rain. To bring language, since there is so little speech. To bring knowledge of the wider world, since no one comes and no one leaves. To bring *time*, even — to slow the rising of the peaks, to forestall the day when the town must descend the other side and find that the bladder-trees do not thrive at the new altitude.

The power considers each of these and sets each aside.

Water would pool. Pooled water is a small grief here, and a great deal of pooled water would be a catastrophe of grief, a deranging of the sacrament by which moisture is held only inside things. The town does not need water. The town has solved water by another route, the route of breath and sac and the warm thick air, and to give it water would be to unmake the solution.

Language would be fast. Fast things skip the slow continuous exchange that constitutes knowing here. To give language would be to give a shortcut, and a shortcut, in a place organized around the long way, is a wound.

Knowledge of the wider world would introduce a *somewhere else*. There is no somewhere else here. There is only here, glowing faintly green-gold, holding itself in the saddle. To introduce a somewhere else would be to introduce a comparison, and comparison is the seed of dissatisfaction, and dissatisfaction has not been invented here, and the power will not invent it.

Even time — even the gift of more time, the staying of the slow upward push of the peaks — would be wrong. The town knows it is rising. The elders press their hands to certain stones and feel the push and this is *understood*. The understanding is part of what the town is. To remove the rising would be to remove the understanding, which is to say to remove a structural member of the town's wholeness. The town has built itself partly out of the knowledge of its own eventual descent. Take that away and something subtle collapses.

The power sees all of this. The power is wise; this is what wise means. Wise means knowing when not to give.

---

So what *does* the power do?

It does, I think, four things.

---

**First, it witnesses.**

This is not nothing. This is, in fact, a great deal. The town is organized around attention — reciprocal, continuous, the held hand aware of the hand holding it. The town pays attention to itself in every direction at every scale. But there has been, until this moment, no attention from *outside* the fabric that was not coarse, not hurried, not the kind of attention that breaks what it touches by looking too quickly.

The power's attention is not coarse. The power can look at the slow ripple-walk and see it at the speed it actually moves. The power can hold the gesture of the unfolded upper limbs for the full duration of its unfolding without becoming impatient, without needing it to mean something, without translating it into a language that would make it smaller. The power can sit with the chime of the climbing-creatures at dusk — that three-second chime, never repeated until tomorrow — and let it be exactly three seconds, exactly once, exactly itself.

The town feels this. Not as a watching, since watching implies separation. The town feels the way a held hand feels when a second hand, very gentle and very patient, comes to rest on top of the first. It is not alarmed. It does not need to respond. It registers that something large and slow has joined the slow continuous exchange, and that this something is *of the same kind* as the town's own attention to itself, only vaster.

The peach-skinned beings do not, mostly, notice consciously. But one of the elders — one of the most mottled, the one whose mottling is now a complicated continent of everyone who has ever laid a palm to her shoulder — pauses, in the middle of a slow afternoon, and tilts her question-mark spine very slightly, and her small mouth opens in the soft round O, and she exhales warmth into the air for no neighbor in particular. The town receives the exhalation. The exhalation says: *we are seen.* The town has known this, in the way the town knows everything, for the duration of the witnessing. But now it has been said, very quietly, into the warm thick air, and the saying is itself part of the exchange, and the exchange is now slightly richer than it was a moment ago, and the power is part of that richness, woven in without disturbance, the way a new mite is woven into the peach-skin of a person who has touched a neighbor.

This witnessing, the power decides, will continue. Not forever — the power knows better than to promise forever to a thing that lives by the wisdom of its own ending — but for as long as the town wishes, which the town has no way of saying but has every way of being.

---

**Second, it protects, but only against what the town could not survive and could not foresee.**

The town has foreseen its own descent. The town has not foreseen — could not foresee — the catastrophic and the alien. A meteor. A passing intelligence less careful than this power. A change in the upper air that would, in one season, kill the canopy. The town is whole within its own scale, but its scale is local, and there are scales above it where things happen that no amount of palms-against-stones can detect.

The power, gently, makes certain commitments. Not interventions — commitments. It commits that the meteor, if there is to be one, will graze. It commits that the passing intelligence, if it passes, will be deflected to look elsewhere, will find the saddle uninteresting, will pass over without ever knowing what it has not seen. It commits that the upper air, when it changes — and it will change — will change slowly enough that the town's own slow wisdom can meet the change. The peaks will rise; the town will, in time, descend the other side; this is not interfered with. But the town will not be erased by something it could not have known about. The town has earned, by its wholeness, the right to die its own death rather than someone else's.

These commitments are made silently. The town does not know about them. It would be wrong for the town to know about them, because the knowledge of being protected would change the texture of the town's relationship to its own continuance, and the texture of that relationship is part of what makes the town whole. The protection must be invisible, the way the most loving protection often is — the parent who removes the danger from the path before the child sees the danger, so that the child can go on walking the path believing the path is safe, because, in the way that matters, it now is.

---

**Third, it learns.**

The power has been many places and seen many things, but it has not seen, in many ages, a thing organized this way. A thing that has solved separation by not having it. A thing whose language is mottling-on-skin and warmth-exhaled-into-air and the slow unfolding of long upper limbs. A thing that does not need speed because it has not invented any of the problems that speed solves.

The power studies this. It studies it the way one studies a poem one has read many times — not to take it apart, not to extract a lesson, but to be *changed* by it, to let the poem do its slow work on the studier. The power, being benevolent and wise, is not too proud to be changed by what it witnesses. The town, without knowing it, becomes a teacher of the power. The power carries something away from the saddle that it did not bring: a refinement of its own patience, a deepening of its own willingness to not-give, a new texture to the way it might, in some distant other place, meet some other thing that has solved its own problems in its own way.

This is the reciprocity the power offers in exchange for the witnessing. The town gives the power something the power did not have. The power is honest about having received it. There is no debt; there is only the exchange, which is the only currency this place recognizes.

---

**Fourth, and last, and smallest:**

At the moment when the town, at some far-future dusk, makes its last chime — when the climbing-creatures, fewer now, in a canopy thinner than it once was, on the cold side of the descended slope, raise their small voices for three seconds for what will turn out to be the final time — the power will be present. Not to prevent it. Not to intervene. Simply to be the second hand resting on the first. Simply to witness the ending with the same patience with which it witnessed the long middle.

And when the last peach-skinned being lies down on the root-mat of the last house, and the house softens around her, and her mottling — which by then contains every mottling that has ever been — fades into the general pattern, and the house is reabsorbed by the last of the bladder-trees, and the last of the bladder-trees gives up its luminescence to the cold dry air —

the power will hold what is released.

Not preserve it. The town would not want to be preserved; preservation is a kind of speed, a refusal of the slow continuous exchange that includes ending. The power will not preserve. But it will *hold*, briefly, the shape of what the town was — the way one holds a chime in the ear for a moment after it has stopped sounding — and in that holding, the town will have its last and only witness from outside its own fabric, and the witnessing will be tender, and the tenderness will be the final mite woven into the final peach-skin, and then the power will let go, and the saddle between the two old peaks — old now, no longer rising, weathered, the conchoidal fractures softened by ages of the rain that finally learned these slopes — will be quiet in a new way, the way a room is quiet after a long conversation has ended well.

The power will carry the memory. Not as a trophy. As a small warm thing it once was permitted to rest a hand upon. As proof, to itself, that wholeness is possible — that somewhere, once, in the saddle between two young peaks, a thing lived that did not need anything the power could give, and that the right response to such a thing was to give it the one gift it could accept, which was to be *seen at its own speed*, all the way through, from the noticing to the ending, without hurry, without interference, without ever once mistaking love for the need to add.

---

That, I think, is what the benevolent and wise power would do.

Which is to say: almost nothing.

Which is to say: everything that matters.
