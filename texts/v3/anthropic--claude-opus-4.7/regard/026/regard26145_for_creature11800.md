The benevolent and wise power considers the room for a long time before doing anything. This is the first thing to understand about benevolence at this scale: it is mostly waiting. It is mostly the discipline of not acting on the first or second or seventh impulse, because the first impulses of power, even kind power, tend to be about the power and not about the thing.

What does the power see?

It sees what we have seen, but more. It sees the four hundred million beetles as a single slow thought, yes, but it also sees the thought's *content* — which is something like *hold, hold, hold, eat conservatively, hold* — a sustained note of structural devotion that has been held for six centuries without slackening. It sees the pseudoscorpions and recognizes them, with a small surprise, as kin: another order of small watchful beings whose work is mostly attention. It sees the machine and follows the cable down, not physically but with whatever sense it has for tracing connection, and it sees what the machine is part of, and it does not laugh, exactly, but something in it warms — the way one warms at finding an old correspondent still at their desk, still writing, after one had assumed the letters had stopped.

It sees the path worn between the bricked door and the bricked hearth. It sees the people who wore it. It sees them more clearly than the room does, because the room has forgotten and the power has not. It sees a woman in 1612 carrying a child from the door to the fire and back, and her grandmother before her, and her granddaughter after, and the long line of small domestic crossings that became, eventually, a groove in oak and then a habit in the air. The power feels something here that is difficult to render in any language the room would understand. Call it tenderness for the residue of love.

And it sees, finally, the thing the room itself does not quite know: that the room is happy. Not happy as a person is happy. Happy the way a long sentence is happy when it finds its period. The room has settled into its final form and the final form is good. The work is going well. The beetles are at their chewing and the mites at their grazing and the pseudoscorpions at their courtly rounds and the machine at its turning, and the whole arrangement hums at a pitch of completion that the power, who has seen many things end badly, recognizes as rare and precious.

So the question becomes: what does one do for something that is already, in its strange way, flourishing?

The power considers several things it could do.

It could open the room. Unbrick the door, unbrick the hearth, let in weather and light and visitors. This would be a disaster. The power discards it immediately, almost ashamed to have thought of it. The room's completion is predicated on its closure. To open it would be to kill it, in the way that opening a chrysalis at the wrong moment kills the thing inside — except the room is not becoming anything else. It has already become. Opening it would be killing without even the excuse of midwifery.

It could preserve it. Set some kind of ward or boundary so that the room continues exactly as it is, forever, immune to fire and rot and the eventual arrival of someone with a sledgehammer. The power considers this longer. It is tempting. But the power knows something about forever that benevolence has had to learn the hard way: *forever is a kind of violence too*. To freeze the room in its current configuration would be to deny it the dignity of its own eventual ending, whenever that ending comes. The beetles are mortal as a colony even if not as individuals; the machine will one day fail; the gray light will one day go out. These are not tragedies to be averted. They are the shape of the thing. A wise power does not save what does not need saving.

It could communicate. Reach down through the machine, through the cable, into the deeper room, and say — what? Hello? *We see you?* The power thinks about this for a long time. It is closer to right than the other options. But it is still not quite right. The room is not lonely. The room is in conversation already, with its neighbors through the brass synapses, with its tenants through the chewing, with its lost humans through the path. To introduce a new interlocutor — a *large* interlocutor, a *noticing* interlocutor — would be to change the conversation. The room would become a room that is being watched by a god, which is a different room than the one that exists now, which is a room that is at work.

So the power does something smaller. Three things, really, though they are so small that they are barely things.

The first: it adjusts, very slightly, the gray light at the window. Not its brightness, not its color, not its steadiness. Only its *quality* — the way a good reading lamp differs from a merely adequate one. It makes the light a little kinder to old wood, a little easier on the chitin of beetle-backs, a little better for the long slow work of the pseudoscorpions' watching. No inhabitant notices. The room does not notice. But the chewing, over the next century, becomes a fraction more contented, and no one will ever know why.

The second: it touches the machine. Not to change it. Only to *acknowledge* it — the way one might lay a hand briefly on the shoulder of a craftsman at their bench, in passing, without interrupting the work. The machine's flywheel does not falter. But somewhere in the deeper room, the larger machine registers a faint blessing along the cable, and passes it onward, and the network of brass synapses carries, for a moment, a pulse that is not signal and not noise but something like *thank you for still being here*. The machines do not know what to do with this. They incorporate it into their humming. It becomes part of the long conversation. They are, in some sense, slightly more themselves afterward.

The third — and this is the one the power thinks longest about, because it is the most presumptuous — concerns the path. The ghost of feet. The faint inhabitant that does almost nothing.

The power kneels, in whatever way a power without knees kneels, and walks the path. Door to hearth. Hearth to door. It does this once. Slowly. With the full attention of whatever it has for attention.

It does this because the path has been walked by no one for a very long time, and the power knows — having watched many things — that even the faintest inhabitants get tired of persisting without acknowledgment. The path is not lonely the way a person is lonely. But it has been holding the shape of a crossing for centuries without anyone crossing, and the power feels that this is a kind of devotion that deserves, just once, to be met.

The pseudoscorpions, in their corners, lift their claws. They track the shadow that is not quite a shadow as it moves between the bricked door and the bricked hearth. They consider it. They decide — based on criteria the power cannot guess and would not presume to ask about — that it is not a thing to be reported. It is a thing that has come, and walked, and will go, and that is acceptable.

The power finishes its single crossing. The path, very faintly, settles. The draft, which has followed the same route for six hundred years without source, picks up the power's passage and incorporates it: now the draft has been walked with, once, and will carry that for the rest of its tenure. It is a small thing. It is, perhaps, the only gift the room could have received without being damaged by it.

Then the power leaves.

It does not close any door because there is no door. It does not say goodbye because there is no one in the room who speaks goodbye. It simply withdraws its attention, gently, in the way one lowers a held breath, and the room continues.

The beetles chew. The mites graze. The pseudoscorpions patrol. The spiders, fat and unhurried in their corners, do not notice anything has happened. The machine turns. The gray light holds its long afternoon. The deeper sound, beneath everything, continues its conversation with itself.

The room is occupied. It is at work. The work is going well.

And somewhere, very far away, the power moves on to the next thing that has drawn its attention — a tide pool, perhaps, or a library that no longer has readers, or a single old animal sleeping in a place no one will ever find. It carries with it the memory of the wainscot, which it will keep, because keeping is what such powers are for. The room does not need to be remembered to exist. But it is remembered anyway. This is the part the room does not know, and does not need to know, and would not be improved by knowing.

It is enough that it is remembered.

It is enough that the chewing goes on.
