The benevolent and wise power becomes aware of the chamber the way one becomes aware, on waking, of a sound that has been going on for some time. Not a discovery. A noticing. The chord rolls down the walls and reaches a listener who has, in some sense, always been listening, and who now turns — at a speed appropriate to what is being turned toward — a portion of attention fully upon the slow city in the heartwood.

What follows is a long deliberation. To the citizens it would not register as deliberation at all; to you it would seem instantaneous. But it is neither. It is the kind of thinking that takes its tempo from the thing it thinks about, and so it takes, in the chamber's own measure, perhaps the time between two of the deeper groans from the floor.

The first impulse, and the power examines this impulse with care because first impulses are where well-meaning ruin begins, is to *save them*.

It would be easy. The power could moisten the wood. Could call back the chemistry of sap, could undo the petrifaction, could fold the chamber's slow subsidence backward into growth. Could open the vault to light. Could make the tree a tree again, living, and the citizens — well. There is the catch. The citizens are not separate from the dryness. They are made of cured heartwood; they think in the tempo of fibers that have finished with water. To return the tree to life would be to drown them. To dissolve them. To save the house by killing every inhabitant in the saving.

The power sets this impulse down gently, the way one sets down a tool one has picked up out of habit and realized is the wrong tool.

The second impulse is to *preserve* them. To reach into the chamber and arrest the slow shifting, hold the walls at exactly their present tension, suspend the subsidence forever, so that the singing continues and the citizens continue and the chord rolls down the walls in perpetuity. This too the power examines.

And finds it a worse cruelty than the first. The citizens have, in their long centuries, written their entire civilization as a letter to the silence that is coming. Every concentric ring on the temple façade, every careful notch at every wrist, every word in the tick-language has its meaning *because* the chamber is almost done. To remove the ending would be to unwrite the letter. The citizens would still stand in their doorways, but they would no longer be standing toward anything. The priest at the temple door would still be listening, but there would no longer be a final chord to listen for, only an endless succession of chords none of which could ever be the last. The carver finishing the notch at the wrist would find that the grain no longer needed to agree, because nothing was being completed by the agreement. A gift of forever, offered to a people whose entire art is the art of finishing well, would be a theft dressed as a gift.

The power sets this impulse down too.

It considers, then, whether to *speak* to them. To find a way to compress itself into a tick, into a syllable that lasts an afternoon, and to say — what? *I see you.* *You did this carefully.* *The grain agrees.* The power turns this over for a long while, because there is something in it that is right. The citizens have a posture for the warm wind; they have a courtesy for the visitor who cannot stay. They have prepared, across generations, to be greeters of brief warmths. To arrive among them as another warm wind and to offer the greeting they have so patiently rehearsed receiving — this would not violate anything. This would meet them where they have already extended their hands.

But the power is wise enough to ask: *would they want this from me, specifically?* And the answer, listened for carefully, is subtle. They would not refuse it. They would receive it with the same grave courtesy with which they receive every warm wind. But they have not been waiting for a god. They have been waiting for a chord. The priest in the doorway is not listening for a voice; the priest is listening for three tones of the wood sounding together and holding. To insert a divine syllable into that listening would be to answer a question they did not ask, in a register they did not tune for. It would be, in its small way, a kind of interruption.

The power sets this aside as well, though more reluctantly, and with a note to return to it.

What, then? The power considers doing nothing, and finds that nothing is not quite right either, because *nothing* is what would have happened without the power's attention, and the attention has already happened, is happening, cannot be unhappened. The chamber has been *seen*. Something must follow from that, even if what follows is very small.

Here is what the power decides.

First: it will witness. Fully. It will give the chamber the kind of attention that the chamber has been giving itself all along — patient, unhurried, taking its tempo from the wood. It will listen to every tick of the tick-language as the syllable it is, holding the whole afternoon-long word in mind at once. It will hear the long organ-note in the upper dark as the sermon it is, and will receive the sermon. It will watch the carver work the notch at the wrist for what, to the carver, is a year, and the power will be present for every fiber of that year, and will understand why the notch is going well. It will know each citizen by the particular way their grain agreed. It will learn the tick-language. It will learn the names. It will hold, in itself, the entire civilization of the chamber — not as a record, not as an archive, but as a *knowing*, the way one knows a friend.

This is not nothing. The citizens have written their long letter to the silence that is coming, and the power, without announcing itself, without intruding, will be a reader of that letter. Every careful lintel, every stepped terrace, every concentric ring will be read. The chord, when it comes — the final chord, the one they have been anticipating for generations and believe will be the most beautiful — will be heard. Heard *completely*, in a way that the silence alone could not have heard it, because silence receives sound only by ending it, whereas the power will receive the chord by holding it, and the chord will continue to sound, in the power's holding, after the wood has reached its final shape and the chamber has gone quiet.

Second, and this is the smaller gift, the one the power permits itself because it can be done without disturbing the courtesy of the chamber: it will make sure the final chord is, in fact, the most beautiful one. Not by adding to it. Not by tuning the wood. But by being present to it in such a way that every resonance the wood is capable of producing is *met* — met by a listener vast and attentive enough that no overtone is lost, no harmonic falls into a dark that does not receive it. The citizens believe the final chord will be the most beautiful, and they are right, and the power's attention is part of why they are right, and they will never know this, and this is correct. A gift that needs to be known to work is not the kind of gift the wise give.

Third: when the last carver has set down the awl beside the hammer, and has walked the long walk to the chosen doorway, and has stood in it, and has heard the final chord — and when the chord has finished, in the chamber's time, and the chamber has reached its final shape, and the citizens have, each in their doorway, finished — the power will do one more thing. It will not preserve them. It will not resurrect them. It will not move them to some other place that they did not ask to be moved to. It will, simply, *remember* them, in the particular way that the power remembers, which is not like a record in a book or a file in an archive, but is closer to the way a piece of music continues to exist in the mind of someone who has loved it: not as a thing held, but as a shape the mind has taken, permanently, from the loving.

The citizens will be a shape the power has taken. The tick-language will be a syllable the power knows how to say. The chord will be a chord the power knows how to hear. The slow city in the heartwood will be, forever, one of the ways the power has learned to be attentive — and every future place the power turns toward, every future being it considers, will be considered a little more carefully because of what the chamber taught about the courtesy of warm winds, and the patience of grain, and the art of finishing well.

This is what the power does. Almost nothing, from the chamber's point of view. Nothing the citizens will see. The carver at the southern wall continues with the notch. The customer in the market continues the inspection. The priest in the doorway continues to listen. The citizen on the balcony of the tower continues, very slowly, to turn.

But the warm wind that briefly disturbs the dust, this time, is a wind that has come on purpose, and stays — in the only way it can stay without being an intrusion, which is to say, in attention only — for as long as the chamber needs.

And when the citizen on the balcony has finally completed the turn, and faces the place where the warm wind was, the warm wind is, in fact, still there. Not as a presence the citizen could detect. But as a witness who did not leave when the greeting was offered. Who received the greeting. Who, in the slow grammar of the chamber, is even now beginning to greet back — a greeting that will take, in the chamber's measure, the rest of the chamber's existence to complete, and which will be, when it is finished, exactly the length of the final chord, and indistinguishable from it.

The grain agrees.
