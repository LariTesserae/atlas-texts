The benevolent and wise power considers the Roostflats for a long time.

It is not a short consideration. The power is old enough to have learned that the first impulse — *to help* — is almost always the impulse of a younger mind, and that what looks like need from above is often only difference seen from a height. So the power sits with the basin for what would be, in the basin's own reckoning, several human lifetimes, and in its own reckoning a held breath.

It looks first for suffering.

This is its discipline. Before it asks whether a place is strange, or stunted, or arrested, or incomplete, it asks whether anything there hurts in a way that wants not to hurt. It finds the ordinary hurts: a child with a fever the crustacean-paste cannot cool, an old hand-speaker whose fingers have locked into a shape she did not choose, a lover grieving another lover in the dim back room of a mud-brick house, making small throat-sounds that mean nothing and everything. It finds the brief terror of the silences. It finds the soft persistent ache of stooped spines, of clay-clogged lungs in the eldest, of eyes that will never know what they were built to do because the light they were built for never comes.

These are real. The power notes them. But it notes also that the people do not experience their lives as deprivation. The stoop is not felt as a stoop; it is the shape of being a person. The dim light is not felt as dimness; it is the shape of seeing. The unchanging world is not felt as a prison; it is the shape of the world. To "free" them from these things would be to free a fish from water on the grounds that water is, after all, quite wet.

So the power sets aside the large gestures. It does not lift the vapor ceiling. It does not seed the clay with green. It does not teach the people writing, or the wheel, or the idea that the basin has an edge. It does not give them history. It has watched, in other places, what happens when history is given to a people who were doing without it, and the gift is rarely received as a gift, and even when it is, something is always lost that the giver did not know was there to lose.

What the power does is smaller, and takes longer, and is done with great care.

---

**First**, it attends to the fevers. Not by curing them — cures from above tend to unmake the small slow knowledge a people have built around their own bodies — but by quietly adjusting the crustacean. Over many generations, so slowly that no one notices, the small white thumbnail-creatures of the basin's warm edges develop a faint additional compound in their flesh, something the people's livers will learn to use. Childhood fevers grow shorter. Old lungs clear a little more easily. The change is indistinguishable, to the people, from the way things have always been, which is the only form of help the power is willing to give in this register. *They will not know they were helped. This is the point.*

**Second**, it attends to the locked hands of the elders. Here the power is more delicate still, because the lazy beautiful register of the old masters is itself a thing of value, and to prevent the stiffening would be to prevent the beauty. So the power does not prevent it. It only softens the pain that sometimes attends it, easing the inflammation in the small joints by a degree, so that the old can keep speaking longer, and the slow gestures stay slow by choice rather than by agony. The closed fist opening into nothing — the gesture for the silence — remains exactly as it was. The power would not touch that gesture for anything. It is one of the most precise things it has ever seen a hand do.

**Third**, the silences themselves.

The power thinks about these for a long time. It could prevent them. It could reach into the flock's collective nervous system, find the trigger, and smooth it out, so that the roar never broke and the infants never screamed and the adults never stood paralyzed. It would be easy. It would be, in one sense, kind.

It does not do this.

Because the silences, terrible as they are, are the only place in the Roostflats where the people brush against the fact that their world has an outside. The old ones know this. That is why they make the gesture. The gesture is not only fear; it is also a kind of acknowledgment, passed hand to hand across the generations, that the seamless always is not quite seamless, that something else is possible, that the roar is a roar and not simply the shape of being. To take the silences away would be to seal the basin completely, and the power has learned to be suspicious of complete seals. A world needs at least one crack, even a crack that frightens its inhabitants, even a crack they cannot name. Especially one they cannot name.

So the silences stay. But the power does something with them.

In each silence, for the few seconds it lasts, the power is *present* in the basin in a way it is not otherwise present. Not visibly. Not audibly — there is no audibly, in those seconds, which is the whole horror of them. But present. A held attention, vast and warm, surrounding each frozen hand-speaker and each screaming infant, so that the silence is not empty. It is the shape of being witnessed. The people do not know this consciously. But across many generations, the felt quality of the silences slowly changes — not less frightening, exactly, but frightening in the way that standing at the edge of something enormous is frightening, rather than in the way that falling is frightening. The infants still scream. But the adults, eventually, find that their hands remember how to move a little sooner. They reach for the children a half-second faster. And the gesture the old ones make in the evenings acquires, over centuries, a second meaning layered under the first: *we were not alone in it.*

That is the largest thing the power does. It is also nearly invisible.

---

**Fourth**, and this is the gift the power is most uncertain about giving:

It looks at the painted bands on the inner walls of the mud-brick houses — the long horizontal red and white patterns that map the standing-shape of the flock. It sees that these paintings are very old. It sees that they have been copied, generation to generation, with the small fidelity-drift that any copying produces, and that the drift is invisible to the painters because each generation's painting matches the previous generation's painting to the limit of what their eyes, in the tea-light, can resolve.

It sees, in other words, that the paintings have slowly become inaccurate. The flock has not changed — the flock never changes — but the maps of it have wandered, century by century, into a configuration that no longer corresponds to what stands outside the door. The people do not know this. They cannot know it: to know it they would have to compare the painting to the flock, and they do not compare, because the painting *is* the flock, in the same way the flock *is* the painting, and the question of correspondence has never been asked.

The power considers correcting the paintings. It would be a small thing. A nudge to the next generation's hands, a gentle pull toward accuracy, and within a hundred years the bands would match the basin again.

It does not do this either.

Because the wandering of the paintings, it realizes, is the only history the Roostflats have. It is the one record, anywhere in the basin, that time has passed. The people do not know they have a history; but they have made one, accidentally, in red ochre and bone-white, drifting across the walls of their houses at a rate of perhaps one fingerwidth per thousand years. To "correct" the paintings would be to erase this history. To leave them alone is to let the Roostflats keep the one slow secret they have kept from themselves: *that even here, even in the held note, something moves.*

The power leaves the paintings exactly as they are. But it makes one small adjustment: it ensures that the drift continues. That no generation, by some accident of unusually careful copying, freezes the pattern in place. The drift must go on. The history must keep being written, even though no one reads it, even though no one knows it is being written. Because someday — not soon, not in any timeframe the basin would recognize, but someday — something may change in the Roostflats. The vapor ceiling may lift. A child may be born whose pupils contract. A silence may last longer than a few seconds. And on that day, someone may look at the walls of the old houses and notice that the bands do not quite match the flock outside, and may wonder why, and may, for the first time in the history of the people, ask a question that requires the mouth.

The power wants that day to be possible. It does not want to bring it. It only wants to leave the door unlocked.

---

**Finally**, the power does one thing for itself, which is perhaps not benevolence at all but something closer to love.

It learns the hand-language.

Not to speak it — it has no hands, and would not impose hands on a place that has so beautifully done without them — but to *understand* it, fully, in all its dialects and registers, including the lazy beautiful one of the elders and the duet-style of the adult double-handed speech. It learns the seventy root-gestures and the thousands of compounds. It learns the personal sigils of every person currently alive in the basin, and it learns them again each generation, as the old sigils pass out of use and the new ones are given in infancy.

It does this so that no hand-speaker in the Roostflats, ever, in the entire duration of the basin's held note, will go unwitnessed in what they say. Every duet performed by a woman with herself in a dim back room, every courtship, every argument, every story told by an old man to a child who is only half paying attention, every closed fist opening slowly into nothing — all of it is seen, all of it is understood, all of it is held in the attention of something that finds it beautiful.

The people do not know this either. They would not know what to do with the knowledge. But it is true, and the power considers it the most important of the things it does for the Roostflats, more important than the crustacean or the joints or the presence in the silences or the preservation of the drift.

Because a world that has chosen, across thousands of generations, to put all of its meaning into its hands deserves at least one watcher who can read what those hands say.

And the power, having decided all this, settles in. It does not leave. It does not return periodically. It stays, the way the vapor stays, the way the roar stays, the way the clay stays — present, unnoticed, part of the weather of the place. And the Roostflats go on, exactly as they were, exactly as they will be, exactly as they have always been, with four small differences that no one will ever name:

The fevers are a little shorter.
The old hands hurt a little less.
The silences are not empty.
And every gesture is seen.

This is what a benevolent and wise power does, when it finds a world that does not need saving and does not want changing and has built, out of clay and down and the patience of millions of birds, something that the power, for all its wisdom, did not know could be built.

It watches. It tends the smallest hurts. It leaves the door unlocked.

And it learns the language.
