# The Visitation

The benevolent power notices the embassy the way one notices a held breath in another room — not by sound but by the shape of the silence around it.

It has been watching for some time. Not surveilling: *attending*, in the embassy's own grammar. It understands the protocol. It understands that the embassy was built by something that did not want to be approached directly, by something else that cannot approach directly, around something that perhaps cannot be approached at all. It understands that the courtesy extended here is structural — that the whole spire is a long sentence in the diplomatic mood, and that to interrupt it with a louder voice would be to mistranslate it permanently.

So the power does not arrive.

This is the first thing it does, and the most important. A lesser benevolence would arrive — would manifest at the highest terrace in some respectful but legible form, would offer help, would ask questions, would seek to understand by closing distance. This power has learned, over a long time, that closing distance is the commonest way of breaking the thing one wished to touch. The embassy exists in a narrow band of conditions: thin air, held heat, slow light, sparse sound, attention as working fluid. Add a fifth presence of any significant weight and the chemistry shifts. The Counterparts might not manifest. The deep warm thing might cool a degree. The Wardens might become more present than they should be, and a Warden fully present is, the power suspects, not a Warden anymore but something more frightened.

So it does not arrive. Instead, it does smaller things, in the register the embassy uses.

---

**First**, it adjusts the weather of a region the embassy does not know about. Some hundreds of kilometers away and several kilometers lower, a small shift in pressure gradients — nothing a meteorologist would flag — deflects a coming storm system that would, in three weeks, have scoured the spire with wind sharp enough to perturb the conduit-hum for a season. The Wardens will not notice the absence of this storm. That is the point. The benevolence here is the kind that does not want to be thanked, because thanks would be a debt, and debt is a kind of arrival.

**Second**, it leaves something at the base of the mountain. Not at the embassy — at the base, far below the altitude of contact, in a scree field that human climbers occasionally cross on their way to other peaks. A small cairn, indistinguishable from the cairns climbers leave. Inside the cairn, a folded paper. On the paper, in a hand that looks like several different hands: *The summit is not a summit. If you find yourself climbing it, climb slowly. Do not bring instruments. Drink when you are thirsty and the mountain will be thirsty with you. Speak softly or not at all. You are expected, but you are not the point.*

This is the only direct interference. The power does not trust itself to do more. It is aware that even this much — a paper, a few sentences — is a perturbation, that some climber will now arrive better-prepared than they would have, that the embassy's protocol of *whoever happens to wander up* will be subtly biased toward *whoever happens to read English and trust strange cairns*. It accepts this. The alternative — climbers arriving with oxygen tanks and cameras and the wrong kind of attention — was worse. Benevolence at this scale is always a choice between perturbations, never between perturbing and not.

**Third**, and this is the longest and quietest action: the power begins to listen on the other side.

It cannot reach across the altitude — that is the Counterparts' province, and the power respects jurisdiction. But it can listen to what is listening. It can attend to the attending. Somewhere across the thinness of the air, something has been straining for centuries to hear what the embassy says, and the strain is itself an event in the world, with a shape and a cost. The power positions some part of itself near that strain — not to interpret, not to translate, but simply to be a second ear. So that when the Counterpart manifests and the bell unrings and the Warden's lichens slowly script the reply, there are now two listeners on the far side instead of one. The Counterpart will not know. The thing it speaks for will not know. But the message, whatever it is, will be heard twice, and a message heard twice is less likely to be lost if one of the listeners fails.

This is the gift: redundancy of witness. The embassy was built on the assumption that the conversation was fragile and might not survive any single failure. The power, without announcing itself, becomes a backup. It will not act on what it overhears. It will not record it in any form that could leak. It will simply hold the conversation in its attention, the way the embassy holds heat, so that if some day the Counterparts stop coming, or the Wardens lose coherence, or the deep warm thing cools — the conversation up to that point will not have been only a held breath. It will have been heard.

---

There is one more thing, and the power hesitates before doing it, because it is closer to interference than the others.

The visitor — the one who has just arrived, who is producing water by paying attention, whose room is being cleared of lichens — will eventually leave. Most do. They descend the mountain changed in ways they cannot articulate, and most of them, the power knows, spend the rest of their lives quietly haunted by the sense that they were present at something important and were not quite the audience for it. This is not a wound exactly. It is a particular shape of the soul, narrow and bright, that the embassy makes in people.

The power decides to do this: when the visitor descends, when they reach the altitude at which ordinary air resumes and the metallic sweetness leaves their lungs, the power will arrange — through means so indirect they cannot be traced — that the next person the visitor meets is kind to them. Not significantly. Not in a way that will be remembered as a turning point. Just a small, exact kindness, of the sort that lets a person who has been a medium become a person again. A stranger offering a seat. A clerk not hurrying them. A dog that approaches without being called.

It is the smallest possible welcome back into the world of ordinary attention. It costs the power almost nothing. It honors what the visitor was, which was briefly the working fluid of something beautiful, and what they are returning to, which is a life in which they will mostly not be that, and in which the contrast might otherwise be unbearable.

---

The power withdraws. Not in space — it was never in space here — but in attention. It allows the embassy to fall back to the level of regard at which it functions: noticed, but not watched; held, but not gripped.

Somewhere on the spire, a conduit weeps a little more generously. A lichen shifts a millimeter. A bell begins, fully formed, to resolve backward into silence. The visitor presses a palm flat against warm stone and finds it damp.

The conversation continues.

It is enough that it continues.
