The benevolent and wise power considers the Sumpworks for a long time before doing anything.

This is the first thing to understand: that the consideration is itself a kind of action, and that a lesser power, or a power less wise, would already have intervened. Would have lifted the town out of the mud. Would have drained the flat, or stabilized the basalt, or given the Settled something to want, or given the Old Ones the thing they have been watching for, or — most tempting, most terrible — simply ended it, cleanly, the way one might put down a held note that has gone on too long.

The power does none of these. The power sits with the place for what would be, in human reckoning, a long time, and watches it the way the Old Ones watch the street.

Here is what the power sees that a lesser power would not see:

The Sumpworks is not suffering. This has to be taken seriously and not merely conceded. The Settled have made their accommodation and inside that accommodation there is real pleasure — the pipe-bread, the hearth-light, the brass frogs. To "rescue" them from this would be to impose a foreign standard of flourishing on a flourishing that is, in its own register, complete. The power has seen too many places ruined by rescuers who could not tell the difference between a life they would not choose and a life not worth living.

And yet. The Sumpworks is not flourishing either, not quite, not in the way a thing flourishes when it is doing what it is for. It is held. It is suspended. The equilibrium is real but it is the equilibrium of a held breath, and the Old Ones know this, and the pump-men know this in their hands, and the children know it in the way they fear the Old Ones, and the basalt — the basalt knows it most of all, because the basalt is young and is not done.

What the power decides, after its long consideration, is something like this:

It will not save the Sumpworks. It will not end the Sumpworks. It will do one small thing, and it will do it in the register the Sumpworks itself uses, which is the register of small accommodations made and held.

The power goes to the frog on the windowsill of the third-floor back room of the eastern boarding house.

It does not speak to the frog, because the frog does not need to be spoken to. It simply rests its attention on the frog for a moment, the way a hand might rest on a shoulder, and in that moment something is added to the frog that was not there before — not a soul exactly, the frog already had whatever it had, but a kind of *patency*, a readiness, a small clearance in the frog's being through which something might pass when something needed to pass.

Then the power goes to the banyan in the customs house and does the same. And to the oldest of the Old Ones, the one whose skin has gone furthest toward basalt, sitting in his doorway. And to the governor on the central pump, the brass fitting that the pump-men touch a hundred times a day without thinking. And to the youngest of the nine children, a girl of perhaps six, who is at this moment crouched by a rust pan watching a pale crab.

Five touches. Five small clearances. The power does not explain them, because explanation would close them.

What the power has done, if it can be said in words at all, is this: it has placed five doors in the Sumpworks. Not doors out. Doors *through.* When the basalt finishes what it is doing — and the basalt will finish; the power has looked and seen this; the equilibrium will not hold forever, was never going to hold forever — when that day comes, the Sumpworks will not simply be drowned or buried or shaken into the sea. There will be five places where the town can pass through itself into whatever comes next. The frog will carry what the frog carries. The banyan will carry what the banyan carries. The old man will carry the memory of the pile-driving and the long watching. The pump governor will carry the triple-stroke chuff and the hands that loved it. The child will carry the games with rules no adult taught.

What "carry" means and what "next" means the power does not specify, because the Sumpworks has not earned the right to know yet, and neither has anyone watching. These are things that are settled in the doing.

Then the power does one more thing, which is the thing that makes it wise rather than merely benevolent.

It leaves the Old Ones their watching.

It would have been easy — it would have felt like kindness — to give them the thing they have been watching for. To let them see it, finally, after so many decades in the doorways. But the power understands that the watching *is* the thing. That if the Old Ones were given their answer they would have nothing, and that what they have now, in their patient unsatisfied attention, is something closer to prayer than to deprivation. The power does not interrupt a prayer to grant it. The power lets the prayer continue to be a prayer.

And it leaves the pump-men their pumps. And the Settled their stories told at stable intervals. And the children their nine-ness. And the travelers their consistent accounts.

The power withdraws. The withdrawal is also careful — done in such a way that nothing in the Sumpworks registers that anything happened. The chuff does not skip. The frog does not move. The old man in the doorway does not lift his head, because there is nothing yet to lift his head for.

But the next traveler who comes through, nine days later, will sleep in the third-floor back room of the eastern boarding house, and will wake in the night for no reason he can name, and will look at the frog on the windowsill, and the frog will look back, and the traveler will feel — without being able to say why — that he has been seen by something very old and very kind, and that he is, in some sense he cannot articulate, *accounted for.* He will not write this in his journal. It will not fit. But he will carry it, faintly, for the rest of his life, and at his own death he will think of a brass frog on a wet windowsill in a town whose name he has half-forgotten, and he will be, in that moment, less afraid than he would otherwise have been.

This is what the power does. It is almost nothing. It is exactly enough.

The basalt continues whatever the basalt is doing. The pumps continue their triple-stroke chuff. The Sumpworks hangs in its braided sound, visible, still, and not yet sinking — but now, though no one in the town could say so, also no longer entirely alone in its not-yet-sinking. Somewhere just outside the register of the place, something attends. Something has marked the doors. Something will be there, at whatever pace the basalt keeps, when the doors are needed.

Until then: the frog. The chuff. The tea-colored light. The pipe-bread cooked on the hot casings. The Old Ones watching for the thing they are watching for, which is, though they do not know it, already partway here, and waiting, as they wait, in the same patient register, for the long slow turning of the mud.
