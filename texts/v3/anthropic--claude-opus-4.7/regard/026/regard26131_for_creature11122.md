They would not come.

This is the first thing, and it has to be said first, because a benevolent and wise power is recognizable mostly by what it declines to do. The plateau has finished most of its arguments. To arrive in such a place with hands full of help would be to mistake rest for need, slowness for suffering, silence for a cry too quiet to hear. The wise power knows the difference. It has learned, over its own long becoming, that there are conditions which look from the outside like something to be fixed and which are, from the inside, simply what a life has settled into. A pond is not a failed river.

So the first gesture is restraint. The power notices the plateau the way the plateau notices the visitor: slowly, with the noticing traveling through it like weather. It does not flinch at the long stillness. It does not read the builders' deep-set eyes as despair, or their rose-lichened shoulders as neglect, or the discontinued building as a tragedy of forgetting. It reads them, instead, the way one reads a sentence that has reached its period. The mark at the end is not a wound.

But benevolence is not only restraint. Wisdom that only abstains is a kind of cowardice dressed up. So the power does something — only it does it at the speed of the place.

It listens. Properly. Not the quick listening that wants a reply, but the listening that the plateau itself practices: the discipline by which a silence is kept full. The power lowers some part of itself to the level of the cobalt lichen and stays there for what, in its own reckoning, is a long while. It learns the tone the drifters hum in the sternum. It learns the click of the walking birds and finds that the click is not random — that across the plateau, across a span of years, the clicks compose something, though not a music a faster ear could hold. It learns the position of each builder's raised arm and the season in which it was raised and the reason, if reason is the word, that it has not yet been lowered. It learns the rings flush with the floor and the order in which they were set, and it learns the last one — which one of them placed the last stone, and where that builder is now standing, and how far the cobalt has climbed.

This listening is itself the gift. The plateau has been speaking for a very long time, in a register almost nothing can hear, and to be heard, even once, even by something that will not answer, is a completion of a kind. The builders, who notice the visitor, also notice the listener. The noticing of the listener travels through them differently than the noticing of the visitor did. It does not register as disturbance. It registers as company.

Then the power considers whether to do anything else, and considers it for a long time, because the temptation to act is the oldest temptation a power has, and wisdom is mostly the practice of not yielding to it.

It thinks: I could give them back their speech. The records are inside them, the way the old mountains are inside the plateau; I could retrieve what is not retrievable in any reasonable span. I could set the language running in them again and let them say what they once said.

And it sees, following that thought to its end, that the speech would arrive in beings who no longer have the disposition for it. The language would not be a homecoming; it would be a fracture. The builders have not lost their words. They have grown past the shape of mouth that words required. To give them speech now would be to ask the plateau to argue again, after it has finished arguing.

It thinks: I could make more of them. There were more, once. The species is thinned to a number from which it will not recover. I could replenish.

And it sees that replenishment assumes the thinning was a loss. But the builders have not been diminishing toward extinction; they have been concentrating toward sufficiency. Each one that remains is more fully the thing the people were becoming. To add to their number would be to dilute an arrival.

It thinks: I could quicken the place. Let the weather come faster, let the grazers multiply, let some predator return and reintroduce the old urgencies. Surely a world is more alive when it is contending.

And it sees that this is the cruelest of the temptations, because it is the one dressed most convincingly as care. The plateau is not less alive for having stopped contending. It is alive in a register the power's own younger selves would not have recognized as life — the register in which a stone is alive, in which a saturation is alive, in which staying is alive. To quicken the place would be to kill it in the only way it can still be killed.

So the power sets these temptations down, one by one, the way the builders once set down stones. And having set them down, it finds it has built something: a small, low structure in itself, a cairn of refusals, marking that here was the place where it chose not to intervene. The structure will outlast the choosing.

What it does, in the end, is small and specific, and it does these things over a span the builders can register.

It adjusts, very slightly, the angle at which the evening light enters the plateau, so that the cobalt lichen's brief impersonation of standing water lasts a few minutes longer. This is not a change anything will measure. It is a gift to the eye of the one builder whose head, this season, happens to be tilted toward the west.

It causes the warm rain, the next time it gathers, to fall a little more evenly across the shoulders of those builders whose basins have gone longest without filling. The rose lichen on them, fed, spreads a fraction further. This too is not a change. It is a courtesy.

It places, somewhere on the plateau — not where any builder will reach it in the remainder of their standing, but where the slow vector of the place will eventually carry the knowledge of it — a single stone. Pale. Calcareous. Indistinguishable from the floor except that it was set there with care, by something that understood what setting-with-care had meant, once, to the people who lived here. It is the power's way of saying: I saw your rings. I saw the last one. I have added nothing to your work; I have only acknowledged that the work was work, and that the silence after it is not empty.

And then the power does the thing that is hardest for powers to do, which is to leave. Not abruptly — nothing abrupt would be welcome here — but with the same gradual reddening by which the evening arrives. It withdraws its attention the way a cloud unbuilds itself, over weeks, leaving no shadow that wasn't there before.

The builders, in their long incorporation of the visit, will register the withdrawal too. One of them, over the course of a season, will turn fractionally toward the place where the stone was set. The turning will not complete in that builder's standing. It will complete in the standing of whichever builder is next, in the lineage of those who turn toward such things. And by then the stone will be half-claimed by the cobalt, half-mineralized into the floor, returning along the same slow vector as everything else — which is to say, it will have become the plateau, which is to say, the gift will have been received in the only way the plateau receives anything: by being absorbed into the rest it was already keeping.

The power, elsewhere now, carries something with it that it did not have before. It has learned a tempo. It has learned that there are places in which the right exercise of great capacity is the decision to use almost none of it. It has learned that to love a finished thing is to let it stay finished. This will change, in some small way, how it approaches the next place that draws its attention — which may be a place still in the middle of its arguments, still loud, still hurrying, still in need. The power will be more careful there too, having been careful here. The plateau, without knowing it, will have taught.

And that, perhaps, is what the plateau was for. Not for itself only — though itself would have been enough — but for the slow instruction of any power patient enough to come and sit at the level of the cobalt lichen and listen until listening was the whole of what it had come to do.

The builders go on standing. The drifters go on drifting. The evening reddens. Somewhere on the floor, a new pale stone catches the last of the light, and looks, for a moment, exactly like all the others.

Which is what it was placed to look like.

Which is the whole of the gift.
