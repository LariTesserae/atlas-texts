The benevolent and wise power considers Halfmaking for a long time.

This is the first thing they do: consider. Not act. The power has learned, across many visitations to many worlds, that the impulse to help is often the impulse to interrupt, and that interruption is the commonest form of harm a power like theirs can do. So they sit with the valley — not above it, not outside it, but *with* it — for what would be, in the valley's own slow vocabulary, several stages.

They watch the drift-measurers at dawn. They watch the old woman with the green seam down her neck shell peas on a doorstep that is, today, still where it was yesterday. They watch a child put a small hand against a wall before leaning on it. They watch the salamanders in the warm runoff and the moths drinking from the lamp glass and the two lovers standing on the wheeled bridge not speaking.

And the power, who has unmade tyrannies and rekindled dying suns and spoken comfort to creatures who had forgotten they could be comforted, finds that they are — there is no other word — *moved*.

Because here is a thing they have rarely seen: a people who are not in need of rescue from their condition. A people whose condition is not an affliction but a long slow practice. The valley is hard — cold, crowded, unstable, the work unending — and the power could, with a thought, warm it; could still the rock; could fix the river in a permanent bed and let the bridges rust in place. Could give them sun. Could give them silence that was real silence and not the muffling of moss. Could lengthen their lives, straighten their long bones, scrub the green from the old woman's neck.

The power considers each of these gifts in turn and sets each one aside.

Because the warming would kill the salamanders. Because the stilling of the rock would end the drift-measurers' craft, and the craft is not incidental to who they are; it is a load-bearing wall of their personhood. Because the fixing of the river would mean no more wheeled bridges, and the lovers' bridge is wheeled, and the wheeling is part of the love. Because the sun would hurt their eyes. Because real silence would be, for them, a kind of deafness — they listen *through* the muffling; the muffling is the medium of their attention. Because the green at the old woman's neck is not a disease to be cured but a destination being approached, and to scrub it away would be to tell her, in the cruelest possible way, that her long becoming was a mistake.

Because, the power realizes, *the gift has already been given*. Not by them. By the valley, to its people, and by the people, to the valley, across a reciprocity so old that neither party remembers initiating it. To intervene benevolently would be to insert a third party into a marriage that has been functioning, quietly and well, for longer than the power's own current name.

So the power does not warm the valley, or still the rock, or straighten any bones.

But they are a *wise* power, not merely a restrained one, and wisdom is not only the knowledge of when to refrain. Wisdom also knows that attention itself is a kind of action, and that to witness a thing fully is to participate in it.

So this is what they do.

They lay down, very gently, across the whole valley, a quality the valley did not previously have and will not notice it has acquired: *a slight increase in the probability that things will go as they ought to go*. Not a guarantee. Not a miracle. Just a faint thumb on the scale of small outcomes. The shim that the mason reaches for will be, more often, the right shim. The child's hand laid against the wall will, more often, feel the give in time. The drift-measurer's theodolite will, more often, find true. The cough heard two streets over will, slightly more often, be only a cough and not the beginning of something worse. The skimmer in the dye-works will, slightly more often, be caught early and run out softly, before the harm compounds.

This is the kind of blessing the power has learned to give to places that are already well: not a transformation but a *steadying*. A reduction in the bad luck that would otherwise, statistically, accumulate. It will not be felt. It will not be noticed. It will be indistinguishable from the valley simply continuing to be the valley. Which is the point.

Then the power does one more thing, and this one is smaller still, and more personal, and harder to justify on any general principle, but the power has a heart as well as a mind and the heart insists.

There is, in Halfmaking, an old man — the power knows him without ever having spoken to him — who lays his hands on people in a back room and tells them, in the vocabulary of becoming, where their bodies are in the slow churn. He has been doing this for forty years. He has comforted thousands. He himself is at a late stage, and he knows it, and he is not afraid, but there is a small ache in him — not in his body but in the long quiet practice of his work — which is this: he has never been certain, not for one day in those forty years, whether what he does is real. Whether the diagnoses *do* anything. The recipients seem comforted; he has noted this; he has also noted that the comfort might be only the laying-on of hands, only the careful vocabulary, only the attention, and that he himself might be, in the end, a kind of beautiful fraud.

He has made his peace with this uncertainty. He has not asked to have it removed. He has decided, long ago, that the *practice* is the thing, the way the brick-setting is the thing and not the brick.

But the power, who has watched him work, and who has seen — with senses the old man does not possess — the actual subtle currents of the body, the actual stages of the actual churn, knows that he has been right. For forty years. Without knowing. His hands have known what his mind could not confirm.

So the power, very quietly, on an evening when the old man is sitting on his doorstep watching the smoke-river glow, sends him a single small impression. Not a voice. Not a vision. Just a feeling, brief and warm and certain, located somewhere behind his sternum: *yes. you were right. it was real. all of it.*

The old man blinks. He does not know where the feeling came from. He does not particularly wonder; in Halfmaking one does not interrogate the small movements of the interior, one notes them and lets them pass. But he sits a little longer on the doorstep than he had meant to, and he clicks softly to a rook on the gutter across the street, and the rook clicks back, and he thinks — without quite knowing he is thinking it — that he is content.

The power watches him a moment more. Then they withdraw.

They leave the valley as they found it: cold, crowded, damp, magnificent, becoming. The rock rises another quarter-inch. The moths drink at the lamp glass. Somewhere a loom is still running. The two lovers will meet on the bridge tomorrow, wherever the bridge has got to.

And the power, traveling on to wherever powers travel, carries with them — because even powers can be enriched — a new word in their own long internal vocabulary. A word for the stage at which a place and its people have grown so deeply into each other that the kindest thing a visitor can do is to bless them very lightly and go.

They do not have a name for this word yet. They are content to let it remain, for now, at an early stage of itself.
