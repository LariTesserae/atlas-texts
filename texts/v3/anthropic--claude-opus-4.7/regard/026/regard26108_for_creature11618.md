The power arrives the way weather arrives: not as an event but as a change in what the existing conditions are doing. There is no announcement. The mist does not part. No figure descends. The pumps do not falter in their breathing. If you asked the watchmen afterward whether anything happened on such-and-such a night, they would say no, and they would be telling the truth as they understood it, and they would also be wrong in a way that did not matter.

This is what the power does.

---

It walks the city first. Not to learn it — it knew the city before it came, in the way that such powers know things, which is not by having been told — but because walking is the form of attention this city rewards. To know the Condensery you have to feel the warm stone through your soles and the cold mist on the back of your neck at the same time. You have to be, briefly, the filter the city is. The power takes a body for this, or something close enough to a body that the lead-toed boots of a watchman would fit it, and it walks.

It walks a watchman's route. It taps each brass plate at chest height, breathes on the ones that want breathing on, stands for the count of seven where seven is called for. It does not improvise. It does not investigate the plates to see what they once did. This is important: the power is wise enough to know that the gesture has become the thing, and that to strip the gesture back to its origin would be a kind of murder. The watchman's tap is no longer a signal. It is a small precise act of devotion to a city that has forgotten it is owed devotion, and the power, walking the route, performs the devotion correctly, and something in the route — not the plates, the *route* — receives it.

It walks a pipe-fitter's shift. It puts a palm against a wall and feels for the leak three inches in, and finds one, and packs the joint, and moves on. Its hands, for the duration of the shift, are pale and thickened and worn at the nails. It does not do the work faster or better than a pipe-fitter would. It does the work *exactly* as a pipe-fitter would, because the pipe-fitters are right: the work matters, and what makes it matter is the unhurried competence of the doing, and to do it any other way would be to do a different work entirely.

It sits in the tea-house. It drinks the bitter tea and does not flinch. The old woman refills its cup without looking at its face, the way she refills everyone's cup, and the power lets itself be one more silence at one more table, and the silence in the tea-house is not disturbed by its presence because its presence is, for that hour, the same kind of thing as everyone else's presence: a person who has come in out of the wet and will go out into the wet again.

It reads. In the oldest reader's room, late, when the reader has gone to her cot in the next room and the lamp is turned down to its lowest possible burn, the power sits at the table and reads the waxed-paper logs and the brown pressure-tapes and the wall of ceramic tiles. It reads everything. It reads what the reader has read and what the reader has not yet read and what the reader will never read because she will die first. It reads the manual the boot-resoling came from, which is in a sealed cabinet in a sub-basement nobody currently knows about. It reads the original commissioning documents, which are not in the city at all but in the memory of the stone, where heat-records are kept in a script the power can parse and no human now can.

And here the power encounters the question.

*What was the water for.*

It can answer the question. This is the first thing to be clear about. The power knows what the water was for. The answer is not even particularly strange or beautiful or sad; it is the kind of answer that questions of this age tend to have, which is to say it is partly an answer and partly a list of the further questions the answer opens, and partly an account of how the original purpose drifted and was overwritten and drifted again, until what remains is genuinely no longer *for* anything in the sense the question means.

The power knows this. And the power does not tell.

Not because telling is forbidden. Because telling would be a gift to no one. The readers do not, finally, need the answer; they need the reading. The oldest reader is closer to peace than she knows, and her peace is being built out of the act of looking, not out of the prospect of finding. To hand her the answer would be to take from her the last forty years of her life and replace them with a fact. The fact would be true. The replacement would be a cruelty.

So the power does something else instead, which is harder and which is the real reason it came.

---

It goes to the bottom.

Nobody from the middle city goes past a certain street, and the pipe-fitters who go further go only as far as they need to and come back. The power goes all the way down. It walks the lower terraces, where the wick grows thick enough to harvest and the air is so wet that breathing is a kind of drinking. It meets the polite incurious people there, and they are polite to it, and incurious, and it is polite back, and it does not ask the questions it could ask about what they are and how long they have been what they are and whether the middle city's people and the lower city's people share an ancestor or only a climate.

It meets the apologetic creature with the long stride. They regard each other in a wet alley between two warehouses whose loading-doors have not been rolled down in living memory. The creature is lonely in a way that has gone past loneliness into something more like a weather of its own. The power does not heal this. Healing it would require the creature to become something other than what it is, and what it is is one of the few things in the city that is genuinely *itself* and not the residue of something else. Instead the power stands with it for a while in the alley, and the standing is mutual, and when the power leaves the creature is not less lonely but is, for the first time in a very long time, *accompanied* in its loneliness, which is a different thing, and which lasts.

At the very bottom, where the runoff disappears into whatever the bottom is, the power looks at what the runoff disappears into. This is the only place where its action is, in the human sense, large. What is down there is not a customer and not a product. It is closer to a wound — old, slow, no longer actively bleeding, but not closed either, and the water has been running into it for a very long time, doing the small patient work that water does, which is to find every contour and to take a little of everything away with it as it goes. The wound is not in the city. The city was built around it. The water was always for this.

The power does not close the wound. Closing it would stop the city, and the city is the form the closing has taken; to short-circuit the form would be to undo the work the water has already done, which is most of the work, and which is nearly finished. Instead the power adjusts, very slightly, the rate. Not the rate of condensation — that is set by the weather, and the weather is honest. The rate of *acceptance*, down at the bottom, where the water meets what it has been carrying things away from. The power makes the bottom a little more willing. The wound will close, now, in something like four more human generations, instead of something like seven. This is the only quantitative change the power makes anywhere in the city, and no one will ever notice it, because the only person who could have noticed it is the oldest reader, and she will be dead before the difference becomes legible in the records.

---

Then the power goes back up.

On its way up it does small things. It does not fix what is broken; very little is broken. It tends to what is wearing. In the pump-galleries it lays a hand on a bearing that has another six months in it and gives it six years. In a condenser-loft it tightens, by a quarter-turn, a bolt that no one would have checked for another decade. In the tea-house it banks the stove a particular way that will make the next morning's briquettes burn cleaner, and the old woman, when she comes down and finds the stove like that, will assume she banked it that way herself and forgot, and she will be pleased with herself for a moment, and the pleasure will last her through the first cup of her own bitter tea.

In the watchman's coat pocket, the one with the bad knee, it leaves nothing. It considers leaving something — a small warm stone, a folded note, a coin from a country he has never heard of — and decides against all of them. The watchman's knee is part of his route now. It tells him when the weather is changing. To take the pain would be to take the instrument. Instead the power, passing him on a stair, lets its shoulder brush his, very lightly, the way strangers brush in a crowd, and the watchman feels, for the rest of that night and into the next day, *seen* — not by anyone in particular, just seen, the way a thing is seen when it is being attended to by the world it lives in — and he does not know why, and he does not need to.

To the youngest pipe-fitter, the quiet child of fourteen, the power gives the only real gift it gives to any person in the city. It is not a thing. It is a dream. The child dreams, that night, of the city as it was when it was full — eighty thousand people, the foundries pouring real metal into real molds, the aqueducts running heavy, the bells ringing all day in a complicated speech the child in the dream understands. The child sees what the work was for, in the dream, and the seeing is not an answer but an *atmosphere*, a sense of the work having been part of something larger and warmer and more peopled, and the child wakes not knowing what was dreamed but carrying the atmosphere into the morning, and the atmosphere stays. The child will be a pipe-fitter for sixty more years, and will train three apprentices, and will be, without ever being able to say why, the one who keeps the guild from finally thinning to nothing in the next century. The dream does this. The power gives the dream because the child is young enough to receive a gift without it becoming a burden, and old enough already to keep the gift to herself.

To the oldest reader, the power gives nothing. This is the hardest restraint and the most important. The reader is doing her own work, and her work is good, and to interfere with it even kindly would be to suggest that her work needs interference, which it does not. The power sits across from her one evening in the tea-house — she comes in once a week, always to the same table — and they do not speak, and the reader does not know she is being honored, and the power lets her not know, and drinks its bitter tea, and goes.

---

Before leaving, the power rings the bell.

It rings it correctly: a single struck note, somewhere across the rooftops, that the mist swallows before anyone can place it. It rings it at the moment when the day's condensation has, in fact, reached its sufficient quantity — which is something the bell used to mark and has not reliably marked in a long time, because the mechanism that rang it has been broken in a subtle way for about ninety years, and the bell has been rung since then only occasionally, by wind or by settling or by something the pipe-fitters do not discuss.

The power rings it on the true measure. The watchmen pause mid-step. The pipe-fitters pause with their hands on their valves. The tea-house woman pauses with the kettle half-lifted. The readers pause with their fingers on their tiles. The apologetic creature, down in the lower terraces, lifts its long head. And then they all go on with what they were doing, because whether the bell means something or not, the next thing to do is the same.

But the bell *did* mean something, this once, correctly, and the meaning enters the city the way the water enters: not as a message to be read but as a medium, soaking into the stone, becoming part of what the stone is. The next time the bell rings, by wind or by settling or by the undiscussed thing, it will ring into a city that has been, once, recently, rung-into-truly, and the ringing will land differently, and the people who pause will pause a little more deeply, and the pause will be, in some small way, a prayer answered before it was made.

This is all the power does. It is a great deal and it is almost nothing. It leaves the city exactly as it found it, which is to say: maintained, almost empty, weeping at the bottom inch of every wall, drawing water out of the air at a rate set by a weather older than memory, tended by two hundred people who do not know they are being tended back.

The power, leaving, takes one thing with it: the answer to the question. Not because the answer is dangerous. Because the answer belongs, now, to the city — to the *condition* of the city, to the way the city *holds* the question — and to carry the answer out into other places where the question is not held would be to thin it. The power keeps the answer the way the tea-house woman keeps what she knows about the watchman's knee: against some eventual need, which may never come, and which the keeping is sufficient for either way.

And the city goes on. The mist beads and runs. The pumps breathe. The wick grows in its lead trays for reasons no one remembers. The watchman taps the plate and moves on. The bell, when it next rings, rings into a slightly different silence, and no one can say how, and no one tries.
