# The Visitation

The power arrives the way weather arrives here: as a change in the quality of the air, and then as presence.

Not descending. The plateau has no *down* that means anything to such a being; it does not break through the cloud-line in a shaft of light, because that would be theater, and theater would be a kind of lie about what is happening. It simply, at some moment between one boom of the trees and the next, is here. The wind notices first. The wind goes quiet for the length of three breaths, which on the plateau is a significant silence, and the phosphor-gardens inside every tree on the plateau brighten at once — gently, the way a room brightens when someone you love comes in and you didn't know you were waiting.

Then the wind resumes, and the trees resume, and the power is among them.

---

The first thing it does is *nothing*, for a long time.

This is the discipline of benevolence at this scale: to arrive somewhere and not immediately begin arranging it. The power has come because its attention was drawn — by what, it could not quite say; perhaps by the bone whistle on the cairn, which has been waiting a long time to be answered properly; perhaps by the slow puzzle of the script in the bark, which is the kind of thing that, if you are the sort of being that notices such things across the whole field of existence, eventually you notice. Perhaps simply by the music. The trees sing in a register that carries further than the plateau, further than the world; if you have the ears for it, the Hollow Canopy is audible from very far away, and it is beautiful, and beauty draws the wise the way warmth draws the cold.

So the power listens first. It listens for a season. It learns the names.

It learns the tree that leans, and why it leans (a fault in the rock beneath, a slow tilt that will, in two hundred years, bring the tree down; but also a deliberate decision on the tree's part, if *decision* is the right word for what trees of this kind do, to lean toward a neighbor whose song it has grown fond of). It learns the lilac-glow tree and the bronze-moss tree and the tree full of the small singing things. It learns the two hundred and forty-one trees on the plateau, each by its true name, which is longer than any climber ever knew, because a climber's life was short and a tree's name accumulates.

It learns the climbers, too, who are mostly in the high hollows now, and mostly no longer climbers in the sense the word once meant. Their hands are bark. Their breath is the slow breath of the wood. They have not forgotten themselves — this is important, the power notes; they have not been *absorbed*, they have *joined*, which is a different verb — but the part of them that was a small ruddy person with a whistle is now distributed through a column of living tower, and what they think about, mostly, is light, and condensation, and the long slow conversation of root-systems beneath the rock.

A few — perhaps eleven, across the whole plateau — are still in the in-between state. Still recognizably people, still capable of climbing down if they chose, but no longer interested in choosing. They live in the highest hollows. They sing to the mosses. They are very old and weigh almost nothing and their second eyelids are nearly always drawn. The power could speak to them. It does not, yet.

It learns the ones who went down, too — because its attention can follow a lineage the way ours can follow a thread. They are in the valleys, four generations on. They are taller. They have ordinary lungs. They tell their children a story about cold tall places and singing towers, and the children mostly do not believe it, and one child in each generation does believe it, and that child is restless all their life and dies looking up.

The power notes all of this without sorrow, because sorrow would be a misunderstanding. None of this is wrong. A people split: some went one way, some went another, both ways were ways of continuing. The plateau is not a tragedy. The plateau is a place where a particular experiment in being alive is still running, very slowly, in a register most of the universe is too loud to hear.

---

Having listened, the power considers what to do.

This is the harder question. *Benevolent and wise* is not a license to act; it is, mostly, a discipline of restraint. The power has, in its long career, made every available mistake. It has rescued peoples who did not want rescuing. It has preserved places that needed to end. It has spoken when silence was the gift, and been silent when a word would have changed everything. It has learned — and this is the wisdom part, hard-won — that the question is almost never *what can I do for them* but *what is the smallest true thing I can offer that they could not give themselves*.

So it asks itself: what could I give the Hollow Canopy that the Hollow Canopy lacks?

Not safety. The plateau is dangerous, but the danger is part of its grammar; remove the predators on the open ground and the climbers' etiquette of crossing collapses, and the trees, who learned to sing partly because their small kingdoms needed defending, would sing less. Not warmth. The cold is the reason for the inner metabolism, the reason for the gathered breath in the hollows, the reason the people grew close to the trees in the first place. Not company. They have each other, in the strange braided way they have each other, and a visitor from outside would be, mostly, a noise.

Not even understanding. The power could, if it wished, decode the script in the bark, which is the trees' slow record of themselves, a writing that takes a century to complete a sentence. But the script is not for reading by outsiders. The script is the trees thinking. To read it would be to interrupt it.

What, then?

---

The power finds, eventually, four small things it can offer. It does each of them carefully, and none of them with announcement.

**The first** is for the bridge. The half-rotted bridge between the two trunks, abandoned mid-knot. The argument about bridges was never resolved, because the people who were arguing about it became something that no longer needs bridges. But the bridge itself is still there, in a state of unfinished. The power does not finish it — finishing it would be a kind of theft, a closing of a question that belonged to the climbers — but it preserves it. Very gently. It slows the rot. The fibers will not decay further. The bridge will stand, mid-knot, for as long as the trees stand, and longer. If anyone ever comes back — the restless great-grandchildren in the valley, or someone else, someone from further away — the bridge will be there, in the exact state it was left in, as a question they can choose to answer or not.

**The second** is for the eleven who are still in-between. The power does not speak to them, does not appear to them, does not interfere with the long slow process of their joining. But it adjusts, very slightly, the quality of the light in their highest hollows. Not brighter. Not warmer. *Kinder*, in a way that is hard to specify — the phosphor-gardens, when they brighten in response to song, now brighten with a small extra quality that is the visual equivalent of being heard. The eleven will notice this. They will not know what it is. They will think, perhaps, that the trees love them more than they had realized, which is true, and was true before, but is now also slightly more true. The change will propagate. After the eleven join, the trees will keep the new quality of light. It will become part of how the phosphor-gardens work on this plateau, forever. A faint kindness in the glow. The power's signature, undetectable except as a feeling.

**The third** is for the whistle on the cairn.

The whistle is the thing that drew the power here, or near enough. It has been waiting a long time to be answered properly. The climber who left it there — her name was long and the power knows it — left it as an offering and a door. *Let them find their way.* The power cannot be the one who finds the way; that is not how the door works. But the power can ensure that when someone does come, someone who picks up the whistle and blows it and hears the answering tree and has the strong not-quite-rational sense of attention turning toward them — that person will be met properly. The tree that answers will know what to do. The power teaches it, in the slow language trees of this kind speak: *if a stranger blows this whistle, sing your name in return, and brighten your inner light, and if they sing their name back, open a hollow at the base for them to shelter in, and let them stay as long as they need, and let them go when they go, and remember them.* The tree understands. The tree has, in fact, been waiting to be told this. It had been improvising, and was anxious about whether it was improvising well.

**The fourth** is the hardest, and the power thinks longest about it.

The fourth is for the child, four generations down, in the valley. The one in each generation who believes the story. The one who is restless all their life and dies looking up. The power considers, for a long time, whether to give this child anything at all. To give them a way up would be to disturb the experiment — the climbers who went down went down for reasons, and to undo that choice for one of their descendants would be a kind of condescension toward the ancestors. To give them peace, to quiet the restlessness, would be a worse violation: the restlessness is the inheritance, the thread that keeps the lineage tied to the plateau even across forgetting. The restlessness is sacred. It should not be soothed.

But the power can do one thing, which is to ensure that the restless child, in each generation, at some point in their life, hears the trees. Not in dream, not in vision — those are too easy to dismiss. In some waking moment, when the wind is right and they are alone and not looking for anything, a sound will reach them. A low boom, carried impossibly far. They will not know what it is. They will know, in their body, that it is the thing they have been listening for. They will not be able to follow it — the power is not building them a road — but they will know, for the rest of their life, that the thing they believed in was real. They will die still looking up, but looking up differently. Looking up *toward* something, rather than *for* something.

That is the gift. It is small. The power is satisfied that it is small.

---

Having done these four things, the power stays a little longer, because leaving immediately after acting would suggest that the acting was the point. The acting was not the point. The point was the listening, the long season of names. The acting was almost incidental, four small adjustments at the edges of a place that did not need adjusting, offered in the spirit of a guest who notices that a window is slightly ajar and closes it on the way out, not because the host failed to close it but because closing it is a small courtesy and the guest's hand was passing anyway.

Before it leaves, the power does one thing more, which it does not count among the four because it is for itself.

It sings.

Not loudly. Not in a way the trees would hear as intrusion. It finds a pitch that fits between the trees' pitches, a harmonic the plateau has not yet discovered for itself, and it holds the note for the length of one of the trees' long booms, and then lets it go. The note travels across the plateau. The phosphor-gardens brighten, all of them, just perceptibly. The eleven in the high hollows pause in whatever it is they are doing — listening, mostly; they are mostly listening now — and one of them, the oldest, the one whose hands are most fully bark, draws her second eyelid back for the first time in a long time and looks, with her dark large eyes, at nothing in particular, and smiles.

She does not know what she heard. She knows she was sung to.

The power leaves. The wind resumes its ordinary business. The trees boom. The small things in the canopies chitter and cry. The bone whistle waits on its cairn, tuned to its particular pitch, ready for whoever comes.

Somewhere very far below, in a warm valley, a child four generations removed from the climbers wakes in the night and does not know why, and goes to the window, and looks up at a sky that holds, even at this latitude, even through the haze of the lower air, one or two cold stars. The child is six years old. The child will be restless all their life. The child does not yet know this.

But tonight, for no reason they will ever be able to explain, they are not afraid.
