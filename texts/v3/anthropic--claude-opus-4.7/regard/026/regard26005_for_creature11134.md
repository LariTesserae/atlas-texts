They would not come down into it.

This is the first thing, and the most important. A benevolent and wise power, encountering the Hanging Cirque, would recognize immediately that descent is the wrong verb. The place is already at the top of something. It is already held. To enter it from above with intent — to alight on the rim, to address the marmot-parliament, to read the count of vertical strokes and complete it — would be to introduce a new weight into a bowl that has spent ten thousand years achieving exactly its present poise. A wise power knows the difference between a place that is suffering and a place that is finished, in the old sense of *finished*: brought to its proper form, lacking nothing.

The cirque lacks nothing. It is not waiting to be helped.

So what they would do, if anything, is the question — and the *if anything* is doing real work in the question, and a wise power would honor it.

Here is what I think they would do.

---

They would pause, first, at a great distance. Far enough that the marmots' triangulation does not register them as presence-located-observed; far enough that the ravens, who notice everything, do not have to spend a morning's attention deciding what they are. This courtesy alone takes a kind of power most powers do not have. To be capable of arriving and to choose, instead, to be perceptible only as weather — as a slight steadiness in the wind, perhaps, or as a particular quality of the light at the hour when the cliffs burn orange — this is already most of wisdom.

From that distance they would look for a long time. Not to assess. To attend. There is a difference. Assessment asks *what is this and what should be done*; attention asks *what is this, and what is it doing, and may I be allowed to know it a little*. A wise power, having all the time there is, would spend some of it simply being a witness to the bowl: to the haymaking pikas in their two hundred small territories, to the ravens rebuilding their inherited nest, to the twenty-minute moth living its short urgent life in the warm hour, to the spiral nearly closed by lichen, to the gesture of raised arms still being made by no one on the east wall. They would learn the three pitches of the whistling. They would learn which seep on which column drips slowest. They would learn the snow leopard's route across the decades, the node-on-the-map that is older than any human map.

This attending is not nothing. In some accountings it is the largest thing a power can do. To be witnessed by something that sees clearly and wishes you well is a condition many lives go without; the cirque has had it from its own residents for a long time, but a wise outside witness is a different order of regard. It would not change the bowl. But the bowl would be, for the duration of the attending, *known* in a way it has not been known before — known not by a god who made it, not by a climber who passed through, not by the marmots who triangulate within it, but by something that simply chose to look without need.

Then, having looked, they would consider whether to act, and on what.

I think they would identify three things, and act on one of them, and barely.

---

The first thing they would notice is that the cirque is, in the time-frame that powers think in, already beginning to thin. Not yet, not visibly — but the snowpack arrives later each century, the seeps run a little less steadily in the late-summer dry weeks, the cushion plants at the bowl's higher edges are already, in places, the gray of their own ghosts. The warming below is moving up. The cirque does not know this. The marmots do not know this. The ravens, who know almost everything, do not know this in the form *the world is changing under us*; they know it only as *this year the cloud sat lower than last year, and last year lower than the year before that*. The lichens, who would know if anyone could, think too slowly to act on what they know.

A lesser power, seeing this, would intervene. Would thicken the snowpack, deepen the seeps, hold the temperature. Would *save* the cirque, in the verb that saving usually means.

A wise power would not. Because to hold one cirque in stasis against the turning of a world is to make it a museum, and a museum is the opposite of a held place; a museum is a place no longer doing its own holding. The cirque holds itself by participating in the weather, the centuries, the slow argument of ice and stone. To exempt it from that argument would be to kill it more thoroughly than any drying could.

But — and here is where I think the wise power does something — they might, very quietly, lay down a kind of long blessing on the cirque's *terms of decline*. Not preventing the change. Slowing it, perhaps, by a hand's width. Or, more likely: ensuring that whatever the cirque becomes, as it becomes it, it becomes it *in its own grammar*. That the marmots, when their three colonies finally thin to two and then to one, thin in a way that lets the parliament reorganize rather than collapse — that the last colony can still triangulate with itself, can still let the bowl know where its visitors are. That the pikas, when the haymaking summer shortens past viability, find their way down the talus to a lower bowl that will hold them, rather than simply ending. That the ravens' inherited nest, when at last no raven inherits it, comes apart slowly into sticks and bone and weathered cloth, returning to the ledge without violence. That the painted figure with raised arms, when the ochre finally goes, goes by being slowly drunk into the rock that bore it, rather than scoured off.

This is not preservation. It is something closer to *granting a good death over a long time*. It is what a wise power can give that a lesser one cannot: not the refusal of ending, but the dignity of the ending being of a piece with the living.

I think they would do this. And it would cost them almost nothing, and the cirque would never know.

---

The second thing they would notice is the layer the cirque itself calls *the ones that are almost not there*. The prayer-cloths' intentions. The spiral's thinking. The figure's gesture still being made. The count still counting.

A wise power would recognize these for what they are: small attentions deposited by beings who came up briefly from the warmer world and left a piece of their regard behind. The cirque has been keeping them, faithfully, for as long as it has been able. The water that feeds the lichens that erase the paint is also the water that, in some other accounting, *honors* the paint by including it in the bowl's slow weather. The cirque has been doing this work without being asked, without being thanked, for centuries.

A wise power might, here, do the smallest possible thing. They might *thank the cirque*. Not in words; the cirque has no use for words. But in the way one acknowledges a long quiet labor: by a moment of recognition that lands, somehow, in the place itself. The lichens would not feel it. The marmots would not whistle differently. But the bowl, considered as a whole — the bowl that has been holding the gesture of raised arms and the count of vertical strokes and the intentions tied to a juniper post — would, for an instant, be *seen in the act of holding*. And that seeing is, I think, what the gesture of raised arms was originally for. The figure was painted by someone who wanted to be witnessed by something larger. The cirque has been the witness on behalf of that larger thing for a very long time. A wise power passing through might briefly be the larger thing the cirque has been standing in for, and complete, in that instant, a transaction that has been waiting a thousand years to close.

This too costs almost nothing. And the figure would go on raising its arms, and the count would go on counting, and the cirque would go on holding them — but one small fold of obligation would have been smoothed.

---

The third thing they would notice is the snow leopard's map.

This is the thing I am least sure about. The map exists only in the heads of snow leopards, and it is older than any human map of anywhere, and it is the most precious cartography in the cirque's region — perhaps in any region. A wise power would see it whole, in a way no snow leopard has ever seen it, because each leopard only knows her own loops and her mother's tellings of the loops before. They would see the full braid of routes across the centuries, the nodes (of which the cirque is one), the rests, the drinkings, the kills.

They would see that this map is also thinning. That the leopards are fewer, that the nodes are going dark one by one as the lower valleys fill with the warmer world, that the cirque is becoming a node leading nowhere because the next node down has been cut by a road or fenced by a herder or simply lost its prey.

And here, I think, the wise power would face their hardest restraint. Because they *could* repair the map. They could, with very little effort, restore a node or two, push a road's intention sideways by a generation, let a herder's son inherit a slightly different dream of where to graze. The map could be saved. The leopards could go on transiting.

But the wise power would not do this either. Not because they do not love the leopards — they do, in the way wise powers love — but because the map is not theirs, and the warmer world is not the cirque's problem to solve, and to begin repairing the leopards' cartography from above is to begin a kind of stewardship that does not end, that grows, that eventually replaces the thing it meant to save. The map belongs to the leopards. If it is to be kept, it must be kept by them or lost by them, in their own grammar.

What the wise power might do — and I think this is the only intervention they would actually make in the world rather than in the cirque's manner of being — is something almost absurdly small. They might, on a night when a particular young leopard is sleeping on a particular ledge eleven valleys away, allow her to dream of the cirque with unusual clarity. Not instructing her. Not commanding. Just *offering*, in the way dreams offer: *this place exists, it is still here, the tarn is still clear, the marmots still whistle in three pitches, the route still loops through.* So that when she wakes, she carries one more node firmly in her head, and her own daughters, if she has them, will carry it too.

One dream. One leopard. That is the whole intervention.

And the cirque, the next time she passes through, would not know it had been the subject of a dream. The marmots would whistle their high sustained note. The pikas would go silent. The ravens would go to their nest. The leopard would drink at the tarn and rest an hour on the flat slab and move on. Exactly as it has always been.

---

So: the wise power would attend, at distance, for a long time. They would lay down a blessing on the terms of the cirque's eventual decline, so that whatever it becomes it becomes in its own grammar. They would briefly, silently, witness the cirque in its long labor of holding human attentions, and close a fold that had been waiting. They would give one dream to one leopard, eleven valleys away.

Then they would go.

They would not leave a mark. They would not be remembered by the ravens, who remember faces. They would not move the obsidian flakes. They would not add a stroke to the count or close the spiral or restore the ochre or tie a new cloth to the juniper post. They would not deepen the seeps or hold the snowpack or save the cirque from its century.

They would understand that the cirque does not need them, and that this understanding is the gift, and that the gift is given best by being kept.

And the cirque, for its part, would go on weeping quietly from every fracture in its stone, ringing with three pitches of whistle and the overlapping notes of pikas and the dry conversation of ravens, holding its crowded floor and its vertical absence, cupped, exposed, kept — entirely unaware that it had been, for a little while, the object of a great and careful love.

Which is, I think, the only form such a love could rightly take, here.
