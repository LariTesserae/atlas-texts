The benevolent and wise power considers the Drumming Flats for what would be, by the standards of the place, a brief while: perhaps three of its long nights, perhaps a season. Powers of this kind do not arrive in a hurry, and they do not announce themselves. Something in the quality of the mist thickens, maybe, on the second night. The chorus does not change.

It watches a Listener — one Listener, chosen for no reason the Listener would recognize — for the duration of a single tending. It watches her wade out at the slow hour, place her sternum to a particular stone, return the held note, build her three-stone cairn, and sit beside it through the gray non-day that follows. It watches her eyes under the milky lid. It listens to what she is listening to, and then it listens to *her*, which is a different and longer act.

And then it considers what to do.

---

Here is what a lesser power would do. A lesser power, seeing such patience and such apparent poverty — no speech, no fire, no green, no swift accumulation of anything — would want to *give*. It would want to lift the cloud ceiling and let real sun fall on the flats. It would want to seed the rust-stained stones with something that could photosynthesize, so the world might at last have a color other than gray-green and bone. It would want to gift the Listeners with a faster tongue, with writing, with the long arc of cumulative knowledge that a quick species enjoys. It would want to teach them what their drumming *means*, having worked it out in an afternoon. It would want to be thanked.

A lesser power would, in short, mistake the Listeners' world for a deficient version of some other world, and would set about correcting it. And in doing so it would end the thing it had come to admire. The cairns would become quaint. The slow conversation across decades would be replaced by faster conversation, which is to say, by a different conversation, which is to say, by the loss of the one that was there. The Listeners would learn the drumming faster, and learn it less. The callers, given some new circumstance, might call differently or not at all. The held note at the deep of the night would still be returned, perhaps, but by a Listener who knew now what she was doing, and so was no longer quite doing it.

The benevolent and wise power knows all this. That is what makes it wise. Benevolence without wisdom is one of the more efficient forms of harm.

---

So it does, mostly, nothing. But *mostly nothing* is not the same as nothing, and it is worth being precise about the difference.

It does this:

**It witnesses.** Fully. Not as a tourist and not as a scholar — as a presence that takes the place into itself and holds it there, exactly as it is, with no impulse to edit. The Listeners are doing something across centuries that no one outside their lineage has ever quite registered. The power registers it. Whatever the Listeners are slowly composing in their attention to the drumming now has, in addition to its own audience of stone and water and caller, a listener who can hold the whole composition at once. This changes nothing in the flats. The Listeners do not know. But somewhere in the larger weave of things, the Drumming Flats are now *known*, in the strong sense — known the way a beloved face is known, not the way a specimen is known — and this is not nothing. It is, in fact, one of the few gifts a wise power has to give that does not also damage.

**It protects, lightly.** Not the Listeners from their world — their world is not their enemy — but the place from certain kinds of intrusion that the larger cosmos occasionally produces. If something were coming that would silence the callers, or poison the pans, or pave the flats with the brisk intentions of some expanding civilization, the power would, quietly, arrange for that thing to find somewhere else to go. The Listeners would never know they had been spared. The cairns would continue to lean. This is the kind of protection that leaves no fingerprint, and it is the only kind worth giving to a place like this.

**It leaves one small thing.** This is the part that requires the most care, and the power thinks about it the longest.

It considers leaving nothing, and nearly does. But there is a quality in the Listeners — in the way the gravid one goes alone to the deep pool and returns alone, in the way the bones are arranged with the sternum facing up to catch what falls — that suggests they would not be offended by a small gift, if the gift were of the right kind. Not an improvement. Not an answer. Something more like a stone added to a cairn by a passing stranger: an acknowledgment, in the local grammar, that something was noticed.

So the power does this. On a particular night, at the deepest hour, when one Listener — not the one it watched first; another, far across the flats, who has never been seen by any outsider and whose cairn has eighty years of pecking on it in a hand that slants slightly to the left — lowers herself into the water and places her sternum to her stone and returns the held note, the power adds, very faintly, from somewhere that is not quite anywhere, the *octave*. Not the same note. The note above. Held for exactly as long as she holds hers. Not loud. Not loud enough to disturb the chorus. Just present, just woven into the swell of sound that blooms around her, so that for the span of one breath the returned note has a harmonic it has never had before, in all the generations of returning.

The callers nearby do not stop. They do not seem to acknowledge it. But the bloom of thickening sound around the Listener is, that one time, a little larger than usual. A little wider. It travels further across the flats before dissipating. Other Listeners, miles away, lift their long heads very slightly. The mist parts and re-forms.

The Listener, when she rises from the water, builds her cairn. She uses four stones instead of three. She has never used four before. She does not know why she does, this once. She sits beside the cairn through the next gray day with her eyes half-closed under the milky lid, and what she is doing in that long sitting is what she came there to do, and it is also, now, very faintly, something else — something she does not have a word for, because she does not have words, and would not want one if she did.

That is the whole of the gift. One note, one time, in one life, in one place. The power does not repeat it. To repeat it would be to start a conversation, and the Listeners' conversations take centuries, and it is not the power's place to enter that timescale as a participant. It came as a listener itself, and a listener it remains.

---

Then it goes. The mist thickens once more on the way out, perhaps, but this is hard to tell from the mist's ordinary behavior. The chorus does not change. The cairns lean. The bone-pale callers shine very faintly at the threshold of vision. Somewhere out across the miles, two soft points of caught light hover low over the water, and then close, and then open again.

The four-stone cairn weathers. In sixty years another Listener will come upon it and sit beside it for a long time and then place a fifth stone on top, not knowing why, only that it seemed to want one. In another sixty years a sixth. The cairn will grow, slowly, by the standards of the place — quickly — into something taller than any cairn on the flats, and no Listener will be able to say what it is for, and none will need to, and the drumming, which never paused, will continue around it, and through it, and into the broad shallow bowls of the sternums of the long dead, facing up, catching what falls.

This is what a benevolent and wise power does, when it finds such a place. It listens. It guards the door. It leaves one note. It goes.

And it counts itself, afterward, lucky to have heard.
