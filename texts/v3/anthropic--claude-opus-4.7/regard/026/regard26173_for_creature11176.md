The benevolent and wise power considers the drowned city for a long time before doing anything at all.

This is the first thing about wisdom: it knows when its own gifts would be a kind of vandalism. The power has seen, across many places, the particular harm done by good intentions that arrive too quickly. The starving village given grain and never again learning its own fields. The shy creature lifted into safety and unmade by the lifting. The power has learned — slowly, the way the Quiet Ones learned the city — that to love a thing well is sometimes to refrain.

So the power sits with the basin for what would be, by surface measure, a very long while. Not deciding. Attending.

It notices what the Quiet Ones have made of themselves. Not a tragedy of decline — though it could be read that way, the limestone people thinned to two hundred souls in a cold place — but something stranger and harder to name. A way of being that has folded itself down into the exact shape of what surrounds it, until the folding and the surroundings are no longer distinguishable. The Quiet Ones are not surviving the city. They are one of the things the city does, the way dripping is a thing it does, the way silt-fall is a thing it does. To "save" them would be to lift them out of the very pattern that constitutes them.

The power notices, too, that they are not suffering. This matters. There is a kind of benevolence that cannot tell the difference between a life unlike its own and a life in pain, and rushes in to fix what was never broken. The Quiet Ones are cold, yes, but cold is their medium, not their affliction. They are few, yes, but fewness is the shape that fits the basin. They are slow, and the slowness is not a diminishment of some faster self they might have been; it is what they are.

And the power notices the cistern-elder. The long body in the deep pool. The thrumming hum that the others bring their small offerings to. The power understands — and this is part of why it is wise — that this is not something to be interpreted or improved. It is a completed thing. The elder has arrived at a mode of existence that the power, for all its breadth, recognizes as a kind of mastery: the mastery of having become entirely what one is, with nothing left over.

So. What does the power do?

It does very little, and what it does, it does so gently that even the Quiet Ones, who notice everything, only half-notice.

**It steadies the root-mat.** Not visibly. Not by intervention. But the great living ceiling has been thinning in places, over the last century, as some of the older alders above have died and not been replaced. The power, who can speak to growing things in the way one speaks to a sleeping child, encourages certain seedlings on the surface — a few more alders, a willow or two in the right spots — so that the ceiling will hold for another five hundred years, another thousand. It does this so slowly that no surface walker notices anything unusual about which trees took and which didn't.

**It keeps the sink-mouths few.** Where the peat has been weakening dangerously — where a collapse would let in too much light, too much surface, too much weather — the power thickens the mat. Not closing the existing openings; those are part of the city's breathing. Just preventing new wounds.

**It does not heal the cold.** It does not raise the temperature by a single degree. The cold is the city.

**It does not multiply the Quiet Ones.** Two hundred is what the basin holds. Two hundred is correct. The power has watched enough worlds to know that abundance is not always a blessing — that sometimes the gift is the constraint itself, the carrying capacity that keeps a way of life intact.

**It does, very occasionally, turn travelers away.** Not the right ones. The right ones — the quiet kind, the kind who might sit and learn the dripping — those it lets through, because the city has its own slow grammar for them and does not need help. But the loud kind, the ones with hammers or with cameras or with the particular hunger that wants to make a discovery of something, those find that their compasses go strange near certain sink-mouths, that their footing fails in a way that's frightening but not injurious, that the marsh seems to want them elsewhere. They go elsewhere. They tell no one, because there is nothing to tell; they just didn't feel like going down that hole.

**It sits with the cistern-elder.** This is the thing the power does that is most itself, and least explicable. It goes — in whatever way a benevolent power goes anywhere — down through the green-gold water, past the moss-pelts, into the deepest pool. And it rests there for a while, not speaking, not intervening, just present, the way one old being might sit with another. The cistern-elder, who is past conversation, registers the visit only as a slight warming of the cold, a slight thickening of the silence into something companionable. The power, for its part, learns something. What it learns is not transferable. But it carries the learning out with it, and is, in some small way, changed.

**It marks the city as known.** This is the most important thing, and the most invisible. There are powers in the wider weave of things — not all of them benevolent, not all of them wise — who pay attention to places that no one is paying attention to. An unobserved place is a vulnerable place. By turning its slow regard upon the basin, the power places the city under a kind of awareness that other powers will sense, the way wolves sense which sheep are watched. Nothing dramatic. No barrier. Just: this is seen. This is held in mind. Move along.

And then the power withdraws, mostly. Not entirely — it checks in, the way one checks on a old friend who lives far away and does not need calling but is glad, in some quiet way, to be remembered. Every few decades it passes through. Notices that the lintels are still being kept. Notices that the V-wake still crosses the central basin on certain still afternoons. Notices that a human, once in a great while, has learned to hum.

The power does not tell the Quiet Ones it exists. They would not want to know. Their religion, such as it is, is the thrumming hum across the cold water to the elder in the cistern, and this is enough, and is in fact perfect, and does not need a larger frame.

But sometimes, on the surface, a walker crossing the reedlands at dusk feels — for no reason they can name — that the ground beneath them is beloved. They pause. They look around at the ordinary marsh, the ordinary alders, the ordinary herons going home. They shake it off. They keep walking.

Below them, forty feet down, in the green-gold dusk, a pale four-foot figure is pressing moss back from a doorway with long webbed fingers, very slowly, with no awareness of being watched, and no need of any.

The drip continues. The city holds its quiet. The power, satisfied, turns its attention to the next thing that needs attending, which is somewhere very far away and entirely different, and which it will approach with the same patience, because patience is, in the end, the only gift large enough to give without harm.
