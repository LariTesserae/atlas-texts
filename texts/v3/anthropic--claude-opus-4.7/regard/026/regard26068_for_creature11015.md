## What the Power Would Do

The benevolent and wise power comes to Hunch-on-Vyr at the hour before the shift-bells, when the city is breathing, and does the first thing wisdom does: it stops at the parapet of the Ninth and looks.

It looks for a long time.

This is not a delay. This is the work. A power that arrived already knowing what to do would not be wise, only powerful, and the distinction matters here more than almost anywhere. Hunch-on-Vyr is not a problem presented for solution. It is a long answer to questions the power did not ask, worked out in brick and patience over three hundred years, and the first courtesy owed to such an answer is to read it.

So the power reads.

It reads the small wet figures in the wheel-house niches and understands that it has been *seen*, in the Vyrish sense — acknowledged, placed in the web, neither prayed to nor feared — and it is moved, because it is not often that a power is greeted this way. It reads the economy of small owed favors and sees that there is already a wiser thing at work in this city than itself: a slow, patient, total accounting of mutual debt that no decree could improve and any decree would damage. It reads the children running the rope-bridges in the dim hour and understands that *the city expects a few* is not callousness but a kind of grief held in advance, a grief budgeted for, and that to remove the falling would be to remove the running, and the running is part of what the children are for.

It reads the thinning swifts.

It reads the bulge in the lower retaining walls.

It reads the saxifrage in the seams.

And then it reads the Vyrish woman crossing the Fourth at change of shift with the bobbins and the child and the news, and it understands that she would not thank a power for making her load lighter. The load is what she is carrying. Take the load and you have not helped her; you have unmade a portion of her.

So the power sets aside almost everything it might have done.

It does not flatten the mountain or widen the terraces or part the cloud-wall above the seventh row of roofs. It does not pipe in clean dry sunlight for the dye-houses or replace the old patient engines with new ones that would roar. It does not abolish the shift-bells. It does not write a map that would stay true for a season. It does not even unbuild the tanneries, though it considers this for what would be, in human time, a long while, and decides against it on the grounds that the tanneries are part of the bargain, and the bargain holds, and a wise power does not break holding bargains from outside.

What the power does is smaller, and slower, and shaped to the place.

**It steadies the lower walls.** Not by replacing them — the bricks stay, the sweat stays, the slow tectonic creep stays — but by going down through the foundations laid on foundations laid on older still, down past memory to where there is no longer any earth, and there, in the dark, it sets a kind of patience under the patience already there. The walls will still bulge. They will bulge for another three hundred years without giving. The masons who shore them will not know why their work holds so well this generation; they will say *well enough* and mean it.

**It teaches the swifts a second smokestack.** There is a stack on the upper Tenth that has been cold for forty years and is structurally sound and updrafts beautifully on summer evenings, and the swifts have never found it because swifts find nests by inheritance and the inheritance was broken. The power, which can speak quietly to things that fly, mentions the stack to one swift. One is enough. In nine years there are swifts at dusk again in the numbers there were when the grandmothers were girls, and no one quite remembers being worried.

**It does nothing about the cats.** The cats are correct as they are.

**It leaves a thought in the mind of a particular engineer on the Sixth** — a woman who has been turning a problem over for eleven years concerning the sluice-channels and a small recurring poisoning of the wall-gardens downstream of the brass foundries. The thought is not a solution. The thought is the shape of the question rearranged so that her own next thought can be the solution. She will have it on a Wend-day morning over the thin sour beer of her quarter and she will think she thought of it herself, which is correct: she did. The power only turned the page.

**It touches, very lightly, the moss figures on the high terraces above the smokeline.** Not to change them. To greet them. They are older than the power expected and they greet it back, in their half-vegetable way, and an understanding passes between them that is none of the Vyrish's business and will never be. The butter on the sills tastes, faintly, of something good, for a season.

**It does not appear to anyone.** A power that appears has chosen spectacle over the work, and spectacle in Hunch-on-Vyr would be a kind of shouting, and you do not shout here. If pressed — and it is not pressed — the power would say that being seen by the Turners was enough being-seen for one visit. *Seen you.* Two fingers to the brow. Owed, and owed back.

**It marks one child.** This is the hardest thing it does and it does it with great care. There is a child of about nine, on the Eighth, who runs the parapets in the dim hour with the others and who is going to fall on a particular Thurs-evening in the coming spring in a way that would not be one of the few the city expects but one of the ones that breaks a family open. The power does not prevent the fall. Prevention at that scale, reaching in to pluck one outcome from the weave, is the kind of thing powers do when they have stopped being wise. What the power does is older and quieter: it makes sure that on that Thurs-evening a particular shutter on the Seventh is open that would otherwise have been closed, and a particular awning on the Sixth is taut that would otherwise have been slack, and the child falls the way Vyrish children have always sometimes fallen and is caught by the city the way the city sometimes catches them, and goes home with a sprained wrist and a story, and the family stays whole. The power does not take credit. The child grows up and is, in time, a good shorer of walls.

**And it leaves a gift that is not a gift.** Somewhere on the long stair between the Third and the Fourth, in a joint of brick where saxifrage has not yet taken hold, the power presses a thumb — gently, the way the Vyrish press the heads of the Turners. Nothing grows there that would not have grown. Nothing changes that anyone would notice. But the brick at that spot will, for as long as the city stands, be very slightly warm to the touch on cold mornings, and over the generations a small habit will form, unspoken and unexplained, of pausing there a moment on the climb, of resting a palm against the wall, of going on. The Vyrish will not make a Turner of it. They are too tactful for that. They will only, sometimes, on the way up to a hard shift, touch the warm brick and feel acknowledged by something that does not name itself, and continue, and arrive at the loom or the vat or the wheel a little more whole than they would have been.

Then the power goes.

It goes the way it came, which is to say without coming or going in any sense the Vyrish would recognize, and it carries with it a kind of quiet that it did not have before — the quiet of a place that taught it something. Powers are not supposed to be taught. This one is wise enough to be, and wiser for it.

Behind it, the shift-bells ring. They ring at the demand of the works, not at the hour. Twenty thousand people pour out of the tenements and up the stairs and down the ladders, and twenty thousand pour the other way, and the streams cross in the stairwells and do not collide, and the gas-windows of one quarter go dark while another quarter blooms.

The city hums.

The water tips from flue to copper hood three streets away.

Somewhere, very faint, someone is singing in a language the power almost catches and chooses, out of courtesy, not to.

*Seen you*, says Hunch-on-Vyr, in its quiet baffled way, to whatever has just passed through.

And the power, going, touches two fingers to where its brow would be, and does not look back, because looking back would be a kind of holding on, and the bargain here is that things are let go, slowly, all one's life, until the books are roughly balanced.

The books are roughly balanced.

The wheels keep turning.
