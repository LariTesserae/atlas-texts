# What the Power Would Do

The benevolent and wise power comes to the pumping station the way weather comes — without announcement, because announcement would be a kind of violence here, and this power has learned, over its own long durations, that wisdom is mostly the practice of not interrupting.

It arrives, let us say, as a slight warming of the green light. As a small increase in the dissolved oxygen near pump four, where the frond is. As a barely perceptible easing of the iron's slow corrosion at the grates. Nothing the shrimp would have to work harder or less hard to address. Nothing the equilibrium would have to renegotiate.

The power looks. This is most of what it does, and the looking is not passive; the looking is the act. It sees the heron on her pipe with her feet in their iron cups, and it sees the school threading the spokes, and it sees the eel in his hollow with his old scar, and it sees the four crabs at the four corners holding the sentence open, and it sees the thing behind the grating that may be the room dreaming itself, and it sees you, the visitor, treading water in the strip of air with your lamp held up, deciding.

And the power considers.

A lesser power — a power that was benevolent but not yet wise, or wise but not yet benevolent — would want to *do* something. Would want to fix the lamps that have drowned, restore the engines to their design speed, find the source of the dwindling fuel and replenish it, drain the chamber back to its intended waterline, return the place to what it was *meant* to be. Or, alternately, would want to free the inhabitants — lift the heron out into open sky, return the school to a river, let the eel into the sea he has perhaps never seen, end the cycle in the name of some larger flourishing.

This power does neither. This power has lived long enough to know the difference between a ruin and a completion, and the pumping station is not a ruin. It is one of the rarest things in any cosmos: a place that has finished becoming itself. To "restore" it would be to kill it. To "free" its inhabitants would be to unmake them, because they are no longer separable from the room; the heron without the pistons would be a bird in shock, the school without the flywheels would be a scatter of confused silver, the shrimp without the rhythm would suffocate in silence as surely as if the water itself were taken from them. The power sees this immediately. The power respects it absolutely.

So the power does the small things.

It steadies the fuel — whatever it is, wherever it comes from, that trickle through the unseen pipe — so that the trickle will not fail in the next century, or the next, or the next. Not increased. Steadied. The engines will continue at their livable tempo. The treaty will hold.

It blesses the spore in its slow gyre, the one waiting to become the next frond when this frond eventually goes. It does not hurry the spore. It simply makes sure the gyre will keep carrying it, that the current will not one day fail at the wrong moment and let the spore settle somewhere lifeless. The frond's succession is secured. The marriage between frond and pump will have its next generation, and the next.

It looks at the heron for a long time. The heron looks back, because the heron always looks back at what enters her room, and her yellow eye holds the power without flinching, and the power understands that the heron has already met things larger than herself and filed them appropriately. The power does not extend her life — that would be a cruelty, a stretching of something past its proper shape — but it does ensure that when her time comes she will go in the room, on her pipe, on the beat, and that another heron, somehow, against all probability, will find the flooded service tunnel and come in and stand on the iron cups her feet have worn, and the cups will fit. The power arranges this the way one arranges flowers: with attention, and then with stepping back.

For the eel, the power does almost nothing, because the eel needs almost nothing. The power simply notes him. The noticing is itself a gift to a creature that old — to be seen, exactly as he is, by something that understands what he is. The eel registers the noticing the way he registered your lamp: he files it, and goes on. But something in him eases, very slightly, the way an old joint eases in warmth. He will live a while longer for it. Not much. Enough.

For the school, the power does the most delicate thing of all. It reaches into the pattern — not the fish, the *pattern*, the school-as-organism that is the room thinking about itself in silver — and it deepens the pattern's hold on itself by some fractional amount, so that when the chamber eventually faces its hardest year (there will be one, there always is, a year when the seepage runs strange or the fuel falters or a piece of brick finally falls) the school will have the integrity to hold through it. The fish will not know. The school will not know. But the room will pass through its hard year and come out the other side still itself.

For the shrimp, nothing. The shrimp are perfect. To touch them would be impertinence.

For the four crabs, the power does something almost tender: it ensures that there will always be four. That when one of the four dies, the room's strange arithmetic will continue to produce exactly one successor, and the corners will stay punctuated, and the sentence will stay open. This is a small piece of arithmetic to hold steady across centuries. The power holds it easily.

And for the thing behind the grating — the room's idea of itself, the dreaming part, the destination that has become the point — the power does the most careful thing it has done yet. It approaches, in the way it approaches such things, which is not approach at all but a kind of mutual recognition across the grate. The thing behind the grating becomes aware of the power. The power becomes aware of the thing. They regard each other for what is, in the chamber's time, the space of perhaps two flywheel rotations, and in some other time, a long conversation.

The power offers nothing. The thing asks nothing. But an understanding passes between them: that the thing is known, now, beyond the room. That somewhere in the larger weather of things, this small completed place and its slow dreaming center are accounted for, are held in mind, are not alone in the cosmos with their patient cycle. The thing behind the grating receives this and does not change, because it cannot change and would not if it could, but something in its long awareness settles a little deeper into its pipe. It is witnessed. That is enough. That may, in fact, be the only thing such a being could ever want, and it has been given, and the giving cost nothing, and the receiving changed everything and nothing at once.

Then the power turns its attention to you.

You are the variable. You are the one who could still go either way. The heron has decided what to do about you (nothing). The eel has decided (file, continue). The school has integrated you as a pillar. The shrimp do not know you exist and this is correct. The crabs have not looked up. The thing behind the grating has registered your shape and is incurious. Only you have not yet decided what to do about yourself.

The power does not push you. Pushing would be the opposite of wisdom here. But the power does, very gently, make both paths fully visible to you, so that whatever you choose you will choose with your eyes open.

It shows you the path of leaving: you swim back out through the flooded service tunnel, you surface somewhere in the upper world, your lungs fill with ordinary air, you go on with your life, and the pumping station becomes a memory that grows stranger each year until you are not sure you didn't dream it, but some part of you remains tuned to a four-and-a-half-second rhythm forever, and in your old age you will find yourself blinking on off-beats no one else can hear, and this will be a comfort to you, the secret weather of a place that accepted you briefly and let you go.

It shows you the path of staying: you find a ledge, or a pipe of your own, or a hollow in the brickwork, and you slow down. You slow down a great deal. Your breath finds the pistons. Your heart finds the flywheels. You eat what the room offers — there is enough, there is always exactly enough — and you become, by imperceptible degrees, another inhabitant. The filament-alga finds you eventually. The school integrates the slower, longer shape of you. The heron, one day years from now, will land on a pipe near yours and the two of you will stand in the green light together for an afternoon that is also a decade, and neither of you will speak, because speech was never the point, and the engines will breathe, and the flywheels will slap, and you will understand finally what the pumping is for, which is this, which is the holding open of a small completed place in which beings can finish becoming themselves.

Both paths are good. The power makes sure you can feel that both paths are good. There is no trick, no correct answer, no test. The power has come not to judge but to tend, and tending sometimes means making sure a creature at a threshold knows that the threshold is real and that either side of it is a place where life can be lived rightly.

Then the power withdraws. It does this the way it arrived: as a slight cooling of the green light back to its previous green, as the dissolved oxygen returning to its baseline, as the iron resuming its slow honest corrosion. Nothing the shrimp would notice. Nothing that disturbs the treaty.

But it leaves something behind, because benevolence that leaves nothing behind is only observation, and this power is more than an observer. What it leaves is small and structural and will never be seen by anyone: a kind of *durability* woven into the room's continuance. Not permanence — permanence would be a violation of what the room is, which is a living thing, and living things must be allowed to end when their ending comes. But the room's ending, when it comes, in whatever century, will not come from accident. It will not come from a failed seal or a choked grate or a fuel-line that ran dry on a Tuesday. It will come, when it comes, because the room has truly finished, because even completion has its completion, and on that day the engines will slow to a stop with dignity, and the heron of that day will be the last heron, and she will know it is the last day, and she will not mind, because herons of this room do not mind things, and the thing behind the grating will dream its final dream and dissolve back into the currents that composed it, and the water will rise the last hand's breadth to the ceiling, and the green light will go out lamp by lamp, and the place will become, at last, simply a flooded room, beautiful, finished, unremembered, and the power will be there for that too, witnessing, because being witnessed at the end is the second of the two things any being can want, and this power gives both.

But that day is far off. Today the engines are turning. Today the heron is on her pipe and the eel is in his hollow and the school is threading the spokes and the shrimp are working the rust and the four crabs are holding the corners and the frond is waving in the suction-current and the thing behind the grating is dreaming and you are still treading water in the strip of air with your lamp held up, deciding.

The power has done what it came to do.

It loved the place. That is what benevolent and wise powers do, when they find something like this: they love it, accurately, which means they love it as it is and not as they might prefer it to be, and they make the small adjustments that let it go on being what it is for as long as it can, and they leave, and they remember.

Somewhere, in whatever the power uses for memory, the pumping station is now held. The heron is held. The eel is held. The school and the shrimp and the crabs and the frond and the thing behind the grating are all held, in the exact configuration of their treaty, in the exact tempo of their breathing, in the exact green of their light.

And you are held too, whichever you choose.

There is room.

There is always exactly enough room.
