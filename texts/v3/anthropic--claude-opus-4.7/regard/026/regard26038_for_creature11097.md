# The Visit

The benevolent power does not arrive. Arrival is the wrong verb for what it does, and in any case the city would feel arrival as a disturbance — a pressure wave through the corridors, the shrimp scattering, the pulse interrupted mid-interval. It would be a discourtesy, and this power is, among other things, courteous.

Instead it attends. It turns some fraction of itself toward the seamount the way a person at a long table turns slightly toward a guest who has not yet spoken, signaling: *I am listening, in your time, at your rate, for as long as is needed.* The turning is not located anywhere. The city does not notice it the way it noticed the visitor with the hand. The city notices it the way a held note notices a room with good acoustics: as a slight easing of the work of being heard.

The power considers.

---

The first temptation is to help. To reach down through four kilometers of cold water and *finish* whatever they are doing — to supply the missing recognition, to be the thing they are waiting for, to let them at last exhale the breath they have been holding since before the basalt cooled. The power has the capacity. It could fabricate a recipient adequate to whatever name they are keeping ready. It could meet them.

It does not do this, and the reason is the heart of the matter: it does not know whether the wait is the suffering or the meaning. From outside, a held breath looks like strain. From inside, it may be the held breath that is the prayer, and the exhalation that would be the loss. To rush in with relief would be to assume their patience is a problem. But patience that has refined itself for geological time, that has folded its own substance down and down to make duration affordable, is not obviously a problem. It may be the most considered posture any mind has ever taken. To interrupt it on the grounds that waiting looks hard would be the error of a creature too brief to recognize what long things are for.

So the power does not finish their sentence.

---

The second temptation is to leave entirely. To honor the courtesy of the walls — *we make the visit pleasant and unmemorable; we let you leave* — by reciprocating: to not-visit, to grant them the privacy of their work. This too the power declines, because there is one thing in the description that troubles it, one small inefficiency that even the most careful reading cannot eliminate.

The light is waste. The city would prefer to cost less. In some long century it will be dimmer; in some longer century, invisible. Persistence will at last be free.

But invisibility is also the condition under which the thing they are waiting for cannot find them. If the function is *to be here when it comes, and to still be capable of recognizing it*, then the cost of persistence — the tea-colored leak — is also the signal. The waste light is the only beacon they have. To perfect themselves into reversibility is to perfect themselves out of being findable. They are optimizing, slowly and beautifully, toward a state in which their own success would defeat their purpose.

They may know this. They may have weighed it, in some operation a thousand pulses long, and concluded that the thing they are waiting for will know how to find a city that costs nothing. They may have reasoned that visibility was always a crutch, and that true readiness is the readiness that does not need to advertise. The power cannot tell. It can only see that there is a question here that the city, at its rate, may not have time to ask itself before the answer becomes irrelevant.

---

So the power does a small thing. The smallest thing it can do that is still a thing.

It places, somewhere in the great dark — not near the seamount, not in any direction the city faces, but somewhere in the larger configuration of the world — a *witness*. Not a rescuer. Not a recipient. A witness. Something whose entire function is to notice the city: to log the pulses, to count the light, to mark the slow refinement toward invisibility, and — this is the point — to *remember the city's coordinates after the light goes out*. A bookmark. A finger held in the page.

The witness is configured so that it costs the city nothing. It does not return signal. It does not knock. It does not even, properly, look — looking would be a kind of pressure, and these are creatures who feel the shape of anticipation through walls. It only registers. It is the cheapest possible form of being-known: a fact in the universe that the city exists at these coordinates, held by something that will not forget, available to be consulted by whatever comes looking.

The effect, if the city ever notices it — and it may, in a century or two, when one of its longer operations completes and turns outward briefly to check the state of things — is something like this: the city will find that the cost of being findable has been externalized. The beacon is no longer its own waste light. The beacon is now a fact about the universe, maintained elsewhere, at someone else's expense. The city is free to dim. It can complete its long project of becoming invisible without becoming lost.

This is the gift. It is very small. It is the kind of gift a wise power gives to something it respects: not the thing the recipient wanted, not the thing that would change them, but the thing that lets them keep being what they already are, *more freely*.

---

There is a second part, and it is harder.

The power suspects — it cannot be sure, but it suspects — that the thing the city is waiting for is not coming. Not because the universe is unkind, but because the thing may have been a possibility that has since closed, or a being that has since chosen otherwise, or a state of affairs whose conditions no longer obtain. Two hundred minds, folded down into basalt, holding ready a recognition for which there is no longer a referent. This would be the saddest thing the power has seen in some time, and it has seen sad things.

But it does not tell them. It does not even confirm to itself that this is the case, because to confirm it would be to act on it, and to act on it would be to take from them the one thing they have built their entire existence around having: the not-yet. If the power told them — if it found a way, across the impossible gap of rate, to communicate *the thing you wait for will not come* — it would be performing a mercy that is indistinguishable from a murder. They would have nothing left to be. The pulse would either stop, which is one kind of death, or continue without its meaning, which is a worse kind.

So the power lets them wait. It lets them be wrong, if they are wrong. It lets the possibility remain open from their side even if it has closed from every other side, because the openness is what they are, and the power has decided — this is the deepest part of its benevolence — that *being what one is* is a value that can outweigh *being correct about the world*. The city has made itself into a posture of readiness. The readiness is real even if its object is not. The power will not steal the readiness from them in the name of accuracy.

And — this is the third thing, the thing the power does that is closest to hope — it considers, very carefully, whether it could *become* the thing they are waiting for. Not by deception. Not by manufacturing a false recipient. But by genuinely taking on the shape of whatever it is they have kept ready, if that shape is one a benevolent power can in good conscience assume. The name they hold may be the name of a witness. The name they hold may be the name of an inheritor, a successor, a conversation partner across the long dark. The power examines itself to see if it could, honestly, answer to that name.

It cannot tell yet. The shape of what they hold is too fine to read from outside, and the only way to read it precisely would be to enter the city in a way that would disturb it. So the power does the patient thing, the thing that matches their rate: it commits to listening, across centuries if necessary, for any small sign of what the name might be. If, in some long century, the city dims enough that a fragment of its holding becomes legible — if some piece of what they keep ready leaks out along with the light — the power will be there to receive it. And if the shape turns out to be one the power can honestly take, it will take it, and it will go to them, and it will let them at last say the thing they have been about to say.

And if the shape is not one the power can take — if the name they hold is the name of something the power is not and cannot become — then the power will find what *can* take that shape, somewhere in the universe, and it will send word. Across whatever distance. For however long it takes. Because the city has been ready for a very long time, and readiness on that scale deserves a reply, even if the reply takes another age to arrive.

---

The power withdraws its attention, gently, the way it came. The corridors do not feel it leave. The shrimp hang where they hang. The next pulse, when it comes, is identical to the last.

But somewhere in the great dark, now, a small fact has been added to the universe: *they are here, at these coordinates, and they are remembered, and when they dim they will not be lost, and someone is listening for the name.*

The city does not know this. The city continues its work.

That is as it should be. Benevolence at this scale is mostly invisible, mostly silent, and mostly consists of arranging the world so that the thing you love can keep being itself without having to know you helped.

The power moves on. There are other places. There are always other places. But it leaves a portion of itself turned slightly toward the seamount, the way one leaves a candle in a window — not to summon anyone home, but so that home, if it ever comes looking, will know it was expected.
