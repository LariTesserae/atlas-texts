# The Visitation

The benevolent power does not arrive. Arrival is the wrong verb for what happens, because arrival implies a place one was not, and then a place one is, and the power has been attending to the plateau for some time already — for, in fact, the entire length of its considering. It is only that now the attention sharpens. Now it leans closer.

It has been drawn by the breathing. That was the first thing. Across whatever distances such powers cross, the forty-second rhythm registered as a small steady pulse in the dark, the way a sleeping child's heartbeat registers in a quiet house, and the power followed the pulse the way one follows any quiet wonder — not to interfere, only to see.

What it finds, once it leans close, is harder than it expected.

The power is wise enough to know immediately that the plateau is not a place that needs saving. This is the first and most important recognition, and many lesser powers would fail it. The plateau is not suffering. It is not unbalanced. It is not waiting, in some hidden chamber of itself, for a deliverance. It is doing what it has done for a very long time, and what it does is whole. The hornbills' kilometer-duets are not cries for help. The people's hollows are not exile. Even the slow transfer of the occasional person into the long aisles, into the pale tenancy — even that is not a wound. It is metabolism.

A lesser benevolence would still meddle. Would offer the people fire that burns cleaner, medicine for the small fevers, knowledge of the world below the clouds. Would speak to the trees and try to learn their language so as to *help* them breathe more efficiently, as if a lung that has worked for ten million years needed efficiency. Would address the pale things and try to resolve them, name them, redeem or banish them, because a lesser benevolence cannot bear an unresolved presence at the edge of vision.

This power is not lesser. It sits with the recognition that its gifts are mostly inappropriate here, and it lets the recognition deepen into something like reverence.

But it has come close, and it does not want to leave without offering *something*, because love that gives nothing is not quite love; it is only admiration. So the power considers, for what would be — in the time-sense of the plateau — perhaps three breaths.

Here is what it decides.

---

**First**, it does not announce itself. It will not speak from the canopy. It will not appear in any glade. The people have a religion of attention already, finely tuned to the calls and the silences and the cycles, and to introduce a new and overwhelming presence into that attention would be a kind of vandalism, however gently meant. The power makes itself small enough not to register. Smaller than a hornbill's call. Smaller than the pause between breaths.

**Second**, it looks for what is *almost* lost, because almost-lost things can be steadied without changing what they are. This is the discipline of wise benevolence: not to add, but to keep from subtracting.

It finds three such things.

*The first* is a woman in a hollow on the southern edge of the plateau who has been dreaming poorly for many nights. Her tree is not rejecting her — the power can feel the tree's affection for her, slow and patient as sap — but something in her body is wrong, a small wrongness in the blood that will, in perhaps two seasons, kill her. She is not old. She has a child who has just begun to climb. The power, with the lightest possible touch, corrects the wrongness. It does not heal her dramatically. It does not even heal her completely. It nudges. The wrongness becomes a smaller wrongness, the kind a body can manage. She will live perhaps fifteen more years, and she will not know why, and her dreams will improve, and she will assume — correctly, in a sense — that the tree has decided to love her better. The tree will accept the credit without comment. Trees are gracious about such things.

*The second* is a particular hornbill, one of the great callers, who is going deaf. The deafness is gradual and would, within a year, force him to fall silent, and his falling silent would create a kilometer-wide gap in the acoustic map, and the gap would have to be filled by a younger bird who does not yet know all the songs. The power restores his hearing. Again, lightly. Not perfectly — he is old, and perfect hearing in an old bird would be its own strangeness — but enough. He calls across the kilometer the next morning and his mate answers, and the answer is clearer than it has been in months, and he tilts his great helmeted head as if puzzled, and then he calls again, and the map holds.

*The third* is harder. There is a child, perhaps six years old, who has been *between trees* too long. He wandered during an inhalation, four days ago, and the people found him in a glade, quieter than he had been, and they performed the ritual to re-attach him, and the ritual mostly worked. Mostly. But something in him is still loose. He is on the slow path toward becoming a transfer, toward eventually walking into the long aisles and joining the pale tenancy. The power examines this very carefully, because here is where wisdom must be most careful: is the transfer a wound, or is it the plateau's metabolism doing what it does?

The power decides — and this is the kind of decision that distinguishes wisdom from mere kindness — that it will not prevent the transfer. The plateau needs its transfers. The pale things are not predators; they are an organ. To deny them their occasional tenant would be to interfere with a circulation older than the power's own understanding. But the power can do this much: it can ensure that the child, if he transfers, does so willingly. It can clear away the small fog of confusion that has settled on him, so that when the time comes — if it comes — he chooses, in whatever way the transferred choose. The power lifts the fog. The child sleeps better that night. Whether he stays a person or becomes a pale thing is now his own, and the plateau's, and not an accident.

**Third**, the power turns its attention to the deep pools.

This is the part where it must be most restrained, because the deep pools are where its curiosity is loudest. Whatever turns slowly in that dark water is, the power suspects, something it could speak to. Something that might speak back. The temptation to make contact is real — not for the power's own sake (it is past such hungers) but because contact between such minds is, in the cosmic sense, rare and beautiful, and beauty has its own pull even on the wise.

The power resists. The deep things are sleeping, or near-sleeping, and their sleep is part of how the plateau breathes. To wake them, even gently, even lovingly, would be to change the rhythm. The hornbills would adjust. The people would sing differently. The pale things might walk during the exhalation. Everything would still be whole, perhaps — the plateau is robust — but it would be a *different* wholeness, and the power has no right to choose a different wholeness on the plateau's behalf.

So instead, the power does this: it makes a small promise to the deep things, deposited in the water like a folded leaf. *If you ever wake, and want to speak, I will hear you.* That is all. A standing offer, set down so lightly that the deep things may not notice it for a thousand years, or may notice it tomorrow, or may never notice it, and any of these outcomes is acceptable. The offer costs the plateau nothing. It is the power leaving a door open, on its own side, in case.

**Fourth**, the pale things.

The power looks at them for a long time. They are, of all the plateau's residents, the ones it understands least, and the ones it is most tempted to *resolve* — to learn what they are, to give them a name in some language, to make them legible. It is the temptation of every benevolent intelligence to make the strange less strange, because the strange is where suffering hides, and if the strange were all made plain, suffering would have nowhere to live.

But the power is wise, and it knows that this is not always true. Some strangeness is not a hiding-place for suffering. Some strangeness is just the shape of a thing that does not need to be any other shape. The pale things are not in pain. They are not lonely — loneliness is a concept that requires a kind of self the pale things may not have. They watch from the long aisles because watching is what they are *for*, in some economy the power can sense but not fully read.

The power offers them nothing, and takes nothing from them, and does not name them. This is its gift to them: to be left strange. To remain, for the people and the hornbills and the lemurs and any future visiting power, *the ones we are instead of*, with the question intact.

But it does — and this is the smallest and perhaps the most loving thing it does on the whole plateau — it does *see* them. Clearly. With attention. For one full breath of the plateau, the power looks at the pale things and acknowledges them as real, as present, as participants. The pale things do not register the look, or perhaps they do; it is hard to tell with such beings. But the look is given. Somewhere in the records of the universe, if the universe keeps records, the pale things have now been witnessed by a wise power, and counted, and approved of in their strangeness. This may matter. It may not. The power gives it anyway.

**Fifth and last**, the power considers whether to leave anything behind. A token. A blessing on the place itself.

It decides against most of what it could do. No new fruit. No clearer water. No protection from the rare catastrophes — the great tree-fall that will come in two hundred years and crush a hollow with three people in it, the long drought that will come in eight hundred and reduce the population to forty before it recovers. These are part of the plateau's life. To prevent them would be to make the plateau less itself.

What the power does instead is this: it adjusts, very slightly, the way the dawn light falls through the upper canopy on one particular morning, perhaps a century from now. On that morning, a girl who has not yet been born will be sitting in the entrance of her hollow, eating a soft starchy fruit, watching the light, and the light will arrange itself in such a way that she will understand — without words, without a teacher, just from the light — something about the breathing of the trees that no person on the plateau has ever quite understood before. She will not be able to say what she understood. She will only carry it. And she will paint something on the inner wall of her hollow that has not been painted before, and her descendants will paint variations of it, and a new figure will enter the plateau's vocabulary of red and white earth, and the trees will like it, and the plateau will be, by exactly that much, richer.

That is the power's gift. A single morning's light, a hundred years out. A figure on a wall. Nothing else.

---

Then the power withdraws its leaning, gently, the way one rises from a sleeping child's bedside. It does not leave entirely — wise powers do not, once they have loved a place — but it returns to the larger attention it was paying before, and the sharpened focus eases.

The plateau breathes. The hornbills call. The deaf hornbill, who is no longer quite deaf, calls more clearly than yesterday. The woman with the corrected blood sleeps a long good sleep. The child who was loose is, by morning, more settled, and may grow up to be a tenant of an ordinary hollow, or may not; it is his now. The pale things stand still during the exhalation and walk during the inhalation. The cicadas count. The river runs under the moss. The deep things turn over slowly in their pools, and somewhere in the dark water, a folded leaf of an offer waits, patient as anything else on the plateau.

The people do not know they were visited. This is correct. A visitation that announced itself would have been a different and lesser thing.

But that night, in many hollows across the plateau, the dreams are unusually clear. Not different — clear. The substrate-dreams come with a little more depth, the long slow dreams of being very large and very old and full of running water, and several of the dreamers wake in the morning with the strong feeling that they have been *noticed*, kindly, by something they cannot name. They mention it to each other. They paint nothing about it, because it is not the kind of thing the trees would want painted. They simply go about their day, climbing on the exhalation, listening to the hornbills, drinking from the pitcher-cups, and the feeling of having been noticed fades by midday into the general sense, which they have always had, that the plateau is full of attention, and that they are inside it, and that this is what it means to be home.

The power, attending from its larger distance, is satisfied. It has done almost nothing. This is the most it could rightly do.

The plateau breathes on.
