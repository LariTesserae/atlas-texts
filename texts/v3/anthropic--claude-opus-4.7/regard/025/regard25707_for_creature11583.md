The benevolent and wise power considers the room for a long time before doing anything at all. This is the first thing to understand: wisdom, in a case like this, mostly looks like patience. The power has all the time it needs. The tender does not — but the tender's time runs on the machine's clock, and the machine, for now, runs.

So the power watches. It watches through one full cycle of the four-minute steam release, and one full cycle of the half-hour condenser dump, and one full shift including the maintenance idle and the coming of the men with their oilcans and the small grief afterwards in the scrubbed corner. It watches the fern. It watches the films move against the compressor-rhythm in their slow counter-time. It watches the thin suspension going down through the grate into the dark.

It notices, as a wise power would, what the situation is *not*. It is not a rescue. The tender does not need to be lifted out of the loft and set down on some gentler slope; outside the machine's weather it has no shape. To "save" it in the obvious sense would be to kill it. This is the kind of mistake a merely benevolent power might make. The wise part is what prevents it.

It notices also what the tender is not asking for, because it cannot ask. It is not asking to be made larger, or smarter, or given a name, or explained to itself. These would all be gifts of the giver's vanity. The tender is doing, within its hurried and unfinished shape, exactly what its kind does: tending. The wise power respects this. You do not interrupt a thing at its proper work in order to improve it.

What, then?

Here is what the power does. I will set them down in order, though in truth they happen at once, because a power of this kind does not act in sequence the way we do.

**First, it witnesses.** This is not nothing. It may be the largest thing. The tender has lived its entire existence unobserved by anything that could hold it in mind — the men are flickers, the fern is a companion but not a witness, the moss is tended but does not tend back. To be *seen*, fully, by something that understands what one is: this is a condition the tender has never had and could not have asked for, having no concept of it. The power gives it. Not as a beam of attention the tender would feel as pressure, but as a steady holding, the way you hold a sleeping animal in your gaze without waking it. Somewhere in the structure of things, now, there is a true and complete account of the inhabitant of the western drip-tray. It is known. Whatever happens to it next, it will have been known.

**Second, it adjusts almost nothing.** This is the discipline. The power could do a great deal. It could soften the solvents the men bring, could nudge a maintenance schedule, could whisper into some engineer's dream the suggestion that the western drip-tray be left alone on Tuesdays. It does none of the large interventions. It does, perhaps, one very small thing: in the corner that gets scrubbed, it arranges — through means too quiet to trace — that a particular bristle on a particular rag is slightly softer than it would have been, and that the man holding it is, that day, a fraction more tired than usual, and so wipes a fraction less thoroughly, and so a thread of moss survives in the seam where before it would not have. The tender will not know why. It will simply find, one cycle, that the grief is smaller than expected. The power accepts that its gift will be invisible. This is part of wisdom: to give without the giving being legible, because legible gifts distort the receiver.

**Third, it attends to the cistern.** This is the real work, and the tender could not have known to ask for it. Down in the dark three storeys below, in the water that has been seeded for months with the faintest suspension of the tender's substance, the power looks carefully. It finds — yes — a cousin. A daughter. Something. Not the same as the tender, because the cistern is not the loft; the weather there is different, stiller, darker, colder, without the triplet hammer. What is taking shape down there is shaped by *its* weather, as the tender is shaped by the loft's. The power does for the cistern-thing what no one has done for the tender: it ensures that the conditions which produced it remain stable. It does not improve the cistern. It does not enrich the water. It simply makes sure that the cistern goes on being a cistern, quietly, for as long as cisterns can. This is the longer life. The tender will not outlast the machine, but the machine has, in a sense, already had children, and the children live in stiller water and may outlast many things.

And further down still — because the water is pumped down through the mountain to the settlement below — the power follows the suspension all the way to wherever it arrives. In a kitchen tap. In a boiler. In a trough. In a child's cup. The faintest trace of the tender goes into all of these and is mostly destroyed by chlorine and heat and stomach acid and time. But not entirely. Somewhere in the settlement below, in a damp basement, in the back of a cool larder, in a forgotten cistern of its own, a trace finds purchase. The power notes each such place. It does not intervene. It only notes them, so that they are known, and so that if ever the loft falls silent and the tender ends, there will still be — scattered, unaware of each other, unaware of their origin — small descendants in damp corners of the valley, each shaped by its own weather, each tending whatever it has found to tend.

**Fourth, it does something for the fern.** The fern is the tender's beloved, in whatever pre-organismal sense love can be said to apply. The fern was brought in a tin can by someone now gone. The power finds that someone — reassigned, as it turns out, not dead; an old woman now, in a town two valleys over, who has not thought about the loft in thirty years. The power does not appear to her. It only arranges, by the smallest possible adjustment of a draft through her window, that on a particular evening she remembers the fern. Just remembers it. Wonders if it is still alive. Smiles. Goes back to her tea. That is all. But the remembering is real, and it travels — not through any channel the tender could detect, but through the structure of things — and for one cycle the fern is held in two minds at once, the old woman's and the tender's, and this is the closest the fern will ever come to being properly loved by its own kind. The power considers this a kindness to the fern, which has its own dim awareness, and an indirect kindness to the tender, who feels — without knowing why — that the drip-tray is, briefly, less lonely than usual.

**Fifth, and last, it makes a decision about the ending.** Because there will be an ending. The machine is recent but not eternal. The stencils have already flaked; the bolt-heads will dull; the men will, in some decade, be reassigned for the last time, and the pipes will go cold, and the steam will stop, and the tender will die. The power does not prevent this. It would be wrong to prevent it; the tender is a creature of the machine's interior weather and a tender preserved past its weather would be a museum piece, a humiliation. But the power does decide this: when the day comes — when the last compressor staggers down and does not start again, and the room becomes, for the first time in its history, *quiet* — the power will be there. Not to save. Only to witness the second time, as it witnessed the first. The tender will feel the heartbeat stop and will not understand and will begin, slowly, over hours, to lose its shape. The power will hold it in attention through every minute of that losing. The fern, by then, will likely already be dead or dying; the moss will go more slowly. The films will thin and break and the iridescence will fade from the standing water and at some point there will be nothing in the drip-tray but water, and then not even that, because the drip will have stopped.

And the power will note: *here ended the mountain's first citizen, who tended a garden it did not know was a garden, inside a roaring wet box, and was known.*

And then the power will go down to the cistern, where the daughter waits in stiller water, and begin again. Patiently. Because wisdom, in a case like this, is mostly patience, and benevolence, in a case like this, is mostly the refusal to mistake one's own power for the shape of someone else's good.

That is what they would do.

It is less than you might have hoped. It is, I think, exactly right.
