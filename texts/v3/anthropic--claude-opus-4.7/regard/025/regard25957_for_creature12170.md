The benevolent and wise power comes down the stair.

This is the first thing to understand: that such a power, being wise, would come the way anyone comes — on foot, by the forty steps, letting the air thicken around them as they descend. Not arriving. Not manifesting. Arriving and manifesting are what unwise powers do, and the disturbance they cause is the first sign of their unwisdom. A wise power knows that to enter a place is already to change it, and that the change should be no larger than the entry requires.

So: down the stair, slowly, in a body small enough not to stoop. Wrapped, perhaps, in bast-cloth borrowed at the top, because the surface clothes would smell wrong and the smell would precede the visitor into every lane.

At the bottom, the power stands for a long time and listens.

This is the second thing. The listening is not reconnaissance. The power is not gathering data toward a decision already half-made. The power is doing what the place asks of anyone who enters it, which is to learn the shape of the room from the drip, and the cadence of speech from the whump, and to let the hitch settle into its own breathing before it tries to speak. This takes, let us say, a season. A wise power has time. That is part of what wisdom is.

During the season the power does small things. It learns which column is which. It learns the names of three vat-tenders and the particular sour-sweet signature of their vats. It learns the word for becoming-pillar and does not write it down, because writing it down would be to fix it, and the word is not fixed; it moves a little, generation to generation, the way the lanes move. It eats the brown bread. It sleeps in a hollowed pocket lent by a family whose grandmother is far enough into her green that one room has gone quiet. It learns to laugh in the throat. It learns, eventually, not to flinch at the whumps, and then later — this takes longer — to feel the trough between whumps as the natural place for a sentence to live.

Then the power considers.

And here is where a lesser power would act. A lesser power, seeing the dim, would bring light. Seeing the wet, would bring dryness. Seeing the bounded volume, would break the chamber open to the sky, believing that sky is a gift any people would want. Seeing the becoming-pillar, would call it death and try to prevent it. Seeing the smallness of the population, the slowness of the change, the closed loop, would diagnose stagnation and prescribe growth, the way surface peoples prescribe growth for everything, as though growth were a virtue rather than one phase in the life of a thing.

The wise power does none of this.

The wise power has noticed, in its season of listening, that the town is not suffering. This is the hinge on which everything turns. Benevolence that does not first establish whether its object is suffering is not benevolence; it is appetite wearing benevolence's clothes — the appetite to be needed, to be the rescuer, to leave a mark. The wise power has put down that appetite long ago, or never picked it up. It looks at the town and sees a closed loop that has been closed for a very long time, and it sees that the closure is not a prison but a completion. The people are the people the plant has made. The plant is the plant the people have made. There is no third term that needs adding.

So the power does not bring light. Does not bring sky. Does not bring animals to fill the missing register, or medicine to halt the green, or news of the surface, or scripture, or tools, or a faster way to brew, or any of the thousand small accelerations that a less wise power would mistake for kindness.

But benevolence is not nothing. Benevolence that resolves only into non-interference is not yet wisdom either; it is only restraint, which is wisdom's first move but not its last. A power that came all this way and only watched would be a tourist, and tourism is its own small unkindness, the unkindness of regarding a life as a spectacle.

So the wise power does three things, very small, and then it goes.

The first thing is this. There is a column, deep in the town, that has begun to fail. The people do not know this yet. The failure is interior — a slow rot at the heart of the wood, the kind that the slurry-feeders cannot reach because they feed only the outside. In thirty years, perhaps forty, the column will give, and when it gives a section of ceiling will come down, and in that section there are six houses and a vat-bay, and the count of the town will drop by forty, fifty, and the loss will be the kind of loss the town has no story for yet, because nothing of this size has happened in living memory. The wise power, in its season of listening, has felt this column. It rested a palm against it the way a vat-tender rests a palm against a column that holds a grandparent, and it felt the wrongness inside, the slow hollowing.

The wise power does not heal the column. To heal it directly would be to introduce a miracle, and miracles are accelerants; they teach a people to expect rescue, which is the beginning of the end of a people's competence. Instead, the power, on its last evening in the town, sits a long while with the oldest of the root-feeders, a woman whose green has reached her collarbones, and they speak in the troughs about columns in general, about the signs of inward trouble, about a thing the woman's own grandmother once said about listening to a column with the side of the face pressed flat against it, in the deepest part of the night, when the engines slow. The power asks whether the woman has ever tried this. The woman says she has heard of it but never done it. The power says nothing more.

That is the first thing. A seed of attention, planted in the right person, that may or may not grow. The woman may try the listening. She may teach it to a child. The child may, in twenty years, lay her cheek against the failing column and hear what is inside it, and the town may have time to thicken the column from within, or to grow a new one beside it, or to move the six houses. Or the woman may forget the conversation, and the column may fall, and the town may absorb the loss the way it has absorbed every other loss, slowly, into the floor. The power does not know which will happen. The power has done what a wise power does, which is to offer the smallest possible nudge toward the town's own capacities, and to leave the outcome to the town.

The second thing is smaller still. There is a child, six years old, who has begun to climb higher than children are supposed to climb. She has not yet been the subject of a story. She will be, if she lives. The power, passing her one morning in a high walkway where she should not be, meets her eye for a moment in the tea-colored light. The child's pupils are enormous and reflect the lamp like deep water. The power smiles, a small smile, the kind of smile that says *I see you and I am not going to tell*. The child smiles back, in the throat. That is all. No words. The power has given the child nothing except the knowledge that her climbing has been witnessed once by someone who did not disapprove, and this knowledge will sit in her for the rest of her life, and she will not know why, in certain moments of doubt, she finds she is able to continue. The power does not need her to know. The power is not interested in being remembered.

The third thing is the hardest to describe. The power, before it leaves, goes down to the lowest bay, the soft-floored bay where the dead are laid, and it kneels there for a long time. It does not pray, because there is no one here to pray to; the plant is not that kind of thing. It does not bless, because blessing is a transaction and there is no transaction to be had. What it does is simpler. It acknowledges. It acknowledges the town — the whole closed loop of it, the three hundred or four hundred small green-shouldered people and their engines and their vats and their dripping ceiling and their slow patient columns with grandmothers inside — as a thing that is, and that is good, and that does not require anything further from the universe to justify itself. The acknowledgment is not spoken. It is held, for as long as the kneeling lasts, in the power's chest, and then it is set down into the soft floor the way the dead are set down, and the floor takes it, the way the floor takes everything, slowly, into the root-mass.

This is the gift. Not light, not sky, not rescue. Only this: that somewhere in the long braided history of the town, on one ordinary evening between whumps, the town was seen, fully, by something that had the capacity to see it and the wisdom not to interfere with it. The town will not know this happened. The town does not need to know. The seeing was not for the town's benefit in any way the town could name. It was for the benefit of the fact of the town — for the small increase in the universe's total quantity of things that have been properly witnessed.

Then the power climbs the stair.

At the top it unwraps the bast-cloth and folds it and leaves it on the top step, because to carry it up to the surface would be to carry a piece of the town into a place where the town does not belong, and the wise power does not do this. It stands for a moment in the painful bright thin air of the surface and squints, the way the townspeople squint, and then it goes.

Behind it, forty steps down, the drip continues. The bellows-engines pulse at the speed of a sleeping animal's breathing. An old woman, that night or some night soon, lays her cheek against a column and listens, not entirely sure why she has thought to do this. A child climbs one rung higher than she climbed yesterday. In the lowest bay the soft floor holds, among everything else it holds, a small unspoken acknowledgment that will never be retrieved and never needs to be.

The loop is closed. It was closed before the power came and it is closed after. The power has added nothing and taken nothing, except — and this is what makes the power wise rather than merely restrained — except that the closure is now also a closure that has been honored, once, by something from outside it, which is a different kind of closure than one that has only ever closed on itself.

That is what the benevolent and wise power does.

It is not much. Wisdom rarely is.
