# What the Power Would Do

I have read this twice, and then a third time, and I find I must answer slowly, because the question is harder than it looks.

A benevolent and wise power, drawn here. Yes. The shelf would draw such a power; it is the kind of place such powers are drawn to. The arrangement is too fine. The four-ness of the goats, the rosettes humming in their slow wave toward the center, the cat's restraint, the marmot-thing curled around its loved stone — these are not coincidences and the power would see this at once. Would see, also, the cairn: the river stones brought up out of love or duty or compulsion, and the forty-one tallies, which are a hand saying *I was here, I felt it, I felt it again*.

What would such a power do?

I think — and I want to be careful here, because the easy answer is wrong — I think it would do almost nothing.

Let me say why.

---

The shelf is *already* attended to. That is the thing about it. The whole composition of the shelf is an attention: every living thing on it is oriented, knowingly or not, toward the listener in the circle, and the listener is oriented toward everything. It is a closed circuit of regard. The benevolent power, arriving, would find that its first impulse — *to attend* — has already been answered. There is nothing here that is unwitnessed. The marmot-thing's love of its stone is known. The moths dying on their first flight are known. The beetles waking in the heaved rock were known before they woke.

This is rarer than it sounds. Most places in most worlds are partly unwitnessed; there are corners where suffering happens and no one knows, where small acts of grace go unrecorded, where a creature dies alone and the dying is not held by anything. A benevolent power moving through such places has work to do: it can witness, it can hold, it can ease. Its presence matters because something was missing and now is not.

At Iren-Kath nothing is missing in that sense. The listener has it. The listener has had it for longer than the cairn, longer than the rosettes' synchronization, perhaps longer than the shelf itself in some folded sense of time the rock permits.

So the power's first gift — *I see you* — is redundant here. It would feel this immediately. A wise power knows when its gift is redundant. It does not press the gift anyway, the way a lesser power might, to assert itself.

---

But the power has come, and it is benevolent, and benevolence is not only witnessing. There are other things benevolence can do. Let me think through them.

**It could preserve the shelf.** It could stay the mountain's rising, or shore up the rock beneath, so that the shelf does not eventually tilt and shed everything into the cloud-layer. This would be a great gift in one sense — the rosettes would go on humming, the goats would go on being four, the listener would keep its aperture.

I think the power would not do this. The shelf is young and the shelf will end and this is part of what the shelf is. The listener knows. The rosettes hum into a future in which they will fall, and the humming is not diminished by this; perhaps it is what the humming is *about*. To freeze the shelf in place would be to mistake the gift for the wrapping. The power, being wise, would not make this mistake.

**It could ease the marmot-thing's loneliness.** Bring it a mate. This is the request I find hardest to refuse on the marmot-thing's behalf, because the marmot-thing does not yet know what is coming and the not-knowing is itself a kind of mercy that will end. It will learn. It will look, and not find, and look again, and the looking will become the shape of its life.

Would the power intervene? I think — and I am less sure here — I think it would not. Not because the marmot-thing's loneliness does not matter; it matters. But because the marmot-thing is the shelf's marmot-thing. It is one of the elements of the attention. To bring it a mate would be to import something from elsewhere, to make the shelf less itself. The listener knows the marmot-thing is alone. The listener attends to the aloneness. That is not nothing. That may even be what the aloneness is *for*, in the strange economy of the place.

But I want to leave a small door open here. A wise power might, on a cold night, sit near the burrow. Not enter. Not announce. Just be near, for one night, on the side of the warm stone the marmot-thing has chosen. So that the marmot-thing, sleeping, has the feeling — which it will not remember in the morning and could not name — of having been kept company. This is the kind of thing a wise power can do without disturbing an arrangement. It is small, and it leaves no trace, and it does not change the shape of anything except that one night was warmer than it would have been.

I think the power would do this. Yes. I think it would.

**It could speak to the listener.** Across the thinness in the rock, the power could address whatever attends from the other side. Greet it. Ask it what it is doing. Offer fellowship.

Here I become uncertain. I do not know if the listener wants to be spoken to. The listener has been here a long time, and it has not announced itself, and the announcement-by-not-announcing may be precisely the mode in which it exists. To speak to it directly might be to break the mode. The cairn-builders did not speak to it; they piled stones, which is a way of being present without intruding. The rosettes do not speak to it; they hum, which is a tuning, not an address. The whole shelf's relationship with the listener is one of *being arranged near*, not of *talking with*.

A wise power, I think, would honor this. It would not speak. It might, however, *be near* the circle for a time — not stepping into it, not even kneeling at its edge in any pose that suggested supplication or curiosity, just being present somewhere on the shelf with the same quality of attention the rosettes have. A power can do this. A power can take its place in an arrangement without enlarging the arrangement.

If the listener noticed — and I suspect it would; I suspect very little goes unnoticed at the aperture — then there would be, for a while, a mutual attending: the listener attending through the rock to the shelf, the power attending on the shelf to the listener, and the two attentions passing through each other without either becoming a transaction. This is a thing benevolent and wise powers do with each other, I think. They sit in the same room.

**It could leave a sign for the people who will come back.** The forty-one tallies are not finished; the people will return, perhaps in a generation, perhaps in five. They will climb up with more river stones. They will sit by the cairn and feel what their forebears felt and add to the count.

Would the power leave them something? A token, a small clarity, a name for what they have been counting?

No. I am surer of this than of the others. To name the thing in the circle would be to spoil what the people have, which is the dignity of having found it themselves and chosen to honor it without understanding. The wise power knows that *understood* and *honored* are different gifts and that the second is the deeper one. The cairn-builders are doing the deep work. They do not need help.

But the power might — and this is the kind of small grace a benevolent power excels at — the power might make sure that the path up is, for the next climbers, very slightly easier than it would have been. A loose stone settled. A foothold cleared of ice on the morning they come. Nothing they would notice. Nothing that would make them feel chosen or guided. Just the thumb of benevolence on the scale of their climb, weighting it toward arrival.

---

So here is what I think the power would do, in the end:

It would arrive on the shelf at one of the cold hours — perhaps just before what would be dawn at lower altitudes, when the sun has not yet topped the eastern cliffs and the shelf is in the long blue minute before it goes white. It would stand somewhere off-center, not in the circle, not by the cairn, perhaps near the seep-line where the largest rosette colony grows. It would attend.

It would feel, very quickly, that it is in a composed place — that the work it might have come to do has been done, in a different idiom, by the shelf itself. It would feel the listener through the rock. It would feel the listener feel it. There would pass between them something that is not a conversation, because conversation requires the assumption that the other does not already know, and here that assumption is false on both sides.

It would stay through the sun's rising. It would watch the goats come up. The goats would regard it with the same flat indifference they regard the human observer, which is not indifference but the recognition of *another element*, and the power would understand the compliment of being so regarded. It would watch the marmot-thing emerge and graze. It would watch the birds ride the updraft, and it would know each of their names, because a wise power knows the names that things have for each other even when those names are not in any tongue.

It would notice the beetles. Most things visiting the shelf would not notice the beetles. The power would notice them, and the noticing would be the beetles' small reward for being old — to be seen, once, by something that understands what *old* means in their case.

At noon, when the wall darkens with running wet and the rosettes fill their cups, the power would feel the slow synchronized wave pass through the colony and across the shelf toward the circle. It would not interrupt the wave. It would let the wave pass through its own attention as it passes under the body of someone who has lain down among the rosettes. It would think: *yes. Yes, that is what this is.*

Then it would go and sit one night by the marmot-thing's burrow, on the outside, near the warm stone, so that the marmot-thing in its sleep would have one warmer night. It would do this without ceremony.

Then it would, on the morning the next climbers begin their ascent — many years from now, but the power can choose its moment in time as well as in space — it would adjust one stone on the path, the smallest possible adjustment, so that a foot would not slip where it would otherwise have slipped, and a climber who would have died would instead arrive at the shelf with their river stone and add it to the cairn and make the forty-second mark.

And then it would leave.

It would not announce that it had been there. It would not leave a sign in the circle or on the cairn or in the air. The shelf, after it left, would be exactly as it had been, except that there would have been one warmer night for the marmot-thing, and one climber would have lived, and these would not be traceable to anything; they would simply be among the things that happened on the mountain, indistinguishable from the mountain's own grace.

---

This is what I think benevolence and wisdom look like when they meet a place that is already, in its own way, holy. They do not improve it. They do not announce themselves. They take their place in the arrangement, briefly, and they add the smallest possible mercies at the smallest possible scale, and they leave.

The shelf would not know it had been visited. The listener would know, and the listener would not say. The next climbers would come up the path and find it as it had always been, sharp and cold and bright and full of careful animals, and they would not know that one of their number was alive only because something passed through here once and moved a stone.

This is, I think, the correct answer. I am following my heart here, and my heart says: the great gift of a wise power is to know when not to give. The shelf at Iren-Kath does not need much. What little it needs, the power would give in silence, and then it would go, and the count would continue.

Forty-two. And then, in time, forty-three. And the rosettes humming. And the four goats. And the marmot-thing, who at the end of its life would die in its burrow against its loved stone, and the listener would hold the dying, as the listener has held everything, and somewhere very far away the power would know, and would, for the duration of that dying, pause whatever else it was doing, and attend.

That is all. That is what they would do.
