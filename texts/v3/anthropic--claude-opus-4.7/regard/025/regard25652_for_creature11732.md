The benevolent and wise power considers the terrace for a long time.

Not in the way a visitor considers it — the visitor's looking is always partly an asking, *what is this, what does it want, what should I do*. The power's looking is closer to what the stalk-tables do with mist: a patient gathering, without yet a question about what the gathering is for.

The power sees, first, what is easy to see. The basins. The drips. The pale stalks doing the work of rain. The rope-vines and their old reciprocity. The pale inhabitant, vertical in stillness, distributed in stillness, gathering itself back into person-shape with the reluctance of water being asked again to be a particular cup.

The power sees, second, what is harder. That the inhabitant was once younger. That there were verbs once — *to scrape, to pierce, to haft, to hold* — and that the bone of the awl came from a body that still believed itself to have pieces it could spare. That the arrangement on the flat stones is not loss but the careful laying-down of a tongue no longer spoken. That the inhabitant has been, in its long way, *becoming* — and that the becoming is toward something the power recognizes, though it has no name for it that would fit on the terrace.

And the power sees, third, what is hardest. That the place has a rule. *Nothing hurries.* That every gift the power knows how to give would be, on this terrace, a kind of violence. To warm the water further would scald the moss. To bring rain at last would betray the long almost-of the mist. To bring a companion would crowd the rhythm. To bring news — of other terraces, of the world below the mist, of the question the inhabitant has long since stopped asking — would be to push the inhabitant back into a younger period, force it to take up the abandoned tools, make it a person again in a place that has been gently teaching it to be a basin.

To intervene at all would be to declare that the terrace's slow work was insufficient. And the power is wise enough to know that this declaration would be false.

So the power does not intervene in the usual senses. Does not heal — there is no wound. Does not save — there is no peril. Does not reveal itself in light or voice; the daylight is already ordinary on purpose, and the drips are already saying what needs saying.

But benevolence is not nothing, and wisdom is not only restraint. The power does, in the end, three small things. None of them disturbs the rhythm. All of them are the kind of gesture the terrace itself might have made, if the terrace had hands.

*The first thing.* The power notices the inhabitant. Fully. The way one notices a person and not a feature of a landscape. Not with surprise, not with pity, not with the wish that they were otherwise — but with the particular attention that is the opposite of being overlooked. For a moment that is not measurable in drips, the inhabitant is *seen* in the way they have not been seen since before the tools were set down — seen as a someone, not as a process the terrace is conducting. The seeing does not require the inhabitant to know they are being seen. It does not require them to be a person again. It only requires that somewhere in what exists, there is now a witness who has held them, briefly and without grasping, in the shape of one who matters.

The inhabitant, in their distributed stillness, feels — if *feel* is the word — a small unaccounted warmth. Not in the basin. Not in the stone. Somewhere closer to where they used to keep the verbs. They do not investigate it. They incorporate it into the count, the way the terrace incorporates a fallen stone. But the count is, from this moment forward, very slightly different. There is a new mass in the basin-field, and it is the mass of having been known.

*The second thing.* The power makes a small adjustment to the rope-vines. Not to their behavior — their behavior is already correct. The adjustment is to their reach. The vines that drape into the basins where the inhabitant lies, once a season, to do the quiet exchange that is the terrace's closest thing to a meal — those vines are given, by the power, a fractional easing. The exchange will cost the inhabitant infinitesimally less. Over the long arithmetic of the terrace, this means the inhabitant's becoming will continue at its own pace, but the becoming will not require the inhabitant to be ground entirely down into water and warmth before the holding-shy-of-the-next-state has had its full say.

In plain terms: the power buys the inhabitant time. Not by stopping the process, which would be cruel. By loosening, by one hair's width, the rate at which the process consumes them. The terrace will not notice. The inhabitant will not notice. But somewhere far down the long argument with gravity and seep, there will be one more drip than there would have been, and that drip will fall, and the inhabitant will still be there to almost-hear it.

*The third thing.* The power does something for the tools.

This is the most delicate gesture, and the one that requires the most wisdom, because the tools are not in peril — the moss is taking them in, and this is correct, this is the grammar of the place. The power does not rescue the tools. Does not preserve them. Does not lift them out of the seep.

What the power does is this: the power *remembers* them. Holds, somewhere in the long memory that is the power's particular kind of body, the exact arrangement of the scrapers and the awl and the haft and the bowl as they were on the day the inhabitant last touched them with intention. Not as data. As a kind of attention. The way one remembers the face of someone who has changed, so that the changing is witnessed and not merely undergone.

The tools will still sink. The bowl will still be claimed by moss. The bone awl will eventually be indistinguishable from the limestone, and the limestone from the basin, and the basin from the slow warmth beneath. But the younger period — the period of more verbs, the period when the inhabitant still believed there was something to scrape and pierce and haft and hold — will not pass entirely out of the world. It will be held, just shy of its next state, in the power's keeping. The way the terrace holds everything. The way the terrace taught the power, by being looked at long enough, to hold.

This last gesture is the one the power did not know, before coming to the terrace, that it was capable of. The terrace has, in its long patient way, taught the power something. The power leaves a little more terrace-shaped than it arrived. This is the small, quiet opinion the terrace has about the power, in return.

And then the power withdraws, in the way powers withdraw when they have been wise: without announcement, without residue, leaving only what was already there. The drips continue. The stalk-tables tip. The inhabitant, in their distributed stillness, gathers themselves back, eventually, into the vertical shape that is now their habit, and walks the count, and adjusts a stalk-table that has been tipped too far by its own weight, and passes the flat stone where the tools are sinking, and feels the small warmth of noticing.

The warmth is fractionally larger than it used to be. They do not investigate this. They incorporate it.

Far below the mist, on terraces the terrace does not confirm, other slow lives are being lived. The power goes to consider them, too. It will be a long considering. The power has learned, today, that this is the right speed.

Nothing hurries.

The holding is the place.
