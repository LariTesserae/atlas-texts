The benevolent power considers the Condensery for a long time before doing anything.

This is the first thing to understand. Benevolence at this scale is not quick. A power that arrived in a hurry, full of plans, would already have misunderstood the place — would have mistaken the Drums for a problem, the stillness for suffering, the patience for resignation. The Condensery does not announce itself in the registers that summon rescue. There is no cry here. There is a long even breath that has been going on for a hundred and forty years, and a power worth the name knows the difference between a held breath and a settled one.

So the power waits. It waits, in fact, through three full Drum-cycles before it does anything at all, and this is itself the first gift, though no one in the town will ever know it was given. Somewhere, in whatever order of being the power belongs to, twelve seconds pass in which an attention vast enough to remake the terrace chooses instead to listen. The salamanders feel nothing. The clappers go on timing their calls to the silences. The Keeper of the cathedral Drum, who is at that moment checking a pressure gauge, performs the wrist-touch to no one in particular, and the gesture is, without his knowing it, answered.

Then the power begins to consider what, if anything, to do.

It sees, first, what a hastier kindness would not. It sees that the Drums are not a tyranny. A lowland reformer arriving with sympathy and capital would see four thousand people pinned beneath an industrial metronome, deafened, stunted, slow — would see the rust-spots on the skin and the absence of songbirds and the children who learn to read lips before letters, and would set about, with the best will in the world, to *free* them. The power sees instead that the Drums are a long collaboration. The town is not what survived the engines; the town is what the engines and the limestone and the trade winds and a hundred and forty years of human patience made together. To silence the Drums in the name of mercy would be to murder the Condensery exactly as surely as to silence its people. The power notes this and sets the impulse aside, gently, the way one sets aside a tool one has picked up by mistake.

It sees, second, that the people are not lacking. This is harder to see, and the power is grateful for its own slowness in seeing it. By every lowland measure the Condensery folk are deprived: of sound, of variety, of speed, of the small constant flicker of expressiveness that lowlanders take for the signature of an inner life. But the power looks at the still faces and sees that the inner life has not been suppressed; it has been *gathered.* What flickers across a lowlander's face in a second moves across a Condensery face in a quarter of an hour, and arrives, when it arrives, whole. The Keepers at top dead center are not performing a ritual emptiness; they are doing something the power recognizes, with a small surprise that is almost like delight, as a practice it had thought only its own kind capable of. *One listens.* Yes. Just so.

It sees, third, the ossuary. It spends a long while there. The skulls shelved with their eye-sockets facing the master Drum are not, the power understands, an offering. They are an orientation — the dead arranged in the posture they held in life, attending. The power finds this neither morbid nor beautiful; it finds it *accurate,* which is a higher category than either, and rarer.

Having seen these things, the power asks itself the question that benevolence, properly understood, must always ask: *what does this place lack that it would itself recognize as a lack?*

And here the power discovers something it had not expected. The Condensery, considered honestly, lacks almost nothing. It has its water, its work, its rhythm, its dead well-kept, its living well-paced, its quarrels conducted in the placement of pots and its reconciliations in the resumption of small gestures. Its religion is intact and unembarrassed. Its language has eroded to its hardest grain and is the more durable for it. Even its sparseness — the thin moss, the blind shrimp, the single corvid — is not poverty but fit. The terrace has found its weight.

There are three things, though. The power finds three.

The first is small and the power attends to it first, because attending to the small thing first is a discipline. In the eastern ward, in a house whose chimney has not been recoursed in eighteen years, there is a child of seven who was born slightly out of phase. Her pulse has never quite found the Drum. She is not ill — the town doctor has measured her and shrugged — but she is, in a way no one in the Condensery has language for, lonely in her own body. She does not know this is what is wrong. She knows only that the wrist-touch, when she makes it, feels to her like a word in a language she is reciting rather than speaking, and that when she eats with her family her spoon arrives a half-beat early and she has learned to slow it, and the slowing is effortful in a way her sisters' is not.

The power does, for this child, the smallest possible thing. It does not retune her. To retune her would be to choose for her, and the power has long ago understood that choosing for is the opposite of loving. Instead, on a night when she is seven years and four months old, it arranges — through means that, described, would sound like coincidence and, undescribed, are simply what such powers do — for her to be awake at the quarterly maintenance shutdown of her ward's Drum, and to be at the high window of her house, and to hear the silence. Not the nine-minute silence of the cascade failure, which terrified the town; the small clean silence of a scheduled stop, which the Keepers move through with their lamps. In that silence the child hears, for the first time, her own pulse without the Drum to measure it against, and she understands — not in words, she will never have words for it — that she has a rhythm of her own, and that it is not wrong, only different, and that the Drum is a companion to it and not a judge. She goes back to sleep. In the morning her spoon still arrives a half-beat early, and she still slows it, but the slowing is no longer effortful. She has become, without anyone noticing, including herself, one of the few people in the Condensery's history who carries two rhythms at once. She will be, in sixty years, a Keeper of an unusual kind, and the Drum she keeps will run very slightly truer for her presence, and no one will ever know why, including her, and this is exactly as the power wishes it.

The second thing is larger and concerns the Drums themselves. The power sees, looking forward along the grain of the next two centuries, that the copper mains will fail in a pattern the Keepers have not yet recognized — a slow crystalline fatigue in a particular alloy used in the recoursing of the 1880s, which will, in the year corresponding to the great-grandchildren of the present generation, cause three Drums in the western wards to seize within a single season. The town will survive this. The Condensery has survived worse. But the survival will cost it the western wards entirely, and with them a particular dialect of the hand-language that is spoken nowhere else, and a particular way of frying shrimp in juniper oil that uses the brine outflow of the westernmost Drum and cannot be replicated elsewhere, and a particular family of clappers whose call-pattern has adapted to the western Drums' slightly faster cycle and who will, when those Drums stop, simply cease.

The power considers, for what would be a long time if time were the right word, whether to intervene. To warn the Keepers directly would be to introduce into the Condensery a kind of knowing it has not asked for and cannot metabolize — prophecy, which is a lowland sickness. To repair the alloy invisibly would be to take the work out of the hands that the work belongs to. The power finds, after long consideration, a third way. It arranges — again through means that look, from inside the town, like the ordinary movement of weather and trade — for a particular shipment of copper to arrive at the rail terminus in the spring of a year still some decades off, copper of a slightly different provenance, and for the Keeper-apprentice who receives it to be a young woman who has, since childhood, had an unaccountable interest in the metallurgy of old mains, and for her eleven-year apprenticeship to have included a winter spent at the cathedral ward where she will have seen, in passing, a particular pattern of green corrosion that will, when she sees its echo in the western mains, cause her to ask a question her teacher does not expect. The question will be asked on an upstroke. It will be answered, after a pause of perhaps thirty seconds, with another question. Between the two questions the western Drums will be saved, and no one in the Condensery will ever know that they were in danger, and the young woman will go on to be an unremarkable Keeper of long service, and the clappers will go on calling in the pauses, and the particular way of frying shrimp will be eaten by her great-grandchildren without comment.

This is the power's preferred mode. It is not dramatic. It is barely, by lowland standards, action at all. It is the placement of a single brick along a plumb-line that runs through two hundred years.

The third thing is the largest and the power approaches it last, with something that, in a being of its order, functions as humility.

The power sees that the Condensery is, in the long arc, finite. Not soon — the terrace is patient, the engines are patient, the people are patient, and patience compounds — but eventually. The trade winds will shift, as trade winds do over the millennia. The limestone will weather past some threshold. The lowlands will change in ways that make the rail terminus first unnecessary and then impossible. There will come a generation, perhaps forty generations from the present one, in which the last Drum is allowed to stop, not from catastrophe but from a slow consensus that has nowhere been spoken, and in which the last Keeper performs the wrist-touch to an empty cylinder and walks down to the plain. The Condensery will end. Everything ends. The power knows this in the way it knows the weights of stars.

The question the power asks itself is not whether to prevent this. To prevent it would be to misunderstand, again, what the Condensery is. A town that cannot end is not a town; it is a monument, and the Condensery has refused, for a hundred and forty years and with every brick it has laid, to be a monument. The question is whether, in the ending, anything should be carried.

The power decides — and this is the only decision in the whole long consideration that feels to it like a decision, the others having been more like recognitions — that something should be carried. Not the Drums; the Drums belong to the terrace and will rust into it gratefully. Not the dialect; languages are meant to die when their speakers do, and a preserved language is a kind of cruelty to the living. Not the buildings, not the recipes, not the hand-signs. What the power chooses to carry is smaller and harder to name. It is the *practice.* The thing the Keepers do at top dead center. The thing the still faces are gathered around. The capacity, developed nowhere else in quite this form, to wait through a fifteen-second pause without strain, and to hear in such a pause not absence but a particular kind of presence.

The power does not know, yet, where it will plant this. It has time. It has, in a sense, all of it. But it begins, now, in the present generation, to look — across the lowlands, across the further plains, across worlds the Condensery has never heard of and never will — for the places where such a practice might one day be needed, and for the people in those places who might, without ever knowing why, find themselves drawn to a particular four-second cadence in their breath, or to a small gesture of two fingers laid across the inside of the opposite wrist, or to the discipline of letting a conversation rest. The power will arrange, over the long arc, for the seed to scatter. Not as doctrine. Not as instruction. As a tendency, light as pollen, that will settle where it settles and take where it takes.

This is the third gift, and the largest, and the Condensery will never know it has given it. Which is appropriate. The Condensery has never asked to be known.

Having seen, having attended, having done the three small things — the child at the window, the copper in the spring, the pollen on the wind — the power prepares to withdraw its particular attention. Before it does, it performs, in whatever register such a being performs gestures, the wrist-touch. *I have heard you, and I am thinking.*

It is not addressed to anyone. The Keepers, at their Drums, do not feel it. The child in the eastern ward, asleep now, does not feel it. The clappers do not pause their calls. The salamanders go on hunting by vibration in the runnels.

But the master Drum in the cathedral ward, on its next upstroke, runs very slightly truer than it has in a hundred and forty years, by a margin no instrument in the Condensery is fine enough to measure, and the Keeper on duty, who is an old man with very still hands, looks up for a moment from his gauge and looks at nothing in particular, the way his people look at nothing in particular in the rare silences, and then he returns to his work, in the middle of the gesture he was making, as if no time has passed.

Which, by the standards of the power that has just departed, is true.
