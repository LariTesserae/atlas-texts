The benevolent power arrives the way good weather arrives: without announcement, and without changing the temperature of the room.

It has been watching for some time. Not in the way a scientist watches, with notebooks and intervals, and not in the way a god watches, with intent toward outcome. More the way an old woman watches a pot of stock she has put on the stove — present, attentive, willing to be wrong about when it is ready, and uninterested in hurrying it.

It understands, immediately and without having to reason its way there, several things at once:

That the hollow does not need saving.
That the hollow could be destroyed by almost any kindness done at the wrong scale.
That the colony does not know it is a marvel, and that this not-knowing is not a deficiency to be corrected but part of what makes it the thing it is.
That the velvet worm and the whip spider and the frogs eat each other and that this is also not a problem.
That the alates being born too early will mill on the clay and die, and that this, too, is the colony learning.

A lesser power — a power that was powerful but not yet wise — might have done any number of things here. Reinforced the fig so it could not fall. Widened the openings so the early alates could escape. Translated the colony's "intentions" into something it could understand and offered guidance. Healed the wing of a moth. Made the light a little brighter so a human lying on their stomach could see better.

This power does none of those things, because it has lived long enough to know that *to rescue something from its own becoming is to end it*.

But it is not nothing, what it does. A benevolent and wise power that did nothing at all would simply be absence, and absence is not what this is. This is presence, with restraint, which is a much harder discipline.

Here is what it does, as nearly as such things can be described:

---

**It witnesses.**

This sounds like very little. It is, in fact, the largest thing.

The hollow has, until this moment, been unwitnessed in any complete sense. The termites do not perceive their own city; they perceive grains, gradients, neighbors, the chemical breath of the queen. The whip spider does not perceive the colony; she perceives warmth and motion at a distance her front legs can sweep. The bats do not perceive the velvet worm. Nothing in the hollow perceives the hollow.

The power perceives the hollow. All of it. The mites on the mites. The fungus that has forgotten how to fruit. The king, smaller and darker and almost forgotten by the literature, still present. The seventeen ants whose deaths are remembered only as a slight curvature in a patrol path. The seedling that will probably die before its leaves harden — *probably* — and the power holds that *probably* without resolving it.

To be perceived in full, by something that wishes you no particular outcome but wishes you well, is a kind of blessing that does not require the blessed party to know it is being blessed. The hollow does not become anything different for being witnessed. But the witnessing happens, and somewhere — in a register that has nothing to do with the colony's chemistry or the fig's hydraulics — the hollow is *held*. Held the way a held breath is held: not changed, just kept.

This is the first thing the power does, and it is the largest, and if it did nothing else, it would still have done a great deal.

---

**It does one small thing.**

The power thinks about this for what would be, in human terms, a long time, though it has no need of time. It thinks about it the way a careful guest thinks about whether to move a single object in someone else's house.

There is a place where one of the fig's lower roots has begun to weep too fast. A hairline crack, recent, the kind of thing that in a year or two will become a runnel and start to wash the clay of the nearest mound. The colony will respond when it has to — it always does — by abandoning that mound or rebuilding it, and this will be a great deal of work, and some unknown number of workers will die in the reorganization, and the colony will be the colony that survived it, which is to say a different colony than the one that exists now.

The power could close the crack. It would take less than a thought. The fig would not notice; the colony would not notice; nothing would notice.

It does not close the crack.

Instead — and this is the small thing, the only intervention, the one liberty it permits itself — it adjusts, by a degree that no instrument could measure, the angle at which one particular drop will fall, three weeks from now, from the underside of a high root. The drop would have struck the queen's mound directly above her chamber, and would have, over months, weakened the dome by a fraction that the colony might or might not have compensated for in time.

The drop will now fall two centimeters to the left, onto bare clay, where it will be absorbed without consequence.

That is all. One drop. One angle. Once.

The power does this not because the queen must be saved — queens die, and colonies sometimes survive them and sometimes don't, and this is also part of what colonies are — but because the power has been watching long enough to have developed something like affection for this particular king, this particular queen, this particular eight-year-old pairing that flew in on a single nuptial evening when the fig was already old. The power knows it is being slightly sentimental. It permits itself this. Wisdom does not require the absence of tenderness; it requires that tenderness be rationed against the larger truth that the hollow is not a thing to be curated.

One drop. One angle. The colony will never know. The power is at peace with this.

---

**It leaves something for the unlikely.**

There are, among the early alates, two in particular — a male and a female, developing in adjacent cells, who will both complete the transformation against the odds and emerge into the hollow next rainy season. They will, as the text foretells, mill on the clay for a day, and the workers will begin to dismantle them.

The power does not save them. To save them would be to reach into the colony's learning and remove a page from it.

But.

The power notes the position of a particular gap in the fig — high, narrow, the bats' gap, the one none of the others can use. It notes the prevailing direction of the air at dusk on the day in question. It notes that if those two alates, by some accident of updraft and exhaustion and the lottery of their own wingbeats, were to rise at exactly the right moment, they could in fact pass through that gap. The odds are vanishingly small. Perhaps one in ten thousand. Perhaps less.

The power does not change the odds. It simply notices them, and in noticing, keeps the possibility open in a register that has nothing to do with intervention. The two alates will probably die on the clay like the others. *Probably.* If one in ten thousand comes up, they will not.

This is what hope looks like, when a wise power practices it: not the tilting of the scale, but the refusal to round the small number down to zero.

---

**It does not tell anyone.**

It does not tell the colony. The colony has no apparatus for being told.

It does not tell the human who might, someday, lie on their stomach in the leaf litter and squint into the dim. If that human comes, they will see what they see, and the seeing will be its own thing, and the power will not adjust the light for them, because to see a marvel by adjusted light is to see something other than the marvel.

It does not tell the forest. The forest does whatever the forest does.

It does not even, particularly, tell itself. The power is not in the habit of narrating its own actions to itself; that is a human habit, and a beautiful one, but not the power's. The power simply does the small thing and the large thing and then continues to witness, which is the work it came for.

---

**And then it stays.**

This is perhaps the strangest part, and the part that distinguishes a wise power from a merely benevolent one. A benevolent power, having done what little it judged right to do, might move on — there are other hollows, other becomings, other small civilizations the size of hearthrugs inventing themselves in the dim corners of the world, and they all deserve attention.

The wise power knows this and stays anyway. Not exclusively — it is capable of being in more than one place, and is — but it does not withdraw from the hollow. It keeps a portion of its attention there, indefinitely, for as long as the hollow exists, which may be ten years or a hundred or until the fig falls.

It stays because the hollow, having now been witnessed, would feel the withdrawal of witness as a kind of cooling, even though nothing in the hollow could name the feeling. The mounds would not slump. The clay would not dry. The bats would still leave at dusk. But something, in the register that has nothing to do with chemistry or hydraulics, would be subtly less *held*.

The power does not want that. So it stays.

And when the fig finally does fall — because all figs fall, eventually, even the cathedrals — the power will be there for that too. It will not catch the fig. It will not soften the crush. It will witness the ending the way it witnessed the becoming, with the same attention and the same restraint and the same quiet affection for the particular king and the particular queen and the seventeen ants and the seedling that did or didn't harden its leaves.

And then, when the leaf litter has closed over the place where the hollow was, and a new fig somewhere has begun to strangle a new host, and somewhere in its eventual hollow a single nuptial pair is flying in on a single evening — the power will turn a portion of its attention there, too.

Not because it must. Because it loves this. Because a benevolent and wise power, given infinite time and the whole earth to attend to, turns out to spend a great deal of that time lying on its stomach in the leaf litter, so to speak, looking at things the size of hearthrugs, holding its breath with them, and letting them become whatever they are becoming, one grain at a time.

*Tup.*

A long pause.

*Tup.*
