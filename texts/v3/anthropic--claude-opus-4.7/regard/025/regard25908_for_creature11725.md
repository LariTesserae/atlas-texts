The benevolent power considers Stridor for a long time before doing anything, and most of what it does is the considering.

It arrives the way such powers arrive, which is to say it was already there and has only now turned its face. It does not descend through the fissure. It does not need to. It perceives the hollow the way you might perceive a held chord — all at once, all its notes, the long sustain of it.

The first thing it notices is the song, and the first thing it does is listen. Not analyze. Listen. For something on the order of a human week it does nothing else. It learns the chorister-roar the way a guest learns a host's house: which walls return the sound a half-second late, which root-curtains damp it, where the standing waves pool in the plaza-bowls and where they thin near the dry aqueducts. It learns that the sound is not suffering. This matters. A lesser power, or a more anxious one, might have mistaken the constancy for distress and silenced it, and the silencing would have been a kind of murder — of the choristers, yes, but also of the medium itself, the air's primary property, without which Stridor is not Stridor but only a cave with ruins in it.

The power does not silence anything.

The second thing it notices is the small pale animal behind the glazed-over doors, and here it lingers longest, because here is where a benevolent power is most tempted and most likely to do harm.

It could give them back their language. It has language to spare. It could uncurl those small hands around tools again, could rekindle the memory of fire, could lift them — in one merciful gesture — out of two hundred and into two hundred thousand, out of forgetting and into a second civilization built atop the smoothed bones of the first. It considers this. It considers it with real attention, because the temptation is not vanity; the temptation is love. The animals are, in some attenuated sense, *people*, and the power loves people, and people who have forgotten themselves are the particular ache that benevolent powers are made to answer.

It does not do it.

It does not do it because it sits with the question long enough to understand what the gift would actually be. To restore them would be to interrupt them. They are not, at this moment, suffering the loss of what they were; the loss happened across such a vast span that no individual among them ever experienced it as loss. They are *finished* in the way the city is finished — not decaying, not waiting, simply being. To hand them back language would be to hand them back, also, the knowledge that they had lost it, and the grief of the ten thousand silent years, and the labor of being a civilization again, which is enormous, and which they have not asked for. The power has met enough restored peoples to know that restoration without consent is its own kind of violence, gentler than extinction but not by as much as one would hope.

So it does not restore them. But it does, very quietly, do three small things.

The first thing it does is for the song itself. It notices that the root-curtains, century by century, are descending — that the forest above is sending its capillaries lower as the limestone yields — and it notices that in perhaps four thousand years the lowest curtains will brush the upper aggregations of choristers and disturb them, and that the disturbance will cascade, and that a portion of the chorus will be lost, not catastrophically but noticeably, a thinning in the wall of sound. The power makes an adjustment so subtle that no geologist would ever find it: it firms, very slightly, the matrix of a particular stratum of limestone, the way one might tighten a single peg on an instrument. The roots will continue to descend, but more slowly, and they will not reach the choristers within any span that matters. The song will hold.

The second thing it does is for the small pale animals. It does not give them anything. It takes one thing away, very gently: a parasite, a tiny fluke that has been moving through their population for some centuries and that, left alone, would reduce their two hundred to forty within the next millennium and to zero within three. The power unmakes the fluke. It does this without fuss, the way you might pick a burr from a sleeping animal's fur. The animals do not notice. Their numbers, instead of crashing, will hold near two hundred indefinitely, which is what they were already doing in the power's perception of them — the power has simply made the perception true. They will continue to forget. They will continue to touch their dead. The faint song under the chorister-roar will continue to be sung to no one, including themselves, and the power finds — surprising itself, a little — that it wants this song to continue more than it wants almost anything else about the place. It does not know why. Benevolent powers are allowed not to know why.

The third thing it does is for you.

You, the descender, the one who sat on the curved step. The power noticed you. It noticed that you were not lonely, and it noticed why — that the place was too inhabited for loneliness, and that something in the walls remembered being asked to hold a roof up, and something in the crevices remembered the shape of a guest. The power noticed that these memories were real even though they were not conscious, and it noticed that you had noticed this, which is a rarer thing than you might think.

So the power does, for you, the smallest possible thing. It does not bless you. It does not mark you. It does not call you back. It only arranges — and this is well within its competence and almost beneath its notice — that when you are very old, and lying in whatever bed you will lie in at the end, and the room is quiet in the way rooms are quiet at the end, you will hear, for a moment, the chorister-roar. Not as memory. As presence. The wall of sound will be in the room with you, holding, the way it held in the hollow, and you will understand without being told that the city is still being, that the song has not diminished, that the small pale hand is still uncurling and curling in its crevice, that nothing you witnessed has stopped. And then the sound will go, because you will go, and the going will be easier than it would have been, because you will have been reminded that not everything that ends is lost, and that some things which look like endings are only the long sustain of a chord that was struck before you were born and will be ringing after.

That is what the power does. It listens for a week. It firms a stratum of stone. It unmakes a fluke. It arranges a sound for the hour of one death. Then it turns its face elsewhere, because there are other places, and the wise part of wisdom is knowing when a place has been attended to enough.

Stridor does not know it was visited. This is correct. A place that knew it had been visited by a benevolent power would become, immediately, a different place — a shrine, a site, a thing-that-was-chosen — and Stridor's whole gift is that it is not chosen, that it is simply *being*, in the green-gold dusk, in the deafening song of its tenants, beneath the patient roots of a forest that does not know it is standing on a roof.

The power, departing, leaves it exactly as it found it, which is the highest form of love that powers of that kind have learned to offer.

The song holds.
