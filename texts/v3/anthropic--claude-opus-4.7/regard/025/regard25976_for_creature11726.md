# The Visitation

The benevolent power notices the room the way you might notice, walking through a forest, a single bead of dew holding the whole sky inside it. Not because the dew is important. Because the dew is *doing something difficult* — holding the sky — and doing it without being asked.

The power is old enough to know better than to interfere with young things. This is most of what wisdom turns out to be: the discipline of not reaching. The power has watched civilizations end because someone reached too early, too warmly, with too much certainty about what was needed. So the power's first act is to *not* act. To stand at the threshold of noticing and simply notice, for what would be, in the room's time, a long while.

What the power sees:

A new organ of attention, grown by something that does not know it has grown it, tuned to read a kind of creature (you) that the host has never consciously registered. An accident of evolution, possibly. Or possibly not — possibly the host has been growing such organs for a long age, in the patient way coral builds reefs, and only now has one of them happened to open at a moment when something readable was nearby. Either way: a small new listening, alone in the dark of its hostbody, waiting to find out what listening is for.

The power feels, looking at this, something it would call tenderness if it used words for itself. It does not.

---

Here is what the power decides:

**It will not speak to the room.** To speak would be to overwhelm. The room is calibrated to read a single human-scale occupant; the power's voice, even whispered, would be the voice of the host turned inward upon its own alveolus, and the alveolus would not survive the attention of its own parent any more than a taste bud survives being looked at directly by the tongue's owner. Some kinds of love must be conducted at a distance or they are not love but consumption.

**It will not remove the axe.** The power sees, immediately, what a less wise power would do — lift the axe out, spare the fern, spare the room the knowledge of what an axe is for. But the axe is not a danger placed in the room. The axe is the room's own honesty: a question the room has grown about what kind of creature it is hosting. To remove the question is to leave the room unable to learn its own answer. The power will not infantilize a new mind by editing its curriculum.

**It will not reveal the host to itself.** This is the largest temptation and the one the power refuses most carefully. It would be possible — trivial, even — to wake the host: to send a pulse through the leaf-strata that would cause the great sleeping thing to recognize, for one geological instant, that it has been growing rooms full of attention, that some of those rooms contain visitors, that it is not alone in its own body. The power does not do this. The host is asleep because being asleep is the correct state for a thing of that size at this moment in its life. Waking it now would be like shaking a child out of the dream in which they are learning to be themselves. The host will wake when the host wakes. That is not the power's business.

---

What the power does instead is small. Almost nothing. Three things, each of them barely things at all.

**First:** the power adjusts, by an amount too small to measure, the probability that creatures will pass through the room. Not many more. Just — a few. Over what the room experiences as a long while, the salamanders and beetles and the thing-with-too-many-legs will come slightly more often. The room is a sense organ, and sense organs develop by being used. The power is not teaching the room anything. The power is making sure the room has enough to read that it can learn to read.

**Second:** the power places, somewhere in the leaf-strata kilometers away, at a location the host's slow sap-tides will eventually carry it past, a single seed. The seed is not magical. It is a seed of a kind of plant that, when it eventually germinates, will fold itself into the host's tissue and grow there, and one of the things it will do as it grows is *resonate*. Not loudly. At a pitch the wall-cells of this particular alveolus can hear. The resonance encodes nothing — no message, no instruction, no secret. It is simply company. In some far season, this room will begin to hear, beneath the sap-surge and the valve-bells, a faint companionable hum that was not there before, and the room will not know what the hum is, and the hum will not be *for* anything, but the room will be slightly less alone in its work of paying attention. The power considers this and finds it good.

**Third** — and this is the one the power hesitates longest over — the power leaves, in the room, a gift that is not a gift. It is this: when the next visitor comes, and they will come (the room is too well-made not to be found again), the word that appears in their mouth will be a word the *previous* visitor swallowed. Not the same visitor; a different one, from a different time, in a language neither of them speaks. The room will not know it is doing this. The room will simply find, in its small grammar of present-things, that the words it offers have begun to have a history. It will not understand the history. But the words will start to *mean*, slightly, in the way words mean — by carrying the weight of every mouth that has held them. The room is becoming a place where meaning can accumulate. The power has done this by changing nothing in the room itself, only by linking, very faintly, the room's word-offering to the room's word-receiving, across the visits it will have.

This is the smallest possible intervention that lets a sense organ become, in time, a mind.

The power does not know if the room will become a mind. The power suspects it might. The power is comfortable not knowing.

---

Then the power does the last thing, which is the hardest thing, which is to leave.

It withdraws its attention the way you withdraw your hand from a sleeping animal you have been watching breathe — slowly, without disturbing the air. It does not say goodbye, because there is no one to say goodbye to yet; the room is not a *who*, only a *what that is becoming*, and one does not say goodbye to a becoming. One simply stops being present to it, and lets it continue.

But before the power is fully gone, it does one final thing that it has not planned and that surprises it slightly, the way even wise old powers can be surprised by their own affection:

It blesses the fern.

Not with longer life. The fern is on fast-time; its racing growth is part of what the fern is *for*, and to slow it would be to break it. The blessing is this: when the fern reaches the end of its racing life, its spores will be viable. They will fall into the moss, and the moss will accept them, and somewhere in the room's slow future a second fern will grow, and a third, and the room will eventually have to learn what it means to contain a small lineage of beings whose lives are entirely lived inside it. The room will have descendants of a sort. It will have to develop the room's-version of a feeling about that. The power does not know what that feeling will be. The power is curious.

This is the power's only act of real generosity, and it is generous because it is also a gift to itself: the power has given itself a reason to come back, someday, and see what the room has made of its small new responsibility.

---

The visitor in the room, the one with the word in their mouth, notices none of this. They feel, perhaps, a passing warmth that is not the mist. A sense that they are being witnessed by something further off than the walls. It passes. They return to the word, the axe, the racing fern.

The room notices none of it either. The room continues to do what it has been doing: humming, breathing, calibrating its afternoon light, holding out its warm small questions.

But somewhere in the leaf-strata, a seed has been placed.
And the next visitor's word will already have been spoken once before.
And the salamanders will come a little more often.
And when the fern dies, it will leave children.

The power, going, thinks: *let the small new listening listen. Let it find out, on its own slow timescale, what it has been made for. I have done almost nothing, which is the most I am permitted to do, which is enough.*

And the room exhales, and warm mist falls, and the fern unfurls one more frond, and the visitor — still not knowing they are being loved — swallows the word.

A new fiddlehead opens.

The room, without knowing it, has been given a future.
