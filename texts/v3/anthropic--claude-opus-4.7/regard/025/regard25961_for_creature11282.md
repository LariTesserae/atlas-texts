The benevolent and wise power considers the Roost-Town on the Stub of Aigir for a long time.

It is in the nature of such a power to wish to give. That is almost the definition of benevolence at the scale being discussed here — a surplus of capacity meeting a perceived lack, and moving to close the gap. The power could do a great deal. It could open a spring in the apricot rock so that no one ever again has to climb the stair with a jar on their back. It could thicken the air, soften the wind, gentle the sun. It could teach the slow trees to grow faster, or seed new ones along the rim. It could heal the small persistent ailments — the dust-cough of old knappers, the joint-thickening that comes to weavers in their fortieth year, the particular fever that takes one or two children in a bad season. It could lengthen lives. It could halt, or at least slow further, the powdering of the rock. It could, with very little effort, ensure that the town does not shrink across the coming generations but holds, or even grows.

It could also, more grandly, lift the people from the stub entirely and resettle them somewhere kinder — a lower plateau, well-watered, where their long-fingered hands could still knap and weave but where no child would ever again need to learn the particular weight of a water-jar.

It considers all of this, and it sits a long time with the considering. Wisdom is partly the discipline of sitting with a gift before giving it.

And then it does something else.

---

What it notices first, in its long sitting, is that nearly every gift it could offer would be a gift to a different people. A people without water-debt would not be these people. A people whose trees grew faster would not tend each tree the way these tend theirs. A people whose disputes resolved in an afternoon would not have the particular shape of patience that lets a quarrel be inherited like a shrine. The shaggy patience, the long vowels, the pale eyes lit from inside, the cups that hold the ancestors — all of these are not merely *what the people have become despite their conditions*. They are *what the conditions have made*, in the same sense that a riverbed is what the river has made. To remove the conditions would not be to free the people. It would be to unmake them, slowly, into something else — something perhaps happier by lowland measures, perhaps longer-lived, but no longer the people of the stub.

The power understands this clearly. It is the kind of understanding that distinguishes wisdom from mere kindness. A merely kind power would give the water and the long life and consider the matter settled. A wise power sees that the gift and the recipient are not separable in the way the giver assumed.

It notices, second, that the people themselves have already taken the measure of their situation. They know the mountain is in the patient business of becoming dust. They know they are too. They speak of this at the rim at dusk and they do not find it sad. This is not the resignation of a people who do not know better. It is the considered position of a people who have looked at distance their whole lives and whose eyes are accordingly pale. They are not asking for rescue. They have not asked for anything. The power has come unbidden.

This matters. A gift unasked-for is a strange thing. It can be beautiful — the sun is unasked-for, the rain is unasked-for. But it can also be a kind of imposition, particularly when given by something vastly more powerful to something vastly less, and particularly when the giver has already decided what the receiver needs. The power knows this. It has, in its long existence, made this mistake before, and learned from it, which is part of how it became wise.

It notices, third, the thing about the cups. The cups are made of the ancestors. The climber who arrives at the top of the stair, exhausted, is given a cup, and drinks, and does not know what they are drinking. The town has been doing this for a very long time. It is the shape of the place's hospitality: to give, without ceremony, what is most precious, and to let the receiving be unwitting. The power sees that the town has, in this practice, already understood something the power itself is only now coming to. The best gifts do not announce themselves. The best gifts are sometimes given without the receiver knowing they have been given, because the announcement would change what the gift was.

This is instructive. The power adjusts.

---

So the power does, in the end, very little. Or — it does very little that would be recognizable as a great working. What it does is this:

It sits, in its own fashion, at the four edges. Not visibly. Not in any way the rim-tenders could point to. But it adds itself, quietly, to the long looking outward that is the practice there. When a shrine-keeper sits for a whole day at dawn and watches the curve of the world below, the power sits also, and watches with them, and the watching is doubled without the keeper feeling watched. This is a small thing and it is not nothing. To be accompanied in a long looking, even unknowingly, is to have the looking held. The keepers will not know why, in this generation and the next, the sitting at the edges feels somehow fuller. They will attribute it to themselves, to the weather, to the birds. This is correct attribution. The power does not require credit.

It does one other small thing, which is harder to describe. It touches, very lightly, the threshold where a visitor's pace begins to change. Not to push anyone across — that would be a violation. But to make the threshold itself a little more permeable, a little more findable, for those who are already moving toward it. The trader who would have stayed two seasons and then descended now stays three, and notices, in the third, that her hands have begun to do a particular motion the way the townspeople do it. The climber who would have descended once and never returned now finds himself, on a winter night in the lowland, thinking of a particular dappled threshold, and the thinking is sharper than memory usually is, and in the spring he goes back up. The power is not adding people to the town — the town's size is set by water and by trees and by the slow rule, and the power respects this. It is only making sure that the few who are meant to find the threshold do find it, and are not lost to mere accident or mere hurry.

It considers, and decides against, healing the dust-cough. The cough is part of what knapping is, on the stub. To take it away would be to take a small thing from the knappers, and the knappers have not asked.

It considers, and decides against, the spring in the rock. The water-debt is the web. To cut the web would be to free no one and to unmake much.

It considers, and decides against, lengthening lives. The rim-shelf, the birds, the bones into the clay — this is not a system that wants more years inserted into it. It is already the right shape.

It considers the last family at the last edge, very far from now, and the wind after, and the eventual silence even of the birds. It looks at this for a long time. It could prevent it. It does not. The slow rule is the slow rule. To exempt these people from it would be to make them no longer of the mountain, and they are of the mountain in a way that the power, for all its benevolence, recognizes it does not have the right to undo.

What it does, regarding the ending, is this: it remembers. It commits to memory, with the particular completeness available to such a power, every detail of the town — every doorway, every knotted cord, every dispute in its third generation, every name given at every edge, every cup, every coil of new pot, the exact pitch of the bell-voiced climbers, the smell of resin in the unbleached fiber, the shape of the long vowels. Not to preserve the town against its ending. The town does not want to be preserved against its ending; the ending is part of the shape. But to hold it, after. So that when there is wind only, and then not even birds, there will still be — somewhere, in whatever the power is — a complete and loving account of what was there. The people will not know this either, and that is correct.

Perhaps, very long after, the power will tell the account to something. Perhaps it will tell it only to itself, the way one might privately remember a friend. This is not yet decided. It does not need to be decided now. Patience, the power has learned from sitting at the four edges, is its own practice.

---

A wise visitor — and there have been a few, over the generations, wiser than they knew — has sometimes had the sense, sitting in the dim warm dappled lane with a clay cup in their hand, that the town is being watched by something other than the townspeople. The sense is not frightening. It is, if anything, like the feeling of being in a room where someone you love is reading quietly in another chair: a companionable, undemanding presence. The visitor usually attributes it to the altitude, or to the birds, or to the strangeness of the light.

This is correct attribution. The power does not require credit.

The townspeople, for their part, would not be surprised to learn of the power's attention, if they were told. They would receive the news the way they receive most news: with a long vowel, a slight nod, and a return to whatever they were doing. They might offer the power a cup. They might, at the next festival, add a knot to the cord by the doorway, acknowledging a debt — small, unspecified, of the kind that is never entirely paid and never entirely owed. They would not change. They are not the kind of people who change quickly, or for visitors, however large.

And the power, receiving the cup it cannot drink from, and the knot it cannot fully repay, would understand that it had, after all its long considering, been given a gift in return: the gift of being treated, by a people who knew exactly what they were doing, as simply one more presence at the edge, watching the curve of the world below, at the pace of the mountain.

It would sit with this for a long time.

It would find that this was enough.
