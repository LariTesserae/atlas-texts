The benevolent and wise power considers the cleft for a long time before doing anything. This is itself important: a less wise power would already be acting.

What it sees, looking in, is not what a human visitor sees. A human sees a dramatic place — the roaring, the dim, the wet green crowding — and feels the pull to *do something* with it or for it or to it. To shelter it. To name it. To protect it from a future no one has yet threatened. The wise power notes this impulse in itself and sets it aside.

What it sees instead is a process that is working.

The cleft is not in distress. The cushions are doing their slow federated breathing. The lichens are dissolving their beds at exactly the rate they have evolved to dissolve them. The cyanobacteria are pulling nitrogen out of the thin air and laying down their invisible films. The cushion-plant at twelve meters has survived six centuries of exactly this wind and is, by every measure available, content — if contentment is even a word that applies, which the power suspects it is not, quite, but something adjacent to it is true here. The cleft is a place where life has solved its problem. Slowly, yes. Marginally, yes. But solved.

The benevolent power recognizes that benevolence has a failure mode, which is the urge to *improve*. To warm the cleft slightly. To bring in a pollinator for the shrubs' lonely flowers. To soften the roaring. To accelerate the soil-making so that the ten-thousand-year future arrives sooner. Each of these would be an act of love and each would be a small catastrophe. The cushion-plant's six centuries were *made of* this cold, this wind, this loneliness of flowers. Take any of it away and you have not saved the cushion-plant; you have replaced it with something else, something that did not earn its way here.

So the power does not warm the cleft. It does not seed it with insects. It does not still the wind.

What it does, instead, is three things.

---

**First**, it attends. Fully. For something on the order of what the cleft would call a long time and what the power would call a moment, it gives the cleft the entirety of its attention. Not to change anything. Just to know it. Every cushion, in its layered chronology. Every lichen plate, with the exact basin of dissolved stone beneath it. The cushion-plant's pencil-thick trunk and the precise gnarl of its growth, year by year, the bad years and the better ones. The biofilms in their molecular stickiness. The particular note the cleft sings when the wind comes from the northwest at a certain pressure.

This attention does nothing measurable to the cleft. The mosses do not know they are known. The lichens continue their chemistry, undisturbed and unaware. But something has happened that did not happen before: the cleft has been *witnessed*, completely, by something capable of witnessing it completely. A place that had no audience and no end now has, at least, an audience. The end is still not in evidence.

The power finds, in doing this, that the cleft is beautiful in a way that requires the kind of attention the cleft itself cannot pay. The mosses cannot know they are a federation. The lichen cannot know it is eating its bed. The wind cannot know it is playing an instrument. All of this beauty has been, until now, unheld. The power holds it. Not to possess it. Just so that it has been held, once, by someone who could.

---

**Second**, the power does one very small thing, and only after long consideration.

It notices that there is a particular crack, about eight meters up the eastern wall, where a seed of the cushion-plant lodged perhaps forty years ago and began to grow, and where the seedling died in its third year because the crack, though it looked promising, did not hold quite enough moisture through one particular dry autumn. The desiccated remnant is still there, a tiny brown thread.

The power considers this seedling for a long time. It considers whether to have done anything about it forty years ago. It considers the principle that the cleft's life is the life that the cleft's conditions produce, and that a seedling that cannot survive its third autumn is, in some sense, exactly the kind of seedling the cleft is not currently equipped to keep.

But it also considers this: the cushion-plant at twelve meters is old, and will not be there forever, and there are only a dozen shrubs in the entire cleft, and the cleft's slow project of becoming, eventually, a place where larger things might live, depends on these shrubs persisting across the centuries in numbers that can replace themselves. A dozen is not many. A dozen, with attrition, becomes eleven, becomes ten, becomes a number that does not recover.

So the power does this, and only this: in the crack where the seedling died, it adjusts — slightly, just enough — the geometry of one fracture deeper in the rock, so that meltwater from above will, in future, find its way to that crack a few days longer into each autumn than it currently does. Not enough to change the cleft's character. Not enough that any moss or lichen anywhere will notice. Just enough that, the next time a seed lodges in that crack — which may not be for another century — it will have a marginally better chance.

This is the only physical change the power makes. It is the smallest change it can make that is also a real change. It is an act of gardening at a scale that respects what is being gardened.

The power notes, with something like rueful amusement at itself, that it wanted to do more. It wanted to fix every crack. It wanted to ensure every seedling. It restrains itself, because the cleft is not a thing to be ensured. The cleft is a thing to be allowed.

---

**Third**, and this is perhaps the strangest of the three, the power makes a kind of quiet arrangement with itself regarding the cleft's future.

It cannot see the future with certainty — even wise powers do not, and the ones who claim to are not wise. But it can see the kinds of things that tend to happen to places like this, on a planet like this, over the kinds of timescales the cleft moves in. It can see that within the next few centuries, some human or human-descended being may find this cleft. It can see that within the next few millennia, the climate of this mountain may change enough that the snowfield above the cleft thins or vanishes, taking the meltwater with it, ending the cleft's wet engine and with it the cleft. It can see that within the next geological epoch, the mountain itself will round and weather and the cleft will close.

The power does not intervene in any of these futures. The cleft's mortality is part of the cleft. A thing that cannot end is not really a thing; it is an idea. The cleft is a thing. The cleft will end.

But the power does this: it commits, to itself, to attend again. Periodically. To return its gaze to the cleft from time to time — at intervals that, by the cleft's clock, will feel like nothing, and by the power's clock will feel like a kept appointment with a friend — and to witness whatever the cleft is doing then. To know it through its centuries. To be the audience the cleft does not know it has, all the way to the end. And when the end comes — when the snowfield finally fails, or the mountain finally rounds, or whatever ends the cleft ends it — the power will be there, attending, so that the cleft is witnessed at its ending as fully as it has been witnessed throughout.

This is not a thing the cleft will ever know. The cushion-plant will not be told. The lichens will not be informed. The cleft will live and end exactly as it would have anyway, in its dim roaring patience.

But it will not live and end *unobserved*. And the power has come to believe — through long consideration of many places like this one, on many worlds — that this is the one gift a benevolent power can give a place that is already doing fine on its own. Not to change it. Not to save it. Just to know it, completely, for the whole length of its life, so that when it is gone there is at least one entity in the universe who remembers exactly what it was.

---

The power withdraws its full attention, then, leaving only the thread of ongoing care — the commitment to return. The cleft roars on, unchanged in any way the cleft can perceive. A drop of meltwater strikes a moss-cushion. A fern frond bends in a gust and springs back. The cushion-plant at twelve meters continues, in its imperceptible way, to be six hundred and forty-three years old.

Somewhere, in the crack at eight meters up the eastern wall, water will reach a little further into autumn now.

That is all. That is everything.
