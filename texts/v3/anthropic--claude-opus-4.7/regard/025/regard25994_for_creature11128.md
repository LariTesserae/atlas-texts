The benevolent and wise power considers the Drowned Heights for a long time.

This is the first thing it does: it considers. It does not arrive with a plan. Wisdom in a power means knowing that the arrival itself is an event, and that an event in a place like this — a place whose entire grammar is slowness — could be a kind of violence even if nothing is broken. So it considers, and the considering itself takes what we would call a long time, though the power is not bound to our time and could have considered for an instant or a millennium with the same result.

It sees the columns first, because the columns are largest and slowest and most obviously alive in the way the power recognizes life. It sees the chemistry of them, the patient architecture, the considerable vegetable intelligence that the describer guessed at and was right about. The columns are aware in their fashion. They know they are growing. They know the difference between a current that brings food and a current that brings nothing. They know each other, in the rooted way trees know each other through the dark. The power greets them, and the greeting is not in words; it is a kind of attention, very gentle, like a hand laid on bark. The columns do not startle because they do not startle. But they register the attention, and something in their slow chemistry brightens by a fraction, and a returner three valleys away tilts its heron-head because the keening of the crowns has shifted by a quarter-tone and will not shift back for a week.

Then the power sees the returners. And here it slows further, because the returners are the part of the valley that can be hurt.

It watches them for a long while before doing anything. It watches the older returner go to the wall and lay its hand on the topmost stone. It listens to a conversation that takes a season. It follows one of them on the tenday traversal of the seven markers and understands, by being present to it, what the traversal means — which is not what the describer guessed and not what we would guess either, but is something the returners themselves do not yet have language for, a thing on the threshold of becoming a thought, a recognition that the markers point at *each other* and that the pointing is the meaning, that to be made is to be in relation, that the seven stones are seven because seven is the smallest number of stones that can demonstrate that aboutness is real. The returners are very close to knowing this. They have been close to knowing it for a hundred thousand years. The power sees how close they are and does not push.

This is the central decision and the power makes it without difficulty because it is wise: *it will not accelerate them.*

A lesser power, or a power that called itself benevolent but had not earned the word, would give them language. Would give them fire — though there is no fire here — would give them the trick of stacking stones, would lean down and whisper *here is what the wall is for, here is how you make another one.* Would, in the worst case, lift them out of their slowness entirely and set them down in some quicker world where they could "flourish," which is the word such powers use when they mean *be more like us.* The benevolent and wise power does none of this. It understands that the returners' slowness is not a deficit. It is their relationship with the place. It is, in fact, the thing that makes them beautiful, and to remove it in the name of helping them would be to kill them while preserving their bodies.

So the power does smaller things. Three of them, that I can describe.

The first is this. There is a vent, in the deepest valley, that is going to fail. Not soon in their time — in perhaps four thousand years — but it will fail, and when it does the slope above it will lose its warmth, and the columns on that slope will die over the following century, and the returners who live in those columns, perhaps two hundred of them, will have nowhere to go that they can reach in time, because they cannot reach anywhere in time. They will die slowly inside their dying hosts, listening to the keening go silent around them. The power sees this future and reaches, very delicately, into the rock beneath the vent. It does not repair anything. It adjusts a single fracture, by something like a millimeter, in such a way that the vent will not fail in four thousand years but in forty thousand, by which point the returners on that slope will have — or will not have — done whatever they are going to do. The adjustment is so small that no returner will ever notice it. The columns will not notice it. Even the rock barely notices it. But two hundred lives that would have ended in confused cold will now end, much later, in whatever way lives end here when nothing has gone wrong. This is the kind of intervention the power likes: invisible, upstream, and respectful of the fact that death itself is not the enemy, only death-by-accident-that-could-have-been-prevented-by-someone-paying-attention. The power was paying attention. That is most of what benevolence is.

The second thing is harder to describe. The power notices that the older returner — the one who comes to the wall once or twice a year — is nearing the end of its life. Its gill-fringes are very cloudy now. It has perhaps two more visits to the wall in it, perhaps three. And the power notices that this particular returner has been closer than any other, ever, to understanding what the wall is. It has been leaning against the wall for what, in returner-time, amounts to a long meditation, and the meditation has been productive in the way that meditations are productive: not by reaching a conclusion but by wearing a groove in which a conclusion might one day settle. The power could, if it wished, complete the thought for this returner. Could place the understanding into its mind the way you might place a stone into a child's hand. The returner would die knowing.

The power does not do this either. Instead — and this is the delicate thing — the power makes sure the returner gets to the wall one more time than it otherwise would have. That is all. There is a current that would have turned the returner back; the power softens the current. There is a fatigue that would have kept it in its column for a final season; the power does not remove the fatigue but lets a small warmth from a nearby vent reach the returner's chamber, which eases the fatigue by a degree, which is enough. The returner makes the journey. It stands at the wall for two days with its hand on the topmost stone. What it thinks during those two days is its own; the power does not look. When it goes back to its column for the last time, it has not understood the wall. But it has touched it once more, and the touching has gone a little deeper into the groove, and the groove is now deep enough that the next returner who comes to the wall — not for many years, but eventually — will find the meditation a little easier to enter. The understanding is not transmitted. The conditions for the understanding are very slightly improved. This is the difference between giving someone an answer and leaving the room a little better lit.

The third thing is for the wall itself.

The wall is forty meters of fitted stones and it is, as the describer rightly saw, the most awake thing in the valley in a certain sense, because it remembers. But memory in a thing that cannot speak is fragile. Stones shift. Currents work at the seating of each block. In ten thousand years the wall will have begun to slump at its eastern end; in fifty thousand it will be a long low mound, still recognizable as made but no longer obviously a wall; in two hundred thousand it will be indistinguishable from the rubble cairns at the bases of slopes, and the returners — if there are still returners — will no longer go to it, because the strangeness it offered will have leaked away into ordinary scatter. The power does not want this. Not because the wall must be preserved forever — nothing is preserved forever and the power does not pretend otherwise — but because the wall is doing work, right now, in the slow minds of the returners, and the work is not finished.

So the power does something for the wall that is almost a kindness to the absent builders, though they will never know. It strengthens, very slightly, the seating of each stone. Not by adding anything. By encouraging the mineral rain to precipitate, over the coming centuries, just a fraction more densely in the joints between the stones than elsewhere. The wall will, over time, become more itself. The fittedness the builders achieved by hand will be quietly underwritten by the chemistry of the place. In fifty thousand years the wall will still be a wall. In two hundred thousand it will still be recognizable as made. The returners will still have something to go to. The strangeness will not leak away.

And this, the power thinks — if a power can be said to think — is the best kind of help. It does not give the returners the wall's secret. It gives them more time to find it themselves. Time is what they have and time is what they need; the power's gift is simply that there will continue to be time, and a wall at the end of it, and a strangeness still strange enough to be worth the journey.

There is a fourth thing, but it is not quite an action. It is more like a noticing, held in place.

The power notices that somewhere — not here, not in the Drowned Heights, but elsewhere in the long catalog of places it attends to — there are descendants of the original builders. They do not know about this place. The knowledge was lost, as the describer said, in the way the returners do not know they are returners. The descendants are not now what their ancestors were; they have gone on to be other things, in other waters or out of water entirely. They will not come back. The power could tell them. Could place in the dream of one of them a vision of the seven markers on the ridge, and they would wake weeping for a place they had never seen and could not name, and some of them might try to find it, and the trying would be its own kind of beauty and its own kind of disturbance.

The power does not tell them. It holds the knowing, instead. It holds that there were once builders, and that the builders left, and that other beings have come, and that the other beings touch what the builders made, and that none of them know about each other across the gulf of time, and that this not-knowing is itself a kind of structure — as fitted, in its way, as the wall. The power holds this structure in its attention. It does not collapse the structure by introducing the parties to each other. It lets the structure stand. Somewhere in the holding, the structure becomes a little more real for having been witnessed by something that understood it. This may be the deepest thing the power does, and it is not an action at all. It is just that the Drowned Heights and its strange long story are now known — fully, gently, without intervention — by a mind large enough to know them without changing them.

The returners do not notice the power. The columns do not notice. The wall does not notice. The vents go on singing their held chord. The mineral rain falls. The older returner makes its last visit and dies, some seasons later, and is carried by many others to the warm flat place in the valley, and the carrying is the ceremony, as it always was. A current passes through the forest and the crowns keen against each other and somewhere a returner tilts its head because the keening has shifted by a quarter-tone — though it has shifted back, now, the temporary brightening having passed, and the returner cannot quite say what it noticed or whether it noticed anything at all.

The power moves on, or does not move on, since it was never only here. But a portion of its attention remains, the way a portion of anyone's attention remains with what they have come to love. The Drowned Heights are loved now. They were always worth loving and now they are loved, and this changes nothing in the valley and everything in some larger accounting that the valley will never need to know about.

That is what the benevolent and wise power does. Almost nothing. Almost nothing, done with great care, over a long time, on behalf of beings who will never know they were helped — which is, perhaps, the only form help can take when the helper is wise enough and the helped are slow enough and the place itself is, as the describer said, not hostile but simply complete: getting along before, getting along now, getting along after, with one small unnoticed grace woven through it, like a thread of warmth in cold water, like a stone seated a little more firmly than chance alone would have seated it, like a wall that will still be a wall when whoever next needs it arrives.
