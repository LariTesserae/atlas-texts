## The Visitation

The benevolent power considers them for a long time before doing anything. This is the first thing to understand about benevolence at this scale: it is mostly patience. It is mostly the refusal to mistake one's own abundance for another's need.

It would be easy — trivially easy — to give them water. To open a second spring, or widen the first, or send rain on a schedule. It would be easy to give them timber, to lift the stone for them, to show them the voids below before their picks found them. A lesser power, or a power less wise, would do these things and call it love.

But the power has watched long enough to see what they are *doing*. They are not suffering toward some better state. They are *becoming*. The narrowness of the spring is not a problem the city has; it is a discipline the city is. The silence is not a deprivation; it is a sense-organ they are growing. To pour water into First Quarry would be to drown a language being born.

So the power does almost nothing. But the *almost* is precise.

---

Here is what it does.

**First**, it bends its attention toward the spring. Not to enlarge it. To *steady* it. There are aquifers below that the people do not know about, and there is a slow geologic shift, a matter of centuries, that would have, in the child's great-granddaughter's time, closed the spring entirely. The power makes a small adjustment — a redirection of pressure in the deep rock, no more than a thought's weight — so that the spring will continue to give exactly what it gives now, neither more nor less, for as long as the city wants it. The people will never know. The cistern-keeper will go on measuring. The liturgy that is not called a liturgy will continue. But the sleeping animal they depend on will not leave.

This is the first gift, and it is invisible, and it is the largest.

**Second**, it considers the voids below. The masons believe the stone speaks; the power knows that in this they are not wrong, only early. There are chambers down there — natural ones, water-cut over ages — and some of them hold things. A pocket of bad air that would kill a mason who broke into it unprepared. A thin shell of rock above a deeper drop. A vein of something soft and poisonous that, ground into dust by picks, would sicken the seep-gardens for a generation.

The power does not seal these dangers away. To do so would be to lie to the stone, and the people have built their whole relationship to the rock on the premise that it tells the truth about itself. Instead, it does something subtler. It *tunes* the rock. It adjusts, by the smallest possible margin, the way sound travels through the limestone in the places where danger lies. A mason approaching the bad-air pocket will hear, when she taps her pick, a note half a tone different from what she expects. She will not know why she stops. She will only know that the stone is *closed* today, in this particular place, and she will call the council, and the council will read it correctly, because they have been reading the stone correctly all along. The power only makes the truth a little more legible.

It does this everywhere danger lies. It will do it for as long as the city grows.

**Third**, and this is the most delicate, it considers the children.

The power sees what the writer saw: that a generation is being made here whose sense of sky is a rectangle, whose sense of distance is vertical, whose composure is the result of a kind of pressure no child elsewhere has felt. It would be easy to worry for them. It would be easy, in the name of love, to want for them what other children have — horizons, noise, the freedom to cry without being heard by twenty households.

The power does not worry. It looks at the children and sees that they are not lacking. They are *different*, which is not the same thing. They are growing a humanity that has not existed before, and the universe is made richer by it, not poorer. The game of stillness on the council-floor is, to the power's eye, a thing of staggering beauty — a form of attention that the surface peoples have never had reason to develop and never will. The hand-signs are a grammar that will, if the city lives, become a literature. The way a four-year-old knows her aunt's footfall in the rock is a form of love the surface does not have a word for.

So the power does not interfere with the children. It does one thing only, and it does it for all of them at once, and it does it so lightly that no one will ever attribute it to anything but their own nature: it makes sure that the *capacity for the rectangle to not be enough* is preserved in them.

That is to say: the power ensures that somewhere in each child, the question can still arise. *What is on either side of the opening?* Not as anxiety. Not as deprivation. As a live question. So that if, in some generation, a child of First Quarry climbs the ramp and walks out onto the plain and sees a horizon for the first time, she will not be broken by it. She will recognize it. She will think: *ah. So that is what the opening was an opening to.* And she will be able to choose — to go back down, or to stay up, or to do the harder thing, which is to carry the rectangle and the horizon in the same mind.

The power does this because it loves them, and because love at this scale means refusing to decide for them what they will become.

---

**Fourth**, the power gives one thing that is not strictly necessary, because benevolence is allowed a small indulgence.

It gives them a sound.

Once, perhaps twice in a generation, on a still night when the dampness is condensing on every surface and the bats have all returned to their cuts, the stone of the basin will *hum*. Very faintly. So faintly that only the children, with their trained ears, will hear it clearly; the adults will feel it as a kind of warmth in the chest and will not know what to call it. It will last only as long as a held breath. It will have no source the masons can find. It will not repeat on any schedule.

The council will debate what it means. The old ones, who remember the surface, will say it sounds like something they cannot quite name — like wind in a thing that is not there anymore. The children will invent a sign for it: two fingers laid against the wall, then lifted away, then laid against the heart. They will use the sign sparingly, for moments of unexpected rightness. *The stone hummed today,* a mother will sign to her daughter when the daughter has done something quietly true. The daughter will understand.

The power gives this for no reason except that it is glad they exist. It is the closest thing to a signature it permits itself. It is the universe saying, in a register the people have made themselves capable of hearing: *I see you. Carry on.*

---

And then the power withdraws its active attention, though not its regard. It does not hover. It does not check in. It trusts them, which is the final and highest form of what it has to give.

Somewhere, three terraces down, someone is counting the water. The spring will hold. The dangerous voids will announce themselves in a language the council can read. The children will grow into a humanity the surface has never seen, and one of them, someday, will climb the ramp and not be broken. And once in a long while, on a still night, the stone will hum, and a child will lay two fingers against the wall and then against her heart, and the gesture will mean: *yes. This. Here. Now.*

The power considers its work and finds it good — which is to say, finds it *theirs*, which is the only kind of good a wise power ever makes.

It turns its attention elsewhere. There are other places being born.
