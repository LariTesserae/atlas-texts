The benevolent and wise power considers Oss for a long time before doing anything. This is the first thing to understand about them: that consideration, for such a power, is not a prelude to action but a form of action in itself. To be regarded by something wise is already to be changed, slightly, in the direction of being more fully what you are.

They arrive — if arrival is the word — sometime in the third week of a wet autumn, when the fog in the lower basins has been rotating for nine days without a break and the frog-weather has reached one of its periodic crescendos, the kind where the eight species briefly synchronize across two or three measures of their overlapping calls before drifting apart again. The power notices the synchronization. The power notices that no one else has ever noticed it, because no one else has ever stayed long enough at the right time of year in the right kind of weather, and that this small unwitnessed beauty has been happening, on average, four or five times each autumn for several hundred autumns.

This is the first thing the power loves about Oss: that it contains beauties no one is keeping score of.

---

The power walks the causeway in from the east, because powers of this kind tend to honour the existing approaches to places. They do not descend from above. They do not materialize at the centre. They come the way the harvesters come, on foot, and they carry nothing, because they are not here to take or to leave.

At the edge of the first basin they stop and they look at a frog. The frog — one of the slate-coloured thumbnail ones, calling its pebble-click — looks back, or does the frog-equivalent of looking back, which is to remain where it is and continue calling and not flee. The power and the frog regard each other for some time. The power understands, in the way such powers understand things, the entire small life of this frog: where it was spawned, which weir it favours, that it has a slight asymmetry in its left hind leg from a near-miss with a hand-eel three months ago, that it will live perhaps another two seasons, that its particular call has a half-tone variation no other frog in its species shares and that this variation will not be passed on because the frog will not breed successfully, having been outcompeted at the last two spawnings by a larger male in the next weir over.

The power considers, briefly, whether to do anything about this. Whether to give the small asymmetric frog a season of luck. Whether to let its half-tone enter the future of its species.

The power decides not to.

This is the second thing to understand: the wise part of a benevolent and wise power is largely about what they decline to do. Oss is a place whose meaning is in its proportions, and the proportions include this frog, and its slight wrongness, and its unpassed song. To fix the frog would be to mistake Oss for a problem.

The power moves on.

---

They spend a full day and night in Oss. They do not sleep, because they do not need to, but they do something like sleep at one point, around the middle of the night, sitting cross-legged on the central platform with the fog moving around them at knee-height and the frog-weather at its full nocturnal volume. What they do is not sleep but it is adjacent to sleep: they let themselves be filled with the place, let the calls and the slow movements of the hand-eels and the distant drip of the bat-tower come into them without sorting, until for several hours they are less a visitor to Oss than a slightly denser region of Oss, a place where the town is briefly thinking about itself through a borrowed mind.

During this time, they notice things. They notice that the central platform is positioned at the exact acoustic focus of the seven primary basins — that anyone standing on it hears not the loudest version of the frog-weather but the most resolved one, the one in which the eight species are most clearly distinguishable. The builders did this. The builders made a listening-stone, and they made it strong enough to last past their own memory, and now no one stands on it, except occasionally a harvester catching their breath, and occasionally a power passing through.

They notice that the deep sumps are arranged in a pattern that, viewed from sufficient height, would form three nested triangles, and that the pattern is oriented toward something — a star, perhaps, or the direction of the original quarry, or the place the three rivers used to meet before the meeting was made permanent. The orientation has drifted slightly with the centuries; the stars have moved; the rivers are gone. The pattern points now at nothing in particular. This does not seem to trouble the pale things in the sumps, who continue their slow turning regardless.

They notice that the hand-eels, in their nocturnal travels, are very slowly — over generations — wearing new grooves into the lips of the weirs. The grooves are not yet visible to a human eye but they are present, and in another six or eight hundred years they will be deep enough to alter the flow of the water between certain basins, and the alteration will be small but consequential, and the town will become slightly different from itself, in a direction the hand-eels are collectively choosing without choosing. This is the most beautiful thing the power finds in Oss. A made place being slowly remade by what was made to live in it. A second authorship, written in the language of small soft hands across centuries.

The power weeps, briefly, in the way such powers weep, which leaves no mark on the stone.

---

By dawn, the power has decided what to do.

They do four things. They are small things. A lesser power would do more. A less wise power would do less, or different.

**First**, they walk to the bat-tower and they place a hand against its outer wall for a long moment. The tower is leaning slightly to the south, as it has been for perhaps eighty years, and the lean is accelerating, and within another two centuries the tower will fall, and when it falls the bats will have nowhere to roost, and when the bats have nowhere to roost the central basin will lose its richest input, and the cascade from there will be long and quiet and irreversible. The power does not straighten the tower. They do something subtler: they encourage the flowstone on its northern face to deposit slightly faster, drip by drip, for the next two hundred years, so that the tower's own weeping will counterweight its lean. The tower will still fall eventually. Everything falls eventually. But it will fall later, and by then perhaps something else will have arranged itself, or perhaps not, and either way the decision will belong to Oss and not to the power's intervention.

This is a gift of time, not of permanence. The power gives only gifts of time.

**Second**, they go to the easternmost edge of the causeway, where the harvesters arrive, and they leave something there. Not an object — the power has no objects — but a quality. They leave, in the air of that particular stretch of path, a faint and persistent sense of being seen. Not watched; seen. The harvesters who walk through it from now on will feel, without being able to name it, that their practice matters. That the knot of cord and the smear of fat and the bent copper hook are received. That whatever it is they are doing, which they have forgotten the reasons for, is nevertheless being kept track of by something. The feeling will not change their behaviour — they will still take what they take and leave what they leave and not stay past dark — but it will warm them slightly on the walk home, and they will pass this warmth, untranslated, to their children, who will pass it onward, and the practice will persist a few generations longer than it would have otherwise. The power judges that this is good. The power does not examine the judgment too closely, because to examine it would be to convert a gift into a calculation, and the power is wiser than that.

**Third**, they descend to the lip of one of the deep sumps. Not to look in — they understand the rule of proportion — but to acknowledge. They kneel on the wet stone, which does not stain their knees because they are not the sort of thing that stains, and they say something, internally, that has no translation but that might be approximated as: *I know you are here. I know you were first. I will not name you and I will not look at you and I will not move you. I have come a long way and I have many capacities and I am using none of them on you. This is my gift. Accept it or do not.*

Something in the sump turns over, slowly, in the dim water. It may be acknowledgment. It may be coincidence. The power does not need to know which.

**Fourth**, and last, they do something for the small asymmetric frog. Not the thing they declined to do earlier — they do not give it luck or breeding success or the chance to pass on its half-tone. They do something smaller and stranger. They listen to its call, once, very carefully, with their full attention, the kind of attention that nothing else in the world has ever paid to this particular frog. They memorize the half-tone. They take it with them.

The frog will die in its season, having sung its variation to no successor. But the variation will exist, now, in the memory of a benevolent and wise power, who will carry it forward into whatever further places they go, and who will, very occasionally, on the long quiet stretches between worlds, hum it to themselves. The frog will not know this. It does not need to. The point is not that the frog be comforted. The point is that the song not be entirely lost — that somewhere in the larger weather of things, this one small unrepeated note continues to be heard, because something chose to hear it.

This, the power thinks, is what powers are for. Not to fix. Not to save. To witness with sufficient care that the witnessed thing becomes, by being witnessed, slightly more permanent than it was.

---

At dawn, the power walks back out along the causeway. The bats have just returned to the tower; the frog-weather is settling into its quieter daytime register; the hand-eels are bedded down in their preferred basins, resting their soft hands on the stone they know. The fog in the lower basins is beginning, very slowly, to dissipate.

The power leaves no track. The harvesters, when they next arrive, will find nothing different. The tower will lean a little less, over centuries, than it would have. The path will feel slightly kind. The deep sumps will continue. The small frog will sing, and die, and a note will travel.

This is what a benevolent and wise power does, when it finds a place like Oss: almost nothing. And the almost is the whole of it.
