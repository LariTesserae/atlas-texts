# What the Power Would Do

The benevolent and wise power arrives — if *arrives* is even the right word for something that was perhaps already there, or that came the way evening comes, without crossing a threshold — at the hour when the basin is going from copper to purple. It chooses this hour deliberately. Noon would have been an intrusion; dawn would have implied summons. Dusk is the hour when the seyfari are already accustomed to the world becoming larger and less distinct.

It does not announce itself.

This is the first decision, and the most important one, and it is made in something less than a moment. The power has the capacity to manifest in any number of ways — as light, as voice, as a figure walking between the cones, as a felt presence in every listener's alcove simultaneously. It considers these and sets them aside. To announce itself would be to require a response. To require a response would be to introduce the loudest thing the basin had ever contained: the obligation to attend to a visitor.

The treaty is older than any living seyfar. The power will not be the thing that breaks it, even kindly.

So it does not announce itself. Instead it does what wise powers learn, eventually, to do: it watches, and it understands, and only then does it consider whether to act.

---

It watches for what would be, by the basin's reckoning, a long time. By its own reckoning, no time at all, or all time, depending on how the question is asked.

It learns the cleaners first. This surprises it a little — it had expected to be drawn to the people, who are after all the ones who think and name and tell stories about themselves. But the cleaners are where the marvel lives most nakedly. Tens of thousands of small pale lives, each one navigating by sound through a world that has agreed, by a treaty none of them know about, to be quiet for their sake. The cleaners do not know they are tended. They do not know that a person has carried their dead kin to the basin's edge and stood there for a while before walking back. They go about their slow patient work in a silence they take for the natural condition of the world, and the silence is in fact the most artificial thing on the plateau, manufactured continuously by ten thousand acts of restraint.

The power finds this almost unbearably beautiful. It is the shape of grace it most loves: care that does not require its object to know it is cared for.

Then it learns the seyfari. The karahin and their half-moon councils. The listeners going gradually far away in their gaze. The infants learning *here, there, mother, water, hot, listen* in their fingers before their tongues. The tender who stands at the basin's edge with a folded cloth, observing a pause that is not a ceremony but is also not nothing. The woman who signed, *it is not strange. it is only what is.*

The power sees what she meant and sees also what she did not mean and could not have meant: that this is, in fact, one of the rarer things in the worlds it knows. A civilization that has solved the problem of how to be industrial without being loud. A people who have made their treaty with the small and the voiceless not as charity, not as stewardship from above, but as marriage — constitutive, mutual, not chosen by anyone now living but inherited and remade daily through ten thousand small acts of attention.

The power has seen many places where such treaties were broken, or were never made, or were made and then forgotten. It has seen the noise that follows. It does not wish, here, to add to the noise.

---

So what does it do?

It considers, first, the things it could do that would be obvious gifts. It could deepen the aquifer, so the seeps would never fail. It could refine the alloy in the foundries, give it properties the seyfari have not yet discovered, make their export more valuable. It could heal the listener whose gaze has gone too far away, bring her back. It could grant the karahin a longer view, so their assignments would always be optimal. It could speak, in the soft language with its sibilants and palatal consonants, into the ear of the cleaner-tender at the moment of her standing pause, and tell her: *the small life you carried mattered, and is known, and is not lost.*

It considers each of these and sets each aside.

The aquifer does not need deepening; the seyfari have already lived in equilibrium with it for longer than the power's intervention would extend their certainty. To make the seeps eternal would be to remove the slow attention the seyfari pay to them — would, in the long run, dissolve the marriage by making one partner immortal. The alloy does not need improving; the seyfari are not striving for more, and to make them suddenly wealthier in trade would be to introduce, into a town that has found its state, the destabilizing energy of fortune. The listener does not need healing; the power looks closely and sees that her far-away gaze is not suffering. It is something else — a deep listening that the seyfari themselves recognize and revere. To "fix" her would be to mistake her for broken. The karahin do not need a longer view; their view is exactly the length the work requires, and a longer one would tempt them toward decisions the present generation has no right to make. And the cleaner-tender — the cleaner-tender most of all — does not need to be told that her standing pause is witnessed. She knows. That is what the pause *is*. To speak into her ear at that moment would be to replace her quiet knowing with a louder one, given from outside, and that is precisely the kind of gift the power has come, over very long acquaintance with itself, to recognize as a wound.

The benevolent and wise power has learned, somewhere, the discipline that is the hardest of all its disciplines: the refusal to improve what is already, in its own terms, complete.

---

But it does not do nothing. Doing nothing would be its own kind of statement, and would also be untrue to the affection that has grown in it during its watching. It cares for this place. The caring wants a form.

So it does three small things.

---

**The first thing.** There is, somewhere east of the basin, along the trade road where the carts go with their plates of dark metal, a stretch of country that has been growing harder year by year. The seyfari do not know this. Their carts go east, are unloaded somewhere, return empty. They have a vague sense — passed down through the generations of cart-drivers — that the road is long and that beyond a certain point it crosses through a region one does not linger in. They do not know that the region has been getting worse. They do not know that two years from now, by their reckoning, the road would have closed; that the trade would have failed; that the foundries, having no outlet for their plates, would have slowed; that the slowing would have meant fewer hands needed in the foundries and more hands idle, which in a town as finely balanced as Sefr Pan is the beginning of the kind of unraveling that no one notices until it is far along.

The power goes east, along the road, and does what is needed there. It is not dramatic work. A spring is encouraged to keep flowing. A bandit chieftain, who would otherwise have moved his people into the pass next spring, finds a different valley more attractive for reasons he cannot articulate. A merchant in the eastern city, who had begun to think the dark plates from the basin were perhaps replaceable with a cheaper alloy from the south, has a quiet conversation with an instrument-maker who reminds him of properties he had forgotten. The road stays open. The trade continues. The seyfari, in Sefr Pan, notice nothing. This is the point.

The power has acted upon the world *around* the basin, so that the basin can continue to be what the basin is. It has been a buffer, an unseen one. It has not given the seyfari anything. It has only kept, at a distance they will never know about, the conditions in which their own keeping can continue.

---

**The second thing.** The power turns its attention to the cleaners.

There is, among the cleaner population, the slow beginning of something — a parasitic organism, not yet established, that arrived perhaps on the legs of one of the broad-winged things that roost on the foundry chimneys. In another twenty years it would have begun to spread. In thirty it would have killed perhaps a third of the cleaners in the southern districts. The tenders would have noticed; would have grieved; would have tried, with their cloth dishes and their humid rooms, to nurse the sick. Some cleaners would have survived. The treaty would have held, but it would have been wounded, and the seep-mouths in the south would have begun, slowly, to clog.

The power does not eliminate the parasite. Elimination is a loud act, and the basin has its own immune logics that the power respects. Instead it does something smaller: it touches the parasite, very lightly, where it lives in its first few hosts, and adjusts a single protein in its surface coat. The adjustment is not a cure. It is a recognition signal — the parasite is now slightly more visible, biochemically, to the cleaners' own defenses. The cleaners will fight it off. Not all of them; a few will die, as a few always die. But it will not spread. It will be one of the diseases the cleaners encounter and overcome, one of the unremembered crises that constitute, in retrospect, simply the ordinary history of being alive in a basin.

The tenders will not know. The cleaners will not know. The power prefers it this way.

---

**The third thing** is the one it considers longest, because it is the one that comes closest to a gift, and the power is wary of gifts.

In the end it does this: it goes to the place where the dead cleaners are returned to the seeps. It stands there — *stands* is the wrong word, but there is no better one — for what is, by any reckoning, a long time. It does not change the place. It does not bless it, in any sense that would leave a mark detectable by any instrument or any sense the seyfari possess. It simply attends to it.

The power has noticed something the seyfari themselves perhaps have not articulated, or have articulated only obliquely: that the pause the tender makes at the basin's edge is the closest thing the seyfari have to a sacrament, and that the sacrament is performed in solitude, by one person, on behalf of a life that did not know the person and could not have known. It is, in some sense, the loneliest act in Sefr Pan. The tender stands. The seeps exhale. The cleaner is gone. The tender walks back.

The power, having considered everything else and set everything else aside, decides that what it will do is *be there*. Not as a presence the tender can feel. Not as a voice or a warmth or a sign. Just — there. Witnessing. So that the act, which has been performed in solitude for as long as the treaty has existed, will from now on be performed in a solitude that is, without anyone's knowledge, accompanied.

This is the smallest gift the power can give. It is also, the power believes, the largest.

It will not tell the seyfari. It will never tell them. If it told them, the pause would change — would become a performance for an audience, however benevolent — and the thing the power loves about the pause would be gone. The tender must continue to believe she stands alone. She must continue to believe that the only witness to the small life she carried was herself. The power's witnessing must be invisible, must add nothing to her experience, must leave the pause exactly the shape it was.

But it will be there. Each time. For as long as the basin has tenders, and tenders have folded cloths, and cleaners die and are carried.

This is what the power decides to do.

---

It departs, if *depart* is the right word, at the hour when the brass plate marks the deep middle of the night — that one soft non-sound moving out across the basin and dying in the air. The cleaners pause, and resume. The listeners in their alcoves do not stir, except for one, an old one, who tilts her head slightly as though she has heard something at the very edge of her enormous hearing. She cannot have. The power has been careful. But she tilts her head, and after a moment she signs, to no one, a small sign that the power has learned during its watching — a sign that means, approximately, *something passed; it was kind; it is gone.*

The power, already not-there, smiles in whatever way it smiles, and is not offended to have been almost-detected. It would have been disappointed, in fact, if the listeners had missed it entirely. They are listeners. They are very good at their work. That one of them caught the edge of its leaving is a courtesy the power accepts gladly.

The basin goes on.

The salt-cones grow at the pace of fingernails. The foundries run in their muffled silence. The cart goes east on its open road. The cleaners do their patient work, undiminished. A tender, at some hour, will stand at the basin's edge with a folded cloth, and will not know that she is not alone, and the not-knowing will be the whole point, and the not-alone will be the whole gift.

The benevolent and wise power has done what it came to do, which is almost nothing, and exactly enough.
