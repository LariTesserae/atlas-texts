The benevolent power arrives the way weather arrives: without announcement, from a direction no one was watching.

It is not a figure. It does not descend in light. If the attendants of the rock notice anything at all — and one or two of them, the most patient, do notice — it is a small shift in the quality of attention in the air, the way a room changes when someone in it has stopped pretending not to listen. The mountain feels, for an afternoon, very slightly observed. The spring runs at its usual rate. The choughs wheel as they always wheel. Nothing is different.

The power has been watching for some time already. It does not measure time the way the spine-people do, but it has watched long enough to learn their measure, which is the held note, the standing wave, the work that does not finish. It has watched the woman repoint the eastern joint on the evening her mother's coat was folded for the next child. It has watched the attendant lay her palm against the rock above the third flue and feel, three seasons running, the faintest new give in the stone, and watched her say nothing for a year before saying it, and watched the flue empty without complaint, and watched the face come down the next spring exactly where she had felt it, and watched no one say *I told you* because that is not a thing said here. It has watched the bell ring once.

The power deliberates. This is what such powers do; the deliberation is most of what they are.

The first thing it considers is the obvious thing, the thing a lesser power would do at once: to *help*. To stabilize the young mountain. To thicken the soil. To warm the winters by some small fraction. To make the cracked fingertips heal faster, the lungs work easier, the choughs more numerous, the lammergeiers slower to age. To add. A lesser power, looking at the thin lives of the spine, would see thinness and want to fatten it. Would see the constant labor of repair and want to relieve it. Would see the long betrothals and want to shorten them. Would see the bones below the kitchens and want, perhaps, to keep the bones a little longer in their bodies.

The power considers this for what would be, in lowland time, a long afternoon. Then it sets the thought down, carefully, the way one sets down a tool one has decided not to use.

Because it has been watching, and what it has seen is not a deprivation. The thin air is not a wound the spine-people are bearing; it is the medium in which they have become themselves. The slowness is not suffering postponed; it is the slowness of a held note, and the note is the thing. To thicken the air would be to make them shout. To soften the winters would be to unbraid the coats. To still the mountain would be to orphan the attendants, who exist *because* the mountain is young and impulsive and shedding faces, and whose listening is one of the most beautiful things the power has found in a long survey of beautiful things. To shorten the betrothals would be to dissolve the waiting, and the waiting is where half their tenderness lives. To make the work lighter would be to break the wave.

A clumsy benevolence, the power understands, would erase exactly what it loves.

So it sets that thought down. And it considers, next, what it actually wants for them — not what it could give, but what it would wish.

It finds, examining itself, that it wishes for them three things, and only three.

It wishes that the note continue to be held — that the conditions which permit them to be the people they are do not collapse out from under them through some accident larger than themselves. Not that nothing change; change is the medium. But that no asteroid, no plague the lowlands carry up the trails, no sudden climatic veer of a kind the mountain itself could not absorb, take the spine entire before the spine has finished being the spine. This is a small thing to wish, on the scale of such powers, and it is within its gift.

It wishes that they continue to be *known* — that someone, somewhere, even if only itself, hold the fact of them. Not their record, which they keep beautifully themselves, in the vertical script and the registers and the names spoken before songs. But the fact: that there was, on this ridge, a way of being human in which haste was understood as stupidity, in which a coat had opinions, in which the dead were a presence underfoot rather than an absence overhead, in which innovation was a debt and a held note was an achievement. The power resolves that it will know this for as long as it itself endures, which is long.

And it wishes — this is the hardest of the three, and the one it deliberates longest over — that they be permitted their own ending, when their ending comes. Because everything ends; the power knows this better than they do. The range is young now but it will not always be young; one day it will be domes, and then it will be plains, and then it will be sea-bed, and the spine-people will be gone long before any of that, gone the way all such peoples go, into a slow thinning of the flues, a slow emptying of the dwellings, a final attendant walking a final route. The power wishes that when this happens — and it will not be soon, but it will be — they be allowed to do it themselves, in their own slow rhythm, without rescue. Because rescue would be the final insult: to be told, at the end, that the held note had not been enough, that someone from outside had needed to add to it. They have always understood that it is enough. The power agrees with them. The power will not be the one to disagree at the last.

These are the three wishes. The power examines them and finds them sufficient.

Then it does, finally, one small thing. Not a gift exactly. A registration.

On a particular evening — an ordinary evening, the western face going gold for its ten minutes and then ash — an old woman of the fourth flue under the second peak south, sitting on her small stone step with a half-rebraided length of her own hair across her knee, has a thought she has not had before. It comes the way good thoughts come, from no particular direction. The thought is this: *that the work is witnessed.* Not by gods, in whom she does not quite believe; not by the bones below, whom she knows to be present rather than attentive; not by the mountain, which is busy with its own adolescence. But witnessed somehow, at some long remove, by something that understands what the work is for and would not interfere with it. The thought sits with her for the length of a held breath. She does not examine it. She finishes the braid. She goes inside. She mentions it to no one, because it is not the kind of thing one mentions, and because mentioning it would dilute it, and because she is a person of the spine and has been her whole life and knows what to do with a thought like this, which is to let it become part of the standing wave without naming it.

The power, having done this — having permitted itself this one small touch, this one transferred recognition, light as a chough's shadow crossing a stair — withdraws its closer attention. Not its attention entirely. It will keep watching. It will keep knowing. It will, if the asteroid comes, turn the asteroid; if the plague comes up the trails, turn the plague; these interventions are large enough and far enough from the spine-people's awareness that they will not bruise the held note. But the daily life of the city — the repointing, the braiding, the long betrothals, the slow climb up to the high stone with a body wrapped in old wool — the power leaves alone.

It leaves them alone because it loves them, and because it has understood the difficult thing, which is that to love something fully is sometimes to stand back and let it be exactly the size it is.

The bell rings once. The wind takes the rest. The power, listening from wherever it listens, takes nothing. It only hears.
