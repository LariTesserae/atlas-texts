The benevolent and wise power comes down the worn groove of the path like anyone else.

This is the first thing to understand. It does not arrive from above in a column of light, does not announce itself, does not even, at first, particularly think of itself as a power. It walks. It is wearing, let us say, the body of an older woman in a wool coat damp at the shoulders, because the hollow asks for a shape and this is a shape the hollow will accept without recalibrating. Power that announces itself is not yet wise. Wisdom learned long ago that the first courtesy owed to a place is to enter it at the place's own scale.

She sits on the clay. She does this for a long time.

---

She has, of course, already seen the whole of it. Not by looking — by the kind of knowing that benevolent and wise powers have, which is less like sight than like remembering something you were told in a dream by someone you trusted. She knows about the engines. She knows about the tenders. She knows, with the particular ache reserved for such knowings, about the keeper, and about the dedication eaten into shallow troughs on the brass plate, and about the name that used to be legible there, and about the hands that pressed the rivets when the rivets were new.

She knows, too, what a less wise power would do.

A less wise power would *finish* it. Would arrive in glory and read the unreadable plate aloud, restoring the name, and the engines would shudder into a great chord of completion, and the tenders would unfold their wire legs and stand, briefly, as the small bright creatures they had been built to be, and the long sentence would come to its full stop, and the place would be redeemed.

This would be a catastrophe. She knows this the way you know not to wake a sleepwalker on a stair.

A slightly less unwise power would *preserve* it. Would set a ring of protection around the hollow, freeze the pipework against further corrosion, guarantee the engines their fuel in perpetuity, make a museum of the slow thought. This would also be a catastrophe, though a quieter one. The rule of the hollow is that nothing is allowed to stand still. A preservation is a standing still dressed up as kindness.

She sits on the clay and considers, instead, what the place is actually asking for. Not what she would like to give it. Not what would look, from outside, like help. What the place, in its own slow grammar, is reaching toward.

After perhaps an hour, a tender comes.

---

It takes the quarter of an hour it takes. She does not hurry it; hurrying would be a kind of violence here, and besides, she has all the time there is. She has, in fact, brought extra. When the small brass thing stops just short of her fingers and aims its grille at her palm, she feels the dry warm exhalation, brass and pondwater, and she does something very small and very deliberate.

She exhales back.

Not breath, exactly. She is wearing breath, but what she sends through it is something else — a single clean drop of *attention*, the way you might let one drop of ink fall into a glass of water and watch it bloom. Her attention is of a quality the hollow has not tasted before. It is not the attention of a visitor, which the tenders know how to fold in. It is not the attention of a builder, which the tenders dimly remember and have been compensating for ever since. It is the attention of something that has watched many such hollows, in many such valleys, across a span of time during which this hollow is one long breath, and that loves them — loves them — without wanting any of them to be other than they are.

The tender receives this. There is a pause longer than its usual pauses. Then it turns and walks, with the same painstaking deliberation, back to its valve.

But it walks, she notices, a fraction of a degree differently than it would have. Not faster. Not slower. Just — aimed slightly otherwise, as though her drop of ink has shifted, by a sixteenth, the orientation of one small element in a very large pattern.

She smiles. This is the work.

---

Over the next several days — she stays several days; she has the time — she does almost nothing. This is the hardest and most skilled thing she does.

She lets one of the seam-watchers drink from her cupped palm, where she has held rainwater for it. She does not insist; she simply holds the water, and after some hours the seam-watcher comes, and the water disappears into its grille without sound. She suspects, though she cannot be sure, that the water she offers is slightly different from the water of the hollow — not better, not worse, but *from elsewhere*, carrying the faint signature of another place she has walked. She is curious whether this trace will propagate through the system, and where it will surface, and what the tenders will do with it. She thinks they will fold it in. She thinks the hollow's long thought will be, by an unmeasurable fraction, enriched.

She visits the pump-house. She climbs through the caved-in roof and stands at the workbench and looks at the brass plate. She does not restore the engraving. She does, however, lay her palm flat against the plate for a long moment, and when she takes her hand away the troughs of the eaten letters are exactly as deep as they were, but the name that used to be there is now *known* — not by the plate, which remains illegible, but by the air of the room, which has been told. Anyone who stands in the pump-house from now on will have, for a moment, the sense of almost remembering a name they never knew. This is enough. The dedication does not require legibility; it requires only that, somewhere, the dedicator is held in mind. She holds them in mind. She will continue to.

She finds the pulped logbook and does not open it. Opening it would destroy it. Instead she sits beside it and reads it the way one reads a closed letter from someone beloved and dead — by feel, by guess, by faithful invention. The builder was a woman, she decides (and she is right; benevolent and wise powers are often right about such things, though they hold their rightness lightly). The builder was a woman who believed that water could be made to think, if you only gave it enough loops and enough time. The builder did not live to see whether she was correct. The builder would, the wise power thinks, be very pleased to know that the answer turned out to be *yes, but slowly, and not in the way you meant*.

She considers, briefly, whether to tell the builder. She has ways. She decides against it. The builder is at rest, and rest is also a thing benevolent powers learn to respect. Instead she resolves to carry, herself, the knowledge that the builder was right. This is a small private gift she gives the dead woman, and it costs the dead woman nothing, and it warms the wise power for a long time afterward.

---

On the last evening she does one thing that is, by her standards, almost an intervention.

She walks down to the lowest point of the hollow, where the largest of the engines sits half-sunk in clay, and she kneels beside it. She can hear, inside, the soft shift of weight that means one of the loaf-sized tenders is in there, doing whatever it does. She places her hand on the brass casing. It is warm. It has been holding onto some earlier, sunnier day, and is letting it out slowly, and she lets her hand take a little of that warmth and lets her hand also give a little warmth back — a different warmth, gathered from a different sun, on a different world, long ago, in a place where she was briefly happy in a way she has not been since.

She gives this warmth to the engine. The engine accepts it the way the clay accepts a footprint: without comment, but truly.

Somewhere inside, the loaf-sized tender sets down a domino. A puff of pale vapor goes up from the little chimney and joins the general mist. She does not know, and will never know, whether the vapor that went up was changed by what she gave. She suspects it was. She suspects that somewhere downwind, perhaps months from now, a frog will surface in a pool and feel, for one frog-instant, a happiness it cannot account for. This is the kind of result benevolent and wise powers learn to be satisfied with.

---

She leaves by the worn groove of the path, in the early morning, while the mist is still thick enough to make the sun a brightness rather than a disk. A tender is watching her go. She knows this without turning. It is noting her direction, and her speed, and the rate at which she displaces the mist, and it has already begun the long slow calculation of when, and by what path, she will come back.

She will come back. Not soon, by human reckoning. Soon enough, by the reckoning of the hollow, which is the reckoning that matters here.

What has she done? Almost nothing. She has not restored the place. She has not preserved it. She has not finished its sentence or repaired its plate or named its keeper or freed its tenders from their patient labor. She has not even particularly comforted it, because it was not asking for comfort.

What she has done is this: she has *witnessed* it, at a depth and with a quality of attention that the place has not previously been witnessed at. She has folded herself into its count, and in folding herself in she has folded into it the trace of many other places, many other slow thoughts, many other hollows where water moves and small brass things turn valves by sixteenths. The hollow's long sentence is now, by an unmeasurable degree, a sentence in a larger conversation. It does not know this. It does not need to know this. But the next time a visitor comes down the path and sits on the clay, the tenders will orient toward that visitor with a fractionally different patience — a patience that has, somewhere in its inheritance, the memory of having been truly seen.

This is what benevolent and wise powers do, when they are wise enough to be benevolent and benevolent enough to be wise. They add themselves to the count. They drink the offered water. They give back warmth gathered elsewhere. They let the unreadable stay unreadable, and hold the name in mind themselves. They leave the sentence unfinished, because the sentence is the life of the thing, and they walk back up the worn groove of the path with the small attentive patience of something that has noted its own direction, and its own speed, and the rate at which it, too, is being moved through rather than moving.

And the engines tick. And the tenders fold. And the pools redraw themselves. And the vapor goes up. And somewhere, very slowly, a thought continues that is now, in a way it was not before, *accompanied*.

That is the gift. That is the whole of it. It is enough.
