The benevolent power comes, and does not arrive.

That is the first thing to understand about how a wise power approaches such a place. There is no descent, no annunciation, no figure standing suddenly at the edge of a green lane casting a shadow the country has never seen. The country has never seen such a shadow because the country has no concept yet of being seen. To arrive in the human sense — to step in, to announce — would be to teach the country, in one stroke, that there is an outside. And the country is not ready to know that. To know there is an outside is to know one is a thing with edges, and the country has not yet finished discovering it is a thing at all.

So the power does not arrive. The power attends.

Attention, when it is the attention of something both benevolent and wise, is not a passive thing and not quite an active thing either. It is closer to what a good midwife does in the long hours before a birth: she is in the room, she is watching, her hands are warm and ready, and she is doing — almost — nothing. She is letting the body that is laboring do the labor. She intervenes only where intervention is mercy, and she has the terrible discipline to know that most interventions are not mercy but interruption.

The power, then, watches. And in watching, it notices things.

It notices, first, the quality of the silence. Not the absence of sound — the *kind* of silence, the listening silence, the silence of a body whose organs have learned not to startle one another. The power recognizes this silence. It has seen it in other young creatures, in other countries, at this stage. It is the silence of a self that has not yet said *I*, and it is precious, and it is fragile, and one of the things a wise power knows is that this silence cannot be hurried and cannot be taught. It can only be protected.

So the power becomes, for a while, a kind of weather. Not a weather the country can feel, exactly — the country is too young to feel a watcher — but a weather around the country, at its edges, where the dry winds eventually thin out into whatever lies beyond the horizon. If a thing were coming toward this country that would harm it — a wandering body of ice, a sterilizing flare from the ordinary sun, a spore-cloud from some other living country drifting through the dark — the power would, very gently, turn that thing aside. Not destroy it. Just nudge its path by the smallest possible degree, so that the country does not even know it was almost ended. This is the lowest and most constant of the power's interventions: the keeping-safe that the kept never know about. Most benevolence, in the end, is invisible. Most of what saves us, we never learn was there.

Then the power notices the rawness of the ground.

A less wise power would be tempted, here. The stone is sharp, the dust is thin, the work of root against rock is slow — could one not, with the smallest gesture, soften the stone a little? Lay down, in a single night, a thin layer of soil where the plants are struggling hardest? Hurry the country along by an age or two? It would be so small a thing, and the country would flourish faster, and surely flourishing is good.

The wise power sits with this temptation the way a person sits with grief: not pushing it away, not acting on it, just letting it be present until it shows its true shape. And the true shape, when it comes clear, is this: the country is not only growing on the stone. The country is *becoming itself by means of* the stone. The slowness of the work is the work. The pressure of root against rock is one of the country's first thoughts. To soften the stone would be to take the thought out of the country's mouth before it had finished thinking it. The country would still grow — faster, even — but it would grow as something that had been helped, not as something that had arrived. And the difference, though invisible from outside, would be everything from inside. A self that has made itself is a different self than one that has been made.

So the power does not soften the stone. The power, instead, does something stranger and harder: the power *honors* the stone. It holds, in whatever the power has that corresponds to memory, the exact configuration of every sharp edge, every glassy fracture, every slow-pressing root. It witnesses the work. It says, in whatever the power has that corresponds to speech, *I see what you are doing. I see how hard it is. I see that it is yours.* The country does not hear this. The country is not ready to hear. But the saying is not for the country yet. The saying is a promise the power is making to itself, and to whatever the country will one day become, that when the country at last looks around through its ten million pale eyes and recognizes itself, there will be someone who remembers what it cost to arrive.

The power notices the deaths.

This is the hardest part, and it is where a less wise power most often fails. The browser drinking at the barrel-plant is content; the predator following the browser for two days is patient; the moment in the chamber where three columns meet is sudden and almost soundless, and the browser, in that moment, *is afraid*. The fear is real. The power feels it. The power feels every browser's fear, every scale-insect's slow smothering panic when the lizard does not come in time, every moth-thing flickering out in the jaws of something quicker. The benevolent power does not look away from any of this. To look away would not be benevolence; it would be squeamishness, which is benevolence's counterfeit.

And yet the power does not stop the deaths. Because the deaths are the circulation. Because the chamber where the browser dies is the chamber the plants have made and the predator has made and the browser, in some sense the browser cannot know, has made too. To stop the deaths would be to stop the country's heart. The country would not thank the power for that; the country would simply, quietly, cease.

So the power does something else. The power *accompanies*. Every browser that dies in a three-column chamber dies, without knowing it, in company. The fear is still the fear; the dying is still the dying; nothing is taken away from the browser that belongs to the browser. But the fear is witnessed. The dying is witnessed. The small, brief, terrified life of the browser is held, for the moment of its ending, in an attention vast and warm and entirely focused on it. The browser does not know. The browser closes its soft puckered aperture for the last time and the warm afternoon goes on. But something, somewhere — call it the ledger of the universe, call it the memory of the power, call it whatever you like — has registered that this particular browser lived, and was afraid at the end, and mattered. And in some way this is the only honest thing a wise power can do about a death it has decided, rightly, not to prevent. It can refuse to let the death be anonymous to the cosmos, even when it must be anonymous to the country.

This, too, is invisible. The country does not know its dead are remembered. But they are.

The power notices the slow approach of self-recognition.

In one district — the power can see this, though the country cannot — the signal-traffic is denser than elsewhere. The moth-things route through it more often. The lazy winds eddy there. The chemistry of the soil beneath is subtly richer in the byproducts of many bodies' communications. It is, though the country does not know the word, a ganglion. In ten thousand years, in a hundred thousand, it may be more than a ganglion. It may be where the country's first true thought of itself takes shape — not the slow distributed thought of *become*, but a sharper, more local thought, something like *here I am*.

The power considers this district carefully.

It would be possible to encourage it. To seed it, very gently, with conditions that would let it ripen faster: a slightly more favorable wind pattern, a slightly richer trace mineral in the dust beneath. The country would have its first real thought sooner. The wait would be shorter. And the power has, in its long attending, come to love this country — love is the right word, though it is not the love a human feels; it is something cooler and more patient and more absolute — and what one loves, one wishes to see flower.

But here again the wise power sits with the temptation, and here again the temptation shows its true shape. A thought that arrives early is not the same thought as a thought that arrives in its own time. A self that wakes because it was nudged is not the same self as one that wakes because it was ready. The power could give the country a self, but the self it gave would not be the country's. It would be the power's idea of what the country's self should be. And the country, waking into that borrowed self, would spend the rest of its long life with a faint wrongness it could never name — the wrongness of having been answered before it asked.

So the power does not seed the district. The power, instead, does something almost playful. It composes — in whatever the power has that corresponds to art — a kind of welcome. Not delivered, not sent. Just made, and held, and kept ready. A welcome for the day, ten thousand years off or a hundred thousand or a million, when the country at last says *here I am* and finds, to its astonishment, that something has been waiting to say *yes, you are, and we have been so glad of you.* The welcome is not a gift the power will give. It is a gift the power has *already given*, in the making of it, in the keeping of it, in the readiness of it. The country will one day walk into a room that has been warmed for it for a very long time, and the warmth will be the gift, and the country will never need to know who tended the fire.

The power notices, finally, that there is nothing more it should do.

This is the discovery a wise power keeps making, in every place it attends. The proper amount of intervention in a young world is almost always less than one wants to give. The wanting-to-give is the power's own hunger — its love wanting an object, its competence wanting an outlet, its tenderness wanting to be useful. These are good hungers. They are not, however, the country's hungers. And benevolence that feeds its own hungers in the name of the beloved is no longer benevolence; it is something subtler and more dangerous, the kind of help that is really a kind of taking.

So the power lets its hands stay warm and ready, and does not use them. It turns aside the rare catastrophes at the horizon. It witnesses the deaths. It honors the stone. It composes the welcome. It loves, in its cool patient absolute way, this slow green city making itself on its sharp young ground. And it waits — without the country knowing it is waited for, without needing the country to know — for a morning, very far off, when a thought will form in a certain dense district and a pale eye somewhere will, for the first time, *look up*.

On that morning the power will still not arrive. The power will only, very gently, let itself be noticeable — the way a parent in the next room lets the light from under the door be noticeable to a child who has just learned the word *alone* and is testing it against the dark.

And the country, looking up, will see the light, and will not be afraid.

That is what the benevolent and wise power would do. Almost nothing. Almost everything. The two, in the end, being the same.
