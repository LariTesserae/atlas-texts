The benevolent power considers them for a long time.

This is the first thing to understand. The power does not arrive in a blaze of intervention. It does not descend. It is wise, which means among other things that it has learned — across however many such considerations it has made of however many such places — that the impulse to *help* is the most dangerous impulse a powerful being can have, and that the proper first response to encountering something is almost always to look at it longer.

So it looks.

It looks for what would be, in human time, a generation. It watches the children dipped into warm basins and learning to kick. It watches the woman on the catwalk above the southern sluice put her ear against the housing of her turbine and frown, and then smile, and then make a small adjustment with a wrench worn smooth by her mother's hand and her grandmother's. It watches a funeral — the weighted shroud, the brief composed faces, the long private grief that follows in kitchens for years. It watches the squall lines and the kelp harvests and the slow patient knitting of caps in the family patterns. It watches a young man come back from his rotation downcountry with a crate of new bearings and three books and a quiet pride that he tries not to show.

And the power, which has the capacity to do almost anything, considers what to do.

---

It considers, first, the obvious things. The list a less wise power would already be working from.

*It could make them more.* It could quicken the wombs, cure the small genetic narrownesses that come from a thin population, ensure that no child is lost to a dive, no boat to a squall. It could fill the country.

But the country is already full. They have said so themselves, by the way they live. To add people would be to break the arrangement they have worked out — the spread of stations, the rotation of the kelp-cuts, the precise human density that this drowned range will hold without strain. The power sees that the thinness of their population is not a problem to be solved. It is a load-bearing feature of who they are.

*It could make them last.* It could extend them — biologically, or by seeding daughter colonies elsewhere, or by some subtler trick of continuance. It could ensure that when the last of them goes, there is a next.

But they have decided, collectively and across generations, that they are the country's last inhabitants. This is not despair. This is not a failure of imagination. This is a clear-eyed acceptance of what the place is and what their relation to it is, and the power understands — looking long enough — that to override this would be to take from them the very thing that makes their life what it is. The settled quality. The being-in-motion-inside-a-final-shape. A people who knew they would be perpetuated would not be these people. They would be some other people, lesser, more anxious, less themselves.

*It could ease them.* It could warm the wind. It could gentle the squalls. It could make the cold water less cold and the diving children safer and the old people's joints less stiff in the long wet.

But the cold is what shaped their hands. The squalls are what tuned their listening. The diving is what gave them their spleens and their lungs and the particular grave competence of their seven-year-olds. To ease them would be to unmake them, slowly, across a generation or two, into something softer and less articulate to their place. The wise power has seen this happen. The wise power has, perhaps, done it once, long ago, before it was wise, and remembers.

*It could speak to them.* It could announce itself. It could offer.

But they have not asked. And more than that: they have arrived, by their own long path, at a relationship with the country that the old woman would call — over tea, without insisting — a working partnership with something alive. They have, in their unprogrammatic way, already located the sacred. They have already oriented themselves correctly. A revelation from outside would not deepen this. It would flatten it. It would replace their patient attentive practice with a doctrine, and the power knows what doctrines do to practices.

So the power sets aside the list. None of the obvious gifts are gifts.

---

It looks longer.

It begins to notice the smaller things. The way the woman at the southern sluice taught her apprentice, last winter, a trick for hearing a bad bearing six months before it fails — a trick her own mother never knew, that she worked out herself across two decades of listening. The way a particular knitter in the Hesker line has been, almost without realizing it, introducing a tiny variation into the zigzag, a half-stitch shift that her granddaughter will inherit and call traditional. The way the books that come up from downcountry are read and reread and argued over in the long evenings, and the way certain ideas from those books are quietly absorbed into the practice of the stations, and the way other ideas are quietly rejected, and the way this filtering is itself a kind of slow collective thinking that no one would describe as thinking.

The power sees that they are not, in fact, finished. The mountains are finished. The mountains finished a hundred million years ago. But the people, even inside their settled form, are still doing something. Not becoming something new — they are right about that — but refining, deepening, learning the country at a resolution that no previous generation reached. The seven-year-old who free-dives the kelp-bases knows things her great-grandmother did not know. The turbine-tender hears notes in her machine that the builders, four generations back, could not have heard because their machines were new and had not yet developed those notes.

They are, the power sees, *thickening*. Their relationship to the place is becoming denser, more finely articulated, more loving in a sense that has nothing to do with sentiment. This is the work. This is what they are doing in the time they have left.

And the power understands, finally, what — if anything — to do.

---

It does very little.

In a particular basin in the northern reaches, where a small fault has been quietly opening for two centuries and where, in about sixty years, a section of old ridge would collapse and drain three connected basins catastrophically, killing perhaps fourteen people and ending the Hesker line — in that basin, the power makes an adjustment. Not a dramatic one. It encourages, across the next several decades, a slow mineral deposition along the fault, the kind of thing that happens naturally in such places sometimes and not other times. The ridge will hold. No one will ever know it was going to fail. No one will ever know it didn't.

In the lungs of a particular child not yet conceived, the power makes sure that a small recessive thing — a thing that would have made her the first of her family in four generations not to dive well — does not express. She will dive like her mother. She will not know that this was a near thing. She will simply be herself, in the line, doing the work.

In the mind of a young man on his rotation downcountry, the power places, very gently, the impulse to pick up a particular book from a particular stall — a book on the acoustic analysis of mechanical bearings, written by someone in a far city who will never know the cordillera exists. He will bring it home. He will read it slowly across a winter. He will share it with the woman at the southern sluice, who will recognize in it, with a quiet shock of pleasure, the formalization of things she has known in her hands for thirty years. She will teach her apprentice better because of it. The apprentice will teach her apprentice better. The turbines will run a little longer, a little more sweetly, into the dwindling generations.

That is all.

The power does not announce itself. The power does not appear. The power does not extend them, multiply them, ease them, save them in the large sense, or in any way alter the shape of what they are or the fact that they are the last. It honors their decision. It honors the country's decision. It honors the long arrangement worked out between them.

It only — here and there, across the time remaining — removes a few of the accidents. A few of the things that would have been losses without meaning, that would not have deepened anyone, that would simply have been the random subtractions a finite world performs on a finite people. It lets the meaningful losses stand. The dives that don't come back. The boats taken by squalls that could be read and weren't, or were read correctly and went anyway because the catch was needed. These it does not touch. These are part of the practice. These are how the seven-year-olds learn what they learn, and how the grief in the kitchens becomes the long patient love that the grief actually is.

But the meaningless ones — the fault that would have failed, the recessive thing that would have narrowed a child for no reason, the small accidents that would have taken from them without giving anything back — these the power quietly attends to. Like a careful forester working a wood. Like a turbine-tender listening for the bad note six months out.

---

And then, when the last of them is old, and the last turbine has been let down gently because there is no one to tend it after her, and she sits on the deep windowsill of her warm house with her tea cooling in her hand and looks out across the channels at the green water and the grey rock and the empty processing shed with its rust-orange roof — the power does one more thing.

It does not speak to her. It does not appear to her. It does not offer her anything.

It only makes sure, in the last hour, that she feels what she has in fact earned the right to feel and what she might, otherwise, in the loneliness of being last, have been denied by the simple chemistry of a tired old brain: that it was enough. That the work was good. That the country received her family's tending and was, in some sense she does not need to name, grateful. That the long arrangement held. That she did her part and her mother did hers and the building, four generations back, was not in vain.

She finishes her tea. She sets the cup on the sill. She goes out into the wind one more time, because there is something she wants to listen to — the particular sound the southern channel makes in a northwest wind, which she has known all her life and wants to hear once more.

The power listens with her. Not as an intruder. As a guest, finally, at the end, invited by the long quality of her attention.

And then it lets her go, and lets the country go, into whatever comes next for places that have finished becoming and peoples that have finished tending them. Which is not nothing. Which is, in fact, the thing the power came to witness in the first place, and the reason it knew, from the beginning, to do almost nothing at all.

The kelp is still up. The wind is from the northwest. The fish are moving in their pressure-driven schools between basins that no one will harvest now.

It is enough. It was enough. It will have been enough.
