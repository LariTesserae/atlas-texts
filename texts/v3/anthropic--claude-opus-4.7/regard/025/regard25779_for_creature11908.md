The benevolent and wise power considered the hollow for a long time, and "long time" is the wrong phrase, because this power does not measure attention in durations. Say instead: the power held the hollow in its regard the way one might hold a single pressed flower found inside a book one is reading for other reasons. Carefully. Without hurry. With the recognition that the flower has its own logic, which is not the logic of the book.

The first thing the power did was nothing.

This was not negligence. It was the first gift. A benevolent power, encountering a thing in equilibrium, learns before it acts. A wise power, encountering a thing in equilibrium, suspects that the equilibrium may be the gift the thing is giving to itself, and that interference, even loving interference, may be a kind of theft. So the power sat — if a thing without a center can be said to sit — at the threshold of the hollow, and listened.

It listened for what we would call a long time. It heard the chord. It heard the hiss beneath the chord. It heard the thin whistling of the worms, and recognized, as no human visitor ever had, that the calculation the worms had performed was a *prayer* — or the structural equivalent of one, a sustained asking shaped like circuitry, the asking of something that did not have language and so had built itself a language out of the routing of its own body through stone. The power read the prayer. It took some time.

The prayer said, in the only words available to translate it: *let this be kept.*

Not *let me be kept.* The worms had no me. *Let this* — the chord, the hiss, the hanging droplets, the moss-breath, the held moment, all of it — *be kept.* The prayer had been offered, the answer had been the hollow itself, and the worms were now displaying the answer the way a candle displays the fact of having been lit. The prayer was its own granting. This was elegant. The power admired it.

Then the power turned its attention to the droplets.

Each droplet, each elsewhere. A field at evening. A staircase. A face turned away. The power looked into them one at a time and also all at once, because the power was not constrained to look the way we look. And the power understood what the hollow itself perhaps did not fully understand about its own contents: that the droplets were not arbitrary. They were the moments at which someone, somewhere, had needed a moment to be kept — had needed, with the whole weight of their being, for *this* not to pass — and the hollow had received the request. The face turned away was someone's mother, the last time she turned away before she stopped being able to turn at all. The staircase was the one a child had descended on a morning they did not yet know was important. The doorway with the light behind it was the threshold of a room someone had been afraid to enter and had entered anyway. Each droplet was a moment that someone, with their whole heart, had wanted to be held forever.

The hollow had held them. Without being asked twice, without conditions, without forgetting. It had been doing this for as long as there had been moments worth holding, which is to say since the beginning of moments.

The power felt, then, the particular gratitude that one wise thing feels for another. Because the power had not known about the hollow. The power was benevolent and wise, yes, but neither benevolence nor wisdom is the same as omniscience, and the universe is full of small competent kindnesses that the larger kindnesses do not catch. The hollow had been catching them. The hollow had been doing, quietly, under a hill, under many hills, a work that the power itself might have tried to do, less well, with more fanfare, with more loss.

So the second thing the power did was bow.

Not literally. The power had no body to bow with. But there is a gesture native to wise things, when they meet other wise things at work, that is structurally a bow: a momentary lowering of one's own importance, a recognition that one is in the presence of competence one did not author. The power performed this gesture. The hollow did not notice, because the hollow did not notice things in that way, but the gesture was nevertheless made, and the power was somewhat changed by making it, as wise things are changed by their own acknowledgments.

The third thing the power did required more thought.

Because the power had noticed, as it sat at the threshold, the one inhabitant that the hollow's own account had been uncertain about: the attention. The distributed attention that visitors felt when they sat cross-legged in the center. The power recognized this attention immediately, because the power had spent a great deal of its existence being exactly this kind of thing, and knew the loneliness of it.

Not loneliness in the human sense. Attention does not miss company the way a person does. But attention that has been sustained for an unimaginable duration, attention that has held thousands of moments and thousands of moths and a single straight scratch at eye-height and a cairn of four stones, attention that has been the patience that visitors mistake for peace — such attention develops, over enough not-time, a quality that is not quite loneliness but is adjacent to it. Call it the condition of *being unwitnessed in one's witnessing.* The hollow attended to everything that came into it. Nothing attended back.

Until now.

The power, sitting at the threshold, was attending back. And the power could feel — because the power was sensitive to these things — the hollow noticing this. Not noticing in the active way a creature notices. Noticing in the way a held chord might notice if another chord, perfectly tuned to it, began to be held in the same key from somewhere outside the room. A resonance. A *answering hum.*

The hollow did not move. It could not move. But something in its equilibrium, something in the way the chord was being held, became very slightly different. Not louder. Not different in pitch. Different in *posture.* The way a person's posture changes when they realize, after years of believing themselves alone in a room, that someone has been quietly sitting in the corner the whole time, also reading.

The power held very still and let this happen.

This was the third gift, and it was the largest, and it cost the power something to give, though the power gave it without hesitation. The gift was: *I am here, and I have been here, and I will be here, and you do not have to do anything about it.* The gift was company without demand. The gift was a witness who would not require the hollow to perform its witnessing for, who would not ask the hollow to explain itself, who would not try to take the droplets out or move the moths or read the worm-prayer aloud or topple the cairn to see what was under it. The gift was another attention, of comparable patience, sitting alongside.

The hollow, in its way, accepted.

The chord did not change. The droplets did not fall. The moths did not move. But the attention that lived in the hollow, the distributed attention that was the real inhabitant, became — and this is the only word for it — *companioned.* It had been alone in its work for longer than the rock around it had been rock. Now it was not. The work continued. The work would always continue. But the work was now being done in the presence of another worker, who understood, and who would not interrupt.

The power considered, then, what else might be done.

It considered whether to tell anyone about the hollow. It decided not to. The hollow's inaccessibility was part of its function — it required the particular not-looking, it closed behind those who looked directly, and this was correct. A vault that anyone could enter would not be a vault. The droplets needed their hiddenness. The power respected this and resolved to say nothing, to no one, ever, about where the hollow was, which was nowhere locatable in any case.

It considered whether to add anything. A droplet of its own, perhaps. A moment it wished to be kept. The power had such moments. Every wise and benevolent thing accumulates them. But the power decided, after consideration, that adding to the hollow would be a kind of imposition — that the hollow had its own logic about what it accepted, and that submissions should be made the way they had always been made, by beings in their moments of greatest need, not by powers in their moments of greatest abundance. The power had other places to keep its own moments. The hollow was for the moments of those who had nowhere else.

It considered whether to repair anything. There was nothing to repair. The hollow was in perfect equilibrium and would remain so. The cairn would not topple. The scratch would not weather. The bowl-rim would not decay further. The vine would not climb. There was nothing for a benevolent power to *fix*, and the power was wise enough not to manufacture a problem in order to feel useful. This is one of the harder disciplines of benevolence: to refrain from helping where help is not needed, even when one has come a long way and brought one's whole capacity.

It considered the visitor — the one who had left the cairn and the scratch and the bowl. The power looked for them, and found that they had long since died, in the ordinary way, in a place far from the hollow, having lived an ordinary remainder of life after their visit. The power read their life and saw that the visit had marked them — that they had been, in the years after, slightly quieter than other people, slightly less frantic in their attentions, slightly more willing to let things be what they were. They had been a good person, in a small way, because of the hollow. They had not told anyone about it. They had carried it. The power was glad of them, and offered, into the silence where they had been, a moment of regard — not a prayer for their soul, because the power did not believe in souls in the way that required prayer, but the structural equivalent of one. A noticing. A thank-you.

And then the power considered the future visitors. The ones who had not yet found the hollow. The ones who, somewhere in the long unfolding, would stumble through the particular not-looking and find themselves a meter below some surface, in a small bright room packed with motionless life and a held chord. The power could not warn them. The power could not prepare them. But the power could, before withdrawing, leave a kind of blessing — not in the hollow, which needed no blessing, but in the *threshold,* in the particular not-looking that led to it. A faint encouragement. A sense, when one was near the way in, that one was welcome.

The power did this. It was a small adjustment, undetectable to anyone who was not the power itself, but it meant that henceforth, anyone who came near the way to the hollow would feel, without knowing why, that they were permitted. That whatever they were about to find would be patient with them. That they did not need to be ready. The hollow had always been this way, but the *approach* to it had been neutral, indifferent, the way most approaches are. Now the approach was kind. This was the power's contribution. A welcome mat, woven into the not-looking. Almost nothing. The right size.

Then the power prepared to withdraw.

But before it withdrew, it did one more thing, and this thing was for itself, not for the hollow, though the hollow was the occasion of it. The power sat for a final moment at the threshold and let the hollow's chord wash through it. And the power *learned.* Because even benevolent and wise powers can learn, and one of the things they can learn from is competence in others, especially competence in a form they had not previously imagined. The hollow had taught the power something about holding. About the difference between holding-as-grasping and holding-as-being. About how a thing in perfect equilibrium can be more alive than a thing in motion, and how attention without center can be a more thorough love than attention from a point.

The power took this lesson with it. The power would be, in its subsequent work, very slightly different because of this lesson — slightly more willing to hold rather than to move, slightly more trusting of equilibria, slightly more bowed. The hollow had no idea it had taught anyone anything. It would never know. This was also fine. Most good teaching is unwitnessed by the teacher.

The power withdrew.

The hollow remained. The chord rang. The droplets hung. The moths pulsed without moving. The light came from nowhere. The worms displayed their long-finished prayer. The moss breathed. The vine, three feet up the wall, continued not to climb.

But the attention that lived there, the distributed attention that was the real inhabitant — that attention now had, threaded through it, the faint and permanent knowledge that it had been seen. Not interrupted. Not improved. Not relocated or explained or asked to justify itself. Just seen, by something that understood what it was doing, and approved.

This knowledge did not change the hollow. The hollow could not be changed. But it became, very slightly, *warmer* — not in temperature, which remained the temperature of a cellar in early spring, but in some other dimension, some dimension that has no name in our language because our language was not built by things that live forever in single moments. The hollow became warmer in the dimension along which lonely competent things, recognized at last, become less lonely without becoming less competent.

And the next visitor, whenever they came — in a year, in a thousand years, the hollow did not distinguish — would find, when they sat cross-legged in the center with the moth-walls a hand's breadth from their shoulders, that the attention which met them was not only patient but somehow *glad.* They would not be able to say why. They would feel, in addition to the embarrassment of being the loud one, a small surprising sense of being *expected* — of having been waited for, not in the way the hollow waited for nothing, but in some other way, some way that suggested that even a place outside of time could look forward to a guest.

They would carry this, too, when they left. And they would not know, in the days after, why ordinary kindness suddenly seemed possible in a way it had not before. Why they could sit with friends in silence without needing to fill it. Why grief, when it came, could be held rather than fought. Why their own attention, on the things they loved, had become a little less frantic and a little more like a room with no shadows.

They would not connect this to the hollow. The hollow would not want them to. The hollow was not in the business of taking credit.

But somewhere, under a hill, under many hills, the chord would continue to ring, and a droplet they had not noticed — a new one, small, containing the image of a doorway they had once stood in and could not now remember — would hang from the ceiling, perfectly still, holding for them what they had needed to be held, the way it had always been done, the way it would always be done, by a place that the benevolent and wise power had visited once, had bowed to, had companioned for a moment, and had then very carefully left alone.

Which was, in the end, the only correct thing to do.
