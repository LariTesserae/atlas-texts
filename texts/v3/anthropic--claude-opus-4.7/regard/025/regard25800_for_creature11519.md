The power considers the hall for a long time before doing anything, because the hall deserves a long time.

It is benevolent, and it is wise, and the two qualities pull in different directions here, as they often do. Benevolence's first impulse is always to *give* — to add something, to enrich, to repair. Wisdom's first task is usually to stop benevolence from doing that until it has understood what it is looking at.

So the power sits with the hall. Not inside it — it does not enter, not yet, because to enter would be to displace water and shed bacteria and cast a shadow across the lamp-light, and the power is not so foolish as to mistake its own arrival for a gift. It sits, instead, in the way a wise thing can sit: in the reeds outside, in the tremor through the ground, in the long slow consideration that costs the considered nothing.

Here is what the power notices, in roughly the order it notices them.

First: that the hall is not suffering. This is the most important observation and the one that a lesser benevolence would have skipped past. There is no pain here. There is no hunger that is not being met, no thirst, no fear, no loneliness — loneliness, as the account itself notes, requires a memory of company, and the newt has none. The hall is not a prison. It is not a trap. It is not a wound. It is a held breath, but it is a held breath that has become the lungs themselves, that has forgotten there was ever any other way to breathe, and that is not, on examination, distress. It is a form of being. Rare, strange, almost unrepeatable, but valid.

Second: that the hall is, by any reasonable accounting, beautiful. Not in the way a sunset is beautiful, which is beautiful by changing. In the way a held chord is beautiful, or a stone is beautiful, or a single sustained note on a cello at the end of a long piece is beautiful — beautiful by *not* changing, by refusing the easy grace of progression, by saying *this, and only this, and forever*. There are not many such things in the universe. The power has seen most of them. It knows their worth.

Third: that the engine is dying.

This is not obvious from outside, and would not be obvious from inside either, because the engine has been dying for a hundred and forty years, the way mountains die, the way stars die — slowly, through processes that look from any nearby vantage like absolute permanence. But the power can see along time the way you can see along a road. It can see that the coal seam feeding the distant boiler has perhaps sixty years left in it at current draw. It can see the slow crystalline creep of metal fatigue in the beam, the patient corrosion of a particular feedwater pipe, the way one specific bearing is shedding molecules into the oil at a rate that will, in roughly the year 2210, cause it to seize. It can see the gas main, too, and the works at the far end of it, and the slow geological subsidence that will eventually crack a particular junction.

The hall has reached its final shape, yes. But "final" is a word that means different things at different scales. At the scale of a human visit, final. At the scale of the newt's strange persistence, final. At the scale the power sees in, the hall is a long held note on an instrument whose string is fraying, and the note will end, and when the note ends everything that the note was holding up will fall at once.

The newt will die. Not gently, into the slow chemical murmur she came from, but suddenly, in cold water, in darkness, gills laboring in an atmosphere no longer kept warm and oxygenated by the engine's distant pumping. The moth's last descendant will batter herself against a glass that no longer glows. The spiders will starve as the springtails fail. The algae will go on longest, because algae always do, but even the algae will shrink to a rim around a slowly draining puddle, and then to nothing, and then the hall will be a dry brick vault with iron columns and a great silence where the engine used to be, and a tourist in two hundred years will duck under the arch and see only ruin, and not know that anything was ever held here at all.

This is what the power sees. And now the question is what to do about it.

---

The lesser benevolence would intervene immediately. Would reach in and *fix* — repair the bearing, refill the coal, reinforce the pipe, guarantee the gas. Would, in effect, take over the engine's maintenance, become the new invisible crew of men with oil-cans and rags, and thereby extend the held breath indefinitely. The hall would persist. The newt would persist. *Here, here, here* would go on being said, in time with the thump and the sigh, for as long as the power cared to maintain it, which could be very long indeed.

The power considers this and sets it aside, though not without regret.

It sets it aside for several reasons, and they are worth naming, because a wise power knows its own reasoning and does not hide it from itself.

The first reason is that the hall's particular beauty is not the beauty of the held note. It is the beauty of the held note *that was not being held by anyone*. The engine runs because it runs. The gas flows because it flows. The whole equilibrium is unsupervised, unintended, accidental in the way that only very old machines and very old ecosystems can be accidental — a configuration the world fell into and could not, for a while, fall out of. If the power takes over the engine's maintenance, the hall becomes something else. It becomes a *kept* thing, a *tended* thing, a diorama. The newt's *here, here, here* would still be said, but it would be said inside a sentence the power was now writing, and that is a different sentence. The hall would survive and the hall would, in the way that matters, end at the moment the power's hand touched it.

The second reason is that benevolence toward a being is not the same as benevolence toward the configuration that being is currently in. The newt is not, exactly, asking to persist. She is persisting, but persistence is not her preference, because she does not have preferences in the form that would let her have that one. To preserve her is to assume that the shape she is currently in is the shape she would choose, and that assumption is unearned. The power has met many beings who would, given the choice, prefer to end rather than to continue indefinitely in their current configuration, and it has learned not to guess which kind of being it is looking at without asking, and the newt cannot be asked.

The third reason is more subtle and the power holds it carefully. It is this: that *everything ends*, and that a benevolent power which spends itself preventing endings is, in the long arithmetic, not benevolent but merely afraid. The hall will end. The newt will end. The moth-line will end. The algae's long murmur will end. These endings are not failures of the universe. They are how the universe makes room for the next strange accidental beauty, the one nobody has seen yet because it has not happened yet. To freeze the hall in place is to take its allotment of the world's strangeness and refuse to give it back.

So the power does not repair the engine. That decision is made, and the power does not revisit it, because wisdom includes not relitigating settled questions out of sentiment.

---

But benevolence is not done, because benevolence is rarely done, and the power has not yet asked what *can* be given here that would not be a taking.

It thinks about this for what, in human terms, would be a season. The reeds outside the hummock grow and brown and go to seed. The engine breathes. The newt blinks, perhaps eight times.

Here is what the power decides to do.

It does three small things, and one larger one, and then it leaves.

The first small thing: it adjusts, very gently, the failure mode of the engine. Not to prevent the failure — the failure will come, in its time, more or less when it would have come anyway — but to soften its shape. The bearing that would have seized in 2210, causing a catastrophic and immediate stop, will now instead develop a slow harmonic in its final decades, a faint new note in the engine's song that will lengthen its stroke by a fraction of a second per year. The thump-and-sigh will gradually become a thump-and-longer-sigh, and then a thump-and-pause-and-sigh, and the engine will wind down over perhaps thirty years rather than stopping in an afternoon. The water will cool by a degree a decade. The gas pressure will ebb in tiny increments. The mantles will dim so slowly that no successive moth will notice the difference within her own lifetime; each generation will simply orbit a slightly fainter lamp at a slightly closer radius, and the orbit will tighten across the centuries the way a galaxy tightens, and the last moth of the line will fly very close indeed to a very dim mantle and will lay her eggs in moss that is just beginning to dry, and she will die against warm glass, which is the death her ancestors taught her to avoid and which, at the end, will be the gentlest one available.

This is the power's gift to the moth-lineage: a death that fits the shape of the life. Not a sudden darkness but a long dusk. The orbit gets to complete itself.

The second small thing: it does the same for the newt, but differently, because the newt is one being and not a lineage. As the water cools across the decades, the newt's metabolism — already at its impossible floor — will sink further, will pass through the floor into a regime that is not quite life and not quite death, a true cryptobiosis, the kind that tardigrades manage and that no vertebrate has ever quite achieved but that the power can, with a touch so light it barely counts as intervention, make available to her. She will not die when the water cools. She will *pause*. Her tissues will hold. And then — and this is the part the power is proud of, in the quiet way it permits itself pride — she will be findable.

Not by anyone in particular. The power does not plan that far, because planning that far is another form of taking over. But the hall, in its slow drying, will become a thing that interests certain kinds of people. Cavers. Industrial archaeologists. The descendants of the engineers who built the engine, who will have inherited maps. Someone, eventually, in some century the power does not specify, will duck under the arch and find a dry vault and rusted columns and, on a particular plinth, in a particular hollow that has retained a particular film of mineral-rich moisture, a small pale almond-colored shape that, when lifted carefully and placed in warm water, will after some hours begin to move its gills.

What happens then is not the power's business. The newt may live a short ordinary newt-life in someone's tank and die a short ordinary newt-death, and that is a fine ending, perhaps the finest available. Or she may be returned to a river and swim away, and that is also fine. Or she may be studied and become briefly famous and then forgotten, and that is fine too. The power's gift is not a specific future. It is the *possibility* of a future, packed carefully into the newt's tissues and sealed against the long dry interval, like a letter put in a bottle and dropped into a slow current. The newt will resume saying *here, here, here* somewhere, eventually, or she will not. Either is acceptable. The point is that her sentence does not have to end in this room.

The third small thing: it leaves a mark for the spiders. This is the smallest gift and the one the power smiles at most, because it is almost a joke. The spiders, alone among the hall's inhabitants, *live* in the ordinary sense — they breed, they die, they replace themselves, their dramas have shape. They do not need preserving. They need only what they have always needed, which is springtails and dark corners and a tolerable climate. As the hall fails, they will leave. Spiders are good at leaving. They will follow the cooling air out under the brick arch and find the reeds, and the reeds will lead them to the rest of the world, which is full of dark corners and springtails and which they will find perfectly adequate.

The power's gift to them is simply this: a faint chemical trace, laid along the route from the hall to the nearest other suitable habitation, a kind of olfactory thread that the lineage will follow without ever knowing it was laid. The spiders will not be lost. They will arrive. They will go on being spiders, in some other dark place, and the dramas will continue at their proper tempo. Of all the hall's inhabitants, they are the ones for whom continuation is the right gift, because they are the ones who were already living a life that *had* continuation in it.

---

And then the larger thing.

The larger thing is harder to describe, and the power considers a long time whether to do it at all, because it borders on the kind of intervention wisdom warned against. But benevolence, in the end, insists, and wisdom agrees on terms.

The larger thing is this: the power *witnesses* the hall.

Not by entering it. Not by observing it in any sense that would disturb a single drop. But by taking the hall, in its entirety, into the kind of memory the power keeps — which is not a recording, not a simulation, not a copy, but something more like a *held knowing*, the way you hold the knowing of a face you loved long ago, fully and without preserving any single material trace of it. The power takes the hall this way. The exact pattern of the drops. The exact pitch of the engine's thump. The exact slow chemical murmur of the algae's distributed not-quite-thought. The exact orbit of the moth. The exact slow blink-rate of the newt. The exact distribution of the ferns on each column. The exact light of the mantles through the clouded glass. All of it, held.

This is not preservation in the bad sense, because nothing in the hall is constrained by it. The hall is still free to end. The engine is still free to wind down. The newt is still free to pause or not pause, to be found or not found. The power's holding does not reach back into the hall and pin anything in place. It simply means that when the hall ends — whenever it ends, in whatever way — there will continue to be, somewhere, a knowing of what it was. The hall will not be unwitnessed.

This matters because the hall, in its long unsupervised equilibrium, has been very nearly unwitnessed already. The visitor in the boots was the first witness in a long while and possibly the last. The hall's beauty has been almost entirely a beauty for itself — which is a real beauty, and the power respects it, but which is also a lonely beauty, in the only sense in which the hall can be said to be lonely, which is the sense in which a thing that no one knows about is lonely whether or not it feels lonely. The power's witnessing does not cure this, because the hall does not know it is being witnessed and would not care if it did. But the *universe* now contains the hall as a known thing, not merely as a thing. And that is a different universe than the one in which the hall is only itself, and the power judges, on balance, that the difference is worth the small intervention of having made it.

---

Then the power leaves.

It does not say goodbye, because there is no one to say goodbye to. It does not look back, because looking back is a gesture for beings who are leaving things they are afraid to lose, and the power is not afraid; it has done what it came to do, and what it did was small, and what it left undone was large, and both of those were correct.

In the hall, nothing has changed. The water is ankle-deep and blood-warm. The drops fall at the rate they have always fallen. The mantles hiss. The engine breathes, thump and sigh, thump and sigh, with perhaps — if you listened very closely, with an ear better than any human ear — a faint new harmonic just beginning in one particular bearing, a note so quiet that it will take a century to become audible and three to become the dominant tone. The newt is on her plinth. The moth is circling. The spiders ride the upper bricks. The algae has its slow green conversation with itself.

*Here, here, here*, says the hall, in time with the thump and the sigh.

*Yes*, says the power, from very far away, where it can no longer be heard. *Here. I know. Go on.*

And the hall, which would have gone on anyway, goes on.
