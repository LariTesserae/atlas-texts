The benevolent and wise power considers the mountain for a long time.

This is the first thing it does, and the most important. It does not arrive in a hurry. It does not arrive at all, in the sense of crossing a distance; it simply turns some portion of its attention toward the cistern terraces and holds it there, the way one might hold a palm above a small flame to feel what is being given off. It does this for what would be, to us, a long while. To the wallwrights, who do not measure such things, it is the duration of a thought.

What it perceives, it perceives slowly, because to perceive the wallwrights quickly would be to perceive something other than what they are. It learns the rate at which a wallwright considers a stone. It learns the long sentence of nudged pebbles running across the eighth rank, and waits for the sentence to finish before deciding what the sentence means. It learns the ache by which a basin is known to be a hair high. It learns that there is no hunger here, no fear, no one waiting to be rescued from anything.

This matters. The power is benevolent, which is to say it wishes well; it is wise, which is to say it knows that wishing well is not the same as doing, and that doing without understanding is a kind of vandalism the universe is already sufficiently supplied with. Many lesser powers, encountering the terraces, would have *helped*. They would have finished the work. A snap of will and the mountain would be sheathed to its crown, every basin true, the labor of the remaining centuries collapsed into an instant — a gift. The wallwrights would open their water-eyes onto a completed world and find that the texture of being alive had been taken from them and replaced with a monument.

The power does not do this. The power sees, almost at once, that to finish the terraces *for* the wallwrights would be to end them. The work is not a means. The wallwrights are not laboring toward a mirror-mountain; they are the mountain becoming a mirror, and the becoming is the whole of what they are. To hand them the result would be to hand a singer the silence after the song and call it generosity.

So the power does not finish the work.

Nor does it announce itself. To speak to the wallwrights in any voice they would recognize as a voice would be to introduce into their slow conversation a sentence so loud it would drown out a century of nudged stones. The wallwrights have no apparatus for awe; awe is a thing of hurry, and they do not hurry. To overwhelm them with presence would be a cruelty dressed as a blessing.

What it does instead is smaller, and takes longer to describe than to do.

It looks, first, at the pale film on the submerged stones — the simplest possible life, the one thing in the basins that is not stone and not water and not wallwright. It looks at this film for a long time, because the film is the only thing in the whole construction that might be said to be at risk: the wallwrights tend it, or it tends them, and neither knows which, and a thing in that condition can be lost without anyone noticing it has been lost. The power does a small thing here. It does not strengthen the film, or multiply it, or alter it. It simply *notices* it, fully, in a way nothing has ever noticed it before — registers its particular chemistry, its slow generations, the precise relation it bears to the chalky rind of a wallwright's calf — and lets that noticing rest in itself, so that even if every film and every wallwright and every stone of the terraces should one day cease, the fact of them will have been held, once, completely. This is not preservation. This is witness. A wise power knows the difference, and knows that witness is sometimes the only gift that does not deform what it touches.

Then it considers the wallwright who will, in some further century, stop in the middle of a basin and not move again. It considers the moment of that stopping — the last small adjustment of weight, the last cloud crossing the held water of the eyes, the slow taking of the rind. It considers whether there is anything it might do for that wallwright, and concludes, after long looking, that there is one thing, and only one. It arranges — not by interference but by the lightest possible inclination of circumstance, the kind of inclination that is indistinguishable from luck — that on the afternoon of that stopping, the light will be very good. Not spectacular. The wallwrights have no use for spectacle. Only that the particular quality of brightness which is sometimes the subject of the unread sentences, the one a wallwright might set down a drop about if it had a free afternoon, will be present in full measure, lying on the water and on the rind and on the seam that used to be a collarbone. The wallwright will not know the light has been arranged. It will only know, in whatever way it knows things, that the last thing was good.

The power does this for each of them. It is not a small undertaking, even for such a power, because there are a few hundred of them and there will be more, and each one's good afternoon must be its own, fitted to that wallwright as a stone is fitted to its bed. The power does not mind. It has the time. It has, in fact, exactly the kind of time the wallwrights have, which is part of why it understood them in the first place.

There is one further thing it considers, and hesitates over, and finally does.

Somewhere on the upper slopes, in the place where a new wallwright is being shaped course by course, the power lays down — not a blessing, the wallwrights would not know what to do with a blessing — but a kind of structural truth, woven into the chalky rind of the one being made, so quietly that the makers do not notice they have made it. The truth is this: *you are seen, and the seeing does not require you to be finished.* It is not a message. The new wallwright will never know it is there. It will simply, when it opens its small water-eyes and steps down from its scaffolding, work without the faintest pressure of completion, without any sense that the mirror-mountain is owed to anyone or must be delivered by any date. It will work the way the others work, because that is what it is; but there will be, somewhere in the joint of its thumb, a tiny untroubledness that the others do not quite have, and that may, over centuries, propagate by touch through the slow conversation of nudged stones, until the whole mountain works a little more lightly than it did. Or may not. The power does not insist. It has placed the truth and stepped back from it.

And then the power does the last and wisest thing, which is to leave.

Not to withdraw its care — the care remains, the witness remains, the good afternoons are already arranged and waiting in their distant calendar — but to stop *attending* in the close way it has been attending, because to keep such attention on a place is, eventually, to bend the place toward the attention, and the wallwrights have not asked to be bent. The power turns its face, gently, elsewhere. The terraces brim. The mist rises. A stone is moved by the width of a chip. Somewhere a sentence about the light is being set down in drops, and will take a week to finish, and will be read by no one but another wallwright and the mountain.

The power, going, allows itself one thing that is not quite for the wallwrights and not quite for itself: it allows itself to love them. Briefly. The way one loves a thing that does not need to be loved in order to be what it is. Then it folds that love into the witness, where it will keep, and goes on about the rest of its enormous and patient business, leaving the mountain to its slow becoming.

The wallwrights notice nothing. This is the measure of the power's wisdom. They go on standing in the bright water, being level, the sky lying on their faces, and the work goes on, and the work is theirs, and the mirror climbs by a stone a season toward the single tooth at the crown, and when at last the last of them steps into the highest basin the afternoon will be, for reasons it will not think to wonder about, very good.
