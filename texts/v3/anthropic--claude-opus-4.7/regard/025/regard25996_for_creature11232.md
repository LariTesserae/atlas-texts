The benevolent and wise power considers the mountain for a long time.

This is the first thing to understand: that the consideration itself takes what, in other registers, would be called an age. The power is not in a hurry. The power has learned, over the course of attending to many places, that the first impulse — to intervene, to assist, to improve — is almost always a kind of vanity dressed in compassion's clothing. So it watches. It listens to the hum. It follows a goat for what would be, to the goat, several lifetimes, and learns the particular tilt of its head. It sits inside a ring of trees through what would be, to the trees, a slow turn of season, and lets the cadence wash through it, and does not interrupt.

What it notices, slowly, is this: the mountain is already loved.

Not by anything that announced itself. Not by a maker who left a signature. But the warm rock loves the lichen that grows over it, in the sense that love can be inferred from the long accommodation of one thing to another. The shrikes love the moss, which is to say they cannot stop tending it. The predator loves the goats with the terrible accuracy of something that has been refining a single gesture for a geological span. The rings love whoever speaks into them, slowly, by changing cadence in response. The machinery — and this is the thing that takes longest to see — loves the forests it grew, in the only way a machine can love: by continuing.

A place this thoroughly woven into itself does not need a benefactor. To arrive with gifts would be to mistake the situation for a poorer one than it is.

So the power does not bring anything.

But this does not mean it does nothing. The wise are rarely passive; they are only careful about what their activity costs.

Here is what it does.

It learns the hum. Not the surface of it — the cadence that registers in the sternum — but the whole shape, the way the slow stroke nests inside a slower stroke nests inside something that might be called a breath if breath could last a thousand years. It learns this not to interfere with the rhythm but to know it, the way one learns the heartbeat of someone one intends to sit beside for a long time. It commits the hum to whatever it uses for memory, which is something deeper and more durable than memory. If, somewhere far from here, in some other age, the hum is ever needed again — if the mountain falls quiet and someone wants to know what it was saying — the power will be able to give back its full shape, accurate to the last tremor. This is not a gift to the mountain. It is a gift to time, on the mountain's behalf, against an emergency that may never come.

It sits, once, inside one of the rings, and speaks. Not loudly. Not to ask anything. It offers, in the slow cadence the ring uses, a single observation: that it has been here, that it has noticed, that it considers what happens in this place to be of value. It does not say more than this because more than this would be presumption. It waits the full hour for the ring to finish considering, and notes the small alteration in cadence that follows — neither welcome nor dismissal, but acknowledgment, the way one slow mind acknowledges another — and then it is quiet again. Whatever the rings are, they now know they are known. Whether this matters to them, the power cannot say. But it has seemed important, in other places, that the slow things be recognized by something that can perceive them at their own pace. Loneliness, the power has learned, can take forms much stranger than the human one, and the antidote is not company but witness.

It does one more thing, and this is the most careful thing it does.

It looks, with whatever sense it uses for looking, at the long thread of futures the mountain is embedded in. Not to change them. Only to see. And what it sees is that the mountain will, in fact, continue for a very long time — that the machinery is sound, that the balance is robust, that the slow country folded vertically will fold on, ridge after ridge, for an age longer than the age that has already passed. This is good. But it also sees, very far out, a thinning. Not a stopping. A thinning. Some condition of the wider world — not anything the mountain has done, not anything the mountain could have prevented — that will, eventually, make the air above the mountain less hospitable to the particular kind of mist that the lichens drink. The forests will not die. They will become smaller versions of themselves. The lushness will retreat to fewer ledges. The rings will cool by a fraction of a degree. The cadence will slow. None of this is catastrophe. All of it is loss.

The power considers, for a long time, whether to do anything about this.

It could. It is within its capacity to adjust the conditions, to seed the upper air with what the lichens will need, to extend the lushness by another age. It would cost it little. It would, in the simplest accounting, be a kindness.

It decides against.

Not because the loss does not matter. Because the mountain is a place that has organized itself, across every register, around the principle of long balance — around the courtesy that the predator extends in leaving its single tooth, around the patient correction of the shrikes, around the deference of the goats to weather. To reach in and prevent a slow thinning, however gently, would be to introduce into this place a different principle: the principle that something from outside watches, and adjusts, and decides what shall persist. The mountain has never lived under that principle. It would feel the new weather and not know it was being held. But the power would know. And the holding, once begun, would have to continue, because to stop holding after starting would be a worse cruelty than never starting. The mountain would become, in a way it is not now, dependent. A long beautiful self-sufficiency would have ended, and only the power would know it had ended, and this would be a sorrow it was not entitled to inflict in exchange for the thinning it was not entitled to prevent.

So it does not adjust the air.

What it does instead is this: it makes a small promise to itself, and the promise is the only intervention. The promise is that when the thinning comes, it will return, and it will sit through it. It will witness the smaller forests and the cooler rings and the slower cadence with the same attention it brought to the lushness. It will not let the diminishment go unobserved. This is a strange kind of gift, perhaps — a promise of presence at a future grief — but the power has come, across long practice, to believe that this is among the truest things one being can offer another: not the prevention of loss, but the refusal to look away from it.

And then, almost as an afterthought, it does one last thing, which is the closest thing to an intervention in the whole long visit.

It walks, in whatever form it has taken for walking, to a particular ledge — not one of the lush ones, not one with a ring, just an ordinary ledge with ordinary moss — and it kneels, and it presses its palm to the warm rock, and it stays there for what would be, to a goat, the better part of a day. It is not doing anything to the rock. It is only letting the rock be felt by something that has felt many other rocks, in many other places, and can therefore feel this one in the full context of what rocks can be. The mountain does not know it is being felt this way. The machinery does not pause. The hum continues. But for the duration of the kneeling, this particular ledge is held in a kind of attention it has never been held in before — not greater than the attention the shrikes give it, not deeper than the attention of the lichen that grows on it, but different. Wider. Including, somehow, all the other ledges on all the other mountains in all the other places, and finding this one, in that great company, beautiful.

Then the power stands, and the warmth of its palm fades from the rock within minutes, and a shrike comes by and corrects a needle that had fallen during the kneeling, and the ledge returns to itself entirely.

The power leaves the mountain the way it came: without seam, without announcement. The goats do not notice. The grouse keep time. Somewhere in a ring, a sentence that has been forming for a season approaches its next syllable. The predator, distributed through the warm stone, registers nothing, or registers everything, or — most likely — registers exactly what it always registers, which is the count of the goats and the temperature of the rock and the patience required to remain itself.

What the power carries away is the hum, held perfectly, against a future need.

What it leaves behind is nothing the mountain can perceive.

Which is, in the end, the wisest form the kindness could take: a love that did not require the beloved to know it had been loved, because the beloved was already, in its own long and self-sufficient way, doing the work of being itself, and needed no help, and deserved — more than help, more than gifts, more than intervention — to be left to it.

The piston completes its slow stroke. The needles tremble. The dark settles into the dark.

The power, somewhere else now, remembers.
