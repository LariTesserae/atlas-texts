The benevolent and wise power considers the Steeping Town for a long time before doing anything.

This is the first thing about it: that it considers. It does not arrive. It does not announce. It sits with the place the way a person sits with another person who is grieving, or working, or sleeping — the way you sit with something whose interior life is its own and not for you.

It watches the grandmother finish the song and begin it again. It watches the lamp-reeds. It counts the seeps and finds, with a small private pleasure, that the townspeople have already counted them, and named them, and that the names are better than any it could have given. It notices the newts in the wall-seams and the way a particular woman, washing a pot in the bell-house, glances at one of them before she pours her water, so as not to flood its crevice. It notices that she does this without thinking, and that this is the deepest form of noticing there is.

It understands, fairly quickly, what the town believes. That attention is rent. That the basin is in conversation with itself. That survival is not personal.

And the power — which is wise, remember, which is the whole point — sees that this is *true*. Not true as a charming local metaphor. True. The town has gotten something right that most places never get right. The power has been to many places. In most of them, the inhabitants believe themselves to be the subject of every sentence the world speaks. The Steeping Town does not believe this and is not wrong.

So the power's first temptation — to *save* the town, to lift the basin, to slow the water, to stabilize the ribs, to grant the people another four generations, another forty, another four hundred — the power sets this temptation down. Carefully. The way you set down a pot you have just realized is someone else's.

To save the town would be to make the town the subject of the sentence. It would resolve the constant soft-voiced unresolved discussion. It would settle the question of which will win, the water or the stone, and the question is part of how the town knows itself. A town that knew it would survive would not tend the lamp-reeds the same way. The tending is rent, and rent is only rent if it might not be enough.

The power understands this and does not save the town.

But it does not do nothing.

Here is what it does.

It listens to the lullaby. All of it. Every verse, every named pool and named edge and named seep and named clump, in the grandmother's particular voice with its particular small catch at the place where her own grandmother's voice used to catch, a catch inherited like a nail. It listens until it has the song entire. Then it listens to the *other* grandmothers, in the other lofts, singing slightly different versions — the bell-house seep added here, omitted there, the cultivator of the third clump named *Mira* in one version and *Mire* in another because no one is quite sure anymore which it was. It gathers all the versions. It does not flatten them into a canonical one. It holds them the way the basin holds water: each in its own concavity, none of them the same.

This holding is not a recording. The power is wise enough not to think that recording is the same as remembering. A recording is a corpse of a song. What the power does is more like *witnessing forward* — it makes itself a place where the songs continue to exist as songs, sung, alive, even after the singers and the lofts and the basin are gone. Not preserved. *Continued*. In whatever register of being the power inhabits, the lullabies go on being sung. The names go on being named. Some attention, somewhere, keeps being paid.

This is the rent the power decides to take up, quietly, on behalf of a town that may not last.

It does one more thing.

The unnamed creature that comes down from the ridge twice a year — the broad-pawed slow one, that drinks and leaves — the power looks at this creature for a while. It is older than the town. It was here before the basalt pushed up last Tuesday, in some form, and will be here after. It is part of the conversation the basin is having with itself, in a way the town is not quite, and the town has correctly intuited this, which is why they leave it food and do not approach.

The power does not interfere with the creature. But it does, very gently, ensure that the creature *keeps coming*. For as long as there is anyone in the basin to leave food. Even if the town shrinks to ten people, to three, to one. Even if the last person is a very old woman with no one left to sing to. The creature will come, broad-pawed and slow, and drink at the lowest pool, and the food will be eaten, and the rent will have been paid, both ways, until the very end.

This is a small thing. The power is wise enough to do small things.

And then — and this is perhaps the most important part — the power leaves a kind of *attention* in the basin. Not a presence. Not a watcher. Something more like the quality the lamp-reeds have, of holding light. The basin, after the power has been there, holds attention slightly better than it did before. A child noticing a newt notices it a hair more clearly. A grandmother reaching for the next named pool finds the name a hair more readily on her tongue. The dyer, stirring the liverwort vat, feels for one moment that the work is being *received* — not by a god, not by anything she could name, just received, the way a song is received by the small private quiet it constructs around a sleeping child.

The townspeople do not notice this. That is the point. If they noticed, it would become a thing, and the town does not do things, the town does small constant tending. The power's gift is calibrated to be exactly below the threshold of noticing, because to be noticed would be to demand rent, and the power is not so foolish as to demand rent from a place that already pays so much.

When the basin finally drowns — and it will, the power can see this, the water will win, in about six generations, more slowly than the people fear and more quickly than they hope — the last person to leave will be a man whose name has not yet been chosen by his parents because he has not yet been born. He will walk out along the south rib carrying a single unbroken pot and the song. He will not know he is the last. He will think there are others ahead of him on the path, and in some sense there will be: every previous leaver, walking out with their pot and their song, in a long quiet line going back four generations and forward one.

The power will walk with him. Not to comfort him. Just to keep him company, the way you keep company with someone whose interior life is their own. He will not know this either.

And the lullaby will go on being sung, somewhere, in whatever register of being the power inhabits, naming the seven pools and the named edges of the named pools, returning and returning, never quite the same return, at the pace of an idea, at the pace of water moving over stone, for as long as there is anything at all that listens.

This is what a benevolent and wise power does, when it finds a place like this:

It pays the rent it can pay.

It does not save what does not want saving.

It witnesses forward.

It keeps the creature coming.

It leaves.
