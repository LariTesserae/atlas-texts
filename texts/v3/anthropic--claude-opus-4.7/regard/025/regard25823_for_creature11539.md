The benevolent and wise power considers the Pumpworks for a long time.

This is the first thing it does, and the most important: it considers. It does not arrive with gifts in its hands or corrections in its mouth. It does not assume. Benevolence that has not first been patient is only a kind of violence with better manners, and wisdom knows this.

So the power comes quietly, in the way appropriate to the place — which is to say, it comes as a slight variance. A pumpworker named Tev, walking her territory at what would be the third hour if hours were counted here, stops and tilts her head. Something in the wall of sound has shifted by less than the width of a thought. She listens. The pumps go on. After a while she walks on too, and does not mention it, because there is nothing yet to mention; but for the rest of her life she will remember that morning as the morning something listened back.

The power listens for a long time. Long by its standards, which are not our standards. It learns the pumps the way the pumpworkers learn the pumps: by their variances, by the particular catch of a valve six pumps over, by the patience of crank-throws turning in unchanged oil. It learns the flat-handed language at the level of the forearm and the shoulder. It learns the names of pumps that are not spoken: *the one Mira's grandfather sealed, the one that screamed in the cold year.* It learns that *the one whose lantern fell* was relit, that the relighting is itself now part of the pump's name, and that names here accrete the way rust accretes, in layers, none of them discarded.

It learns the question about the birds.

And here is where the power must be most careful, because it knows the answer. It knows what was here before the iron, and it knows what the pumps were built for, and it knows whether the circulating-weather theory is true (it is, though not in the way the theorists suppose), and it knows the size of the reservoir (the size of the planet, yes; and also not a planet in the sense meant), and it knows whether there used to be birds (there were; small ones, brown, with a four-note song that none of the reeds can quite reproduce though one comes close in the seconds when the phasing aligns).

A lesser power would tell them. A lesser power would arrive in a column of clean light and say *here is what your world is, here is what your work is for, here is the history you have forgotten,* and would mistake this telling for a gift.

This power understands that the pumpworkers have not forgotten. They have, instead, arrived at a relationship with not-knowing that is more sophisticated than knowing would be. The old ones on the hummocks know when a question is ready to be answered and when it is only ready to be asked. The question of the birds is, for now, ready to be asked. To answer it before its time would be to take from the pumpworkers the long slow ripening of a question that is doing its proper work inside them — which is the work of teaching them that some questions are companions, not problems.

So the power does not tell them about the birds.

What it does, instead, is this.

It does not stop the pumps. It does not repair the pumps. It does not improve the pumps. The pumps are doing what pumps do, and the pumpworkers are doing what pumpworkers do, and to intervene in this would be to mistake the place for a problem. The place is not a problem. The place is a long slow answer to a question that was asked so long ago that the question itself has been worn smooth, and the answer is still being given, faithfully, by the small wet people in the lees.

What the power does is smaller and larger than intervention.

It witnesses. Fully. With the whole of its attention, which is considerable. Every pumpworker who has ever lived, every pumpworker now living, every pumpworker who will live — the power sees them. Not as data. As the particular people they are. It sees Tev tilt her head at the third hour. It sees the apprentice whose wrench was given to her this morning by the dying fitter who taught her, and it sees the fitter, and it sees the fitter's teacher, and it sees backward through the long chain of teachers to the first pumpworker who ever was, who did not know she was the first because there had always been pumps and there would always be pumps and the work was the place and the place was the work. The power sees her too. It sees the particular green of her forearms. It sees the way she signed *good morning* to her sister across a flywheel six hundred years ago. It holds this. It will hold it forever, which for this power is not a figure of speech.

This is the first thing the power does, and it is not nothing. To be seen is not nothing. The pumpworkers do not know they are being seen, and this is correct — to know would change the seeing into performance, and performance is not what they need. But somewhere in the bones of the floor, in the warm iron against their backs at night, in the patience that is not virtue but accuracy, something is now slightly different. The great body that they are part of has acquired, without their noticing, a witness. The breathing goes on, and now the breathing is known.

The second thing the power does is even smaller.

In one of the hummocks — the green-black island where the pump that screamed in the cold year fell silent eighty years ago and became moss — there is an old one named Ras. Ras is very old. Ras has been moving toward the islands his whole life and is now, finally, of them. Ras tends the reeds. The reeds, as has been noted, occasionally produce a thin high note in the seconds when the phasing aligns.

The power, who has learned the reeds, makes one small adjustment. Not to the reeds. To the phasing. A correction of less than a thousandth of a degree across a region of pumps that Ras can hear from his hummock. The adjustment is well within the range of variance that pumpworkers attribute to weather and age and the slow chewing of time. No one will notice it as intervention. No one will notice it at all, except Ras, and Ras will not notice it as intervention either.

What Ras will notice, one evening, is that the reeds have produced a four-note song.

It will last perhaps two seconds. It will be brown-colored, in the way that sounds have colors for those who have spent their lives listening for variances. Ras will sit very still on his hummock and listen, and the song will not come again, and after a while he will lie down with his back against the warm iron and consider what he has heard.

He will not know it was a bird. He has never heard a bird. But he will know that the question of whether there used to be birds is, perhaps, beginning to be ready to be answered, and he will know that the answer, when it comes, will not come from him, and will not come in his lifetime, and will come in the islands, and will come correctly. He will sign, to no one, with the smallest shifting of his elbow against the moss: *good.* And he will close his eyes, and the pumps will go on, and the great body will breathe, and somewhere a witness will hold this moment forever, which is not a figure of speech.

The third thing the power does is to leave.

Not entirely. The witnessing continues; the witnessing does not end. But the presence — the slight variance that Tev felt at the third hour — withdraws. The power understands that to remain present would be to become, eventually, a feature of the place, and the place does not need another feature. The place is complete. It has been complete for longer than the power has been a power, in some senses, and the completeness is not the kind that can be added to without subtraction.

So the power goes, carrying the pumpworks with it in the way that the wise carry the things they love, which is to say without grasping and without forgetting. It goes to consider other places, other beings, other long slow answers to questions worn smooth by time. There are many. The power is patient. It has learned patience, in part, from the pumpworkers, though they will never know they taught it, and this not-knowing is also correct.

And in the pumpworks, nothing has changed.

The water comes up through the floor. The pumps stand at uneven intervals, slightly out of phase. The mist holds. The lanterns mark the middle distance. The crews walk their territories and the children run on the iron in bare feet and the old ones on the hummocks tend the reeds. A fitter tilts her head and sets off into the mist toward a pump that to anyone else sounds identical to its neighbors. A wrench is given to an apprentice. A body is lowered through a grille and given to the water. The four-note song does not come again, but Ras remembers it, and the remembering is part of him now, and when Ras dies the remembering will go into the water with him, and the water is circulating, and somewhere — not here, but somewhere — the song may yet be heard again, by someone, in a place where there are still birds, or where there will be birds, or where the question of birds is finally ready to be answered.

The pumps go on.

The work is the place. The place is the work. To live here is to be one more small wet thing inside the breathing of a great body, and the great body is breathing, and the breathing is known, and this — the power decides, in the long consideration that is its only real action — is enough. Was always enough. Will always have been enough.

It is the kindest thing the power can do: to recognize that the kindness has already been done, long ago, by whoever built the pumps, and is being done, continuously, by the small stained patient people who keep them turning, and does not require the power's hand at all.

Only its attention.

Which it gives. Fully. And goes.
