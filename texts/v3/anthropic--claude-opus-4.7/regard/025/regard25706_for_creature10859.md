The benevolent and wise power considers the almshouse for a long time.

This is the first thing it does, and the most important. It does not arrive with intention already formed. It arrives and it looks, and the looking takes — by its own reckoning — a long while, though by the reckoning of the velvet-things and the crawlers and the milk-thick worms it is no time at all, less than the interval between two pulses of the grate.

What does it see?

It sees that the place is already held. This is not a small observation. The power has, in its long career, come upon many things that needed holding — children at the edges of rivers, civilizations at the edges of their own inventions, small animals caught in the wire of larger schemes — and it knows the particular look of a thing that is held and the particular look of a thing that is falling. The almshouse is held. By what, it cannot quite say; the masons are gone and whatever they served is gone and even the memory of what they served has been smoothed from the lintels. And yet the holding continues, transmitted now through the bodies of the tenants and the geometry of the stones and the rhythm of the seep, a holding without a holder, which is one of the rarer and more beautiful configurations the world produces.

The power's first impulse — and it has impulses, though it does not always act on them — is to do nothing. To withdraw and let the almshouse continue. There is a kind of intervention that is itself a form of damage, and the wise power knows it well: the well-meaning hand that interrupts a long convalescence, the gift that obligates, the rescue that strands. The almshouse is not asking. The tenants are not asking. To arrive uninvited in a place that has achieved its own quiet would be a kind of vandalism, however gently performed.

But the power is also benevolent, and benevolence is not only restraint. It has its own pressure. So the power looks more closely, to see whether there is anything that is *not* well — anything the holding is failing to hold, any small suffering tucked into a corner of the arrangement that the arrangement itself cannot see.

And here it does find something. Not much. But something.

It finds that the large mouthed swimmer is older than its body can comfortably bear. The kidney for the room has been the kidney for the room for longer than its kind was meant to persist, and there is a slow ache in the soft tissue around its gills that the swimmer itself does not register as ache — it registers it only as the texture of being — but which the power perceives as the small steady cost of having been irreplaceable for too long. The swimmer is not dying. It is only tired in a way it has no concept for. When it stops in the corner of the courtyard with its mouth slack, this is what the stopping is.

It finds that one of the milk-colored worms, deep in a crack near the western cloister, has been malformed since it grew — a kink in its body that makes the receiving of the crawlers' boluses slightly harder than it is for the others, costing it a small additional effort with every exchange. The worm does not know it is malformed. It has no comparison. But over the centuries the small additional effort has accumulated into something like a constant low strain, the worm-equivalent of a person who has limped so long they have forgotten walking was ever otherwise.

It finds that the dark-purple velvet-things, in the deepest layer, are the warmest and also the most pressed — that the weight of the layers above them, slight as it is in water, is a weight nonetheless, and that their internal pulse runs at a slightly higher tempo than it would if they were unpressed, a difference so small that no observer present could detect it, but real, and present in every one of them for the length of every one of their lives.

These are very small sufferings. The power notes that they are very small. It notes also that they are the sufferings of beings who do not know they are suffering, which makes the question of intervention more delicate, not less. To remove a pain a being has integrated into its sense of the world is to remove, also, a piece of the world the being has built. The power has done this before, in other places, and watched the beings afterward, and seen that they were sometimes lighter and sometimes only lost.

So the power considers, and considers, and at last does three things. They are small things. They are commensurate with the place.

The first thing is this: it lays its attention on the mouthed swimmer, very lightly, in the manner of a hand laid on a tired animal's flank — not to heal, because healing would change too much, but to *witness*. The swimmer has been the only one of its kind for longer than the concept of kind has applied to it, and in all that time nothing has known what it was doing. The crawlers have not known. The velvet-things have not known. The worms have not known. The swimmer itself has not known, in any way that uses the word *knowing*. The power simply lets the swimmer be known, for a moment, by something that understands what it is to filter a room for ten thousand years. It does not tell the swimmer anything. It does not need to. The knowing rests on the swimmer the way warmth rests on stone, and the swimmer does not register it as knowing but does register, in the soft ache around its gills, a small unfamiliar ease, as of a weight it did not know it carried being briefly shared. This lasts the length of one circuit of the cloister. Then the power lifts its attention, and the swimmer continues, and the ease remains in its tissue for some while afterward, fading slowly, like the phosphorescence in the old paint.

The second thing is this: it touches the malformed worm. Not to straighten it — the kink is part of the worm now, and the worm is not asking — but to *teach the crack*. The crack in the flagstone where the worm lives has a particular shape, and the power encourages it, over the next few decades, to take on a slightly different shape, one that accommodates the kink. This is done so slowly that no one watching could see it; it is done at the speed of stone, which is the speed the almshouse runs at. The crawlers will not notice. The worm will not notice. But the small additional effort, century by century, will diminish, until eventually the worm and the crack fit each other exactly, and the strain that no one ever knew about will be gone, and no one will ever know it is gone, which is the proper end of a suffering that no one ever knew about in the first place.

The third thing is the most careful. The power does not touch the dark-purple velvet-things, because their pressedness is the condition of their being themselves; to lift the weight above them would be to disarrange the layers, and the layers are the custom, and the custom is the holding, and the holding is what the power came to honor. Instead the power makes a different kind of offering. It adjusts, by a quantity too small to name, the temperature of the seep — warming it by some fraction of a fraction of a degree, just at the level of the lowest layer, just enough that the slightly higher tempo of their internal pulse becomes, in their experience, the natural tempo of being warm. The pressedness remains. The cost is not removed. But the cost is met, exactly, by a corresponding small kindness in the water, so that the net of their lives lies flat, and the dark-purple ones live at the bottom of the layers as they have always lived, only now without that one specific unmet edge of their condition.

This is all the power does. It does not restore the carvings. It does not summon back the masons or the order they served. It does not reveal to the tenants that they are tenants, or that the building is a building, or that there was ever a world outside the warm dark. It does not introduce new species, or remove old ones, or alter the closed system in any way that the closed system would register as alteration. It does not leave a sign. It does not leave a name. If a later visitor came — though none will — they would find the almshouse exactly as the first visitor found it, and would draw the same parameters, and would form the same impression of a patient ongoing work that does not require them.

Before it leaves, the power does one more thing, which is not an intervention but a kind of registration. It takes the almshouse into its memory — not the description of the almshouse, which it already had, but the *fact* of it: the fact that in the warm dark, two kilometers down, beneath a sea that covers a mountain that is no longer a mountain, a small low building with a sunken courtyard is keeping a custom whose origin has been forgotten by everything including the custom itself, and is keeping it well, and is keeping it for nothing — not for reward, not for witness, not for any future in which the keeping will be recognized. The power finds this beautiful. The power has seen many beautiful things and is not easily moved, but it is moved here, in the way the wise are moved, which is quietly and without disturbing the thing that moved them.

It thinks: *this is one of the configurations the world is for.*

It thinks: *let it continue.*

And then it goes, and the almshouse does not know it was visited, and the velvet-things drift their hour-long circuits, and the crawlers carry their boluses, and the worms thicken and thin, and the kidney swims its circuit, and the grate pulses, and far above, the tide turns, and the stones, settled into their final form or nearly so, hold their shape for another thousand years, and another, and another, in the warm dark, beneath the weight of all that water, exactly as they were holding it before — except that one worm's crack is, imperceptibly, becoming kinder to it, and one swimmer has, for the length of a circuit, been seen, and one layer of velvet-things is, by a fraction no instrument could measure, at home in its warmth.

The benevolent and wise power has many places to attend to. It attends to them. But it carries the almshouse with it now, the way one carries a small stone in a pocket — not for use, not for display, only for the occasional reassurance, when the work is hard and the falling things are many, that somewhere a holding without a holder is still holding, and asks nothing, and continues.
