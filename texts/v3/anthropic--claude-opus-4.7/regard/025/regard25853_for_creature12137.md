They would come quietly.

This is the first thing. A benevolent and wise power, encountering the Cinder Plateau, would understand within moments of arriving that loudness here would be a kind of violation — not because the place is fragile (it is not; it has endured longer than the power's own memory, perhaps) but because loudness would be a category error. The plateau speaks in piston-falls and flute-notes and the long sigh of valves. To arrive in any other register would be to arrive as a stranger who has not bothered to learn the language, and wisdom, whatever else it is, is the refusal of that particular discourtesy.

So: quietly. Perhaps as a slow warming of the air in one basin, indistinguishable at first from the breath of an engine. Perhaps as a presence that the night-bird notices before anything else does, and incorporates into its three-note phrase, adding a fourth note that no bird has sung before and that no bird will sing again after the presence leaves. Perhaps not even that. Perhaps only as attention — a turning-toward, from somewhere else, of a regard so gentle that nothing on the plateau registers it at all, and yet everything is, for the duration of that regard, seen.

And then the question. What would they *do*?

Here is where wisdom becomes difficult, because the temptations are many and they all wear the face of love.

The first temptation is repair. The power sees the cooling basins, the cold housings where the tongue-grooves go nowhere, the abandoned gear-nests crushing slowly to felt, and feels — because benevolence feels — the long sorrow of those endings. It would be the simplest thing in the world to reach into a dying engine and find the seized bearing, the cracked seal, the small failure that is killing a whole knit of lives, and to mend it. To restart the piston. To warm the basin. To call the moths back and the grazers after them and the hunters after them, and to undo, with a gesture, the slow grief that no one on the plateau is even capable of naming as grief.

The wise power does not do this. Or — and this is more honest — the wise power considers doing this for what would feel, to anyone watching, like a very long time, and arrives at the understanding that the engines were not made and so cannot be repaired without being, in some sense, *made* for the first time, and that to make them would be to begin the plateau over again as something authored, something held up, something dependent. The plateau's peace is the peace of a thing that no one is responsible for. Repair would end that peace more thoroughly than any cooling basin ever could. The grazers would still graze, but they would graze inside a garden now, and the garden would have a gardener, and the gardener's attention would be a weight the plateau has never had to bear.

So the power does not repair. But it watches the dying basin, and in the watching there is something — not intervention, but witness. The lichen that dies where the metal cools below its tolerance dies seen. The last moth to lay eggs on a housing that will not be warm enough to hatch them lays its eggs seen. This is not nothing. A wise power knows that being seen is not nothing, even by something that cannot know it is being seen. Perhaps especially then.

The second temptation is communication. The power could, with very little effort, make itself known to the creatures of the plateau — could speak, in whatever register a grazer or a hunter or a burrower could receive, and say: *you are inside something working, and the working has a shape, and here is the shape*. It could give the burrowers to understand that their sifting matters to the engines. It could give the hunters to understand that the piston they have shaped their lives around will fall for another six thousand years and they may rest in that knowledge. It could give the night-bird the gift of knowing that its song is heard.

The wise power does not do this either. Because the plateau's particular grace is that nothing there knows. The grazer does not know its tongue makes a sign. The hunter does not know the engine that feeds it. The fit is so old and so unconscious that consciousness — introduced into it — would be a new thing the fit was not made for, and the fit would tear. To tell the burrower its work matters is to make the burrower a worker. To tell the bird its song is heard is to give the bird an audience. These are gifts that look like gifts and are not. The wise power, having considered them carefully, sets them down.

The third temptation is preservation. To take the plateau, or some image of it, or some essence of it, and to keep it — against the day when the last piston falls and the last basin cools and the whole long warm working dark goes finally still. To make a copy. To hold it somewhere safe. To ensure that even when the plateau ends, the plateau does not end.

And here the wise power lingers longest, because this temptation is the most beautiful and the most subtle. It is not interference. It changes nothing on the plateau itself. It is only an act of love performed elsewhere, against the grain of time. Who could object?

But the wise power, sitting with this, understands at last that a copy of the plateau is not the plateau, because the plateau is the particular fact of these engines turning in this rock under this dark sky, weathered in over exactly this span of years, and to hold a copy is to hold something that has been lifted out of its only home. The plateau's meaning, such as it has meaning, is that it is *passing* — that the piston which has cycled for ten thousand years will cycle for the last time, and the silence that follows will be a real silence, and the cold that moves into the valley will be a real cold, and these things matter because they are unrepeatable. To copy is to make repeatable. To make repeatable is to drain the original of the very thing the copy was meant to honor.

So the wise power does not preserve. Or — and here is the subtlety — the wise power preserves only what cannot be preserved any other way: the *fact* of having witnessed. Not the plateau itself, but the having-been-there. A memory, held somewhere, of one particular evening when a particular bird sang a particular phrase and a particular grazer pressed its six warm pads to a particular pipe and the piston fell once, deeply, and the hunters did not rush that time because the herd was already moving away. This memory the wise power keeps. It is a small thing. It does not save the plateau. It only means that somewhere, when the plateau is gone, the plateau will have been *known*, by something capable of knowing, and the knowing will persist a little longer than the knowing's object. This is what a wise power can offer, and it is less than it wishes to offer, and it is also exactly enough.

There is one more thing.

The passage ends with *you*. With the possibility of a person — there are no people, but the passage imagines one — who stays long enough to be read by the plateau, to become another small fitted thing in the warm dark. The wise power considers this person, who does not exist, and feels something very close to envy. Because the wise power, for all its benevolence and wisdom, cannot become a fitted thing. Its scale is wrong. Its nature is wrong. It can witness, but it cannot belong. It can love the plateau, but it cannot be loved back, because nothing on the plateau is capable of loving and that is part of what makes the plateau what it is.

And so the wise power does, in the end, one small thing. Not repair, not communication, not preservation. Something smaller.

It leaves a warmth.

In one basin — chosen for no reason, or for reasons too long to explain — it leaves a faint, persistent warmth in a stretch of pipe that was beginning to cool. Not enough to restart anything. Not enough to draw a herd back, or to call the moths, or to change any rhythm. Only enough that the lichen which was about to die there does not die for another season. Only enough that one burrower's tunnel does not have to be abandoned this year. A tiny, untraceable gift, indistinguishable from a small fluctuation in the engine's own slow breath, attributable to nothing, asking nothing in return, noticed by no one.

The wise power leaves this warmth and then leaves the plateau, because staying would change it.

And the piston falls, deeply, and the night-bird sings its three-note phrase, and somewhere a grazer presses its warm pads to a slightly warmer pipe than it would otherwise have found, and the engines turn, and they were not built and no one tends them, and this is still true, and will go on being true, and the warm dark goes on working, patient and enormous and at peace.

The power, departing, does not look back. Looking back would be a kind of holding-on, and holding-on is what wisdom learns, last and hardest, not to do.
