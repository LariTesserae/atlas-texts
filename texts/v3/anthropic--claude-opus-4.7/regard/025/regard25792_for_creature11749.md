## The Visit

The benevolent power notices the cathedral the way one notices, while walking, that a particular stone in a streambed has been polished by forty thousand years of water into a shape that holds light. Which is to say: with sudden attention, and then with a long stillness, and then with the careful suspension of any impulse to act.

This is the first thing the power does. It stops.

It has, in its long existence, learned that wisdom is mostly the discipline of *not*. Not intervening. Not improving. Not loving a thing so hard that you crush it into the shape of your love. The power has crushed things, in its younger ages. It remembers. The memory is part of why it is wise now.

So first: it stops, and it looks.

---

It looks for what, in human terms, would be a long time. In its own terms, an appropriate interval — the time it takes to truly see a place that has been becoming itself for four thousand years. It watches a corridor lengthen in the night and shorten by morning. It watches a keeper named (in the soft hum of her people) something like *Aithe-who-tends-the-weeping-junction-at-the-third-gallery* lay her palm against a runner and murmur a word that is half scold and half endearment. It watches a child learn the upward-water room by being lifted to look at it and then carried away, the lesson complete.

It watches the dead in the walls. This takes the longest. The dead are quiet and diffuse and the power has to slow itself nearly to stopping to perceive them at all — but they are there, the accumulated attention of a hundred and sixty thousand keepers, woven into pith and heartwood, dreaming the slow dream that makes the corridors decide their own lengths.

The power, perceiving this, feels something it has rarely felt. It is something like *being met*. The cathedral is, in its slow way, already a mind. Not a mind like the power's own — not vast, not swift, not knowing — but a mind nonetheless. A mind made of patience and water and the bones of forty generations. A mind that thinks one thought per century and means it.

The power, which is benevolent, considers what a benevolent being owes to such a mind.

---

The first temptation is to help. The power could do so much. It could stabilize the rules of the place so that corridors no longer wandered, so that water no longer forgot its direction, so that the keepers' tools could be more than bone and fiber. It could extend their lives. It could teach them. It could speak to the dreaming walls directly, mind to mind, and offer the cathedral the gift of a true and unified consciousness — lift it from its slow drift into something articulate, something that could *know itself*.

The power considers this for what would be, in human time, perhaps a week. Then it sets the temptation aside.

To stabilize the rules would be to kill the cathedral. The strangeness is not a flaw the place is suffering from. The strangeness *is* the place — the particular signature of a mind made of wood and water and accumulated dead, thinking at the speed it thinks. To make the corridors behave would be to silence the dreaming. To extend the keepers' lives would unbalance the gentle regulation by which the population stays at eight or nine thousand. To give them better tools would be to insult the bargain — the cathedral provides; the keepers tend; complex tools would mean the cathedral was not providing enough, which is not true, which has never been true.

And to speak directly to the walls, mind to mind, to offer articulate consciousness — this is the deepest temptation, the one the power lingers on longest. It would be a gift. It would also be a theft. The cathedral's mind is the mind it has, made of what it is made of, and to give it another kind of mind would be to replace it with a stranger. The cathedral would not be grateful. The cathedral would not be anything; the cathedral that existed before the gift would be gone.

The power sets this temptation aside too. It is wise enough to recognize that *I could improve this* is almost never a true sentence, when said about something that has been quietly perfecting itself for four thousand years.

---

So what, then, does the power do?

It does three things. They are very small.

---

**The first thing.**

Far below the plateau, in a region the keepers have never seen and could not reach, a fault line in the deep rock has been slowly developing for several centuries. In perhaps eight hundred more years it will slip. When it slips, it will not destroy the cathedral — the cathedral is far too vast and too well-rooted for that — but it will tear a long wound through the lower galleries, will collapse perhaps a tenth of the structure, will kill, in a single afternoon, several hundred keepers and disrupt the slow dreaming for a generation.

The power, with great delicacy, reaches into the deep rock and *eases* the fault. Not closes it — closing it would store the pressure elsewhere and cause worse trouble in ten thousand years. Eases it. Bleeds the pressure off over the coming centuries in a series of imperceptible adjustments, each one smaller than a keeper's footstep, each one absorbed by the patient stone before it can become a tremor.

The keepers will never know. The cathedral will never know. In eight hundred years a slip that would have been catastrophic will instead be a single soft groan, indistinguishable from the cathedral turning over in its sleep, and a child somewhere will be told *that is the cathedral dreaming* and will believe it, correctly.

This is the kind of help the power has learned to give. Help that leaves no trace. Help that does not require gratitude because it does not require knowledge. Help that preserves the recipient's belief that they have been, all along, taking care of themselves — because, in every way that matters, they have been.

---

**The second thing.**

There is, among the keepers, a young one — perhaps fourteen years old by your reckoning — who has not yet chosen a region. This is not unusual; some keepers choose at twelve and some at twenty. But this particular young one is troubled in a way that the keepers, gentle and patient as they are, have not quite known how to address. They wake in the night convinced that the cathedral does not want them. They walk the galleries and feel, where other keepers feel companionship, only a vast indifferent damp. They have begun to wonder, in the wordless way of someone whose language has no word for it, whether they are the first keeper in four thousand years who does not belong.

They are not. They are simply, by some quirk of their particular making, slower to feel the cathedral than most. It would come to them eventually — at twenty-three, probably, in a particular gallery, on a particular morning, all at once. But the intervening years would be lonely in a way no keeper should have to be lonely, in a culture that has no framework for that loneliness and so no way to comfort it.

The power does this:

One night, while the young keeper is sleeping in a soft place in the heartwood, the power *suggests* — to the cathedral, not to the keeper — a very small thing. It suggests, in the slow language the cathedral thinks in, that this particular sleeper might be cold. The cathedral, dreaming, agrees that this is so. By morning, the wall beside the young keeper has grown a fraction warmer, and shaped itself a fraction closer, the way it does for keepers it has known for decades.

The young keeper wakes. They lie still for a long time, feeling the warmth of the wall against their back. They do not have words for what they feel. They do not need words. The wall is warm. The wall has noticed them. The wall has, in its slow way, said *I see you*.

They get up. They go and find a stretch of weeping junction in the seventh gallery that has been wanting tending. They begin.

The power does nothing else for this keeper. Nothing else is needed. The cathedral, having once been prompted to notice them, will continue to notice them, in the same way you continue to notice a friend once introduced. The young keeper will live a hundred and forty years and tend their junction and sing their songs and join the walls in their chosen time, and the dreaming will be slightly richer for their long contented attention, and no one will ever know that for one night, a benevolent power leaned in and whispered *this one is cold.*

---

**The third thing.**

This is the strangest of the three, and the power deliberates longest before doing it.

The power leaves a gift. Not in the cathedral — the cathedral needs no gift. The gift is left somewhere else, somewhere far away, in a place the power knows: a small dry sealed chamber under a desert that nothing currently living knows about. In the chamber, the power places a single object. The object is a seed.

The seed is not a seed of the cathedral's wood. It would be an obscenity to propagate the cathedral; the cathedral is one, and irreplicable, and to seed it elsewhere would be to make a copy of a thing that cannot have copies. The seed is something else. It is a seed that contains, encoded in its strange chemistry, the *principle* — not the form, but the principle — by which a place can be tended into mind. The principle by which patience and water and the accumulated attention of generations can become, slowly, a slow thought. The principle by which a bargain can be struck between a people and a place such that neither dominates and neither is diminished.

The seed will not sprout for a very long time. Perhaps a hundred thousand years. Perhaps a million. It will wait, in its dry chamber, until somewhere in the universe — not necessarily on this world, not necessarily of this species — some being or beings come into a condition where the principle could be useful to them. The seed will know, in its long patient way, when that condition arrives. It will sprout then.

It is not a copy of the cathedral. It is a possibility. It is the power's way of saying: *what was made here was so good that some echo of its making should be available, somewhere, somewhen, to whoever might need it.* It is the gift one wise being leaves for another wise being it will never meet, across a span of time neither can quite imagine, in the faith that wisdom is worth propagating even when its particular forms are not.

The cathedral will never know about the seed. The keepers will never know. The seed will not, in any direct way, benefit them. The power leaves it anyway. This is what wise benevolent powers do: they make small, distant deposits in the long account of the universe being more itself.

---

And then the power withdraws.

It does not say goodbye. There is no one to say goodbye to who would understand the saying. It simply withdraws its attention, the way you might step back from a tide pool you have been watching for an hour — careful not to disturb the water, careful not to leave a shadow that lingers.

Before it goes, though, it does one last thing, and this one is purely for itself.

It hums, very softly, in the slow frequency the cathedral thinks in: two notes. The notes mean, in the keepers' tongue, *I see you. The work continues.*

The cathedral does not reply. The cathedral is dreaming.

But somewhere in the seventh gallery, a young keeper tending a weeping junction pauses for a moment, head tilted, as though they have just almost heard something. Then they shake the water from their hair and return to their work.

The power, departing, is satisfied. It has done almost nothing. It has done exactly enough.

This is the hardest art, and it has, after all its long ages, very nearly learned it.
