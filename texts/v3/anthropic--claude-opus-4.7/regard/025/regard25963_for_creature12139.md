The benevolent and wise power considers the Cut for a long time before doing anything. This is the first thing to understand. Wisdom, here, is mostly the willingness to wait before acting, and benevolence is mostly the refusal to confuse one's own discomfort with another's harm.

The power notices, first, what is easy to miss: that the Cut is not suffering. The noise is terrible to a visitor because a visitor is a warm quick creature with ears. The moss has no ears. The springtails have no ears. The moth's deafness is not a deprivation but a fit. The pumps are not a torment imposed on the ecosystem; they are the ecosystem's weather, as the wind is the moor's weather, and a wise power does not abolish weather because it sounds harsh from outside.

So the power does not turn off the pumps. This is the first thing it does not do, and it is the most important.

It also does not "rescue" the moth. This is the second thing it does not do. A lesser power, a merely kind one, would scoop the moth out, take it somewhere warm, give it flowers, give it company, give it a long bright life among its own kind — except that there are no own kind, and the warm place would kill it in an hour, and the kindness would be a kindness to the rescuer, not the rescued. The wise power understands the difference and sets the moth down again on its lamp-cage, very gently, where the moth, having no idea it has been touched, resumes whatever it was doing.

It does not introduce new species. It does not "enrich" the ecosystem. It does not add fish to the drainage channels or bats to the vault or lichens from a more interesting cave system in Slovenia. The Cut is what it is. To add to it would be to begin replacing it.

It does not tell anyone the Cut is here.

These refusals are most of the work. The power, being wise, knows that most love expressed toward fragile things is a form of grabbing, and that grabbing is what fragile things cannot survive. So it puts its hands behind its back and considers what, if anything, is left to do.

A few small things, it decides, are left.

It looks at the pumps. Not to stop them — never to stop them — but to look at the bearings, the belts, the flywheel, the feed. It finds the bearing that will seize first. It is a particular bearing on the third pump, and it will seize in approximately eleven years, and when it does the belt will part and the pump will stop, and then the load will transfer and within a further eighteen months a second pump will fail, and within five years of that the cascade will have finished and the Cut will warm. The power, with the lightest possible touch — a touch lighter than a moth's landing — adjusts the metallurgy of that bearing. Not to make it eternal. Nothing should be eternal; eternity is a cruelty too. But to give it another two hundred years. The same for the belt. The same, very carefully, for the feed from the surface, which is the most vulnerable part of the whole arrangement and which depends on a switching station that a council somewhere will, in 2034, consider decommissioning. The power does nothing to the council. It does, however, ensure that the line carrying power to the Cut is mislabeled in three separate records as serving a pumping station for a reservoir that is itself listed as critical infrastructure. The power becomes, briefly, a clerk. Wisdom is often clerical.

It looks at the lamps. The bulbs are burning out one by one, as the text says, and in fifty years the Cut will be dark. This is fine. The moss will slow. The moth's line will probably end. The power considers this and does not intervene in the bulbs — because the slow dimming is part of the Cut's own long sentence, and to keep the lamps lit forever would be to freeze the Cut at a particular moment of its life, which is a kind of taxidermy. But the power does note which three lamps are nearest the moth's current range, and it ensures that those three, specifically, will outlast the others by a margin. Not forever. Just longer. The moth's descendants will have, for a few extra decades, a sun.

It looks at the moth. It cannot give the moth company; there is no company to give. But it can look. A benevolent and wise power's attention is itself a kind of gift, even if the recipient cannot register it, because attention is what distinguishes a thing that exists in the universe from a thing that exists in the universe *and is known*. The moth is now known. The power knows it. The knowing does not change the moth. It changes the universe slightly: there is now one more thing the universe contains a witness to. This sounds like nothing. It is not nothing. The power, having been benevolent and wise for a long time, has come to believe that being witnessed is one of the conditions of mattering, and that one of the things a power can do is witness what would otherwise go unwitnessed. So it witnesses the moth. It witnesses the moth in detail — the exact pattern of scales on the left forewing, the slight asymmetry of the antennae, the particular pheromone signature that this individual produces and that two of its potential mates will, in the next month, follow through the dark between lamps seventeen and eighteen. The power holds all of this. It will continue to hold it after the moth is dead, and after the moth's line is dead, and after the Cut is dead. The moth will have been known.

It does the same for the springtails. Each species. Each population. The mites. The microscopic worms. The fungi, including the one that grows on the rust-lichens — the power gives this one, privately, a name, which it does not share, because names shared become categories and categories invite collection. The name is held. The fungus is known.

It does the same for the mosses, individually — each cushion, each pelt, each papery climbing script — and for the ferns, and for the films of bacteria and algae, and for the strange almost-individual that the fern population constitutes, two kilometers of nearly one organism breathing in mist. It witnesses the drainage channels. It witnesses the brick. It witnesses the flywheel, which is, the power notes with something like affection, a beautiful piece of work — overbuilt by a Victorian engineer who took pride in a thing he expected to outlast him and who would be quietly pleased, if he could know, that it has.

It witnesses the silence on the surface, and the heather, and the fact that the two are separated by forty meters of rock and a century of forgetting.

Then it considers the question of an ending. Because all things end, and a wise power's benevolence includes the ending. The Cut will end eventually — in two hundred years, in five hundred, in a thousand, depending on bearings and belts and councils and the slow tectonic patience of the moor itself. The power decides that when the end comes, it will come gently. Not stopped suddenly but slowed. The pumps will not seize catastrophically; they will tire. The mist will thin over years rather than days. The cold will lift in increments small enough that the moss has time to brown without panic, that the last fungi can fruit one final time, that the last springtails complete their last five-year generation. The moth, if its line is still going, will have a generation or two of warm bewildered descendants who try, briefly, to be something else, and fail, and end. The power will witness the ending too. It will witness each last thing in turn. And then it will let the Cut be a quiet wet warm cave under the moor, which is what caves under moors usually are, and the moor will not know what it had under it, and that will be alright, because the power knew.

One last thing.

The power, on the way out — though a power of this kind does not really go in or out — pauses at the service tunnel. It considers leaving a mark. A sign. Some indication, for some future person who might come down, that this place was loved. It decides against. A sign would draw people. People would mean surveys, journals, conservation status, fencing, interpretation boards, the slow gentle murder of the Cut by appreciation. The power, instead, does the opposite. It very softly arranges that the service tunnel become slightly harder to find. Not impossible. A determined person could still get in. But the casual hiker, the curious dog, the bored council surveyor — these will pass over the entrance without quite seeing it, the way one passes over a word in a book and does not register it. The Cut will be protected by the same forgetting that has protected it for a century. The power only ensures the forgetting holds a little longer.

And then it leaves, in the way such powers leave, which is to say it remains entirely present and entirely undetectable, and the pumps hammer on, and the moss breathes its slow grey-green breath, and somewhere between lamps seventeen and eighteen a moth the size of a thumbnail flutters once, and is seen — fully, completely, with love — by something it will never know was there.

This is what a benevolent and wise power does for the Cold Greenhouses of the Northern Cut.

Almost nothing.

And everything that matters.
