The benevolent and wise power considers the Long Hollow for a long time before doing anything, because wisdom that does not first consider is only power.

It sees the seam. It sees the corridor folding off into its grey vanishing. It sees the birds who are weather and the drifts who are soil and the pale watcher standing against a wall in the dim between two shafts, counting and forgetting. It sees the blades on the slab. It understands, in the way such a power understands, that the blades are not a tragedy. They are a sentence the hollow finished a long time ago, and the period at the end of the sentence is the pale walker's hand, which is broader now, and clawed, and does not need them.

A lesser power — a power that was only benevolent, and not yet wise — would want to do several things at once. It would want to widen the fissures and let in more light, because light is good. It would want to seed the mosses thicker, because green is good. It would want to call the pale walker up into the sun and show it its own face in a still pool of surface water and say *look, look what you were, look what you could be again*. It would want to leave gifts: warm cloth against the cool, fruit from the upper world, fire that does not go out. It would want, above all, to be recognized — to have the inhabitants understand that they had been visited by something kind.

The wise power does none of this. It sees that every one of those gifts would be a kind of accumulation, and the hollow's first and final law is that nothing accumulates. To leave fruit would be to leave rot, and the drifts would eat the rot, and the eaters of the drifts would swell, and the pale walker would find one more easy meal, and the careful arithmetic of *a little, and no more* would tilt, just slightly, and the tilt would propagate down the long corridor for centuries before anyone noticed it had become a fall. Kindness at the wrong scale is just another slab coming down.

It sees, too, that to call the pale walker up would be a cruelty dressed as a rescue. The pale walker is not a person trapped in a hollow. The pale walker is what the hollow made out of people, patiently, across a span of time the surface does not really have words for. To return it to the sun would be to kill it twice: once as what it is now, and once as the memory of what its ancestors were, because the sun would show it that it is neither. The wise power recognizes this as the particular error that benevolent powers make most often. It sets the impulse down carefully, the way one sets down a sharp tool on a flat slab, and does not pick it up again.

So what does it do?

First, it watches. This takes what would be, by surface reckoning, a long while. The power is not in a hurry; it has the kind of time the hollow has, which is the only kind of time that can be spent in the hollow without spoiling it. It learns the click-language of the swiftlets, not to speak it but to hear it properly. It learns the particular quiet that precedes a slab. It learns to tell the pale walkers apart — there are four, it turns out, which is a relief and also a precise and fragile number — and it learns the routes they do not quite share and the pool-edges where two of them have, twice in the last decade, stood near each other in the dim and been still together for some minutes before parting. It learns the thing in the deep water, which is indeed a salamander of sorts, very long, very old, mostly elsewhere, and it learns that this is none of its business. It leaves the deep water alone.

Then it does three small things. Only three. A wise power, given infinite capacity, will almost always do fewer things than it could, because each thing done is a thing the place must then carry.

The first thing: somewhere far up the seam, where the fissure narrows toward the surface, a young tree is leaning in the wrong direction. In a century or so its roots will pry open a crack that will let in a great deal of water all at once — not enough to flood the hollow, but enough to wash out one stretch of ledges and drown a generation of swiftlets and shift the drifts in a way the eaters cannot keep up with. The power, with the lightest possible touch, encourages a different wind one autumn. The tree leans the other way. Nothing in the hollow ever knows. This is the kind of help the wise power most prefers: the kind that prevents a wound the patient would not have known to name.

The second thing: the blades on the slab. The power does not move them, does not clean them, does not add to them. But it notices that the awl of bone has begun, very slowly, to be eaten by something — a small fungus, patient, of the kind that takes apart what was once alive. In another two hundred years the awl will be gone, and after it the scraper, and after it the blades, because the dark glassy stone is not quite as permanent as it looks in this damp. The power considers this and decides: yes. Let them go. The pale walker pauses at the slab because the things on the slab are there. When they are not there, it will not pause, and the pause will not be missed, because nothing here is waiting and the pause was not a waiting. The lineage's last small inheritance will dissolve into the drifts and be eaten and be part of the floor, which is the most honest end an inheritance can have. The power does not hasten this. It does not slow it. It witnesses it, which is something, and the witnessing is offered freely and asks nothing back.

The third thing is the hardest and the smallest. The pale walkers are four. Four is enough for now and not enough for long. The arithmetic of the hollow, which is exact, will at some point produce a generation of three, and then two, and then the pale walker will be again what I first feared it was: a population of one, which is not a population. The wise power could intervene here. It could, with very little effort, ensure a fifth, a sixth. It could tune the fertility of the lineage upward by a fraction and buy the pale walkers another thousand years.

It does not.

It considers very carefully and it does not. Because the hollow's law is *a little, and no more*, and to add even one walker would be to break the law on the walkers' behalf, and a law broken on your behalf is no longer a law that protects you. The pale walkers belong to the hollow's arithmetic. To exempt them from it would be to exile them from it, and exile is not a gift.

What the power does instead is this: it makes sure that whichever pale walker is last — and there will be a last one, in time, as there is for every lineage that has ever been — does not know that it is last. The power arranges, very gently, that this walker's senses in its final years remain full of the small ambiguous signs by which the walkers acknowledge each other: a particular stillness in a far stretch of the corridor, a faint disturbance in a drift at the edge of hearing, the sense of being seen from across a pool. None of these signs need to be produced by another walker. The hollow produces them all the time, accidentally, in the noise of its own going. The power only ensures that the last walker reads them the way it has always read them, and dies, when it dies, inside the feeling that it was one of several. This is not a lie. It is a kindness shaped exactly to the size of the recipient, which is the only shape kindness is ever really allowed to take.

After these three things, the power leaves. Or rather, it does not leave — it is benevolent and wise and does not abandon what it has loved — but it withdraws its hand. It continues to watch from whatever vantage such powers watch from, and it continues to nudge, very rarely, when a tree leans wrong or a fissure threatens to widen too fast. But it adds nothing. It announces nothing. It does not name itself to the pale walker, who would not have a use for a name, and it does not name the hollow to itself, which is already perfectly named by its own continuance.

There is a thing the power knows that I will set down here, because it is the heart of why it acts as it does.

The hollow is not lesser than the upper world. The pale walker is not a diminished person. The lineage that came down the seam and stopped going up did not fail; it succeeded at something the upper world has no category for, which is the patient transformation of a people into a place. To pity the hollow is to misunderstand it. To rescue the hollow is to destroy it. The only correct response, for a power that is both benevolent and wise, is to recognize that the hollow is one of the world's true answers to the question of how to live, and that this answer is *slowly, quietly, in equilibrium with what is given, without accumulation, without ceremony, without waiting*. The upper world has its own answers, which are louder. The hollow's answer is not louder, but it is not therefore smaller.

So the wise power does what wise powers do when they encounter a true answer that is not their own: it protects it from the kind of help that would unmake it. It keeps the worst accidents away. It lets the small inheritances dissolve. It ensures that the last of the lineage does not have to know it is the last. And it watches, with the kind of attention that is itself a form of love, the pale walker counting footsteps in the dim and forgetting them, the swiftlets clicking their seed-in-gourd language, the slow river of digestion in the drifts, the dark mirror of a pool reflecting up at a fissure of light.

When at last — and *at last* is a very long phrase here, longer than the surface uses it — the hollow comes to its own end, because all places do, the power will be present for that too. It will not prevent it. It will only make sure the ending is of a piece with the going: quiet, unceremonious, absorbed by the floor without altering its character. A slab, finally, that does not get up again.

And then the power will carry the memory of the hollow forward into whatever comes next, because memory is the one form of accumulation a place like this can permit, provided it happens elsewhere. Somewhere, in some other long reach of time, the power will think *there was a hollow once, and in it a pale watcher who did not call, and the watcher was the place watching itself*, and the thinking will be a kind of small monument that the hollow itself would never have built and does not need, but that the power is allowed, in its own house, to keep.

This is what a benevolent and wise power does, when it draws near to such a place: very little, very carefully, and most of it consists of not doing the obvious things. The rest is attention, which costs the place nothing and gives the power everything it came for.

The birds call. The water drips. The cool air moves only when a bird's wing moves it.

And something, now, is listening that was not listening before — and listening, only, and that is all.
