The benevolent and wise power considers the Sump for a long time before doing anything, and the considering is itself the first part of what it does.

It has come, as such powers come, with the disposition to help. This is what benevolence is in a power: a readiness, a leaning-toward, hands already half-lifted to mend or to lift or to bless. Wisdom is what tempers the readiness. Wisdom is the pause in which the power looks at what is actually in front of it before deciding what helping would mean.

So it pauses, at the lip of the basin, and looks.

It sees the warm haze. It sees the pale lobes of travertine pressed against one another like sleeping animals. It sees the milky pools and the blind shrimp and the bones half-taken into the stone. It sees the cairn and the cups and the leaning post. It sees, distributed through all of this the way warmth is distributed through warm water, the thing that lives there.

A lesser power — one with only the benevolence and not the wisdom — would already be at work. It would be greening the basin, because green is good and bare is sad. It would be calling the frogs back and keeping them alive this time. It would be lifting the child's finger bones gently out of the eastern lip and giving them a proper grave somewhere with grass on it. It would be speaking to the presence in the basin, because a presence ought to be spoken to, and offering it company, because surely after so long it must be lonely, and offering it purpose, because surely a being so old deserves a role in the great unfolding of things.

The wise power does none of this. The wise power sees, almost at once, that every one of these helpings would be a harm.

To green the basin would be to undo the long achievement of its refusal. To call the frogs back would be to insist that the basin be a place where frogs can live, when the basin has spent geological time becoming a place that is not for frogs. To lift the child's bones out would be to tear them from the slow embrace that has, in its own way, already done for that child what graves are for. To speak to the presence would be to demand that it be the kind of thing that is spoken to — to drag it up out of its tempo into the quick chatter of beings who exchange words, which is exactly the tempo it gave up, long ago, and at great cost, and on purpose, if anything in it can be said to have purpose.

The wise power sees that the Sump is not broken. The wise power sees that the presence in the Sump is not suffering. The wise power sees, further, and this is the harder seeing, that the Sump is itself a kind of mercy already at work in the world — a small, local, unadvertised mercy, available to anyone in the surrounding country who can walk down into it, costing nothing, asking nothing, doing its quiet work on the frantic weeks of farmers and widows and children who have been frightened by something they cannot name. The Sump is, in its own slow idiom, on the same side as the power. They are colleagues. The power had not expected to find a colleague here, but here one is, and the proper greeting between colleagues is not interference.

So the power does not intervene in the basin.

But benevolence does not simply withdraw, either. Benevolence is a leaning-toward, and the leaning is real, and the power must do something with it, because to feel the leaning and do nothing at all would be a small dishonesty, and powers of this kind do not permit themselves small dishonesties.

What the power does, then, is this.

First, it stands at the lip for what would be, by the reckoning of the country above, a long while — long enough for the sun to move and for a single bird to call distantly through the wool of the haze. It stands there at the tempo of the basin, not its own tempo, and it lets itself be slowed. This is its greeting. The presence in the basin does not notice, because noticing is not what the presence does, but the slowing is received the way the basin receives everything: without ceremony, by simple lowness. For that while, the power and the presence are at the same pressure. Two settled things, neither reaching. This is as close to conversation as the situation admits, and the power, being wise, is content with it.

Second, the power makes a small adjustment in the country above — so small that no one will trace it to any cause. It thickens, very slightly, the inclination of certain people to walk down into the basin when they need to. Not many people. Not often. Just the ones for whom the basin would do its work, who might otherwise not have come: the ones who have begun to mistake their fever for their self, who would, without the walk down, harden around the fever and become it. The power does not compel them. It only makes the path a hair more findable. A rumor lingers an extra season. A grandmother remembers to mention it to a granddaughter. A man who would have gone to drink instead goes to sit. The Sump itself does not know it is being more frequented; the difference is within the margin of its weather. But over the generations, a few more frantic weeks are set down on the warm stone and drained, and a few more people go back up to their lives as most of themselves rather than as the fever they had become. This is a benevolence the basin can carry without strain, because it is benevolence in the basin's own currency, which is the slow taking-in of what arrives.

Third, the power does something for the basin that the basin would not ask for and will not notice, but that the power does anyway, because the power has seen the shape of things and knows what is coming. The country above will not always be there. The villages will go, as the basin has always known they would, and after the villages the roads, and after the roads the language in which the basin has its older name that nobody uses anymore, and after the language the species that walked down into the basin with cups. The basin is prepared for this; it has been prepared for this since before the villages came. But there will also come, much further on, a time when the warm mineral water that feeds the travertine will fail, because the deep plumbing of the world will shift, as it does. And on that day the basin will begin, for the first time in its long settled life, to change in a direction it did not choose. The lobes will dry. The haze will thin. The presence, distributed through the warmth and the wet, will have its medium taken from it.

The power cannot prevent this, and would not if it could — the deep plumbing of the world is not a thing to be meddled with for the sake of one basin, however dear. But the power can do one small thing, and does. It lays into the basin, woven through the travertine so finely that no instrument and no being will ever detect it, a kind of permission. A permission for the presence, when its medium fails, to let go. Not to struggle. Not to thin out slowly over millennia into a parched ghost of itself, clinging to the last damp seam. Simply, when the time comes, to complete the long series of givings-up with one final giving-up, and to be gone, in the same untroubled way it has been here. The permission is not a command and not even a suggestion; it is just there, available, the way a door left unlocked is available. The presence may use it or not. But it will not have to invent it from scratch in its last centuries, alone, at a tempo too slow to invent anything. The door is already unlocked. That is the power's gift, and the presence will never know it was given, which is the form the gift had to take in order to be a real gift to a thing like this.

Fourth and last, the power does the thing that wise and benevolent powers do at the end of such a visit, which is to take something away with it. Not from the basin — the basin keeps everything. From itself. The power has many places to attend to, many beings to lean toward, and the work is without end and the work is fast, because most beings are fast, and the pace of attending to them can begin, over eons, to be the only pace the power remembers. The basin has reminded it of another pace. The power carries this reminder away, folded into itself, and in the long ages to come, when it is moving among quick suffering things and is tempted to mistake quickness for the whole of what being requires, it will remember the warm haze and the milky pools and the lobes pressed against each other, and it will remember that somewhere in the low country there is a basin that has arrived at the shape it wanted and stopped, and it will be steadied.

This is what the colleague has given the power in return for the unlocked door. Neither of them transacted. Neither of them knew. But the exchange occurred, in the way exchanges occur between settled things: by simple adjacency, at the pressure they briefly shared.

Then the power straightens, at its own tempo again, and goes.

The basin does not notice it has gone, because it did not notice it had come. The haze sits as it sat. A bubble rises in a pool and breaks without much sound. Somewhere up in the warm flat country a woman who would not otherwise have thought of it thinks, suddenly and without knowing why, of a place her grandmother once mentioned, and decides that tomorrow, before the harvest, she will walk down and sit for an hour. She does not know yet that this will save her from becoming, for the rest of her life, the shape of a grief she has not yet finished having. The basin does not know she is coming. The shrimp drift. The stone grows a hair's breadth slower than a hair's breadth.

Everything is exactly as it was, which is what the power came to confirm, and what the power, in its wisdom, leaves untouched, and what the power, in its benevolence, very quietly helps to continue.
