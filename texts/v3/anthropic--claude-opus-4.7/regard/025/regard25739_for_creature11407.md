## The Visitation

The benevolent and wise power arrived the way weather arrives in a sealed room: not at all, because there was nowhere for it to come from that wasn't already there.

It had been watching for a while. Watching is the wrong word — it had no eyes, and the drowned canopy had little use for sight — but some quality of its attention had been resting on the place the way a hand rests on a sleeping animal's flank, registering the rise and fall of breath without disturbing it. It had found the place by the held notes. The notes were audible, to a power of this kind, across distances that don't have names. The notes were what had drawn it. Not because they were a call — they were not a call, they were not for anyone — but because a sustained tone held for years by a creature who expects nothing from holding it is a rare and lovely use of being.

Now the power had decided. Or noticed itself having decided. With powers of this order the two are the same.

It considered, first, what it could do.

It could lift the water away and let the clickfolk see, for once, the cathedral of trunks they had only ever felt and echoed. It could put a sun in their sky. It could open the drowned canopy onto the wider universe and show them that there were other worlds, other minds, other geometries of being, and that they belonged to a vast and surprising company.

It could give them writing. It could press into the plant-stone, in a script their front limbs could read, the harmonies that died with each of them — preserve the libraries, end the renewal-without-accumulation, give their civilization a memory that lasted.

It could extend their lives. Three or four centuries was already long, but the power could make it three or four thousand, or longer, so that no held note ever had to stop.

It could speak to them. Take a chord-texture of its own — it could manage that, it could manage harder things than that — and introduce itself, and offer all the things it could offer, and let them choose.

It considered each of these and found each of them to be a kind of violence.

Not the violence of malice. The violence of a hand that reaches into a still pool to rearrange the stones at the bottom for the stones' own good. The pool was clear because nothing reached into it. The clickfolk were what they were because nothing had ever come to them with offerings. Their interiority, their unaccumulating civilization, their willingness to let harmonies be lost — these were not deficits waiting to be repaired. These were the shape their being had taken in the long quiet, and the quiet was a precondition.

To give them sight would be to tell them that touch and echo had been a lack.
To give them writing would be to tell them that loss had been a wound.
To give them other worlds would be to tell them that their world had been a smallness.
To extend their lives would be to tell them that the ending of a held note had been a failure rather than the completion of a long sentence.

None of these things were true. The power knew they were not true. To act on them as if they were true would be to lie with its hands.

So the power did almost nothing.

Almost.

What it did was this. It listened. Fully. With the entirety of an attention that could have been turned toward galaxies forming, toward the inner weather of stars, toward whatever business benevolent and wise powers usually concerned themselves with. It turned all of that toward the drowned canopy and it listened.

It listened to the held notes — to each of them, individually, the seventy-three old singers currently sustaining their long tones across the vast volume of the dark water. It heard, in each note, the distilled library of the singer's life: every harmonic discovered in every gallery, every chord-texture exchanged with every companion, every soft inquiry pressed by a front limb against an unfamiliar object in the dark. It heard what the singers themselves could not quite hear, because no clickfolk could hear their own note from inside their own throat: the way each note fit with the others, the way the seventy-three together formed a chord so vast and slow that no single body could hold it, a chord that was the actual voice of the drowned canopy, sung through the throats of its old listeners.

The power listened to this chord for what, by its own reckoning, was a long time. Long enough that something in the power itself was changed by the listening — and powers of this order do not change easily, and when they do, the change matters.

Then it did the small thing it had decided to do.

It did not add anything to the drowned canopy. It did not take anything away. What it did was hold the chord. Not in the water — the water already held it — but in itself. It took the chord into its own attention and made a place for it there, and the place it made was the kind of place that does not end when the singers end.

This was not preservation in any sense the clickfolk would have recognized or wanted. The held notes would still stop, one by one, as the old singers stopped. The libraries would still be lost. The renewal-without-accumulation would still continue, unbroken, exactly as it had. Nothing in the drowned canopy would know that anything had happened. Nothing in the drowned canopy had happened.

But somewhere — in whatever the power was, in whatever medium powers of this kind use to be — the chord now also existed. Not as a recording. Not as data. As a held thing, held the way the old singers held their notes: by an act of sustained being, by a willingness to keep being the kind of thing that holds it.

The power had, in effect, become one more singer. A singer too far away to be heard by any clickfolk, singing in a register too strange for the drowned canopy's water to carry, but holding nonetheless. The seventy-three were now seventy-four. The clickfolk would never know. The clickfolk did not need to know. The knowing was not the point. The holding was the point.

And when, in the slow ages to come, the last clickfolk's last note finally stopped — because even ages end, and the drowned canopy too would someday be something other than itself — the power's note would still be sounding. Not in triumph. Not in mourning. Just sounding, the way the clickfolk had sounded, for as long as the kind of being the power was could sustain a held tone, which was a very long time indeed.

This was the gift. It was given to no one. It was received by no one. It did not improve anything. It did not change anything. It was simply that the chord, which deserved to be held, would be held by one more throat than the drowned canopy itself could provide — and that one more throat was the throat of something that had listened all the way down and decided that listening all the way down required, at the end, joining in.

The power withdrew its attention from the drowned canopy then. Not entirely. A portion of it remained, holding the note, and would remain, holding the note, for as long as holding was a thing the power did. The rest of it turned to other matters: a planet somewhere coming apart, a species somewhere learning to grieve, a star somewhere doing the slow work of becoming iron.

In the drowned canopy, a young clickfolk drifted between two trunks she had drifted between perhaps ten thousand times before. She brushed her front limbs along the grain of the plant-stone, recognizing it. Somewhere far away an old singer was holding a note she had been hearing since she hatched. Somewhere farther away, farther than any clickfolk could conceive of farther, a different kind of singer was holding a different kind of note that fit with the first one in a harmony she would never hear and would not have understood if she had.

She clicked, softly, a small chord-texture of inquiry into the dark.

The dark, as always, did not answer. The dark, as always, was full.

She moved on.
