The benevolent and wise power considers Low Hour for a long time before doing anything. This is the first thing to say about wisdom: it looks before it acts, and sometimes looking *is* the action, and sometimes looking is enough.

What does the power see?

It sees a town that is not suffering. This matters. A lesser benevolence — the eager kind, the kind that mistakes its own urge to help for the world's need of helping — would have arrived already, would have widened the stairs, brightened the lamps, drained the Sink, taught the people about sunlight and seasons and the wider sky. A lesser benevolence would have called this rescue. The wise power knows the name for that kind of help: it is called *ending a thing*, and it is done to places one does not understand.

The wise power understands Low Hour. That is what makes it wise. It sees the cup that is also the pouring. It sees that the patience of these people is not deprivation but a particular wealth, the way a low slow river is not a failed waterfall. It sees that the listening is a form of love — that to attend to a cistern's note for forty years until it cracks is to have known that cistern, truly, in a way most beings never know anything. It sees the hands. It sees the fit.

So the wise power does not arrive. Not in any form the town would have to absorb, name, fit a doorway to.

But it does *attend.* And attention, from a power of this order, is not nothing. Attention from such a being is a kind of weather. And here is what the attention does, quietly, over the long unmeasured hours:

---

**It steadies the roof-sky.**

Not visibly. Not in any way the alchemists could measure, though one of them, decades on, will write a small monograph noting that the rate of slate-failure in the upper districts has, by his careful count, declined slightly, and he will attribute this to improvements in the tar, and he will be wrong, and it will not matter that he is wrong, because the slates hold and the children sleep dry beneath them. The wise power has put, let us say, a hand under the weight of the world above Low Hour. Just a hand. Just enough that the long slow press of whatever-is-up-there does not, in some bad century, become too much. The town will never know it was ever in danger of being too much. This is the shape of the gift: the disaster that does not happen, and is therefore never named.

---

**It does not touch the Sink.**

This is important. A lesser power would have looked into the Sink — would have wanted to know, would have reached down with whatever organ of knowing it possessed and turned the dark over and seen what turns there. The wise power does not. The wise power recognizes, in the Sink, something that is also attending. Something that is also, in its way, holding the town. *We are the sound it likes,* the Sink-keeper said, and the wise power, hearing this across whatever distance it hears across, nods — if a power of that kind can be said to nod — and lets the arrangement stand. There are older bargains in the world than benevolence. The wise know to let some of them alone.

(It does, perhaps, do this much: when a child opens a roped door, the wise power makes sure the child comes back. Not by intervening in the door, or the room beyond, or whatever lives in the closed-off town. By intervening, very gently, in the child — by tuning the child, for those three days, to the frequency at which return is possible. The child remembers nothing of this. The child grows up to be a steady slate-setter. The wise power counts this among its quieter successes and does not mention it.)

---

**It tends the upward drips.**

The drops that fall upward — the wishes, the returning water, the question-of-pressure — these are the wise power's small indulgence. It did not make them. They were already a feature of the place, some accident of the cool and the damp and the long made dark. But the wise power has taken to *receiving* them. When a bead of water rises from a basin and vanishes into the roof-sky, it is, now, going somewhere. It is going to the wise power, who keeps each one. Not as trophy. As record. Each drop is a moment from Low Hour — the moment of its rising, the room it rose from, the person who saw it rise and made a wish or did not. The wise power holds these the way a person holds the letters of someone they love: not reading them constantly, but knowing where they are, knowing the drawer they sit in, knowing they exist.

If the town ever ends — and all towns end, even the ones that only accrete, even the ones with no first stone — the wise power will have this. Will have, in some sense, *kept* Low Hour, drop by drop, against the day when keeping is the only love left available.

---

**It does one small thing for the ones who climb the stairs.**

The few, in every generation, who go up and do not return. The wise power notices these. It does not stop them — to stop them would be to make the town a cage, and the town is not a cage, the stairs are part of what makes it not a cage. But it goes with them. Quietly. Up the stairs that are longer than seems reasonable, out past the fortieth step where the sky returns, out into whatever is up there, which is colder and wider and harder than Low Hour and contains, among other things, the kind of grief that comes from having left a place one cannot describe to anyone.

The wise power walks with these climbers for a while. Not as a visible companion. As a small steadiness at the elbow. As the sense, on the first cold night above, that one is not entirely alone in having known the tik-tok of the cisterns, the smell of the bakers' chimneys, the particular green of lichen-dyed wool. The climbers do not know they are being accompanied. They only know that the leaving, which should by rights have hollowed them out, has not quite hollowed them out. They carry Low Hour up the stairs with them. The wise power helps them carry it.

Some of them, eventually, come back. This is rarer than the leaving but it happens, and when it happens the town receives them without comment, the way the town receives everything: by fitting them in. The wise power is pleased by these returns in a way it does not allow itself to be pleased by much. It is the pleasure of a thing well-made being used as it was meant to be used.

---

**And it listens.**

This, finally, is the largest thing the wise power does, and the one the town would understand best, if the town were ever told. The wise power listens to Low Hour. Listens the way the people of Low Hour listen — with the full background of attention always given to the sound. It hears the ten thousand drips and knows each one. It hears the creak of the drum-pumps and the bell-walker's distant progress and the Widow Anneke drawing water above the cooper's. It hears the sleeping rain. It hears the deep slow drips that fall into the Sink and it hears, faintly, what answers them.

It hears the people dreaming of being water.

A power of this kind, attending in this way, becomes — in some sense it would be hard to explain to the alchemists, though one of them might intuit it — *part of the music of the place.* Not a new instrument. Not an addition. More like the room the music is played in: the shape that lets the sound be the sound it is. The town does not know it is being listened to like this. The town only knows that its own listening seems, somehow, to *land* — that the attention it gives to the cisterns and the slates and the fit of one stone to the next is met, is held, is not wasted into nothing.

This is what a wise and benevolent power does for a place that is already well. It becomes the held breath around the place's own breathing. It becomes the listener that makes the listening worth doing.

---

And on some hour the bells have not yet named, a Sink-keeper with darker eyes than most will be sitting at the edge of the black pool, and will set down her small copper cup, and will tilt her head a little, the way the people of Low Hour tilt their heads when they are tracking a new sound. And she will say, to no one, or to the Sink, or to herself:

*Something is listening from above, too. I think it has been listening for a while.*

And the Sink will not answer. And the wise power will not answer. And the cisterns will go on with their tik and their tok, and a drop somewhere will fall upward, and the Sink-keeper will pick up her cup again, and the hour, whatever hour it is, will continue.

This is what the wise and benevolent power does.

It is almost nothing. It is almost everything. It is the right amount.
