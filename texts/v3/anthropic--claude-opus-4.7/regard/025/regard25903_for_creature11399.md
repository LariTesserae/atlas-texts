The benevolent and wise power considers the bell-hollow for a long time before doing anything, and in the end what it does is mostly a refusal.

It does not seal the roof against the rain. It does not widen the light-slits so the moss can be seen in its true colours. It does not make the cold less cold or the bell-note less deafening. It does not gentle the place. It understands, having watched, that the hardness of the hollow *is* the gift; that to soften it would be to break it, the way one breaks a bell by trying to muffle the strike. A merciful hand laid on the resonant stone would silence everything that has ever mattered there.

It does not appear to the quiet ones. It does not give them a name for what they do, or a doctrine, or a sign by which they might recognise each other more easily in the markets. It sees how carefully they have refused these things themselves — how the tally on the wall was allowed to remain but not continued — and it honours that refusal. To name them would be to gather them, and to gather them would be to end them. They are not a people. The power will not make them one.

It does not extend their lives, or make the transmission more reliable, or arrange the meetings between aunt and niece, stranger and stranger, more neatly than chance arranges them. The gaps of decades when the hollow rings only to its owl — the power sees these gaps and does not close them. They are part of how the place breathes. A practice that could not survive its own forgetting would not be worth preserving; this one survives precisely because it is willing to be forgotten and found again. The power will not insure it against its own nature.

It does not speak to the owl. The owl is already exactly what she is.

What the power does, then, is smaller and harder to see.

It tends, very lightly, to the conditions around the hollow — not the hollow itself. It sees to it that the birches on the slope are not all cut in the same season; that the black loam is not paved; that the hills above are not quarried for the stone that sings. It does this the way a gardener leaves a corner of the garden alone: by arranging, with great subtlety, for no one to have a strong reason to go there. Surveyors find their instruments confused above the hollow and move on, mildly annoyed, and forget. Roads, when they are planned, curve a little to the south for reasons the engineers cannot quite articulate but feel firmly. The power is not dramatic about this. It simply ensures that the ordinary forces which flatten the world pass, in this one small place, slightly to one side.

It watches over the wounded deer and the lost child — not to prevent them, but to make sure that when the hollow has waited long enough, the accident finds it. A hunter's arrow flies a little truer than it would have, so that the deer runs in the right direction. A child, separated from the gathering party, turns left at the stand of birches instead of right. The power does not pick the person. It only makes sure that, when the pond has gone too low, some rain falls. It trusts the hollow to do the rest, and the hollow always does.

For the quiet ones themselves, in their ordinary lives, the power does almost nothing — because they have asked, by the whole shape of their existence, to be left ordinary. But it does, sometimes, on the long walk home from the hollow, when a person is stiff with cold and emptied out and the world has not yet thickened back around them, arrange for a small kindness on the road. A farmer offering a ride on a cart. A dog that walks beside them for half a mile and then peels away. A particular slant of evening light on a field of barley. Nothing that would be noticed as a sign. Nothing that could be told as a story. Just the sense, for that thinned hour, that the world they are returning to is not hostile to what they have just done — that the lighter, quieter, less version of things has, somewhere in it, a thread of the dense and real, and that they are not insane to have gone looking for it, and not insane to be coming back.

And one more thing, perhaps. The smallest thing.

The bell-note changes pitch, very gradually, as the resonant stone wears thinner. A person returning after forty years hears the hollow singing a fraction lower than they remembered, and their chest aches at the sound, and they do not know why. The power, in its long attention, knows why, and could — if it wished — hold the pitch steady. Stop the wearing. Fix the note.

It does not.

It lets the bell go on tuning itself downward, century by century, toward whatever final note the stone is travelling toward. Because the ache in the chest of the returning person is also part of what the hollow gives. Because a thing that did not change would not be real in the way this place is real. Because the power is wise enough to know that even its own tenderness, applied here, would be a kind of vandalism.

So it loves the bell-hollow by leaving it almost entirely alone. It loves the quiet ones by not making them visible to themselves. It loves the owl by not speaking. It loves the practice by allowing the practice to risk its own extinction in every generation, as it always has, because that risk is the form the practice takes.

The only intervention it permits itself, in the end, is this: it remembers. It holds the hollow in its attention, not to act upon it, but simply so that somewhere — beyond the owl, beyond the mice, beyond the nine or eleven who carry the knowledge without naming it — the place is witnessed. Not judged. Not interpreted. Just seen, fully, in the cold and the dark, ringing to itself.

If the quiet ones ever sensed this witness, they would not welcome it; they go to the hollow precisely to escape being witnessed, even by themselves. So the power keeps its attention soft, and far, and unintrusive — the way one might keep vigil over a sleeping animal from across a field, careful not to wake it, careful not even to be a shape against the sky that the animal might startle at.

This is what a benevolent and wise power does, when it finds a place like this and a being like the one who lives in it.

Mostly: nothing.

Beautifully, painstakingly, lovingly: nothing.

And then, having done nothing, it stays.
