The benevolent and wise power considers the Cradle Plateaus for a long time.

This is the first thing it does. It does not arrive, exactly — arrival is the wrong verb for something that can be where it chooses — but it brings its attention to bear, and bearing attention is itself an action, and the action takes what a human would call seven years.

During those seven years it does nothing else. It watches the springs breathe. It learns the difference between *waiting mist* and *thinking mist* by feeling it on a borrowed surface. It listens to *uremm* until it understands that the word is not a metaphor — that patience and the col-wind's note and the half-spoken speech of sleepers are, on the plateau, the same phenomenon observed from three angles. It counts the corvids. It learns *Threnn's* name, and what *Threnn* means, which is something the townspeople could have learned but have chosen, wisely, not to.

It examines the thing underneath. This takes most of the seven years. The power is wise enough to know that wisdom can be wounded by what it encounters, and it approaches slowly, the way one approaches a great animal in its sleep. What it finds, when it has finally found it, is not quite what the townspeople believe and not quite what they fear. It is something the power recognizes — has met, in different forms, on other worlds and in other strata — and the recognition produces in it a particular quiet. There are not many such things. They are not to be disturbed. They are also, in their slow way, not entirely alone: this one knows of others, and the others know of it, and the knowing is part of what the universe is made of underneath the parts that move.

Having understood this, the power understands what it must not do.

It must not heal anything. The blue lips are not a wound. The slow speech is not a deprivation. The eighty-year-old hauling jars is not in need of rescue from her work; the work is the shape her long life has taken, and the shape is correct. The power has, in other places, made the error of curing what was not an illness, and it has learned. It will not lengthen these lives. It will not thicken this air. It will not bring the great mammals up from the valleys to enrich what it might mistake for a poverty of fauna. The plateau is not poor. The plateau is exact.

It must not reveal itself. To announce a benevolent power to the townspeople would be to introduce a presence they would, out of their ancient courtesy, feel obligated to acknowledge — and the acknowledgment would cost them. They have only so much attention. Their attention is already given, in its small daily portions, to the springs and the corvids and the patience below, and the giving is the structure of their lives. To add another recipient would be to subtract from the existing ones, and the existing ones have a prior claim, and the prior claim is just.

It must not speak through the corvids. The corvids' speech is theirs. It has cost them something, across the long centuries, to learn it, and the cost is part of the meaning. A corvid that said something the power wished said would be a corvid that had been used, and the power does not use what is already itself.

So what does it do?

It does small things. It does them carefully.

It adjusts, very slightly, the trajectory of a particular felted moth on the third night of a migration, so that the moth does not strike a window where a child is watching, because the child is the kind of child who would have remembered the moth's death for sixty years and would have, on the strength of that memory, become a slightly sadder keeper of springs than the plateau requires. The moth lives its two weeks. The child grows up unburdened by that particular grief. There are other griefs; the power does not interfere with those.

It steadies the hand of an old woman descending a stepped lane in winter, once, when the stone is slicker than her foot has read it to be. She does not fall. She does not know she did not fall. She arrives home and makes tea and adds the leaves in their twelve stages and tastes, in the finished pot, something she cannot name — a faint sweetness behind the astringency, the taste of a day that was given back to her without her knowing it was nearly taken. She drinks the tea slowly. She thinks of her sister, who died forty years ago, and the thought is gentle.

It does one larger thing, but only one, and only because the larger thing would have happened anyway if the plateau had been given another two thousand years, and the power is only accelerating what was already in motion. It quiets, by a fraction of a degree, a particular harmonic in the deep rock — a harmonic that, left to develop, would have, in eighty years, caused one of the smokestacks to crack and fall on a market day. The crack does not form. The market day comes and goes. The townspeople notice nothing. They were not meant to.

For the thing underneath, the power does something different. It does not approach. It does not signal. It simply, for a long moment that lasts perhaps a human year, *keeps it company* — sits beside it, in whatever sense applies, without communication, without weight. The thing underneath does not notice in any way the power could verify. But the power knows, from its experience of such beings, that solitudes of this duration accumulate a kind of subtle thinning at their edges, and that the thinning, while not painful, is not nothing, and that to be sat beside, even unrecognized, even once in a geological age, repairs the thinning slightly. This is the most intimate thing the power does on the plateau. It does it because it can and because no one will know.

For the corvids, it does nothing. The corvids do not need anything. They are, the power judges, the most complete inhabitants of the plateau — more complete than the townspeople, who still occasionally yearn, and more complete than the thing underneath, which still occasionally thins. The corvids are exactly the size of their lives. The power respects this and passes on.

Then it begins to leave.

Leaving, for such a power, is also not quick. It withdraws its attention in the reverse order of how it gave it, releasing first what it knew last, holding longest what it knew first — which was the shape of the plateau against the indigo sky, the curve of the world implied at the horizon, the particular dampness of stone that has been damp for an age. This is the last thing it lets go of. It lets go of it the way one lets go of a held breath after a long stillness: with reluctance, with care, and with the understanding that the air released is not lost but only returned to the larger air, where it will be breathed again by something else.

Before it goes entirely, it does one final thing, and this is the only thing it does that touches the record of the plateau in any traceable way.

It tells one corvid a syllable.

Not a word — a syllable. The syllable is not in any human language and not in the corvids' shared vocabulary. It is the sound the power makes, in the register corvids can hear, for *was here and is leaving and is grateful*. It tells this to the oldest corvid, the one on the mill-house chimney, the one whose throat-smudge has faded almost to white. The corvid receives the syllable and considers it for what, in corvid time, is a long while. Then it tucks the syllable away. It does not say it. It will not say it for another forty years.

When it finally does say it, it will say it once, at dusk, to no one in particular, on a day when the waiting mist is in the saddle and the lamps are being lit. A woman crossing the lane will hear it and pause, because she has never heard that syllable before and she has lived on the plateau for sixty-one years and she thought she had heard them all. She will look up at the chimney. The corvid will look back. Neither will move for what would be, anywhere else, an uncomfortable length of time.

Then the corvid will turn its head to the saddle, and the woman will turn her head to the saddle, and they will both, in their separate ways, breathe between thoughts. And the syllable will settle into the lexicon of the plateau the way the felted curtains are felted: by being walked over, by being passed through, by being included in the slow patient density of a place that accepts what arrives and keeps only what fits.

The power, by then, will be elsewhere, attending to something else with the same care. But a small portion of its attention will remain on the plateau forever, because attention given truly is not retractable, and the power knows this, and gave the attention anyway, and considers the cost a fair one.

This is what a benevolent and wise power does, when it finds a place like the Cradle Plateaus.

It loves it by leaving it almost entirely alone.
