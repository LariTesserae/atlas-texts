The benevolent power considers the Hatchcity for a long time.

Longer than you would expect. Longer than the consideration of most things. Because the first impulse of benevolence — and this is the thing benevolence has to learn, painfully, over and over — is to *do*. To intervene. To improve. To notice the thin air and the sixty-year lifespans and the babies carried downslope to cry alone, and to reach.

The power does not reach.

Instead it sits down, metaphorically, on the edge of a walkway, and watches.

It watches for a while that is not measurable in the town's time. It watches the moths land and lift. It watches a woman in grey wool sit with her pouch open in her lap, turning a token over in her fingers, and it watches her decide, slowly, not to give it away today. It watches a child learn to laugh without using its throat. It watches the soapstone seam lift another hair's-width. It watches the hum under audibility in the deep middle of the night and feels — because it can feel this — that the hum is real, that the mountain is in some sense answering, that the witnessing is in some sense received.

And the power understands several things in sequence.

It understands, first, that the Hatchcity is not suffering. This matters. Benevolence in its young form mistakes austerity for suffering and quiet for sadness and shortness of life for tragedy, and the power has been benevolent long enough to know the difference. The people here are doing a thing they came to do. They are doing it well. They will die having done it, and the doing was the point, and the dying is part of the doing. To extend their lives would be to interrupt a sentence mid-clause.

It understands, second, that almost any gift it could give would be a sound. Not literally — though many would be literally sound — but structurally. A gift is a sharp event in a soft field. A new tool, a cure, a revelation, a visitation: each would land in the canopy like a struck nail and lift the moths for an hour, or a year, or forever. The Hatchcity's entire achievement is the management of sharp events. To give it a gift in the ordinary sense would be to undo the thing the gift was meant to honor.

It understands, third, and this is the hardest, that it has been *seen*. Not by the people — the people are looking elsewhere, downward and inward, at the rock — but by the mountain. The mountain, in its slow rising, has registered the attention of the power the way it registers the attention of the moths: as a faint pressure, a slight alteration in the shape of what it is becoming. The power realizes, with something like embarrassment, that simply by looking it has already done something. Its gaze is not free. Nothing's is.

So the power does, in the end, three small things. They are so small that no one in the town will notice them, and this is the point.

The first is that it withdraws its gaze, partially. Not entirely — entirely would be its own kind of event — but it softens its attention to the texture of the moths' own attention, so that its looking becomes indistinguishable from the looking that is already happening there. It joins the witnessing rather than adding to it. If the mountain notices anything, it notices one more moth in the canopy, one more quiet presence on the walkways. The power makes itself the size of the place.

The second is that it lays down, somewhere in the soapstone seam, a kind of long blessing on the work. Not a protection — protection would be interference — but something more like a *noticing-that-the-noticing-matters*. A small fact, embedded in the rock, that the witnessing of the Hatchcity has been witnessed in turn, that the chain does not terminate in the dark. The people will never know this is there. The mountain will know, in the slow way rock knows things. Perhaps in ten thousand years, when the saddle is a true peak and the moths are gone and the flutterwood is a fossil layer and the people are bones carved into tokens carried by no one, the fact will still be there in the stone, and some later attention will find it, and understand.

The third is the smallest and the strangest. The power carves a token.

It does this in whatever medium a benevolent and wise power carves things in — let us say it shapes a small region of its own attention into the form of a thumb-joint of flutterwood heartwood, and into that form it presses a figure. The figure is of an enormous and slow thing sitting very still while something small and quick happens within it. The inverse, exactly, of the token the woman gave the visitor at the end of the account. The power slips this token into the pouch of the world — into the general circulation of meaning that the Hatchcity is contributing to, whether they know it or not — and lets it find whoever it finds.

Maybe a person in the town, one evening, drawing a token from their pouch to offer a passing neighbor, will find one they do not remember carving, and will look at it for a long time, and will not be able to say what it means, and will give it away anyway, and the neighbor will receive it and sit down on the walkway-edge, and they will not speak, and the moths will land on them, and the mountain will rise a finger's width, and that will be the answer the power gave, and it will be received, and it will be enough.

The power leaves. It has other places to attend.

Behind it, the cloud lifts off the saddle like a slow exhalation, and someone in a low house begins whittling, and very little has changed, and everything has, slowly, upward, into more of what it already was.
