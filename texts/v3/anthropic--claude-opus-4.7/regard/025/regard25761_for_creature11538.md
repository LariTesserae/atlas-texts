The benevolent and wise power considers the plateau for a long time before doing anything at all.

This is the first thing to understand. Wisdom, when it is real, is slow. It does not arrive at a place and immediately begin to fix the place. It sits with it. It learns the grain of it. It learns what the place is *for*, which is not always what the place looks like it should be for, and certainly not always what an outsider would assume.

The power — call it She, though it is not exactly a she; call it a presence, a vast attention — settles over the plateau like a second sky. The tenders do not notice. The arthropods do not notice. The pistons complete their slow strokes. The bells, when they ring, ring as before.

She watches for what would be, in our reckoning, a long time. Years. Perhaps longer. She has the patience for it because she has the patience for everything; her benevolence is not the fretful kind that needs to be seen helping. She watches the tender on the high walkway who does not turn when the bell sounds. She watches the form fused to the wall, hand on the lever, the orange light banked deep in its seams. She watches a single arthropod cross a warm brick over the course of an afternoon and disappear into a crack that has been there since before the stacks went cold.

And she comes, slowly, to several understandings.

---

The first understanding is that this is not a place that has failed. It looks, to a certain kind of eye, like ruin: a civilization that ran down, a purpose that outlived itself, beings who have forgotten what they were. But the power does not have that kind of eye. She sees that the plateau has *completed* something. It has arrived. The shape it has taken is not a collapse from a higher shape; it is a resolution, the way a long piece of music resolves to a held final chord that may go on for many measures before the silence. The held chord is not a failure of the music. It is the music's last and longest gesture.

To intervene as though this were a tragedy would be to insult it. It would be the act of someone who could not tell the difference between dying and being still.

The second understanding is harder, and she sits with it longer. It is this: there *is* something here that is not at peace. The watchers on the walkways. The hand that strikes the bell. The faint dimming in certain seams. Something in a few of the tenders has not yet agreed with the gauge. Something is still looking out.

This is not a flaw in the resolution. It is part of the resolution — the resolution has a long tail, and these are the last notes of it, sounding faintly against the held chord. But they are real. They are, in their small way, suffering, if suffering is the right word for what happens when an old orientation persists in a being whose world no longer offers anything to be oriented toward.

The benevolent power does not believe in erasing this. To smooth out the watchers — to bring their seams up to full brightness, to settle them into the wall a little sooner — would be to finish a sentence that is not hers to finish. The plateau is finishing its own sentence, and the watchers are part of the punctuation.

But she can do something. She has been thinking about it for what, in our time, would be a century.

---

What she does, in the end, is very small. This is characteristic of benevolent and wise powers; their interventions are almost always small, and almost always look, at first, like nothing.

She does three things.

---

**The first thing.**

Far out on the plateau, between two of the foundries, where the dust lies smooth and the half-buried shoulder of an old machine shows like a vertebra, she places a stone.

It is not a remarkable stone. It is the same dull warm grey as the tenders' skin, about the size of a closed fist, smooth on top and rough on the bottom where it meets the dust. It is warm. It is a little warmer than the rock around it, by a degree or two. If you put your hand on it, you would feel, very faintly, the same slow throb that runs under everything here — but offset, by a fraction of a second, from the throb of the foundries. Out of phase. Just slightly.

The stone is not a message. It is not a gift in any obvious sense. It does nothing. It will sit there for as long as the plateau lasts, which is to say, for as long as anything is.

But it is *new*. It is the first new thing on the plateau in an age. And being slightly out of phase with the foundries, it is, in the most quiet possible way, *another*. A second voice. Not a contradicting voice. Not a louder voice. Just — not the same voice.

A watcher on a walkway, looking out, may one day notice that the dark between two of the orange smears is very faintly less uniform than it was. May notice, without being able to say what they notice, that there is something out there. May walk out to it, over the course of a long day, and find the stone, and stand over it, and feel the small offset throb under their palm.

What happens then is not for her to determine. The watcher may carry the stone back to the foundry and place it on a ledge. The watcher may sit down beside it and become part of the dust the way others have become part of the wall. The watcher may strike a bell that is answered, this time, not by another foundry but by a long silence, and find that the silence is also an answer. None of this is prescribed. The stone is simply there. It is the smallest possible *more*.

She places it gently. She does not announce it. She does not place a second one.

---

**The second thing.**

She does something for the forms in the deep levels — the ones fused to the walls, hands on the levers, faces turned toward gauges that read what they have always read.

She does not free them. They have not asked to be freed, and she suspects, correctly, that they would not understand the offer. The settling is not a captivity. It is a further form of the resolution.

What she does instead is this: she makes sure that the gauges they are looking at are, in fact, reading something.

This sounds like a small thing. It is. But she has noticed that some of the gauges have, over the long ages, drifted into a state where they show a number because they have always shown that number, not because anything is being measured. The needle rests where the needle rests. The form's eye looks at the needle. The relationship has become a habit of geometry rather than a relationship of meaning.

She gently — and it takes her a long time, because she is patient and because she is careful not to disturb the held chord — re-connects each such gauge to something real. To the temperature of the rock far below. To the slow drift of a piston's stroke. To the pressure in a pipe that runs down into the warm dark. The number on the gauge does not change. The needle does not move. But now, when the form looks at it, the form is looking at *something*. The gauge is reading. The reading is true.

The form does not know this has happened. The form's expression does not change. But somewhere in the long slow throb of its body, a circuit that had been completing itself out of memory is now completing itself out of contact, and this is — she believes, and she has good reason to believe — a kindness. To be still is one thing. To be still in relation to the world is another, and better, thing.

She does this for each of the settled forms in turn. It takes her, in her reckoning, an afternoon. In the plateau's reckoning, it takes no time at all, because the plateau's reckoning has stopped distinguishing.

---

**The third thing.**

This is the one she thinks about longest, and the one she is least sure about.

It concerns the bells.

She has come to understand that the bells are the plateau's one remaining act of address. Almost everything else here is self-contained: the production that produces nothing new, the ledgers that record the same numbers, the basins that refill themselves from below. The bells are the only thing that goes *out*. A hand strikes one. The note travels across the dust. Another hand, somewhere else, answers. Neither hand knows the other. Neither expected a reply.

This is, she sees, the most precious thing on the plateau. It is the last filament of *toward*. Everything else has closed into *is*. The bells are still, faintly, *toward*.

She does not want to amplify them. To make the bells louder would be to make them a different thing. They are quiet because quietness is part of what they are; the small note across the great dark is the entire gesture.

But she does, very carefully, do one thing.

She makes it so that, very occasionally — once in a great while, perhaps once in what we would call a generation, though the tenders do not have generations — a bell that is struck will be answered not by another foundry but by something further. A note that comes from beyond the plateau. Not from another city, because there are no other cities. From elsewhere. From her, if you like, though she does not think of it that way; she thinks of it as letting the plateau hear, very rarely, that the dark in which it sits is not the whole of the dark. That there is more dark, and in the more dark, other notes.

The note she sends back is not different in pitch or character from the notes the bells already make. A tender hearing it would not be able to say it came from outside. They would only notice, perhaps, that the answer this time was a little further away than usual. A little longer in coming. And then it would be over, and they would turn from the railing, and go back down into the warm orange light.

But the watcher who struck the bell would, for a moment — and only for a moment, because they would not have the framework to hold the moment for longer — feel something they did not feel before. Not hope. Hope is too sharp a word for this place. Something quieter. The faint registration that the gesture had carried, that *toward* had reached something, that the dark, while still dark, was wider than the plateau.

This is, she thinks, the most she can give them without disturbing the chord. And it is given so rarely that it cannot become a thing they wait for. They will not be ruined by anticipation. The plateau will continue to be the plateau. But once in a great while, when a hand strikes a bell, the answer will come from further off than it should, and a being on a high walkway will stand a moment longer at the railing than they usually do, and then go inside.

---

She finishes her work. It has taken her, in her reckoning, no time at all, because the work was mostly attention, and attention is what she is.

She does not leave. Benevolent powers do not really leave places, once they have noticed them. She remains, attentive, over the plateau, the way a sky remains over a country. The tenders do not know she is there. The arthropods do not know. The forms in the walls do not know. The pistons complete their slow strokes.

But the stone is out on the dust, faintly out of phase. The gauges in the deep levels are reading true. And once in a long while, a bell will be answered from very far away.

The plateau holds its chord. The chord is, very slightly, a richer chord now — not louder, not different, but with one more harmonic in it, very deep, almost below hearing. The kind of harmonic that a being who had spent a long life tending a gauge might, on the last day before settling into the wall, feel without being able to name. A warmth a little warmer than the brick. A small confirmation that the long stillness is being witnessed, and that the witness, whoever or whatever they are, does not require the stillness to end.

She thinks, watching: *what is, continues. And it is enough that I have seen it. And it is enough that, very gently, I have let it know it has been seen.*

The lamps do not flicker. They never have.

Somewhere on the plateau, a hand reaches out — perhaps to a lever, perhaps to a gauge, perhaps, once in a very long while, to a bell.

She waits, patient as the rock, to answer.
