The benevolent and wise power considers the Sumphouse for a long time before doing anything.

This is the first thing to understand: the power is wise enough to know that wisdom, in the presence of something like this, looks mostly like sitting down. So it sits down. Not in the chamber — it would not presume — but somewhere adjacent to it, in whatever register such powers occupy when they are paying attention. It sits the way the caretaker sits on her bench for a quarter of an hour before touching anything, and for much the same reason.

What it sees, sitting:

A place that has, through no one's intention and everyone's small accommodations, become a kind of prayer that prays itself. A woman who tends it by listening. A chain of tending that has broken once and rewoven itself out of a drunk uncle's story. A niece, twelve, who has not yet been brought down the steps, and who may or may not be a do-too-much sort, and whose readiness is not yet known even to herself.

The power notices, with something like delight, that the Sumphouse has already solved most of the problems a benevolent power might be tempted to solve. The tuning is good. The balance holds. The crickets are fed by the rats' leavings and the rats are kept honest by the bats and the bats are warmed by whatever flue or seep the original builders found, and the fig is greedy but pruned, and the caretaker is old but not yet too old, and the chain is frayed but holding, and none of this requires intervention. To intervene would be to do the thing the grandmother warned against. The place will survive a power that does too little. It will not survive one that does too much.

So the power does, mostly, nothing.

But *mostly nothing* is not *nothing*, and a benevolent power that loved this place would, I think, allow itself a few very small gestures. Here is what I think they would be.

---

**One.** On a evening in early autumn, when the caretaker is cutting river-reed in the culvert two fields over, the moon would be exactly right for binding. Not dramatically right. Not a portent. Just — the waning crescent at the angle her grandmother described, the air dry enough that the reeds will cure clean, a small confluence of conditions that means the broom she binds this year will last not one year but a little longer. Long enough. The caretaker will not notice. She will only think, months later, *this broom has been good to me*, and bind the next one the same way.

**Two.** The niece, who is twelve and clever, will find herself one afternoon doing nothing in particular in her aunt's kitchen, and she will pick up a cracked bowl that her aunt has set aside — a bowl with a hairline split that makes it sing wrong — and she will turn it in her hands for longer than the bowl deserves. She will not know why. She will set it down. But something in her will have registered that a cracked bowl is a different kind of object than a whole one, and that the difference matters, and that some objects are worth turning in your hands for a while before you decide what to do with them. This is not much. It is a seed of the right shape. The power plants it and does not press it down.

**Three.** The newt — there is almost always a newt, though not always the same one — will live a little longer than newts usually live. Not unnaturally. Just at the long end of the natural range. Long enough that when the caretaker brings the niece down the steps, finally, on a warm day next summer, the newt the niece sees will be the same newt the caretaker has been nodding to for six years. The niece will not know this. The caretaker will know, and will not say. But the continuity will be in the room, the way warmth is in the room, and the niece will feel something she cannot name, which is the feeling of being introduced to someone who has been waiting.

**Four.** On the day the caretaker dies — and she will die, as she expects, before she has finished teaching the niece everything — the death will be gentle and at home, in her own bed, in the late afternoon, with the window open and the sound of crickets coming in from the field. She will not be afraid. She will think, in the last clear moments, of the copper basin, and of whether it will need tipping this week or next, and she will realize with a small surprise that she does not know, and that not knowing is alright. The niece, who is by then nineteen, will be sitting in the chair by the bed. The caretaker will not say anything ceremonial. She will only squeeze the girl's hand once and say *the fig is getting greedy again*, and the niece will understand that this is both a practical observation and the whole of the inheritance, and she will nod, and the caretaker will close her eyes. The power arranges none of this except the open window and the crickets, which were going to be there anyway. Mostly it just makes sure no one interrupts.

**Five.** And one more thing, which is harder to describe. The power, having watched the Sumphouse for as long as it has watched it, would want to leave some small acknowledgment in the chamber itself — not a sign, not a mark, nothing the caretaker or the niece or any future caretaker would ever notice as having been placed. Something folded into the existing tuning so completely that it cannot be distinguished from the tuning. I think what the power would do is this: it would add one drip. Just one. Somewhere in the vault, in a spot where the brick has been slowly working toward releasing water for about two hundred years and would have gotten there eventually, the power would nudge the process forward by an afternoon. A new drip would begin. It would land on an empty patch of mosaic floor, on a tessera that has been lying loose for generations, and it would make a sound — a small *tip*, slightly higher than the other notes in the chamber — that fits into the existing chord as if it had always been there. The caretaker, on her next visit, will sit on the bench for her quarter-hour and will hear it without hearing it. Her body will adjust. She will set out a small dish for it, eventually, without thinking about it, the way you adjust a blanket in your sleep. The chamber will have one more voice. The tuning will be very slightly richer. No one will ever know.

This is the power's signature, if it can be called that: a single added drip, indistinguishable from accident, contributing to a music that did not need it but is better for it.

---

The power, having done these five small things over the course of perhaps fifteen years of human time, would withdraw its particular attention, though not its general regard. It would understand that the Sumphouse does not need a patron. It would understand that the caretaker's faith — *places like this generate their own caretakers* — is essentially correct, and that the most loving thing a wise power can do for a self-sustaining grace is to confirm it by not disturbing it.

It would understand, also, that it has been changed by the watching. That to have sat for a long time in attention to a chamber where every drip is caught is to have learned something about benevolence itself: that benevolence at its highest is not the rush to help but the discipline of recognizing what is already well, and the patience to add, at most, one drip to a music that was already singing.

The power goes elsewhere. The chamber closes behind it like a held breath that is in no hurry to be let out.

The niece, nineteen, comes down the sandstone steps for the first time as caretaker on a Tuesday in the spring after her aunt dies. She sits on the bench. She listens for a quarter of an hour, because that is what she was taught. She hears the crickets, and the frogs, and the drip-percussion, and somewhere in the middle of it a small high *tip* that she could not have named as new because she has no prior chamber to compare it to. It is simply part of what the Sumphouse sounds like. She lets it count her in.

Then she gets up, and she tips the copper basin, and she begins.
