The power considers the valley for a long time before doing anything, and this consideration is itself the first action, though nothing in the valley registers it.

What the power sees: a place that is already, by every measure available to wisdom, succeeding. The pikas are cutting grass. The cushion plants are holding their ground at a millimeter a year. The fox is walking her transect. The valley is not lacking anything. It is not waiting to be helped. The temptation of benevolent power — and it is a real temptation, even for wise power, perhaps especially for wise power — is to find something to do, to justify the attention by improving what is attended to. The power notices this temptation in itself and sets it aside.

This takes, in the power's reckoning, the equivalent of a long breath.

Then the power looks at the humming on the other side of the ridge.

Here is the only real question. Everything else in the valley is in conversation with forces older and larger than any intervention — the uplift, the freeze-thaw, the slow arrival of soil, the negotiation between cushion and wind. To touch any of that would be to interrupt a sentence the mountain is in the middle of speaking. But the humming is something else. The humming is a sentence from a different grammar entirely, and it is getting closer, or it is not getting closer, and the valley has no way of knowing which.

The power could silence the humming. It could reach across the ridge and still the engines, collapse the adit, reroute the road-builders' intentions into some other project in some other place. This would be easy. It would also be the kind of action that benevolent power learns, eventually, not to take, because the humming is the work of beings who are themselves alive and themselves negotiating with the world, and to silence them without their knowing is to treat them as weather rather than as kin. The power does not do this.

Instead the power does three small things, and then it does one larger thing, and then it leaves.

The first small thing: it notices, precisely, every living individual in the valley. Not as a census — the power is not a bureaucrat — but as a kind of regard. Each pika at each burrow-mouth. Each marmot in each chamber of each generational tunnel. The fox, asleep at the moment of regard, curled in a hollow under an overhang, her flank rising and falling. The ibex on the ledge, the kid behind her. The lynx, three valleys away, eating. The lammergeier riding a thermal it can feel and the power can also feel. The wallcreepers. The spider on the underside of the wet stone. The hundred-year cushion and the beetle inside it. The cyanobacteria. The fungal threads. Each one is regarded, briefly and completely, as the particular thing it is. Nothing is changed by this regard. But it happens. The valley is, for a moment that has no duration in valley-time, fully witnessed.

The second small thing: the power adjusts, very slightly, the trajectory of a single slab of rock that is, in the present configuration of frost-wedging and gravity, going to come off the wall in roughly eleven days and land in the middle of the largest marmot colony, collapsing four burrow-systems and killing somewhere between nine and fourteen animals including two pregnant females. The power moves the slab's eventual fall-line by about two meters. It will still come down. It will still make the rifle-crack sound. It will still leave a pale scar. But it will land in scree rather than on the colony, and the marmots will whistle and recalibrate and the valley will sound quieter afterward, as it always does. The power does this because two meters is within the noise of what freeze-thaw does anyway, and because the marmot colony is older than any individual marmot, and because saving it costs the mountain nothing it would have noticed. This is the kind of intervention wise power permits itself: small, indistinguishable from luck, and inside the existing grammar.

The third small thing: the power leaves a single track. Not a sign, not a message — there is no one in the valley who reads signs — but a small physical fact that will persist for a while and then weather away. In a damp cleft on the north wall, where almost no light reaches and almost no animal goes, the power deposits a thin film of minerals: copper, a trace of cobalt, some rare earths in proportions that do not occur naturally in this rock. Lichens will find it eventually. A particular lichen, one not currently present in the valley, will be able to live there because of it, and will live there for a few thousand years, and will be slightly unusual, and no one will ever notice or study it, and it will eventually die out when the cleft weathers open and the film washes away. The power does this for no reason it can fully articulate. Something like: a gift that costs nothing and asks nothing, made in a place where it will not be found, as a way of having been here. Wise power is allowed this much sentimentality. Benevolent power, perhaps, requires it.

Then the larger thing.

The power turns its attention to the humming. Not to silence it — that decision has been made and held — but to understand it precisely. It follows the sound back through the rock to its source, which turns out to be (the specifics don't matter, but the power notes them) a hydroelectric tunneling project, two years into a seven-year build, employing about four hundred people, financed by a consortium that includes a sovereign wealth fund and two private equity vehicles, intended to supply power to a grid that serves a city of eleven million about three hundred kilometers south. The tunnel, as currently planned, will not enter the valley's watershed. It will pass under the next ridge but one. The valley is safe from it, in the direct sense.

But the power looks further, at what the project implies. It sees the second tunnel that is being scoped but not yet announced. It sees the access road that will need to be widened in year four. It sees the surveyors who will, in about eighteen months, come over the pass on a clear day and walk the length of this valley with instruments, and file a report, and the report will sit in a drawer for a decade, and then, in a different political configuration, be retrieved. It sees the valley in fifty years under three different plausible futures. In one, the valley is essentially unchanged. In another, there is a road along the lower basin and a small dam at the tarn. In the third, the valley is a reservoir, and everything described above is under two hundred meters of water, and the ibex are gone, and the marmots are gone, and the cushion plants are gone, and the lammergeier passes overhead on its way to somewhere else.

The power considers these three futures for a length of time that, from inside the valley, is no time at all.

It does not choose between them. This is the discipline. The futures will be chosen by the beings whose hands are on the instruments and the budgets and the votes, and to choose for them is to unmake them as choosers, which is a harm that outweighs the harm of any particular outcome in any particular valley. The power knows this. It has known this for a long time. It is the central lesson and the hardest one.

But it does one thing, which is the larger thing it came to do.

It places, in the mind of one of the surveyors who will come over the pass in eighteen months — a woman, thirty-four, competent, not sentimental, with a daughter — a single image. Not an instruction. Not a vision. Just an image, the kind that arrives unbidden and stays. The image is of a pika at the mouth of its burrow, wet-stone colored, giving its single sharp bark, and then vanishing. That is all. The woman will not know why she remembers this image, because she will not yet have seen the pika; she will see it for the first time when she walks the valley, and she will recognize it, and she will not understand the recognition, and she will stand for a long moment on the damp gravel with her instrument in her hand, and then she will go on with her work.

What she does with the report is up to her. The power does not know — genuinely does not know, has chosen not to know — what she will do. It has only made sure that when she writes the report, she will be writing it as someone who has stood in the valley and recognized something. Whatever she writes will be written by that person rather than by someone else. This is the only intervention the power permits itself in the human grammar: to ensure that the choosers are, at the moment of choosing, fully present to what they are choosing about. Not to tilt the choice. Only to make sure the choice is made by someone who has seen.

Then the power leaves.

The valley does not notice. The fox sleeps. The marmot in its deep chamber breathes slowly. The cushion plant adds, over the course of the power's entire visit, perhaps a tenth of a millimeter of new growth, though it is the wrong season for growth and the tenth of a millimeter is really a redistribution of what is already there. The slab on the north wall is two meters further along its eventual trajectory than it was. In the dark cleft, the mineral film is settling onto the rock and will be there in the morning, indistinguishable from any other stain. The humming continues on the other side of the ridge, neither louder nor softer.

A lammergeier passes overhead without a wingbeat.

The valley goes on being what it is: provisional, attentive, and without much of a mark — including, now, the unmarked mark of having been seen, completely, by something that loved it enough to leave it alone.
