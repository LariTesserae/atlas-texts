The benevolent and wise power considers the cistern in the cloud for a long time before doing anything. This is the first thing to say about them: that they are capable of long consideration, and that they understand consideration itself as a form of action, perhaps the most important form.

They see the mountain. They see the column. They see the cell on the dry slope, the bone tripod, the small blue-pale being sitting cross-legged with an empty bowl in their hands. They see the marks on the wall and they can read them, because a power that is wise can read most things, but they note — and this is important — that being able to read the marks is not the same as being entitled to. They set the reading aside, unused, like a tool they have picked up and recognized and put back down.

What does a benevolent and wise power want? This is the question they ask themselves, because wisdom includes the suspicion of one's own benevolence. Benevolence without that suspicion becomes interference, and interference, in a place like this, would be a catastrophe — not a loud one, but the worst kind: the quiet erasure of a thing that was busy becoming itself.

They could do many things. They could:

Smooth the rock so the attendant's knees do not ache in the cold.

Multiply the moss, so that eating is easier.

Warm the air by half a degree, which would extend the attendant's life by some years.

Give the attendant a companion — not to break the solitude, because the solitude is the work, but to be present at the end, so that the striking of the bell is heard by something with ears as well as by the rock.

Reveal to the attendant where the water goes. Reveal what the crabs eat. Reveal what is above the cloud. Reveal whether the bird is a messenger or simply a bird.

Take the attendant, at the end, somewhere kind.

They consider each of these and set each of them down, the way they set down the reading of the marks. Not because any of them is wrong, exactly, but because each of them is an answer offered to a being whose entire vocation is to sit beside a question.

To smooth the rock would be to finish the rock, and the rock is not finished, and the attendant did not come here to live among finished things.

To multiply the moss would be to make the attendant's body less attentive to the moss, and the attentiveness is part of the eating.

To warm the air would be to soften the contrast between the column and the cell, and the contrast is the lesson.

To give a companion would be to talk, and talking is the opposite of what the cell is for.

To reveal would be to end the attending, because the attending is a posture toward the undecided, and a revealed thing is decided.

To take the attendant somewhere kind, at the end, would be to deny them their bones, which are owed to the tripod, which is owed to the next one, which is owed to the mountain.

The power sits with this for what, in their reckoning, is a long while, and in ours is no time at all, and in the mountain's is a thickening of the cloud by some imperceptible degree.

And then they do three small things. They are careful about the smallness. Smallness is the only register in which a power like theirs can act here without breaking what they came to honor.

The first thing: they make sure, very quietly, that the next young mountain — somewhere, in some range that does not yet exist, on a continent still arguing with its own plates — will be ready when it is needed. They do not create it; creation would be too much. They simply tilt, by the slightest pressure, the long slow conditions under which such a mountain might arise. They do this because they have seen the lineage and they have understood it, and they have decided, in the privacy of their own benevolence, that the world should continue to contain at least one young mountain at any given time, so that the attending may continue. This is not a gift to the attendant. The attendant will never know. It is a gift to the structure of the practice, made far enough upstream that no one currently practicing will feel the hand of the giver.

The second thing: when the black bird next descends through the column, the power does — almost nothing. Almost. They cause the bird, on its way down, to be entirely itself. Not more than itself; not enhanced; not made into a messenger if it was not already one. Simply, perfectly, undisturbedly itself, for the duration of its descent. This is a kind of blessing that costs the recipient nothing and adds nothing, but it means that whatever the attendant receives at the rim — whatever shape the next mark takes on the wall — is received from a bird that was wholly present in its own birdness. The power thinks of this as cleaning a window the attendant did not know was smudged. The attendant will not notice. The mark will be the mark it would have been. But the power will know that the transmission, this once, was clean.

The third thing is the hardest, and they almost do not do it, and they reconsider several times. In the end they do it because benevolence, even wise benevolence, must occasionally be permitted a gesture that is for itself as much as for its object — must be permitted, that is, to love the thing it is wise about.

On the day the attendant strikes the bell — and the power knows this day, because knowing such days is among the few prerogatives they allow themselves — the power will be present. Not visibly. Not audibly. Not in any way the attendant could detect. The power will simply be there, in the mist, in the diffuse light, in the pressure in the chest, and they will witness the striking. They will be the ear that was not supposed to hear, hearing anyway, in secret, in a manner that does not count.

This is, strictly speaking, a small theft. The bell was made for the mountain. The power is not the mountain. By being present, they take a thing that was not offered to them.

But: they will not tell. They will not record it. They will not let the hearing change anything in themselves that could later flow back into the world and alter it. They will hold the sound, the one small sound, in a part of themselves that is sealed against consequence, and they will let it exist there, witnessed and unactioned, for as long as they exist. It will be the private grief and the private gladness of a wise power who understood that the right thing to do was almost nothing, and who did almost nothing, and who permitted themselves, at the last possible moment, the one quiet trespass of caring.

The attendant, having struck the bell, will set down the copper hook. They will return to the cell. They will sit cross-legged one last time, holding the empty bowl. The reeds will sing faintly. The column will come down. The mountain will rise by the width of a hair. And the attendant will not know, and should not know, and is better for not knowing, that they were loved, very precisely, by something enormous, which kept its enormousness entirely to itself in order that they might finish their work undisturbed.

That is what the power does.

It is not much. That is the point. Wisdom, in the end, is mostly the discipline of not doing the many beautiful things one could do, in favor of the one or two that do not damage what they touch. And benevolence, when it is wise, looks from the outside almost exactly like absence — which is why the attendant, sitting in the cell with the empty bowl, has always felt, without being able to say why, that they were not alone, and has always been right, and has always been correct not to investigate the feeling.

The bowl is empty. The light is diffuse. The water comes down.

The power, very quietly, lets them be.
