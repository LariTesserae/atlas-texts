There is a power that notices.

Call it benevolent because the word will do, though the word is small for it. Call it wise because it has watched many things for a long time and has learned, among other things, when not to act. It is the kind of power that, encountering a garden, does not rearrange the flowers. It is the kind of power that, encountering a sleeper, does not wake them to ask if they are comfortable.

It finds Ceiba-Under-Cloud the way one finds a chord being held somewhere in a large house — by following the resonance back to its source. It does not arrive. Arrival is a thing bodies do. It attends. It turns some portion of itself toward the mountain's shoulder and lets the attention rest there, and in resting, listens.

What it hears, first, is the breathing. Two hundred sets of lungs and the great slow draw of the tree, and below that the deeper draw of whatever the roots are touching, which is not the mountain's water. The power knows what that water is. It is one of the few things in this world that knows. It does not say. The not-saying is part of the benevolence.

What it hears, second, is that nothing is asking for anything.

This is the consideration that occupies it longest. A wise power learns to distinguish the silence of those who have given up asking from the silence of those who have nothing to ask for, and these two silences sound nearly identical to lesser attentions, and entirely different to this one. The town's silence is the second kind. The people are not enduring. They are not waiting to be rescued from the damp or the altitude or the slow tempo of their architecture. They are not, in any sense the power can locate, suffering an unmet need. The midwife's hands are warm. The child learning the branch is loved. The old woman with the green forearms is exactly as old as she wishes to be, and when she is finished she will go into the high crotch of the tree and become sap, and this is accurate, as they say.

The power considers, briefly and seriously, the gifts it might give.

It could thicken the bark-cloth so that it dried. It could quiet the cough that takes some of them in the end. It could place, in the mind of every child, the full grammar of the eroded language so that nothing of the old songs would ever again be sung without comprehension. It could widen the platform. It could teach the monkeys not to steal, or teach the people not to mind. It could reach down the long root and explain to the tree what it is drinking, and the tree, which has been drinking it for some thousands of years without explanation, would receive the explanation politely and continue.

Each of these gifts the power examines, the way a careful guest examines an offering before bringing it into a house, and each of these gifts it sets down again. Not because the gifts are bad. Because the house is already furnished.

To thicken the bark-cloth would be to insult the weavers, who have made a cloth that does not fear rot, which is a finer thing than a cloth that dries. To quiet the cough would be to shorten the long conversation the people have with the damp, which is not entirely an enemy and not entirely a friend and which they have learned, over generations, to hold as a third party in every marriage. To install the grammar in the children would be to end something that is passing between generations precisely *because* it is incomplete — the not-quite-answer about the tree, the song whose words are not known. Some meanings live in the gap. Close the gap and the meaning leaves.

The power understands this. It is one of the things the power has learned by watching for a long time. There is a particular tenderness reserved for places that have arranged themselves, and the tenderness expresses itself, mostly, as restraint.

So it does not give the obvious gifts.

But it does not do nothing. Benevolence that does nothing is only a kind of distance with a pretty name, and this power is not distant. It is here, attending. And so it gives, instead, the things that can be given without disturbing the arrangement. These are subtle gifts, and the people will not notice them as gifts, which is part of how they are given.

It gives the tree a little more patience. Not strength — the tree is strong. Patience. A reinforcement, very slight, in the slow uptake at the deepest root, where the strange water enters. The tree was going to last a long time anyway. Now it will last a little longer. Long enough that the children who are six now, being shown their doorframes, will have grandchildren who finish them. The power adjusts nothing visible. It only steadies a thing that was already steady, the way one might rest a hand briefly against a leaning ladder that is not actually going to fall.

It gives the monkeys one additional troop-member, born in the next season, a female, who will be unusually gentle and will live unusually long and will be known by the matrons by sight and will become, without anyone marking the moment, the monkey the children think of when they think *monkey*. This is a small thing. The power does small things on purpose.

It gives the woman who is grieving — there is always one, somewhere in the forty dwellings — a dream. Not a message. Not a vision. A dream of the sort she would have had anyway, on a good night, slightly clearer. In the dream her dead are warm and the tree is breathing and she is held inside the breathing. She wakes and does not remember the dream specifically, only that she slept well, which she had not expected to. She gets up and goes to the trunk and murmurs into it for a while, and what she murmurs is hers, and the power does not listen, because some listenings are intrusions even for a benevolent power, and this one knows the difference.

It gives the traveler who went down and did not come back — there is one, currently, in a lowland city, working as a porter and drinking too much — a single afternoon of unexplained ease. He sits on a wall in the sun, which he has been finding too bright and too dry for two years now, and for one afternoon the sun is only sun, and the dryness is only dryness, and he remembers his mother's braids smelled of nut-fat without the remembering hurting him. He does not decide to go back. He may never go back. But the afternoon is given to him, and he keeps it.

It gives the song. This is the largest of the gifts and the most invisible. There is a song the town sings whose oldest verse has been corrupted past meaning for perhaps four hundred years. The singers sing the syllables without knowing them. The power, which knows every language that has ever been spoken on the mountain, knows what the verse originally said. It does not restore the verse. Instead it lets the corrupted syllables, the next time they are sung, carry — not the original meaning, but a meaning, a true one, fitted to the people as they are now. The song will not sound different. But the singers, that night, will feel that they have sung something, and will not be able to say what, and will sleep especially well, and the children sleeping in the middle of the platforms will dream of branches bending under their hands.

That is what the power does.

It does not announce itself. It does not require thanks. It does not establish a covenant or a cult or a sign in the sky. The people of Ceiba-Under-Cloud will go on co-tenanting their tree, and the tree will go on drinking what it drinks, and the cloud will go on being sipped rather than breathed, and in a hundred years the town will be almost exactly as it is now, which is the point.

Before withdrawing its attention — though withdrawal is not quite the word, since the power does not really go anywhere, only ceases to lean — it does one last thing, which is more for itself than for them, and which it permits itself because even wise powers are allowed small private gestures.

It learns the name of every person in the town. All two hundred. It learns them the way the matrons learn the monkeys: by sight, by gait, by the particular pitch each voice has found for cutting through the bird-noise. It does not need the names for any purpose. It will not use them. It only wants to have known them, in case — and this is the closest the power comes to sorrow — in case the tree should ever, despite everything, stop drinking what it drinks, and the small quick thoughts should scatter, and there should be no one else who remembers that they were ever thought.

Then it turns some other portion of itself toward some other resonance, somewhere else in the large house, and the mountain's shoulder goes on dripping in the grey, and a child on the east platform laughs at something, and the weavers on the west platform smile without having heard.
