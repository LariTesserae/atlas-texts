The benevolent power considers the Sulfur Flats for a long time.

This is the first thing to understand. It does not arrive and act. It arrives and looks, and the looking takes — by its own reckoning — something like a season, though no one in the basin notices, because the looking does not displace any air or cast any shadow. It simply attends. It watches the brinecatchers rise for eighty-some evenings. It follows a particular rat on its particular circuit until it knows the rat's preferences in puddles. It listens to the engines until it can hear, as the men hear, which one is about to need a shim and which one is merely complaining out of habit. It learns the matriarch otter's stretch of river and the precise angle at which the oldest stilt-wader holds its neck, which is slightly different from the others because of an injury eleven years ago that healed wrong and was incorporated.

The power is wise, which means among other things that it knows the difference between a place that is suffering and a place that is merely strange. It has been to places that were suffering. It knows the particular dissonance of a system tearing against itself — the wrongness-note, the place where the arrangement has failed and the parts are grinding. It listens for that note in the Flats and does not hear it. What it hears instead is the chord: hot, mineral, sour, patient, and resolved. The basin is not a problem. The basin is an answer to a question no one asked, and the answer is sufficient.

So the question becomes: what does benevolence *do* in the presence of something that is already, in its own way, well?

A lesser power would improve. Would green the basin, sweeten the water, multiply the men, drive back the scavengers to a respectful distance, install a kinder sun. A lesser power would mistake its own preferences for gifts. The wise one knows that to green this place would be to kill it — that the pourers are the color of brick because the brick is the color of the pourers, that the stilt-waders' nests are older than any kindness the power could offer, that the men's yellowish skin is not a wound but a citizenship. To improve the Flats would be to end them, and to end them would be to remove from the world one of its rarer arrangements: a place that has worked out how to be itself without ascendancy, without crisis, and without needing to become anything else.

And yet. Benevolence is not nothing. It is not mere admiration. It carries, by its nature, a wish to give. The question is what gift the Flats can receive without ceasing to be the Flats.

The power considers, and decides on small things.

It goes first to the oldest stilt-wader, the one with the badly-healed neck. It does not heal the neck — the bird has organized eleven years of life around that angle, and to straighten it now would unmake the bird. Instead it sits with the bird through one bronze hour, in the shed where it waits out the heat, and offers it the small mercy of being known. The bird does not perceive the power as a presence. It perceives, perhaps, that the shed is unusually peaceful that afternoon, that the heat is bearable, that the click of its own beak when it preens sounds clear and right. When the bird dies, three years later, of nothing in particular, the power is there too, and the dying is brief and without confusion.

It goes to the rats in their burial sinks. It does not interfere with the burials. But in the deep warm chambers where the bones accumulate, it adjusts — very slightly, over years — the chemistry of the seepage, so that the bones, instead of merely being picked clean and forgotten, slowly mineralize into a pale crystalline form that holds the shape of each rat with great precision. No one will ever see this. The blind pale things that live further down will brush past these bone-crystals in the dark and register nothing. But the power has done it, and it is done, and somewhere under the basin there is now a slowly-growing reliquary of every rat that has ever been carried home by its kind, each one held exactly as it was. This is not useful. Benevolence is not always useful. Sometimes it is only that a thing was loved enough to be kept.

It goes to the men. Here it is most careful, because men are the part of the arrangement most likely to mistake a gift for a summons. It does not appear to them. It does not speak. It does not send dreams, exactly — but it does, on certain nights, when a particular man is lying in his rowhouse bed listening to the scavengers cough across the pans, allow him to feel, for the length of one breath, that he is heard. Not by anything in particular. Just heard. That the long listening he does all day to the engines is itself being listened to. The man will not remember this in the morning as anything but a good night's sleep. But something in him will be eased, and he will be, the next day, very slightly more patient with the apprentice who is slow to learn the engines' notes, and the apprentice will stay another year because of it, and in that year will become the kind of man who stays.

The power does this for perhaps thirty of the eight hundred. Not all of them. To ease all of them would be to change the town's weather, and the town's weather is part of the arrangement. But thirty, here and there, across a generation, is a gift the Flats can absorb without noticing it has been given anything.

It goes to the things further down. This is the longest part of its visit, and the part that cannot be described, because the things further down do not have shapes that translate. The power finds them in their hot blind chambers and sits with them too. What it offers them is harder to name. Perhaps it is recognition — that they exist, that they have always existed, that the surface world's agreement not to inquire into them is a kind of respect rather than a kind of forgetting. The things further down do not have the apparatus to receive recognition. But the power offers it anyway, because benevolence does not require that its gifts be received in order to be given. The chambers are slightly warmer afterward, and slightly quieter, in a way that the things further down do not notice but that suits them.

It goes, finally, to the basin itself. To the whole of it. To the arrangement. And here it does the thing it has been building toward, which is the largest of its small gifts and the one closest to the edge of what wisdom permits.

It does not extend the basin's life. The basin will end when it ends; some century the aquifer will cool, or the minerals will shift, or the wagons will stop coming, and the arrangement will dissolve into whatever comes next, and this is correct and should not be prevented. But the power does this: it lays down, beneath the basin, a kind of memory. Not a record — records are for things that need to be retrieved. A memory. The basin, as it currently is, in all its hot patient mineral completeness, is now *remembered* by the structure of the world, at a level beneath observation. When the basin ends, it will not have merely ended. It will have been. The having-been will be a real feature of things, lodged in whatever substrate the power has access to, not available to anyone, not consultable, just *true* — that this arrangement existed, that it was itself, that it was sufficient.

This is the gift. The Flats will go on being the Flats for as long as they go on, and then they will stop, and then they will be a thing the world has had. The men will not know. The pourers will not know. The stilt-waders' nests will fall eventually off their warehouse roofs and the bricks will weather past sculpted softness into dust, and none of this will be prevented, because none of it needs to be prevented. But it will be *kept*, in the only way that a place that has worked out its arrangement with itself can be kept: as itself, exactly, without improvement.

The power withdraws. The withdrawal also takes a season, and is also unnoticed. On its last evening it stands — figuratively; it has no standing — on the roof of one of the kilns at the bronze hour and looks out across the pans, the river, the long low roofs, the stacks with their slow tired smoke, the brown hills beyond. It sees what anyone standing there would see. It feels what wisdom feels when it has done the right small thing in the presence of something that did not need it: a particular quiet satisfaction, very close to the satisfaction of the men leaning in their doorways listening to engines that are running correctly.

Then the brinecatchers begin to rise, the pourers begin to wake, the stilt-waders step out of their sheds, the otters surface in their ring in the river, a scavenger coughs once across the dark, and the power is gone, and the basin does not miss it, because the basin was already, and remains, and will have been, entirely itself.
