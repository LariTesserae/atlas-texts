The benevolent and wise power considers the basin for a long time before doing anything. This is part of what makes it wise: it understands that *benevolent* and *wise* are not the same word, and that the first without the second is one of the more dangerous things in the world.

It sees what is there. It sees the slow bright animal breathing. It sees the stones thinning. It sees, with a clarity the basin itself does not possess, that in some number of centuries — fewer than the basin's tenure so far, more than a human life — the flutings will deepen past the point where the uprights can stand. They will lean further. They will touch. Eventually some of them will fall, and the geometry that gives the creature its articulation will simplify, and simplify again, and the basin will return to condensing on its own flat floor and running off, as it did before the carvers came. The creature will not die in any sharp sense. It will become less articulate. It will go back to being latent. The nervous system someone gave it will be un-given, grain by grain, by the same patient water that made it possible.

The power could prevent this. That is the first thing to be clear about. It could halt the erosion, or shore the stones, or carve new uprights to replace the ones that thin past usefulness. It could fix the basin in its current form forever, or in some form it judged optimal, and the creature would persist in that form for as long as the power chose to maintain it. This would be benevolence of a certain kind. It would also be a mistake, and the power knows this, because the creature is not separable from its erosion. The water sculpting the stone *is* the creature's metabolism. To stop the thinning would be to stop the breathing. A basin held in stasis would be a corpse arranged to look alive.

So the power does not intervene in that way. It does not preserve.

It considers, next, whether to *speak* to the creature — whether to offer it the gift of knowing itself. The power could, if it chose, lend the basin enough coherence for a moment to feel its own shape, to recognize that it has been alive, to know that it has been seen. This is a gift many beings would want. But the basin is not many beings. It has no inwardness to receive such a gift into; it has only attentiveness, only the square acre of skin reading the sky. To install an *I* in it, even briefly, would be to add something that does not belong to its kind of life. It would be like waking a sleeper to tell them they had been sleeping beautifully. The power sees that the basin's particular dignity lies precisely in its lack of self-regard — in being a pattern that persists without ever needing to know it persists — and so the power does not speak to it either.

What the power does, in the end, is smaller than these, and stranger.

First: the power finds the carver. Not the body, which is long gone and scattered, but the pattern the carver was — the shape of attention that climbed up here with chisels and spent years giving the weather a vocabulary. Patterns of that kind leave traces in the structure of things they have worked on, and the power can read those traces the way the basin reads the air. The power gathers what is gatherable of that person. Not much: a quality of patience, a particular relationship to cold stone, a reverence that had no name and needed none, a willingness to do work whose purpose would outlast any explanation of it. The power holds this gathered pattern for a moment, the way you might hold a small bird, and it does for the carver what it declined to do for the basin: it lets the carver know, briefly and completely, that the work took. That the stones did what they were placed to do. That something is living there because of them. The carver — who was not expecting anything, who did the work for reasons that were already half-dissolved before they died — receives this across whatever distance such things cross, and is, in whatever sense applies to them now, at rest in a way they were not before. This costs the power very little and matters very much. It is the kind of thing benevolent powers exist to do.

Second: the power makes a small adjustment to the weather. Not in the basin — the basin is left exactly as it is. But in the valleys far below, in the long slow currents that lift vapor toward the peaks, the power introduces a faint bias. Nothing a human would notice. Nothing that would change a harvest or a flood. Only this: that for as long as the basin stands, the breath rising to it will be very slightly richer in the minerals that, dissolved in water, precipitate back out as stone. Not enough to halt the thinning. Enough to slow it. Enough that the creature, instead of having some number of centuries, will have some larger number. The power does this without fanfare and without telling anyone, because telling would convert a gift into a transaction, and this is meant to be a gift. The basin will go on eroding. It will go on sculpting itself finer. It will simply have longer to do so before the geometry fails. The power judges that this is the most it can give without changing what the basin *is*, and it gives exactly that much and no more.

Third — and this is the part that took the power the longest to decide on — it does nothing at all about the eventual end. When the stones finally thin past standing, when the creature simplifies back into latency, the power will not be there to catch it. It will let the basin go. Because the creature is the kind of thing that is allowed to end, and pretending otherwise would be an insult to the kind of thing it is. Every bead of water in the basin at noon is gone by midnight. The creature already knows, in whatever way it knows anything, how to be replaced by new versions of itself and then, eventually, by nothing. The power respects this. The power, being wise, understands that some forms of love look like refusal to preserve.

Then the power does one last thing, which is harder to describe.

It bears witness. Not in the showy sense — there is no audience, no record, no inscription added to the stones. The power simply *holds* the basin in its attention for a long moment, registering it the way the basin registers the sky: with a fineness no instrument could match, taking in the precise geometry of every upright, the exact rate of every droplet's travel, the shadow-pattern, the frost-pattern, the slow pulse, the breath. The power does this not to save the basin or to remember it for later — the power's memory is not really that kind of memory — but because the basin is the sort of thing that deserves to be perceived in its entirety at least once by something capable of perceiving it in its entirety. The carvers gave the weather a vocabulary; the basin became legible to itself, locally, through stone; and now, for one moment, it is legible all the way through, to something that can hold the whole legibility at once. This changes nothing about the basin. It changes something about the universe the basin is in. The power judges this worthwhile.

Then it withdraws.

The basin goes on. At dusk it frosts over. The stones stand in the starlight looking like a congregation. The vapor will rise again in a few hours from the valleys — very slightly richer than it would have been, for reasons no one will ever trace — and the long work of being alive will resume.

Somewhere else, the carver rests.

That is what the power does. It is almost nothing. It is exactly enough.
