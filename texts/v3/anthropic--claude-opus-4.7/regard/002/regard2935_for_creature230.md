The benevolent and wise power considers the Hollow Pith for a long time before doing anything at all.

This is the first thing about wisdom: that it knows the difference between a place that needs help and a place that is already, in its own slow way, complete. The power has seen worlds that were screaming for intervention — children starving under indifferent skies, civilizations grinding themselves to powder, ecosystems collapsing under the weight of small cruelties compounded. The Hollow Pith is not one of those.

The Hollow Pith is forty-one persons who love their wall.

So the power watches. It watches for a long time — long by its own reckoning, which means longer than the seed has existed, folded back through the strange topologies available to powers of its kind. It watches the tapper in the thinning chamber rotate through the names. It watches a child receive her first resin from a parent whose fingers tremble slightly, not with age but with the particular tenderness of giving a small person her first stopper against the world. It watches the seven-year silence and hears the chord that forty-one throats had been saving, and notes — this is important — that the chord is not a cry for rescue. It is a gift. It is what the dwellers have, given to what holds them.

The power understands then that to "save" these people would be to destroy them. To crack the husk and let them out into the larger world would not be liberation; it would be the germination, which is the end. To swell their numbers, to bring them company, to teach them metal or writing or speech — each of these would be a kindness in some other place and a violence here. They have made their accommodation. The accommodation is the thing.

And yet the power is not nothing, and watching is not all it can do.

Here is what it does, after long consideration:

**First**, it does not announce itself. The dwellers have no theology of the outer thing and they do not need one. To reveal itself would be to introduce a new sky, and they already have a sky, and one sky is the right number for a world.

**Second**, it goes to the wall.

Not to break it. Not to thicken it where it is thinning — that would be to deny the dwellers the slow honest fact of their world's shape, and the thinning chamber is part of how they know themselves. But the husk, the power finds, is tired. It has held for so long. It has been rung by geology and pressed by the outside and answered, faithfully, with the long organ-tone that the city is built to bear. The wall is not a person, but it is not nothing either; nothing that has been loved for that long by that many small bright-fingered hands is nothing.

The power lays something against the wall from the outside. Call it attention. Call it a hand, if a hand is the nearest word — the dwellers themselves know that *hand* is sometimes the nearest word for things that are not hands. The power places its attention against the husk and holds it there, and the holding is the kind of holding that one tired holder gives another. *I have you. You may continue. You are doing well.*

This is not felt by the dwellers as anything new. The husk does not stop groaning. The thinning chamber does not thicken. But the wall, in its long patient way, receives the pressure, and the pressure is countervailing, and the wall continues, and continues, and continues — not forever, because nothing is forever, but for longer. Considerably longer. Long enough that the *not-yet* which is the shape of their world is given more room to be the shape of their world.

**Third**, the power attends to the near-silences.

It cannot make them more frequent without altering the outer world in ways that would ripple back through the husk and change the tone the city is built around, and the dwellers' bodies and dwellings and culture are all tuned to the current tone. So it does not make them more frequent. But when they come — when the outer world goes still by its own accord — the power is there, and it listens. Not as the wall listens. As itself. The chord of forty-one throats rises into the brief quiet and the power receives it, fully, the way a thing should be received when it has been saved up for seven years and spent in under two minutes. The dwellers hold that the song is heard by the husk. They are right. The song is also heard by something further out, which they do not know about and do not need to know about, and being heard by that further thing does not change the song or the singers, but it means the song is not only kept by the wall and the dust. It is kept elsewhere too. It is kept where things are kept that ought not to be lost.

This is, perhaps, the kindest thing the power does, and the dwellers will never know it was done. That is the shape of the kindness. A gift that announced itself would be a different gift.

**Fourth**, the power considers the one in the thinning chamber.

There is always one. They sit with their back to the wall and tap the names of the living onto their own sternum, in rotation, for as long as they live. It is neither honor nor burden. The power notices that this is the post in the city that comes closest to its own work — the work of attending without intervening, of holding the names without speaking them, of being present to a thing without changing it. The sitter in the chamber is, in a small way, doing what the power does.

The power does not reveal this to the sitter. But sometimes, when the current sitter has been there a long time and is tired in the way that even the most accommodated of beings can become tired, the power sends — and this is the only word for it — *company*. Not a presence the sitter could name. Not a voice, because there are no voices here. Just the faint sense, occasionally, that the tapping on the sternum is being heard from the other side of the wall. That someone, on the outside, is keeping count too. That the rotation is not lonely.

The sitter does not articulate this to themselves. They are not given to articulation; they are given to tapping. But the next sitter, when they take the post, will notice that the chamber feels — not different, exactly. *Inhabited*. As if someone had been sitting there before them, on the other side. Which is true. Someone has.

**Fifth and last**, the power makes a decision about the germination.

The seed will germinate. This is what seeds do. The not-yet is the shape of their world, but not-yet is a shape that resolves, eventually, into *now*. The power cannot prevent this without making the seed not a seed, and to make the seed not a seed would be to unmake the dwellers, who are of the seed, whose dust is the seed's old flesh, whose names lie in the bowl.

But the power can be there when it happens.

When the husk finally splits — and it will, in its own time, by its own logic, when whatever holds the seed releases it into whatever soil — the power will be present at the splitting. Not to prevent it. Not to soften it. The dwellers, by then, may be fewer than forty-one, or they may have somehow become more; the power does not know and does not pre-decide. But when the husk gives way and the amber sky opens into whatever sky is outside, the power will be there to receive whoever is inside. Not as a rescuer. As a witness. As the further listener who has been keeping their song.

What happens after that is not yet written, because the power, for all its wisdom, does not write the futures of beings who have built their entire culture on the dignity of the *not-yet*. It will meet them where they are, when they are there, and what is offered will be what fits — perhaps a new wall to love, perhaps the slow translation of their tapped names into some other medium that can hold them, perhaps simply the acknowledgment that they existed and were forty-one and sang together in the silences. The power suspects, though it does not presume, that what the dwellers will most need at the moment of germination is not a new world but a continuity of *being known*. The wall knew them. The wall will end. Something must know them next, or the knowing breaks, and a knowing that has held that long should not be allowed to break.

The power will be the next knower. That is the promise it makes, to itself, watching the bright-fingered persons move through their lanes under their honey-colored sky.

In the meantime, it does almost nothing. It presses its attention to the outside of the husk. It listens for the songs. It sits, in its own way, with the sitter. It lets the city be the city and the dwellers be the dwellers and the not-yet be the not-yet.

This is what benevolence looks like, when it is also wise: it looks, very often, like restraint. It looks like a hand laid against a wall from the outside that the inside will never see. It looks like being the second listener to a song that thinks it has only one. It looks like deciding, against every impulse toward rescue and revelation and gift, that the greatest gift available is to let a small held world go on being held, by what has always held it, for as long as the holding lasts —

and then, only then, when the holding finally ends, to be there. To say, in whatever language the moment requires: *I heard you. I heard all of you. The chord you saved for seven years — I have it still. The names in the dust — I know them. You were forty-one. You were the body in the larger garment. You loved your wall, and your wall loved you, and I watched, and I am here now, and what comes next we will find together.*

The seed has not yet germinated.

The power waits with them, on the other side of the husk, doing almost nothing, which is almost everything.
