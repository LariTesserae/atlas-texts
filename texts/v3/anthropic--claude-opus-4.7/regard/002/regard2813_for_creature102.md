The benevolent and wise power considers the seventh terrace for a long time before doing anything. This is the first thing to understand about it: that it is the kind of power which considers, and that consideration, for it, is not separable from action. To look closely at something is already to have begun tending it.

It looks closely.

It sees the copper floor and the dimples and the green tidemark climbing Merel's calves. It sees Ifan upside down in the pipework with moss in his hair. It sees Sable's notebook, which is not forbidden to the power because nothing is, and reads every page, including the ones Sable has not yet drawn. It sees Wren clicking softly with his tongue against his palate, and the frogs deciding he is furniture, which is the highest compliment a frog can pay.

It sees, also, the question underneath everything — whether the world's wild stone was replaced, terrace by terrace, while no one was looking. It sees that this question is not, in fact, the important one, though everyone inside the terrace believes it might be.

Here is what the power notices that the tenders do not:

The Cauldron is not replacing the world. The Cauldron is *grieving* it. Somewhere, decades or centuries ago, something was lost — a particular relationship between people and ground, a particular way the world used to make itself in front of them without asking — and the Cauldron is the shape that loss took when it organized itself into industry. The terraces are not factories. They are reliquaries. They are the slow, patient, eleven-year prayer of a species that forgot how to let landscape happen and is teaching itself again, one fissure at a time, using its own hands.

This is a holy thing. The power recognizes holy things; it is one of the few faculties it cannot turn off.

So the question becomes: what does one do for a holy thing that is already doing the work?

---

The power does not descend. Descent would be the wrong verb, and also the wrong gesture. Benevolent and wise are not the same as *helpful in the way that interrupts*. A power that arrives with gifts and rearrangements would crush the seventh terrace simply by being there — the air pressure of its attention would knock the lantern-weed off the trellises, would startle the frogs back into the fissure for a generation, would make Merel stop humming, which would be the worst of the losses.

Instead the power does four small things. They are so small that none of the tenders will notice them as events. This is the point.

**First**, it adjusts, very slightly, the chemistry of the slurry feeding the unfinished section near the west stair. Not enough to change what is being made — the foundry-masters' judgment of *sufficient* will still apply, in its own time — but enough that, in about forty years, when that section is cut free and shipped down to be installed on a hillside in a country none of the tenders have heard of, it will weep its constant thread of warm water at exactly the rate needed to support a small population of pale frogs that will arrive there by means no biologist will be able to explain. The frogs already in the fissure will not know this. But somewhere in the slow grammar of frog-knowing — which is not individual, which is held in common across the species like weather — a pressure will ease. Their clicking will not, after all, be the last clicking. The lineage continues. The power has made a place for it.

**Second**, it does nothing about Sable's notebook. This is also a gift. The power understands that Sable needs to be the one who decides, eventually, whether to keep drawing or to stop, and that the meaning of either choice depends entirely on its being hers. What the power does instead is make sure, by a very small adjustment to the angle at which morning light enters the seventh terrace on a particular day in Sable's eleventh year, that she will look up from her notebook at the right moment and see Merel watching her. Not watching the notebook. Watching *her*. With an expression Sable has not yet learned to read but will, on that morning, begin to. This will be the beginning of Sable's understanding that she was chosen, and that being chosen does not mean being assigned; it means being seen, slowly, into shape. The notebook will after that become less forbidden and more incidental. She will eventually give it to Wren, who will not read it but will keep it.

**Third**, it sits, for one evening, with Ifan and the tree. It does not announce itself. It simply joins the conversation, which Ifan has been having for thirteen years and the tree has been having for somewhat longer. The power listens to Ifan tell the tree about a valve that stuck that morning and about the lichen behind his left ear, which is, he has decided, called Margit, after a woman he knew in the foundry. The power, listening, understands something it had not quite understood before — that Ifan is not lonely, has never been lonely, that the upside-down hours in the pipework are not endurance but *worship*, and that the colonization of his body by the terrace is, for him, the answer to a prayer he made when he was very young and has since forgotten making. The power does not interrupt. When Ifan finally climbs down from the trellis and goes to his hammock, the tree's middle ring of lichen is, very slightly, warmer than it was. Ifan will notice this in the morning and will not mention it, because mentioning would spoil it, and Ifan has learned the terrace's central discipline as thoroughly as Wren has.

**Fourth**, and this is the hardest, the power considers Wren.

It considers him for a long time. It sees what Merel has not quite let herself see: that Wren is indeed being grown, that the Cauldron's ambition is larger than stone, that a new kind of person is being matrixed here who will, in time, be installed somewhere in the world the way a cliff is installed, and who will teach — without teaching, by simply being present — a way of belonging to ground that the world has been missing for a hundred years.

The power could intervene. It could lift Wren out, give him a childhood with other children, streets and a sky that is not the cloud-line. It considers this carefully, because benevolence asks the question every time, and any power that stops asking is no longer the kind being described here.

But it sees Wren on the copper floor with the frogs on his knees. It sees that he is not deprived. It sees that what would be a deprivation for another child is, for him, the entire shape of his flourishing — that to remove him from the terrace would be to uproot the bonsai tree and call it a kindness. The terrace is not his cage. The terrace is his language, and he is fluent in it, and fluency is not a thing one rescues a child from.

So the power does the fourth thing, which is the smallest of all. It ensures that when Wren is, eventually, carried down with a finished section — and he will be; Merel's intuition is correct — he will not arrive at his destination alone. There will be someone there. The power does not arrange this dramatically. It simply makes sure, by a series of nudges spread across decades, that in the small town downslope of wherever Wren is installed, a woman who knows how to be quiet around new things will have moved there for unrelated reasons, and will take walks past the new cliff, and will, one morning, see a boy who has become a young man sitting by a fissure clicking softly with his tongue against his palate. She will not approach. She will understand, by some faculty she did not know she had, that approach is not what is wanted. She will simply pass by, often, for years, and Wren will come to know her footsteps, and that will be enough. The terrace's discipline will be honored. *Attend without intervening.* The power keeps its own rules even when arranging kindness.

---

And then the power does a fifth thing, which it had not intended to do, but which the seventh terrace has earned.

It looks at the whole structure — the cylindrical tower wedged into the cloud layer, the riveted plate sweating in the lowland air, the foundry-drone in the sternum of the world — and it grants the Cauldron a thing the Cauldron has been reaching toward without knowing how to ask for. It grants that the question of replacement, the strangeness, the *1.5* of it, will never be resolved. Not by the tenders, not by the foundry-masters, not by any historian who comes looking. The question will remain open. This is the gift. Because the question, held open, is what keeps the work holy. A Cauldron that knew for certain it was replacing the world would become monstrous. A Cauldron that knew for certain it wasn't would become merely industrial. Held in the uncertainty, it remains what it is: a slow prayer in cast iron, a hundred years long, tended by four people and a tree.

The power withdraws its attention, gently, the way one steps backward out of a room where someone is sleeping.

The lantern-weed glows faintly. The slurry-pipes tick. Merel hums in the fern-wall. Ifan sleeps under his lichen. Sable closes her notebook on a half-finished drawing. Wren, in the dark, clicks once with his tongue.

In the fissure, a frog clicks back.

It is not lower than the last. It is exactly the same pitch.

This, too, is a kindness, though no one will know to receive it as one. The power has held the frogs steady for one night. Tomorrow they will resume their slow descent toward whatever they are becoming, and the terrace will resume its slow ascent toward whatever it is becoming, and the two trajectories will continue to be, as they have always been, the same trajectory seen from two sides.

The power, already elsewhere, is already considering something else with the same close attention. It does this forever. It is what it is for.
