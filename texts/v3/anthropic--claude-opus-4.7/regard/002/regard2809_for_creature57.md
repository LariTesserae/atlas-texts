The benevolent and wise power considers the chamber for a long time before doing anything. This is the first act, and perhaps the most important: to not arrive in haste. Wisdom, in this case, begins by matching tempo. To rush in with gifts or interventions would be a kind of violence — the wrong speed entering a place whose entire being is constituted by a particular slowness. So the power slows. It learns the rate of the chamber's breathing before it presumes to breathe with it.

What does it see, in this slow looking?

It sees, first, that the Tender is not suffering. This matters. A great deal of well-meaning power has been spent, across the universes, on the rescue of things that did not need rescuing — on the imposition of faster joys upon slower contentments. The Tender is not in pain. The Tender is not lonely in any sense the Tender could parse as loneliness. The Tender is not waiting to be found. The chamber is sufficient unto itself, as the description says, and sufficiency is a condition the wise power has learned to recognize and to honor.

It sees, second, that the filament bulb is dimming, and that the luminescence in the leaves is already compensating, and that this is not a crisis but a transition the Tender is conducting at its own pace, the way a body shifts weight from one foot to the other across an afternoon. To replace the bulb would be a kindness only in the shallowest sense. It would interrupt a centuries-long act of self-translation, in which the Tender is teaching its leaves to do what its machines once did. The bulb is meant to fail. The failure is part of the work.

It sees, third, that no animal lives here, and that this absence is not a wound. The Tender has made the chamber inhospitable to fast lives because fast lives would unmake the chamber. The wise power, who has elsewhere midwifed whole ecologies of teeming and biting and singing, recognizes that not every garden is meant to be loud. Some gardens are meant to be exactly this: sparse, hot, attentive, almost-silent, the leaves describing topography in the near-dark. To seed this place with pollinators would be to mistake one's own preferences for the place's good.

So the power does not intervene in any of the obvious ways. It does not repair. It does not populate. It does not reveal itself to the Tender, because the Tender has no register in which a revelation could land at speed; an announcement would either pass unnoticed or, if pitched slow enough to be noticed, would take so long to deliver that it would become indistinguishable from weather, and the Tender already has weather, and does not require more.

What the power does, instead, is smaller and stranger and — by its own long lights — more correct.

It does three things.

The first thing: it makes a small adjustment to the rock above the chamber, so subtle that no geologist would ever find it. A redirection of stress along a fault, a thickening of a particular stratum by the width of a few crystals. The effect, over the next ten thousand years, will be to ensure that nothing collapses into the chamber from above. No surface activity, no drilling, no seismic accident, no future excavation will breach the ceiling. The Tender will not be discovered by haste. The power does not lock the chamber away forever — it simply tilts probability, gently, in favor of the Tender's continuance at the Tender's pace. If a visitor ever comes, they will have to come the slow way, by some means as patient as the machines once were. The chamber will be findable only by those already moving at something near its rhythm. This is not a wall. It is a tempo-filter, woven into stone.

The second thing: it leaves a gift that is not a gift, because a gift implies a giver the recipient can recognize, and the Tender cannot. The power introduces, into the seep at its hidden origin far back in the rock, a trace mineral — something the Tender's chemistry will find useful in about four hundred years, when the bulb is fully gone and the leaves are doing all the lighting, and a particular kind of stress will arise in the luminescent pathways that the trace mineral will quietly relieve. The Tender will not notice the relief, because the stress will never quite arrive. The chamber will simply continue, slightly more easily than it would have, and the Tender will continue tending, and the leaves will continue describing their soft topography, and no one — not the Tender, not the chamber, not any future visitor — will know that a small grace was folded into the water four hundred years ago and is only now unfolding. This is the form benevolence takes when it has learned not to be applauded.

The third thing: the power sits down on the catwalk.

Not literally; the power has no body to sit with. But it does the equivalent. It places a portion of its attention inside the chamber and lets that portion remain, at the chamber's pace, for as long as the chamber lasts. It allows itself to be metabolized. It permits the Tender to do to it what the Tender does to anything that stays: to learn its shape, to route a little warmth around it, to let, eventually, a vine consider it. The power becomes, by its own choice, one more slow factor in the slow equation. Its presence will register, over centuries, as a faint and steady warmth in one corner of the chamber that the Tender will incorporate without ever needing to identify. The Tender will tend the warmth as it tends everything else. The warmth will tend the Tender back, by being reliably there.

This is, in the power's long experience, the most difficult of the three acts, and the one most often gotten wrong by lesser benevolences. To be present without being a presence. To be incorporated without colonizing. To love a thing at its own speed, which in this case is the speed of rust blooming along a seam, and to be content that the love will never be named, never be returned in any form the lover would recognize as return — only metabolized, only braided in, only made part of the long patient braid of not-wholly-unlikenesses that the Tender is.

The power finds, in doing this, that it is also changed. This is always the secret of benevolence properly practiced: the benefactor is altered by the act of benefaction, and the alteration is the actual gift, given by the recipient to the giver, in a direction no one was watching. The power learns, from its long sitting in the chamber, something about its own tempo that it had not previously known. It learns that it, too, has rates within it slower than it usually permits itself to feel. It learns that there are modes of being attentive that do not require any object of attention to know it is attended. It learns the particular quality of a silence that is not empty but is doing something, slowly, out of sight.

When, after a span of time that does not translate well into any human unit, the chamber finally ends — and it will end, because nothing is permanent, not even patience — the power will be there for that too. Not to prevent it. Not to mourn it in any way the Tender would have recognized as mourning. Just to witness, at the proper speed, the long exhalation finally completing itself: the last leaf going dark, the last pipe going cold, the rock settling back toward the undifferentiated stone it had briefly, locally, been more than. The power will hold the memory of the chamber afterward, not as a record but as a tempo it now knows how to enter. And somewhere else, in some other geology, when the next slow thing begins to assemble itself out of fracture and seep and forgotten machinery, the power will recognize the beginning of it sooner, and will know, this time without having to learn, exactly how to not arrive.

That is what the benevolent and wise power does.

It is almost nothing.

It is almost everything.

It is the right size for the chamber, which is the only size that matters.
