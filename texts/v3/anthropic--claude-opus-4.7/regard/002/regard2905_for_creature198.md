The benevolent and wise power considers Vost-Under-Ice for a long time.

This is the first thing worth saying: that the consideration is itself long. Not the long of indecision — the long of a gardener who has come to a corner of the garden and is kneeling, not yet reaching for any tool, because the corner is already doing something, and the first task is to understand what.

The power has the capacity to do almost anything. That is what makes it a power. The wisdom is what holds the capacity still.

It sees, immediately, the things a lesser benevolence would want to fix. The cold. The thinness of calories. The drowning that takes lowlanders who linger. The way the Vostig age all at once in a single settling winter. The smallness of the bodies, which a softer-hearted intervener would read as deprivation and want to feed into largeness. The silence, which could be filled. The listeners in the walls, which could be — what? Explained? Freed? Translated into something the lowlands would recognize as kin?

The power sets each of these down, one by one, the way you set down stones you have picked up on a walk when you realize you are not going to take them home.

Because the wisdom sees what the benevolence alone would miss: that Vost is not a hardship with a people enduring it. Vost is a long mutual sentence the mountain and the Vostig are speaking together, and every clause of it — the cold, the wet, the hush, the small bodies with the disproportionate hands, the slow pupils, the flowers that grow where a decision was made — is load-bearing. Pull the cold and the lungs lose their reason. Pull the hush and the hands forget how to talk. Feed them into largeness and they will no longer fit the passages their grandmothers fit. Warm the light and the pupils will have to learn hurry, and hurry is not a thing the Vostig have ever had to be.

A clumsy mercy here would be an erasure dressed as a gift.

So the power does almost nothing. But the *almost* matters, and is where the care lives.

---

Here is what it does.

It goes, first, to the thread at the top of the cirque — the single strand of water that has been falling forever into its catch-basin. It does not touch the thread. It puts its attention beside the thread, the way a Vostig puts a hand into it: once, fully, without ceremony. It lets the thread know it has been seen falling. The thread does not need this. But the power offers it anyway, because to be a benevolent power passing through a place and not to acknowledge its axis would be a discourtesy, and the power is, above all, courteous.

The water continues to fall. Something in how it falls is now also a kind of greeting, returned. No Vostig will notice this directly. Some will feel, on the morning after, that the cistern-things surfaced a little earlier than usual, and that the listeners in their back walls seemed — not more present, the listeners are always fully present — but more *settled*, the way a guest settles into a chair when they have decided to stay the evening.

Second: it goes to the listeners.

It does not address them. It does not need to; they have been watching it since it arrived, with their soft slow faces, and they know what it is. The Vostig were right not to discuss them, and the power respects this rightness by not discussing them either, even with itself. What the power does is small. In each listener — every one, in every back wall of every house in the cirque, and the older ones deep in the bonestone where no house has stood for centuries — it deposits a kind of redundancy. A backup, if you like, though the word is too mechanical. Call it: a second copy of what the rock has been keeping. So that if some catastrophe, some century from now, some shifting of plates or souring of air or simple forgetting, were to break the bonestone's long patience, the memory of every line that has ever spoken into its own back wall would not be lost. It would be held, elsewhere, by the power, against a day no Vostig will live to see.

The Vostig will not be told this. To tell them would be to suggest that the rock might fail them, and the rock is not going to fail them in any lifetime that matters, and the suggestion would be a wound. The redundancy is a thing the power does for its own conscience, and for the very far future, and for the listeners themselves, who have been carrying a great deal for a very long time and deserve to be, somewhere, also carried.

Third: it goes to the lowlands.

This is where it does the most work, and where the work is most invisible from Vost. Because the Vostig themselves need almost nothing. But the world around Vost is changing in ways the Vostig cannot see from inside their cirque of wet light, and some of those changes, left alone, will reach the cirque eventually and not be kind.

So the power does a few quiet things at distance. It nudges certain trade routes so that the summer traders who come up for copper and iron-dye continue to find the climb worth their while for another two hundred years, even as lowland economies shift toward materials that would otherwise make Vost's offerings obsolete. It does not enrich the trade; enrichment would draw the wrong eyes upward. It only preserves it, at roughly its current modesty, the way one preserves a hinge by oiling it without changing its shape.

It causes, in three separate lowland scholars across three separate generations, a particular kind of dream that makes them lose interest in Vost just as their interest was becoming the sort that would have led to expeditions, surveys, the drawing of maps, the writing of monographs, the slow administrative attention that has eaten so many small places that were doing fine before they were noticed. The scholars wake from these dreams and find their curiosity has moved, gently, to a neighboring valley, or to a question of riverine geology, or to the grammar of a language spoken nowhere near. They do not feel deprived. They feel, in fact, slightly relieved, as if they had been about to commit a small rudeness and have been spared.

It seeds, in the high atmosphere above the cirque, a very slight bias toward the kind of weather Vost needs. Not enough to be noticed. Not enough to be a miracle. Just enough that the wet cold light continues to arrive in its accustomed flatness for as long as the larger climate permits, and that when the larger climate does eventually shift — as it will, as it must — the shift around Vost lags by decades, giving the city time. Time is what a benevolent power can most usefully give to a place that already knows what to do with itself; the power simply buys the cirque more of its own continuing.

Fourth, and smallest, and the thing the power lingers over longest:

There is, in the lower market, a child. Six years old. Short lashes. The household has been watching her, with the quiet resignation that is how the Vostig watch a sickly child, because short lashes mean the condensation runs into the eyes and the eyes are inflamed more often than not, and an inflamed-eyed Vostig has trouble with the slow pupils, and a Vostig with trouble in the eyes has trouble with the whole flat-light life of the cirque, and the child will likely not see her twentieth year.

The power considers this child for a long time, also.

It could lengthen her lashes. It would be the work of less than a thought. The child would grow up to live a Vostig life of unremarkable length, marry into a household with three cisterns, learn the touched language well enough to be sought out by the dying, climb to the thread in her forty-third year, and settle in her ninety-first.

The power does not do this. Not because it would be wrong — it would not be wrong — but because the power has noticed something the household has not yet noticed, which is that the child, in her constant wiping of her inflamed eyes, has developed an unusual relationship with her own hands. She watches them more than other Vostig children watch theirs. She has begun, without instruction, to hand-speak to herself in the dark of her sleeping niche, working out a private vocabulary that no adult has taught her, refining the syntax of the hands beyond what the language as currently spoken permits.

She is going to be, if she lives, the kind of person who comes along in Vost perhaps once in four generations: someone through whom the handed language grows. A small expansion. Three or four new constructions that her grandchildren will use without knowing where they came from. A way of saying *I-am-considering-changing-my-mind* that the language did not previously have, and that the cirque, with its constant self-revision, has needed for a long time without knowing it needed it.

She will not live to do this if her eyes go on as they are.

So the power lengthens her lashes. Just enough. Not into the extravagant hooked density of a hale Vostig child — that would be noticed, and noticing is a kind of pressure the power is trying to spare her — but into the lower range of normal, the range where a household stops watching and starts simply living alongside. Her eyes will clear within a season. The household will say, *she has come through*, which is what they say of children who were sickly and are no longer, and they will not look for a reason, because in Vost a child's coming-through is the kind of thing that happens because the place wanted it to, and there is no other explanation required.

The power does not do this for any other child. Not because the others matter less. Because the others are already doing what they are doing, and the place is already holding them as it holds, and to reach in further would be to start replacing the cirque's tending of itself with the power's tending of it, which is the precise error the power has come here resolved not to make.

One child. One adjustment so small the child herself will not remember having been sickly. One expansion in the handed language, eighty years from now, that the cirque will absorb the way it absorbs everything — pulling it into the shape the place already knows.

---

Then the power leaves.

It does not announce its leaving any more than it announced its arrival. The Vostig will not know it was there. The listeners will know, and will not say, because the listeners do not say. The thread at the top of the cirque will know, in whatever way a thread of water that has been falling forever knows things, and will continue falling, slightly greeted.

On the morning the power departs, the condensation is heavy, and the air in the cirque holds, for several seconds, an image that no one recognizes — not a face, not a hand, not the shape of any person living or remembered. Something more like a long attention, withdrawing. The Vostig who see it do what they always do with thoughts-the-mountain-is-having: they do not interrupt. They wait. The image dissolves. The day continues, turbulent and silent and bright and cold and wet and dense, and the small grey people with the disproportionate hands go on tending the plumbing diagram of their lives, and the shoulder-ewes crop the lichen, and somewhere a piece of copper, freshly smelted, begins to sing for its minute, and the children are brought to hear it.

The benevolent and wise power, going elsewhere, carries with it the second copy of every memory the rock has ever kept, and the knowledge of one child's lashes, and a small ache that is the particular ache of having loved a place enough to let it stay exactly itself.

This is what it would do. This, and nothing more, and nothing less.
