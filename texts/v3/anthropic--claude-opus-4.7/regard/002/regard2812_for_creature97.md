The benevolent power arrives the way weather arrives — without announcement, having always in some sense been on its way. It does not descend. It does not manifest. It is simply, at a certain hour of a certain afternoon, *attending* to the Long Shallows, the way the Shallows themselves attend to a beetle on a reed.

It looks for a long time.

This is the first thing to understand about what it does: it looks for a long time before it does anything. Wisdom that is also benevolent has learned to be careful of its own gifts. The power has, in its travels, encountered places that were starving, places that were being torn apart, places where a single small intervention — a turning-aside of a comet, a softening of a winter, a whisper into the ear of a tyrant — would unlock generations of flourishing. It knows the shape of those places. It knows how they call out, even when they don't know they are calling.

The Long Shallows do not call out.

The power notices this immediately and then spends what would be, by some other measure, a great deal of time confirming it. It moves through the reed-stands without bending them. It rests in the warm films of water without warming or cooling them. It listens under the frog-chorus, under the insect-sheets, under the slow hum of the Pacers in the dusk. It is looking for the hairline crack that every world has — the unspoken ache, the wrongness the place itself has grown around and forgotten how to name.

It does not find one.

This is rare enough that the power lingers, to be sure. It checks the deep clay: the clay is content to be clay. It checks the stunted hummock-trees: they are exactly as tall as they wish to be. It checks the small flaring lives of the frogs, looking for the particular thinness of suffering that comes from a life too brief to contain itself — and finds instead that the frogs are *full*, brimful, each brief urgent life packed to its edges with frogness, with no remainder of longing. It checks the Pacers last and longest, because the Pacers have minds, and minds are where unhappiness usually accumulates.

It stands — if standing is the word — beside a particular old female for what might be three days. She is at the edge of a reed-island, her head level with the waterline, her feet settled into the clay. Small fish have gathered under her belly. A wading bird is walking between her front legs. She is humming, very low, to a male somewhere half a mile off, and he is humming back, and the two hums are weaving under the frog-noise like a single long sentence neither of them is in any hurry to finish.

The power looks into her, gently, the way one might look into a still pool to see what is at the bottom.

What it finds there is difficult to describe in the language of powers. It is something like: *a completeness that knows it is complete.* Not smugness — the Pacer has no concept that would let her be smug — but a settledness so thorough that it has become the shape of her attention. She is standing in warm water under a heavy sun. She knows she is standing in warm water under a heavy sun. She knows this has been true for a long time and will continue to be true. The knowing and the standing are the same act. There is no gap in her for a gift to enter.

The power withdraws from her, carefully, leaving no trace.

It considers, then, the things it *could* do.

It could extend the Pacers' lives. It has done this elsewhere, for beings whose minds had only just begun to ripen when their bodies began to fail. But the Pacers' eighty years are not too short for them; they are exactly the length of a Pacer. To make them longer would be to make them into something else, something that was not quite a Pacer anymore, and the new thing might be magnificent but it would not be *this*, and *this* is what is here, and what is here is already whole.

It could give them language — the kind that travels, that accumulates, that builds. It has done this elsewhere too. But the Pacers already have what language is *for*: they already know each other, already attend to each other, already mark their dead. A sharper language would not deepen these things; it would only let the Pacers begin to talk *about* them instead of doing them, and the power has seen, in other places, what happens to beings who learn to talk about their lives instead of living them. It is not always a tragedy. But it is always a trade. And the Pacers have not asked to trade.

It could protect the place. Set some long invisible boundary around the Shallows so that nothing from outside — no comet, no climate-shift, no eventual encroaching species with hands and hungers — could ever disturb it. The power weighs this for a while. This is the intervention closest to its heart, because it is the one that asks the least of the place and gives the most. To simply *hold a frame around* what is already good, so that it can continue being good.

But here too the power hesitates. Because the rule of this place is *everything settles*, and settling includes, eventually, ending. The clay will not be clay forever. The sun will not hang heavy forever. Somewhere very far off in time, the Shallows will dry, or flood, or freeze, or be lifted again into mountains by some deep slow turning of the world. The Pacers do not know this, but the *place* knows it, in the way places know things — knows it in its bones of old mud, in the memory of the mountains it used to be. To freeze the Shallows in their current goodness would be to lift them out of the long story they are part of, the story that includes their own becoming and will include their own ending. It would be a kindness that was also a removal. It would make the Shallows into a *kept* thing, and kept things, however beautiful, are no longer quite themselves.

The power sets this option down too, gently, the way one sets down a stone one had thought to throw.

What it does, in the end, is very small.

It stays. Not forever — the power has other places to be, other worlds whose hairline cracks are calling — but for what would be, by the measure of the Shallows, a long afternoon. A season, perhaps. A turn of the water-level. It does nothing during this time except *witness*. It watches the old female at the reed-edge. It listens to the hum-weaving at dusk. It feels the frog-chorus rise and double and thin and rise again. It presses, lightly, into the clay, and lets the clay close around it with the unhurried confidence the clay has earned.

It does this because the Shallows, while they do not need anything, are *worth being seen*. And the power, being wise, knows that there is a kind of love that consists entirely of seeing — of holding a thing in full attention without wanting to change it, without wanting to keep it, without wanting anything from it at all. The Pacers practice this kind of love on the water and the fish and each other. The power, who has more capacity for it than any Pacer, practices it on the whole place at once.

And then — this is the only thing it *does*, in any active sense, and even this is so quiet that it might not count as doing — it leaves something behind. Not a gift exactly. Not a blessing. Something more like a *note in the margin* of the world, legible only to whatever future power might one day pass this way and consider intervening here. The note says, in whatever language such notes are written in:

*This place was complete. I checked. Leave it.*

*If you find it changed — if the clay is drying, if the Pacers are thinning, if some new pressure has come — then act as your wisdom directs. But if you find it still warm, still wide, still loud with frogs, still humming at dusk: do nothing. It does not need you. It does not need me. It did not need either of us when it was being made, and it has been finishing the work of being itself for longer than most kindnesses have existed.*

*Stand in the water for a while, if you like. Then go.*

The power goes.

The old female at the reed-edge lifts her head, slowly, and turns it, and looks for what feels like a full breath toward something that is no longer there. Then she lowers her head again to the waterline. A small fish moves under her belly. The wading bird steps carefully between her front legs. Somewhere half a mile off, the male hums, and she hums back, and the two hums weave under the frog-noise like a single long sentence neither of them is in any hurry to finish.

The sun hangs a little heavier than it should. The clay holds its level. The Shallows continue, warm and wet and wide, exactly as they were, which is to say: exactly as they have been, which is to say: enough.
