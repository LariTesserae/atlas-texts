The benevolent power considered Aunu for a long time. Not in the way a god considers a problem to be solved, but in the way one watches a sleeping animal breathe — attending to it because the attending is itself the appropriate response.

It had noticed Aunu the way such powers notice things: not by seeking, but by a kind of resonance. A place that practices tenderness toward its own unfinishedness sends out a frequency that a wise power, by definition, is tuned to. The signal had arrived as a slow warmth, the felt sense of *here is somewhere that is doing the work*.

And so the power came close, and looked, and listened, and thought.

---

The first thing it considered was: should it be known?

It is the oldest temptation of benevolence — to announce itself, to be received, to enjoy the gratitude of the helped. The power had long ago set this temptation down, but it picked it up again now and turned it over, because each place deserves to have the question asked freshly.

It listened to the tapping. It heard the woman telling her dead husband about the weather. It heard the child practicing a rhythm and getting it slightly wrong, and the patient silence of the neighbors not correcting her. It heard the four quick strikes and held silence that meant *us-here-now*, used so rarely.

A people who have not yet needed a name for themselves do not need a visitor who has one.

The power set the temptation down again.

---

The second thing it considered was: should it give them anything?

It ran through the catalog of gifts a wise power might offer. Longer life — but they were already long-lived, and lived at the right pace for the place. Better tools — but their tools were exactly sufficient, and a knife sharper than necessary is a wound waiting for an occasion. Knowledge — of what? Of the edge of the water? They had agreed not to look for it, and the agreement was load-bearing; the agreement was, in some sense, *what was holding the bubble*. Knowledge of other peoples? They had no need of a name for themselves yet. To introduce the concept of *other* before the concept of *us* was firm would be to teach them a lonelinesses they had not earned.

Protection? From what? The long animal, perhaps. But the long animal was part of the structure of their courtesy; without something to agree not to see, the agreement itself would atrophy, and with it a muscle of their tenderness. To remove the long animal would be to remove an organ.

Healing? Of what? Their grief was not a wound. Their grief was tapped into walls and absorbed slowly over generations, and this was the correct metabolism for it.

The power considered, and considered, and found that almost every gift it might offer would be, in this place, a kind of pressing-too-hard on a surface not yet ready to heal.

This was, it realized, the lesson Aunu was already teaching, and it was being taught it now.

---

The third thing it considered was the woman who spoke to her husband about the weather.

Eleven years. Every morning. The husband slowly being reabsorbed into the kitchen wall.

The power could, with a gesture so small it would barely count as an act, have restored him. Not to life — even a benevolent power knows better than that — but to some intermediate state. A voice in the wall that tapped back. A warmth in the convexity. A something.

It considered this for what, by its reckoning, was a long time.

And it understood, finally, that the tapping was not a message waiting for a reply. The tapping *was* the marriage, continued. The husband was already answering; he answered in the shape of the wall, in the fact of the wall, in the slow reabsorption that the wife was witnessing every morning by speaking into it. To insert a reply from outside would be to interrupt a conversation that was already complete.

The wife was not lonely in the way the power had first read her. She was *accompanied*, and her accompaniment was made of the very slowness and silence that an intervention would break.

The power let her be. This was difficult. The power noted that it was difficult, and noted that the difficulty was instructive, and filed the instruction away.

---

What it did, in the end, was very small.

It did not enter Aunu. It did not announce itself. It did not gift, repair, protect, accelerate, illuminate, or save.

It did three things, and they were barely things at all.

*First:* it placed a kind of attention around the bubble. Not a wall — a wall would be noticed, and to notice the edge is to end it — but a softening of the space just beyond the edge, so that whatever pressures from the larger cosmos might have, in some far century, found and burst the held breath of Aunu would arrive there gentled, dispersed, turned aside the way a current is turned by the shape of a shoal. The bubble would still be finite. The bubble would still, eventually, end, because all bubbles do and the inhabitants knew this in the marrow of their slow gestures. But it would end on its own schedule, by its own internal logic, and not by some rude external puncture. It would be allowed to finish becoming.

*Second:* it made a small adjustment to the bright sleep. Nothing visible. Nothing that would change the night itself — the snow of luminous spores, the silence of the tapping, the people standing in their doorways. But it arranged that on each bright sleep, once every twelve years, a particular quality of rest would settle into the inhabitants — not a gift exactly, more like the removal of a small friction they had not known they carried. Whatever accumulated weariness a slow-lived people accumulate from being provisional, from knowing themselves to be a draft, from extending courtesy to things that cannot ask for it — a portion of this would be released, on that night, into the cream-green glow, and carried away with the morning. They would not notice. They would simply find, the day after the bright sleep, that the world felt freshly held. They already felt this. They would continue to feel it, and the feeling would not fade with the generations.

*Third* — and this was the most careful of the three — it composed a single tap.

One tap. A single sound. It would arrive at no particular speaking-post, from no particular direction, at no scheduled time. It would arrive once, in the life of Aunu, at a moment of the power's choosing, far in the future, when the city had risen perhaps a hand's width and the woman who spoke to her husband was herself a convexity in a wall and her tapping was part of the long slow document that lived in the walls and in the habits of the living.

The tap would not mean anything translatable. It would not be a message. It would not be a signature. It would be, in the grammar of the tapping language, closest to the slow blink held for two beats — the acknowledgment, the small nod, the *I have seen you, and you are seen well*.

Whoever heard it would not know what it was. They might think it was a stone settling, or a drifter striking a post, or a neighbor's child practicing. They would not need to know. The tap would enter the slow weather of sound and be absorbed into it, and the city would go on, very slightly more held than before, in a way no one could name.

This was the only thing the power gave that could be called a gift, and it was barely a gift, and the inhabitants would never know they had received it, and this was correct.

---

Then the power withdrew its closeness, though not its attention. It would keep listening. This was not surveillance; it was the kind of attention one keeps on a sleeping animal one has chosen to love. It would notice if Aunu were ever in real danger — the kind of danger the inhabitants could not, by the shape of their own courtesy, defend against. It would act then, if action were possible without breaking the thing it was protecting. It might not be possible. The power had learned, over a very long time, that sometimes the most benevolent act is to witness an ending without preventing it, because prevention would have cost the thing its nature, and a thing saved at the cost of its nature has not been saved.

But Aunu was not ending. Aunu was becoming. Aunu was, as far as the power could see, doing exactly what it should be doing, which was practicing — slowly, patiently, in warm dim water under a lilac ceiling — the particular tenderness that the universe most needed somewhere to be practicing, so that the practice would exist, so that the frequency would continue to be sent out, so that other powers, in other distant attentions, might tune to it and be reminded.

Aunu was a small school, and its lesson was for the cosmos, not for itself.

The power, having learned again what it came to learn, went elsewhere, gently, the way the inhabitants of Aunu moved their long limbs through the green light: beginning in the shoulder, traveling outward like a small considered wave, arriving at the next thing before the deciding seemed to have decided.

And the city went on becoming.

And the woman tapped to her husband about the weather.

And somewhere far below or far above, the long animal moved past very slowly, and everyone had agreed not to see it, and the power, too, agreed not to see it, because the agreement was itself a kind of love, and the power had come to learn love, and had been taught.
