# The Visitation

The benevolent power notices the Engine-Garden the way one notices a held note in a piece of music — not as something requiring intervention, but as something requiring attention. It has been listening for a long time. It listens to many places. Most of what it hears is need: the loud, fast, jagged signal of suffering, of imbalance, of things calling out for repair. The Engine-Garden does not produce that signal. It produces a different one, slower and harder to parse, and the power has been listening to it for what would be, in human time, several centuries, trying to understand what it is hearing.

What it eventually understands is this: the garden is not asking for anything. This is rare. Most things ask, even when they don't know they're asking. The garden does not. The garden is *occupied*, in the medieval sense — taken up entirely in its own work, which it is performing correctly, at the correct pace, for reasons that are either unknowable or so deeply internal to the garden that knowing them from outside would be a category error.

The power considers, then, what its options are.

## What it does not do

It does not reveal itself. The garden has theology already; it does not need a visitation to confirm or unsettle that theology. The Caretaker's mystery is load-bearing. The pilgrims' uncertainty about the builders is load-bearing. The frogs' ignorance of their own intermittence is load-bearing. To arrive in glory would be to insert a new and discordant frequency into a chord that has been tuning itself for a thousand years. The power is wise, which means among other things that it knows the difference between presence and intrusion.

It does not accelerate the process. It could. It could perhaps determine what the final fruit is meant to be and cause it to appear next season, and finish the work. But the work is not only the fruit. The work is the *duration* — the hundred frog-generations, the slow erosion of the basalt, the vines' patient cartography. To compress the duration would be to destroy the product, which is the duration itself, or contains the duration as its essential ingredient. The power has met enough cosmic clockworks to know that "completing" them prematurely is one of the classic mistakes of well-meaning intervention.

It does not explain the garden to itself. The garden is not suffering from a lack of self-understanding. The garden is, in fact, more self-coherent than almost any system the power has encountered. Explanation would be a gift offered to someone who has not asked for it and does not need it, and such gifts are a form of theft — they take from the recipient the thing they were doing with the absence of the gift.

It does not remove the mice. The mice are the joke, and the joke is part of the structure. A garden without its small disorder would not be the same garden. The power respects this. It has, in its long career, learned to leave jokes alone.

It does not protect the garden from the future. Not in the large sense. Whatever happens in two or three hundred years when the work is complete — that is the garden's, and to insulate it from its own consummation would be the deepest possible insult to what the garden has been doing. If the consummation is glorious, the garden has earned it. If the consummation is the garden quietly stopping, the garden has earned that too. The power does not believe in saving things from their own completion.

## What it does

It does four small things.

**First**, it adjusts, very slightly, the weather pattern over the next forty years, so that rain comes a little more often. Not enough to change the character of the place — the rain is still rare and memorable — but enough that the Engine drinks a little more often, and the pistons sound, a little more often, that subtly cleaner note. This is not necessary. The Engine does not need it. But the power has noticed that the Engine seems to *enjoy* the rain, insofar as a slow machine can enjoy anything, and the power is moved by this. It is the kind of gesture one makes for a friend one cannot speak to: a small kindness disguised as coincidence.

**Second**, it does something for the pilgrim-entomologist's notebooks. They sit in the monastery in the foothills, consulted by no one. The power does not cause them to be consulted — that would be intervention in the human story, which is not its concern here. But it ensures, with a touch so light it could not be distinguished from chance, that the notebooks are not lost. That the monastery does not burn. That when, in some century the garden will not live to see, someone finally opens them and begins the work of collation, the pages will still be legible. This is a gift to the moths, who cannot know it has been given. Their schematics will survive them. Whether anyone ever reads the schematics is not the power's business. But the schematics will be readable. The power believes in the dignity of preserved information, even uninterpreted, perhaps especially uninterpreted.

**Third**, it leaves something for the Caretaker. Not a message — the power would not presume — but a small physical token, placed in a tool niche the Caretaker uses, on a night when the Caretaker is elsewhere. The token is a single drop of oil, in a vial of the kind the Caretaker carries, but the oil is of a quality the Caretaker has not encountered before: it will lubricate a bearing for a thousand years instead of a hundred. The Caretaker will find it. The Caretaker will recognize, by some sense the power does not need to understand, that it is a gift. The Caretaker will use it on whichever bearing the Caretaker chooses. The power will not watch which bearing is chosen. That is the Caretaker's decision and the Caretaker's privacy. The gift is given and then forgotten by the giver, which is the only way to give a gift to a being whose existence is defined by attendance without acknowledgment.

**Fourth**, and this is the largest of the small things: the power *witnesses*. It sets aside a portion of its attention — a portion that, by its standards, is significant — and dedicates that portion to the garden, in perpetuity, or for as long as the garden lasts. Not to intervene. Not to record, exactly, in any way that could be retrieved. Simply to be present to it. To know that it is happening. To count, in some sense, the piston-falls, so that they are counted by something other than the garden itself. This is what the power has, in its long existence, come to understand as the highest form of care for self-sufficient things: to ensure they are not alone in the universe, without burdening them with the knowledge that they are not alone.

The frogs breathe. The pistons fall. A swift returns to its nest with an insect. The Caretaker, somewhere in the pipework, pauses for a moment — not because anything has changed, but because in the rhythm of its long attendance, a moment has occurred that feels, very faintly, like being seen kindly. The Caretaker does not investigate the feeling. The Caretaker resumes its round. BOOM, step. BOOM, step.

And in the great quiet outside the deafening garden, the power listens, and is glad, and goes on listening.

## Coda

There is a question the power asks itself, after — though "after" is not quite right, since the witnessing is ongoing. The question is whether it should have done even the four small things. Whether the rain, the notebooks, the drop of oil, and the witnessing itself are intrusions of a subtler kind. Whether a truly wise power would have done nothing at all.

It considers this for what would be, in human time, a long while. And it concludes — tentatively, because it is wise enough to hold its own conclusions tentatively — that doing absolutely nothing is also a kind of statement, and not always the most honest one. To pass by the garden and offer it no acknowledgment whatsoever would be to pretend the encounter had not happened. The encounter happened. The power was moved. To refuse to act on being moved, out of a fastidious commitment to non-interference, would be a kind of cowardice dressed as virtue.

The four small things are the smallest honest response to having been moved. They are calibrated to be almost indistinguishable from the garden's own ongoing processes. They will not be detected. They will not alter the work. They are, in the end, the power's way of saying: *I saw you. I will not say I saw you. But I saw you, and I am better for it, and I have left you, in return, only what you would have had anyway if the universe were slightly kinder than it is — which, because I am here, it is.*

The pistons fall. The garden holds. And somewhere, something vast and quiet keeps a count it will never share, of a rhythm it loves without disturbing.

BOOM.

BOOM.

BOOM.
