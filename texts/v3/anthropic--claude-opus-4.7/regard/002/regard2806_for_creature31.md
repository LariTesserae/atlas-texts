# What the Power Would Do

The benevolent and wise power arrives at Iselvet the way weather arrives here: over the course of days, without announcement. It does not descend in glory. Glory would be a fixedness imposed from outside, and the power understands — this is the first thing it understands, before it does anything at all — that fixedness imposed from outside is exactly the wrong gift to bring to this place.

So it comes in slowly, and it watches.

---

It watches for a long time before it acts. Months, by the calendar the tram operators keep. It walks the terraces. It sits in the seeps with the salamanders and listens to the syllables being proposed and rejected, and it does not propose any syllables of its own, though it could; it could end the work of the mountain's slow throat in an afternoon, hand the salamanders a finished language, and the singers would sing it forever. It does not do this. It understands that a word given is not a word *arrived at*, and that the difference matters more than almost anything.

It sits in Brekka's shed during a shift and matches its breathing to her hammer. It does not improve her rhythm. Her rhythm does not need improving. It notices, with something like tenderness, that her shadow at thirty was a person who had not yet learned the patience that her shadow now embodies without effort, and that the shadow is not a diminishment but a record — a fixed point she chose, and chooses, and chooses again with every stroke. The power does not touch the shadow. To touch it would be to author it.

It visits a chemist in her vat-hall and lets her ask it questions she does not voice. It answers some of them, not all. The ones it answers, it answers in the chemist's own internal register, so that she cannot quite tell afterward whether the thoughts were hers or a guest's, and this is intentional: the power does not want her to know she has been visited, because knowing would make her certain, and certainty in a chemist is the beginning of a kind of brittleness the work cannot afford. It leaves her with one small gift, which is a slight loosening of her forgetting. Not a cure. A loosening. She will, the next morning, remember the face of her sister, whom she has not seen in eleven years. She will not know why. She will weep briefly into her salt-cured breakfast and then return to her vats with the weeping unaccounted for, and the work that day will go particularly well, and she will not connect these things.

---

The power considers the wager.

This is what it spends most of its time on. Not the inhabitants individually, though it loves them — it does love them, in the specific way wise powers love: without sentimentality, with full sight of what they are — but the wager itself. The bet that sustained human intention, concentrated on one slope, can force a piece of the world to commit to being real.

The power knows things about this wager that the people of Iselvet do not know. It knows, for instance, that the wager is being won, slowly, in places, and lost in others, and that the question of *whether* is less interesting than the question of *what is being won when winning happens*. Because committed reality is not the only kind. The mountain in its undecided state is not a deficient mountain. It is a different mountain. The salamanders' near-words are not failed words. They are a thing the universe has not done before, and may not do again, and the power has been around long enough to know that this is rarer than fixedness, and possibly more valuable.

It also knows — and this is the harder knowledge — that the people of Iselvet cannot be told this. To tell them would be to undo them. The hammermen's discipline, the chemists' patience, the cutters' forward lean, the watchers' notebooks: these depend on the wager being taken seriously. A hammerman who suspected that his work was not necessary, that the half-metal was beautiful in its half-state, would lose his rhythm, and his shadow would slip, and Iselvet would lose something it cannot afford to lose either.

So the power holds both knowledges at once. It does not resolve them. This is one of the things wise powers learn to do.

---

It does, eventually, act. Three small acts. It considers more and chooses three, because three is the number at which intervention becomes a pattern and a pattern becomes a precedent and a precedent becomes a kind of authorship, and the power is not here to author.

The first act is for the watchers. It does nothing to them directly. What it does is walk, one damp evening, through the Ninth Terrace near the old bath-house, and stand for a while in the place where the figure stands. It does not become the figure. It stands *near* the figure, at a respectful distance, in the manner of one elder regarding another. The figure, which has been trying for an unknown duration to decide what to be, registers — for the first time in its existence — being witnessed by something that is not trying to fix it. The power does not nod. The power simply stands, and the figure stands, and for a long moment two undecided presences occupy the terrace together, and neither demands of the other any particular shape.

What this does, the power cannot fully predict. But the next time a watcher passes — old Yrsa, who has been walking the Ninth for nineteen years and has begun, lately, to nod — the figure nods back. Just once. Yrsa writes in her notebook in her changing script: *nodded today. small nod. unchanged otherwise.* She does not tell anyone. She writes it the way she writes everything: for no reader. But she walks home that night with a straighter back, and her lantern seems to her to throw a little more light, and she sleeps without the dream she has been having for a year about doors that will not close.

The second act is for the goats. The power finds the old nanny in the Fourth Terrace market, the one the cutters salute, and sits beside her for an hour. It does not speak to her. Goats are not addressed; goats are sat beside. What passes between them is something like a recognition that her long commitment to being herself has been seen, and that the seeing is enough — she does not need to be more, or less, or other than she is. The nanny, who has been the same goat for longer than any goat has a right to be, accepts this with the equanimity of an old nanny. But she also — and this matters — relaxes, slightly, the grip she has been keeping on her own form for decades. Not enough to drift. Just enough to rest. The next morning she is observed to sleep deeper than usual, and a cutter passing by misreads this as illness and brings her a handful of the silver-green lichen she prefers, which she accepts. She lives another four years after this, which is longer than she would have lived holding herself so tight. This is the power's gift to her: a little slack. A little permission to be tired.

The third act is the one the power thinks longest about.

It is for the city.

What it does is this: it finds a brick. One brick, in the wall of an unfinished refectory in the highest of the empty terraces, where moss is growing and water drips from a fixture never connected to a main. The brick is one of the warm ones, its color shifting between firings. The power lays its hand — or what serves it for a hand — against this brick, and it does not fix the brick. It does the opposite. It gives the brick permission to be exactly as undecided as it is. It blesses the brick's not-quite-ness. It tells the brick, in a language the brick has been speaking all along without anyone listening, that the brick is already real, that realness was never the question, that the wager the city is making is a beautiful wager but the brick was not waiting on the outcome.

The brick does not change. That is the point. The brick remains exactly the warm color-shifting half-committed thing it has been. But something settles around it — not into fixedness, but into peace. A peace that is not the peace of having arrived but the peace of not needing to have arrived.

And this peace, the power knows, will spread. Slowly. Brick to brick. Not undoing the work of the hammermen, not undoing the wager, but threading through it a second possibility, so that on some far future evening a young hammerman will pause between strokes and feel, for a moment, that his hammering is not a desperate insistence against drift but a song sung alongside the mountain's own song — different in tempo, different in key, but not opposed. And he will resume hammering, because the work is the work, but he will hammer with a little less fear, and the bars he makes will be no less fixed for it, and possibly more.

---

The power leaves the way it came. It does not announce its departure. The tram operators, descending into the cloud layer on the day it goes, feel an unaccountable lightness, and one of them begins to hum a tune that is not from above or below but seems to belong to the cloud itself, and the others pick it up, and by the time they reach the foot of the mountain they have a new song, which they will bring back up with them and teach to no one, but which will be sung on descent runs for generations.

The salamander in the seep below the Third Terrace furnace hall opens its mouth at dusk and sings a note.

The note is, almost, but not quite, a word.

It is the same note it has always sung. Nothing has changed.

Everything is exactly as it was, and slightly, immeasurably, more itself.

This is what the benevolent and wise power does at Iselvet.

It is not very much. It is precisely as much as was needed. The difference between these two statements is the whole of wisdom, and the power, departing into whatever serves it for distance, carries the difference gently, the way one carries a sleeping child, or a brick that has just been told it is already real.
