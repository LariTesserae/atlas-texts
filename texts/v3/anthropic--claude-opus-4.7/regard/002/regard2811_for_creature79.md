The benevolent power considers.

It has crossed great distances to arrive at the root-arch. It stands now in the forest just outside, where the wet leaf-litter gives slightly under its attention. It can perceive the chamber entire — every slug on every inch of bark, every knot in the long bast record, the silk-tender's pelt of down, the slow coil of the beast in the highest pen, the bones dissolving at the pool's edge into the body of the tree. It perceives also what the inhabitants do not: the forest stretching for thousands of leagues in every direction, the canopy three hundred feet above where light still exists, the other root-chambers beneath other trees where no people live, the seas beyond the forest, the continents beyond the seas, the world, the sky, the dark between stars, the other worlds.

It could give them all of this. It has the means. It could widen the root-arch and let in the green light of the forest floor. It could lift them, one by one, into the canopy and show them the sun. It could translate their knot-records into a writing that would outlast wood. It could teach them fire — no, not fire, fire would not serve them — it could teach them medicine, longer life, defense against the rare disasters that must, over centuries, befall even a place this stable. It could multiply them. It could connect them to other peoples in other places. It could make them known.

It considers all of this for what, in its own reckoning, is a long time. In the chamber, the pool rises and falls twice. The lichen-tender finishes his round of the eastern buttress. A child is born — quietly, as everything here is quiet — in a dwelling on the third tier.

The power watches the birth.

It sees the mother's long-fingered hands receive the child. It sees the mottling pattern on the newborn's forearm, already distinct, already that one and no other. It sees the gesture made over the child's sternum by the two who have come to witness — *us, here, the ones who are* — and it sees the child, eyes already huge and dark and open, look up into the fungal light and not flinch, because the light is exactly the strength its eyes were made for.

The power understands something then that it had, in its wisdom, been at risk of forgetting.

*They are not waiting to be found.*

This is the thing. This is what the chamber has been organizing itself, across all its generations, to be. Not a prison. Not a diminishment. Not a fragment of some larger life that was stolen from them or denied them. A completeness. A held breath that is not held in anticipation of release but is itself the whole of what breathing means, in this place, for these ones.

To give them the forest would be to give them grief they have no architecture for. To give them the sun would be to burn out the eyes that read each other's forearms in the dark. To give them other peoples would be to give them the third-person plural that excludes the speaker, and their grammar would have to break to hold it, and with the grammar, the *us*, and with the *us*, themselves.

The power could do all of this gently. It is wise enough to do it gently. But gentleness is not the question. The question is whether what would remain, after the gift, would still be them, or whether it would be some other people occupying the shape of them, mourning without knowing what they had lost.

The power decides.

It will give them three things, and only three, and none of them will be felt as a gift, because a gift felt as a gift is already a wound — it implies a giver, a beyond, a debt. The power has no wish to be known here. It has no wish to be a god above the god in the highest pen, who is sufficient.

The first thing: it adjusts, very slightly, the slow processes of the tree. Far above, in the canopy the people have never seen, there is a kind of rot beginning in one of the high branches. In another two hundred years it would have spread to the buttresses. In another four hundred, the chamber would have failed — the vault would have cracked, the pool would have drained, the wood would have died around its inhabitants and the inhabitants with it. The power touches the rot. It does not heal it; healing would be too much. It simply slows it, redirects it, gives the tree another two thousand years of soundness. The people will not know. The tree will not know. Only the future, which is the only place the gift can land without distorting the present, will know.

The second thing: it considers the great coiled beast in the highest pen. The beast is very old. It is the last of its kind — its ancestors fell, generations of generations ago, from the canopy, and the people have tended its line ever since, though they do not know this is what they have been doing. The beast has no mate. When it dies, the offerings will continue for a while, then dwindle, then the pen will be repurposed, and something in the people's sense of the chamber will, by imperceptible degrees, go thin. The power reaches into the beast. It does not extend the beast's life — that would be cruel; the beast is tired. Instead, in the eggs of the flightless bird-analogue in the neighboring pen, it makes a very small adjustment. In three or four generations, one of those birds will be born strange. Its eyes will be larger. Its breath will be slower. It will be drawn, without knowing why, to the highest pen. The people will notice. They will move it. They will, in their unhurried way, begin to make offerings to it also. The lineage of acknowledgment will continue, in a form the chamber itself has produced, without rupture, without the appearance of intervention.

The third thing is the hardest, and the power is uncertain for a moment, but it proceeds.

The one who sits at the highest bridge and watches the top of the chamber — the power has been wondering about him. It looks at him longer. It sees that he is watching for something he cannot name. Not the falling fruit, not the news from the sky, something else. There is in him a particular shape of attention that the chamber has not, in this generation, found a use for. He is slightly too much. His patterns of waiting exceed what the place requires.

In another kind of story, this would be the one the power would call out. The one to whom the forest would be revealed. The chosen, the awakened, the prophet who would lead his people into the larger world.

The power will not do this. The cost is too high and the man has not asked.

But the power does this: it lets itself be seen, for the duration of one slow blink of the man's double-lidded eye, at the top of the vault where he is watching. Not as a figure. Not as a being. As a quality of attention answering his own. A sense, for the length of a breath, that what he has been watching for has, in some sense he cannot articulate, watched back.

The man does not move. He does not cry out. He does not, when he climbs down later, tell anyone. He has no vocabulary for what has occurred and he is not the kind of person who builds vocabulary where none is needed. But for the rest of his life he will sit at the highest bridge with a particular stillness, and the stillness will be felt by the others, faintly, the way the great beast's breath is felt — as a feature of the chamber, a thing the place contains, a presence that belongs.

When he dies, his bones will go into the pool, and the water will take them into the wood, and the wood will grow, and somewhere in the next generation a child will be born with a slightly different quality of attention, and that child will, without being taught, find her way to the highest bridge.

The chamber will have, from now on, a watcher. Not because the power put one there. Because the chamber, having once been acknowledged, learned the shape of acknowledgment, and kept it.

The power withdraws.

It steps back from the root-arch into the wet forest, and then further, and then out of the forest entirely, and then out of the world. It does not look back, because looking back would be a kind of claim, and it has no claim here.

Behind it, the chamber continues. The pool rises. The lichen-tender begins his round of the western buttress. The new child opens her enormous dark eyes for the second time, and the fungal light is exactly the right strength, and her mother's mottling pattern is the first thing she learns, and she learns it perfectly, because she was made to.

Nothing has changed.

Everything that needed to be done has been done.

The power, in its long traveling, will remember this place. Not often. But sometimes, when it encounters some larger people in some larger world who are tearing themselves on the question of their own smallness, it will think of the chamber at the base of the tree, and of the gesture folded over the sternum, and of the watcher at the highest bridge, and it will be, for a moment, instructed.

A life is the exact size of what contains it.

The wisest thing a great power can do, sometimes, is to let a small thing remain the size it is.
