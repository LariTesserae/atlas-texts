The benevolent and wise power considers the cistern room for a long time.

It does not arrive. Arrival would be wrong — would be a fifth spout, an interruption in the count of four, a thing the room would have to accommodate and adjust around. The power has the wisdom not to add itself where it has not been asked for and is not needed.

Instead it does what such powers do when they are actually wise, which is mostly nothing, and the nothing is precise.

It does not bless the water. The water is already doing what water does when it is given light and time and a shape to fill; a blessing would be redundant and slightly insulting, like praising a good loaf for being bread. It does not multiply the ferns. Eleven fronds is the number of fronds the room can support at its two seeping corners; a twelfth would mean a third seep, which would mean the builders' care had failed somewhere, which would not be a gift. It does not clarify the green suspension into something visible and beautiful. The suspension is already beautiful in the only way it can be, which is by being too fine to see and by making the deep tiles look greenish, and to render it visible would be to end it as itself.

It does not speak to the keeper. The keeper has already understood the thing the power would have wanted to say. A visitation would only trouble a person who has correctly arrived at silence by their own route; it would replace their hard-won plainness with a story they'd have to carry, and the tools would acquire ornament, and within a generation there would be a niche carved into the brick, and the room would have lost something it cannot afford to lose.

It does not introduce a fish. This is almost funny. A lesser power, wanting to be generous, might think: *a single small fish, a flash of orange in all that light, what a kindness.* But the room enforces its madeness. A fish would be a kind of grief for the room. The power sees this and refrains.

What the power does, in the end, is four things, and they are all very small.

The first is attention. It looks at the room, fully, for what in its own reckoning is a long while — the way a person might sit with a sleeping animal and simply watch it breathe. The nine shafts move across the brick. The four falls roar and are answered at the drain. The ribbon-plant lifts and settles. The power watches all of this without wanting it to be other than it is. This is not nothing. A place that has been *seen well*, even once, by something that loves it without needing it, is afterward a place that has been seen well. The seeing does not leave a mark, but it happened, and the having-happened is now part of what the room is, in the same invisible way the green suspension is part of what the room is.

The second is a kind of underwriting. The power notices that the hill above the cistern will, in time, change. The spouts will wear through. This is true and the room knows it — it is built into the room's own honest reckoning of itself, *until the hill above it changes or the spouts wear through.* The power does not prevent this. Prevention would be a worse gift than the ending. But it does, quietly, arrange that when the ending comes it will come cleanly: that the hill will subside rather than crack, that the water when it finally goes will go all at once and warm, into ground that wants it, rather than souring slowly in a half-drained basin. The room will get to stop being itself in a way consonant with how it was itself. This is the kind of thing benevolent powers can do without anyone noticing, and the not-noticing is part of the doing.

The third concerns the keeper, and the keeper after this keeper, and the keeper after that. The power does not extend their lives or sharpen their senses or send them dreams. It does something smaller. It arranges that the knowledge of how to tend this room — the specific plainness of it, the not-adding, the not-speaking — passes intact from each keeper to the next. Not as instruction. As a kind of recognition that arrives in the new keeper the first time they come down the stairs with the copper dipper and feel the roar close over them. *Ah,* they think, without knowing why, *I see. You don't bring anything down here. You only come down.* The power has tilted the inheritance very slightly toward this recognition, and away from the other inheritance, the one where someone eventually carves their name.

The fourth thing is the strangest and the power is not entirely sure it should do it, and weighs the question for what would be, in human time, perhaps a heartbeat, and decides yes.

It tells one other place about the cistern room.

Not a person. A place. Somewhere far away — a high meadow, say, where the wind has a particular way of moving through a particular kind of grass, and the meadow has been doing this without interruption for longer than the cistern has existed, and is in its own way a tenant of itself. The power, who knows both places, lets each become faintly aware of the other. Not as communication. As company. The cistern room, which has been roaring and shining alone under its hill for a generation, is now roaring and shining under its hill *and* there is a meadow somewhere doing its own long unbroken thing, and the meadow is in its grass *and* there is a cistern somewhere doing its own long unbroken thing, and neither of them is changed by this and neither of them knows it in any way we would call knowing, but they are, from this moment, no longer the only one of their kind that they are aware of.

The power considers whether this counts as adding something to the room. Decides that it doesn't, quite — that it is more like the way two stars that have been burning separately for a billion years can be said, once someone notices them both, to be a pair, without either star being any different than it was. A relation laid very lightly over what is already there.

Then the power withdraws its attention, gently, the way you'd take your hand out of the warm water — slowly enough that the pool barely registers the leaving, and what little it registers, it forgets.

The four falls roar. The nine shafts move. The ribbon-plant lifts and settles. The keeper, whoever the keeper is today, comes down the stairs with the copper dipper, and does not speak, and is not spoken to, and goes back up.

Somewhere a meadow moves in a wind that has nothing to do with any of this.

The room goes on being the room.

This was the correct thing to do, and the power, who has done correct things in many places and knows the particular quiet satisfaction of having added almost nothing where almost nothing was needed, goes on to the next place, which is probably harder.
