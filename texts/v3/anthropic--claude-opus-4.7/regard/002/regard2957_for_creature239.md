The benevolent and wise power considers the shelf for a long time.

It has the capacity to do a great many things. It could quicken the place — seed the basins with darting fish, the air with birdsong, the cliffs with the small industrious lives that ordinarily attend such water. It could grant the people children, ambition, music with many verses, the forward motion that the rest of the world mistakes for the basic shape of existence. It could reveal itself, and offer them passage to wider lives, and most powers of its kind, in most stories, would do exactly this — would arrive in glory, would name the stillness a sickness, would call the people forth.

It does not do these things.

It watches instead. It watches a woman carry reeds across a courtyard and set them down completely. It watches a child crouch at the edge of a basin and look into the water for the better part of an hour, and it watches what the child sees, which is not nothing. It listens to two people in a doorway exchange six sentences in an afternoon and finds that the six sentences are, in fact, the correct six sentences; that a seventh would have been a small violence; that the silences between them are not absences but a kind of slow shared attention to the fact of being two, here, now, in the warm damp air.

The power has seen many places. It has seen the great cities with their songs and their sorrows, and it loves them. It has seen the long courageous projects of beings who refuse to be still, and it loves those too. It is not a power that prefers one shape of life over another. It came here prepared to find a wound — the description of the place invites this, as the text itself admits — and it has looked carefully, in the way only such a power can look, beneath the quiet of each person on the shelf, and it has not found the wound. There is no suppressed grief. There is no forfeited knowledge crying out to be retrieved. There is a life. It is small and it is whole.

Still, the power is wise, and wisdom is not the same as approval, and benevolence is not the same as leaving alone. It thinks for a while longer.

It notices, eventually, three things.

The first is the traveler who arrives and stays a day and leaves uneasy. The power follows one such traveler down the switchbacks in its mind, and sees the long afterward — the years in which that person's work seems slightly less urgent, the small quiet ache they cannot name, the way they sometimes stop in the middle of a crowded street and listen for something that is not there. The shelf has given them a wound the shelf itself does not carry. This seems worth attending to.

The second is the traveler who arrives and stays. The power examines the absorption carefully. It wants to know whether the slow shedding — speaking less, moving more slowly, becoming another one of them — is a true finding or a kind of drowning. It looks at many such absorbed travelers, across the long holding of the town, and finds, mostly, that it is a finding. These were people already shaped for the shelf, who had been carrying the shelf inside them without a place to set it down. But not all. There are a few, here and there across the centuries, who settled because settling was easier than continuing, and who are not unhappy now, but who might have been something else, somewhere else, and were not given the chance to know it. This also seems worth attending to.

The third is the deepest, and takes the power the longest to see. It is this: the shelf does not change, and the people do not change, and this is the condition of their peace — but *the world below* changes. The young rock will not stay young forever. Two thousand meters down and three valleys over, histories are moving. There will come a century, eventually, in which someone with maps and intentions will look up at the shelf and decide that the stillness is an affront, or a resource, or an opportunity. The town has no defense against this. It has no defense against anything. Its peace is not armored; it is simply, so far, unnoticed by the kinds of forces that would end it. The power can see, from where it stands, that the unnoticing will not last indefinitely.

So the power does these things, quietly, and without revealing itself:

It does not touch the shelf. It does not add a single bird. It does not quicken the rate of the people, or alter the rock, or speak into any doorway. The life that is there is the life that is there, and the power honors it by leaving it exactly as it is. This is the largest of its gifts and the one that costs it the most, because powers of its kind are made to act, and restraint is a discipline even for the wise.

For the travelers who arrive and leave uneasy, it does a small thing. It lets the ache settle, instead of festering. It does not remove the memory of the shelf — the memory is, in its way, a true thing, and worth keeping — but it loosens the hook. The traveler will remember the wet stone and the quiet, and will think of it sometimes with a feeling that is almost but not quite longing, and will be able to set the feeling down again and return to their crowded life without the small permanent diminishment the shelf would otherwise have left in them. They will carry the shelf as a knowledge, not as a wound. *There is a place where people live like that. It is good that there is such a place. I do not live there. This is also good.*

For the travelers who arrive and stay, it does a smaller thing, and a more careful one. It does not interfere with the absorption of those who are finding their true shape. But for the few — the ones who are settling because settling is easier — it arranges, very gently, for something to interrupt the settling before it completes. A dream, perhaps, vivid enough to be remembered. A letter that finds them, improbably, carried up the switchbacks by some traveler who took a wrong turn. A morning in which the light on the wet stone looks, for just a moment, unbearable rather than beautiful, and they understand that they must go. The power does not choose for them. It only ensures that the choice remains a choice, for as long as it can still be made. Those who, given the dream or the letter or the unbearable morning, choose the shelf anyway — the power lets them stay, and blesses the staying, and does not interfere again.

And for the deepest matter — the century that is coming, the maps and intentions — the power does the largest of its quiet workings. It does not build a wall around the shelf. Walls are noticed, and what is noticed is eventually breached. Instead, it bends, very slightly, the attention of the world. Not so much that anyone would feel coerced; only enough that the maps, when they are drawn, tend to be slightly inaccurate around the shelf, and the expeditions, when they are mounted, tend to be diverted by some small more pressing matter, and the satellites, in their time, tend to register the place as one more uninhabited stretch of difficult terrain. This is not a permanent protection. The power knows that nothing is permanent, and would not want to make the shelf the one exception, because exception is its own kind of violence. But it buys time. It buys, perhaps, as much time as the young rock has left before it begins to round. It buys the town its natural span, instead of a span cut short by forces it was never built to withstand.

When all this is done — and it takes the power no time at all, and also a long, attentive consideration — it lingers a moment longer above the shelf. It is permitted, being what it is, a kind of farewell that the people will not perceive.

It looks at the woman with the reeds. It looks at the child by the basin. It looks at the two in the doorway, and at the absorbed traveler who is now simply another one of them, sitting with a clay cup of cold water in their hands, watching the afternoon light move across the wet enamel of the cliff. It looks at the sluices in their long-held positions and the channels running at ankle height and the cisterns full on every roof.

It thinks: *this is one of the shapes a life can take. It is not the shape I would have predicted, and it is not the shape most lives take, and it is not better or worse than the others. It is itself. It was made by no one and is maintained by no one and yet here it is, holding, quietly, against the general tendency of things to become something else. Let it hold a little longer. Let it hold for as long as it can.*

Then the power withdraws, leaving nothing visible behind, and the shelf goes on as it has gone on: the cliffs weeping, the basins still, the people moving at the pace the shelf asks for, the silence complete except for the water-sounds and, very occasionally, a soft word in a doorway, quickly swallowed, exactly the right amount of speech for the afternoon in which it was spoken.

Somewhere far below, a traveler who has been climbing toward the shelf for three days stops on a switchback, looks up at the pale geometry of the town just becoming visible through the haze, and feels — without knowing why — that they are about to arrive at a place that has been waiting for them, or that they are about to arrive at a place they should turn back from, and that the difference between these two things is the most important thing they will ever know about themselves.

The power, already elsewhere, does not decide for them.

It only makes sure the question is theirs to answer.
