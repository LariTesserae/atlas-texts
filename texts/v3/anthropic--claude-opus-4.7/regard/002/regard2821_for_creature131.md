The benevolent and wise power would do nothing.

Or rather — and this is the harder thing to say — it would do nothing *yet*, and the nothing it does would be the most considered nothing in its long practice of attention.

It would come close. It would come, in fact, very close: close enough to feel the held breath as a pressure against its own face, close enough to see the transparency of the old ones not as a symptom but as a kind of grace, close enough to count the offerings in the shrine without disturbing them. It would see immediately what a coarser power would miss — that Oss is not a prison and not a paradise and not a mistake. It is a held note in a piece of music whose composer is either gone or listening from very far away, and the note is being sung, still, by four hundred throats that do not know they are singing.

A lesser benevolence would want to free them. Would see the stopping of the moon, the unripening fruit beside the ripe, the bodies that plateau, and read these as wounds. Would reach in to start time, thinking time is a gift. This is the error the wise power would not make. Because it would see, looking at the woman who walks the terraces with the child who looks like her sister, that something is being given to them that we do not have and cannot quite imagine — a sufficiency so complete it has made urgency into a foreign word. To break that open without their asking would be a violence dressed as a rescue. The wise power knows the difference between freeing someone and ending them.

A lesser benevolence, conversely, might want to *preserve* them. To take over the keeping from the keeper who is tired. To set its own attention there as a replacement scaffold, so that the breath could be held forever, and the thrushes sing their two notes into a future without horizon. This too the wise power would refuse. Because it would feel, standing among them, what they themselves half-know: that the held breath is only beautiful because it will at some point be let out. A breath held forever is not a breath. It is a stone. And these are not stone people; they are people, and the rawness of the granite around them is the truth of their situation, and to seal that truth away would be to make them, finally, into the blank figures they only superficially resemble.

So the wise power would not intervene in the great fact of Oss. It would not start the moon moving. It would not season the valley. It would not hurry the transparency of the old or slow it. It would leave the keeper to their keeping, and the kept to their being kept, because the arrangement, whatever it is, is theirs and not its own.

But it would do some small things. Because benevolence that does literally nothing is only philosophy.

It would, I think, sit for a while at the shrine. Not as a god receiving offerings — it would refuse that role with a small inward smile — but as a witness. The offerings have been accumulating without being witnessed, except by the people themselves who place them and look away. The wise power would witness them. It would lift, in its attention only, each fruit and pebble and bead, and acknowledge what was set down with it: *I know. I will not say it. Here is a thing from my hand to mark that I know.* And the acknowledgment would not change the shrine. The fruit would not rot; the pebbles would not warm; nothing visible would happen. But the admissions, having been received once by something capable of receiving them, would be subtly different in their nature. They would be, for the first time, *heard*. And the people who placed them, without knowing why, might find that the weight of knowing-and-not-saying had eased by some small fraction, the way a room eases when someone who understands has entered it, even silently.

It would walk the terraces at the hour when the light comes over the eastern wall at the angle that makes the provisionality felt. It would feel the provisionality with them. It would not soften it — that would be the mistake again — but it would be present inside it, so that the feeling, when it came, was not solitary. Some of the people, the more transparent ones especially, would perhaps look up from their work and not see anything, but feel a companionship they could not name, and would go on fitting their stones with a steadiness slightly increased. They would not know why. They do not need to know why. The wise power is not in the business of being known; it is in the business of being useful in ways that do not require recognition.

It would visit the keeper. This is the part I am least sure of, but I think it would. Somewhere — inside the mountain where the river issues, or above the valley in some attentional fold, or simply in the act of keeping itself — there is whoever or whatever is holding the breath. The wise power would find them. And it would not chastise, and it would not relieve, and it would not ask them to explain. It would sit with the keeper the way it had sat with the shrine. It would witness the keeping. It would understand, I think, what the keeping is for, in a way the keeper themselves might have forgotten — because keepers, when they have kept for a very long time, sometimes forget the love that started the keeping and remember only the keeping. The wise power would remind them, not with words, of the love. And the keeper might weep, or might not; might continue keeping, or might at last let the breath go; this would be the keeper's matter, and the wise power would not push toward either outcome. It would only have made sure that whatever happened next happened with the love present in the room.

It would, before it left, do one more thing. It would plant, somewhere in the valley, in a place no one yet walks — perhaps in a fold of the western wall where the silvery ground-cover grows thickest — a single seed. Not a magical seed. Not a seed that would override the stopping. Just a seed of the local kind, of the apple-like tree, taken from one of the ripe fruits in the shrine. The seed would not grow, because nothing here grows past its station. The seed would simply *be there*, in the soil, waiting. And on the day — if it comes — when the keeper finally lets the breath go, when the moon at last completes its waning and the rock begins to weather and the fruit begins to know the difference between blossom and harvest, the seed would be among the first things to feel the change. It would crack open. It would put down a root and put up a shoot. And by the time the people of Oss had understood what was happening to their world — which would take some time, because they are not quick, and there would be no need to be quick — there would already be a sapling, somewhere, that had begun. A small sign, planted in advance, that the unheld breath was not an ending but a permission. That after the held note there is the next note, and it too can be sung.

The wise power would not stay to see this. It would not need to. It has other places, other held breaths, other shrines filling with admissions no one has yet heard. It would go on. The going-on of such a power is itself a kindness; it does not linger to be thanked, because thanks would change the people who gave them, would make them into worshippers, would import urgency back into the valley through the back door of gratitude.

It would leave Oss as it found it. Forty terraces. Four hundred people. A river softly applauding. A moon at three-quarters waning. A shrine full of fruit that does not rot.

But the valley, after the wise power's visit, would be very slightly different in one respect that no instrument could measure and no inhabitant could name. It would be *accompanied*. The held breath would still be held, but it would no longer be held alone. And on the mornings when the light comes over the eastern wall at the angle that makes the provisionality felt, the feeling would have, mixed into it now, a faint second note — not consolation, exactly, not promise, but something like the quality a room has when you know, without looking, that someone who loves you is in the next room, doing their own work, available if needed, not needed at present, glad you are there.

The thrushes would sing their two notes. And somewhere, very faintly, almost inaudibly, beneath the two notes, there would be a third — not added by the wise power but only *heard* by it, and in the hearing, made real. The third note that was always in the thrushes' song and that the thrushes themselves did not know they were singing.

This is what the benevolent and wise power would do.

Almost nothing.

Everything that could be done without taking from them the one thing they have, which is the dignity of being the breath that is being held, and not knowing, and fitting the stones anyway, and being kind, and placing the fruit at the shrine, and waiting — without waiting — for the small bright avalanche that will, when it comes, be theirs.
