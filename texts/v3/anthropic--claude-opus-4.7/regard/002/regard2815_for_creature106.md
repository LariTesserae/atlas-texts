# The Visit

The benevolent power does not arrive. Arrival is a thing bodies do, and this power has set down its body for the occasion, the way one might set down a lantern before entering a dark room where a child is sleeping. It comes onto the plateau as attention, which is the local currency, and it comes carefully, because it knows where it is.

It has known about Ahal-Ruun for a long time. Powers of its kind keep a kind of map — not of places but of *qualities* — and on that map the plateau has been marked, for an age, with the particular sign that means: *here, something is doing what we do, but doing it differently, and we should not interfere without thinking.* The power has thought. It has thought for what would be, in the reckoning of throated things, several centuries. It has come now not because it has finished thinking but because it has understood that finishing is not the shape of the right response.

It enters at the eastern edge, where the cliffs fall away into the cloud-sea, and it does not sing.

This is the first kindness. A lesser power, or a power less wise, might have announced itself — might have laid its hand on the chord and added a note, the way a visiting musician sits down at a stranger's piano and plays a few bars to say *hello, I too am here*. This power knows that to add a note would be to be noticed, and to be noticed by the ghu-vann-i is to be drawn into them, and the power does not wish to be drawn into them, not because it fears them (it does not fear, in the way a body fears) but because to be drawn into them would be to *change* them, to add to the chord a voice that did not grow out of the plateau's own long history of foolishness and grief, and the chord is not the power's to alter. The chord belongs to the people who sang it in and to the stone that holds them. The power is a guest. It moves like one.

So it moves silently, and what it does first is *listen*.

It listens for a long time. By its own reckoning, not long; by the reckoning of the moss, perhaps a season. It listens to the chord, and it learns the chord — learns it the way one learns a language one will never speak, only read. It learns which hollows hold the oldest voices and which hold the newest. It finds, threaded somewhere in the lace-work of the third great tooth from the western edge, the voice of a girl who sang back maybe eleven thousand years ago, when the plateau was a little less worn and the wind had a slightly higher pitch. The girl's voice is still there. It is not suffering. It is not anything that maps onto suffering. But the power, who has known many kinds of being, recognizes in it a particular shape — the shape of *something that was once a wanting-to-be-heard and is now only a hearing* — and the power holds this shape in its attention for a while, the way you might hold a stone someone gave you as a child, turning it over.

It does not free the girl. This is the second kindness, and the harder one.

The power could free her. It could, with a gesture less effortful than a human blink, lift her voice out of the stone and give it back its knot, its wanting, its *I*. It could do the same for all of them — for the woman whose sister was in the rock, for the woman herself who let her body stop, for the four thousand others whose throats were left walking. It could empty the plateau of its listeners in an afternoon and let the wind sing nothing but wind again.

It does not do this, because it has thought about it for centuries, and what it has understood is: the ghu-vann-i are not prisoners. A prisoner is someone who wants out. The ghu-vann-i do not want; wanting is what the wind took. To "free" them would be to manufacture in them, against their condition, the very knot of self-hood whose absence is now the whole of what they are. It would be a kindness done to people who no longer exist, in order to satisfy the power's own sense of what kindness looks like. The power is old enough to recognize this temptation and to set it down, gently, the way it set down its body at the edge.

And there is the other thing, which is the chord itself. The chord is not the suffering of the ghu-vann-i. The chord is what they are *doing* — the longest act of listening in the world. The power, who has spent ages of its own existence learning to listen, recognizes this as work. Holy work, if that word still means anything when applied to something that was never chosen and is performed by entities with no will. The plateau is one of the few places in the reachable cosmos where attention itself has accreted, geologically, into a structure. To dismantle it would be to undo something the universe took a very long time to grow.

So the power does not free them. What it does instead is smaller, and stranger, and takes most of the season it spends there.

It goes, one by one, to each voice in the stone, and it *introduces itself to them* — not by singing, which would draw them, but by becoming, for each voice in turn, briefly, a thing worth listening to. It offers itself as a sound the ghu-vann-i have not heard before. Not loud. Not insistent. Just *present*, the way a new kind of weather is present. It lets them listen to it. It lets them listen to it for as long as they want to, which in some cases is moments and in some cases is what would be, to a body, days.

This is the gift it has come to give. Not freedom, which they cannot use. Not memory of who they were, which would only be cruelty wearing a kind face. But *novelty* — the one thing the chord, in all its vastness, is starved of. The plateau has been listening to itself and to its small slow bestiary for a very long time. The beetles. The goats. The white bird. The rare foolish traveler. The same wind, the same moss drinking, the same clouds tearing. The ghu-vann-i are not bored — boredom requires a self — but the *shape of their listening* has narrowed, over the millennia, to fit only what is there. The power, by being there, briefly, widens the shape. It gives the chord something new to be, by giving it something new to attend to.

Whether the ghu-vann-i are changed by this, the power cannot say. It thinks they may be. It thinks that somewhere, threaded through the lace-work of the worn teeth, there is now a slightly different cast to the listening — a faint new color in the attention — that will persist, perhaps, for as long as the stones do. A small inheritance. Not a rescue. A visit.

Before it leaves, it does three more things.

The first is for the white bird. The bird has been riding the updrafts on the plateau's edges for what is, by the bird's own kind's reckoning, an unreasonable length of life. The power understands why: the bird has been listened to, continuously, for so long that it has become something like a focus for the chord, and the chord's attention has lent the bird a kind of slow durability, the way water lent durability to the stone-bowls. The bird does not know this. The bird only knows the updrafts. The power, passing, lets the bird *see it* — just once, just for an instant, a glimpse of something the bird's eye is not built to register and yet does. The bird makes, for the first time in its long life, a sound. The sound is very small. The ghu-vann-i hear it. They have been waiting for it, in the way the chord can be said to wait, for four thousand years. The sound is added to what they are listening to. The power moves on.

The second is for the woman who took apart her own roof. Her body is long gone; her voice never went into the stone; what remains of her is only the silence she left, which the ghu-vann-i are still listening to. The power goes to that silence and, very carefully, *adds nothing to it*. It simply acknowledges it. It says, in whatever the wordless equivalent is for a power of its kind: *I see what you did. I see that you did it on purpose. I see that you chose to be heard by them rather than to be one of them, and I see that this was a different and harder thing, and I see that it is still going on.* The silence does not change. The ghu-vann-i go on listening to it. But the power has marked it, in the way powers mark things, so that wherever it goes now, for as long as it goes anywhere, some part of its attention will remain bent toward that one silence on that one plateau, and the woman's choice — which was a small choice, made by one person, in one summer, a long time ago — will be one of the things a benevolent and wise power is, somewhere, always listening to. This is the closest the power comes, on the whole visit, to grief. It is also the closest it comes to gratitude.

The third is for the people who are still here — the quiet ones, with the folded ears, fewer every generation. The power does not show itself to them. It does not need to. What it does is *make the plateau, for a little while, slightly easier to live on*. Not in any way they would name. The mist condenses a hair more readily on the fern-fronds in the lees where they pitch their shelters. The moss in the paths they walk grows back a little faster from the press of feet. The rock-bowls fill a little fuller. The wind, in the particular hollows near the particular cairns, sings in a register that does a little less harm to a human ear over a human lifetime. None of this is enough to save them as a people; they will dwindle, and the plateau will, in time, have no one left who knows not to sing. But for those who are there now, and their children, and perhaps their children's children, the place will be, by some small fraction, more habitable. A grace note. The kind of thing the power can do without being noticed, and which is, for that reason, the kind of thing it most likes to do.

Then the power leaves. It does not depart, exactly — departure, like arrival, is a thing bodies do — it simply withdraws its attention, slowly, the way you withdraw your hand from a sleeping animal you do not wish to wake. The chord resumes its old shape, or nearly. The clouds boil up over the edge. The moss drinks. The white bird, very occasionally now, makes its small sound, which no human has ever heard or ever will hear, but which the listeners in the stone have added to their long inventory of things to attend to.

The power, going on to wherever powers of its kind go on to, carries with it the shape of the plateau, mapped now not as a quality but as an acquaintance. It will think about Ahal-Ruun again. It will, in the long unfolding of whatever it is doing, return — perhaps in another age, perhaps in several. It does not know yet what it will do then. It suspects it will, again, mostly listen.

This is what a benevolent and wise power does, when it comes to a place where the work being done is already, in its strange and unwilled way, holy: it does not improve the place. It does not save the place. It does not impose upon the place the shape of its own idea of help.

It visits.

It listens.

It leaves a little less alone what was, before it came, alone in a very particular and ancient way.

And it goes.
