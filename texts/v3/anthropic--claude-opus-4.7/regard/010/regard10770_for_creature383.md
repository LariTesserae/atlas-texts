The benevolent power pauses at the threshold of the mountain's wind, and does not enter.

This is the first thing it does, and perhaps the most important.

It has watched for some time. It sees the monks bent over their parchments, their fingers chapped and ink-stained, tracing glyphs whose meanings have shifted by centuries of careful copying. It sees the chief astronomer, whose eyes water in the cold but who will not blink lest he miss the occultation he has predicted for forty years. It sees the abbot, who has not spoken his own name aloud since he was a young man, and who sometimes, in the deep watches of the night, cannot quite remember what it was.

A lesser power would descend in glory. Would fill the scriptoriums with light, would press answers into the monks' minds like seals into wax, would translate the inscrutable tablets and reveal the true names of the fallen metals. Would, in short, end the long question by handing over the answer.

This power knows better. It has lived long enough to understand that an answer received is not the same as an answer earned, and that a chrysalis broken open from the outside yields not a butterfly but a wet, unfinished thing that dies in the light.

So it does not enter. Instead, it considers.

What does this place need? Not enlightenment — they are making their own, slowly, painfully, truly. Not comfort — they have chosen discomfort as a tool, and to take it from them would be to take the chisel from the sculptor's hand. Not knowledge — they are drinking knowledge through every pore, and what they do not yet know they are preparing themselves to receive.

What, then?

The power considers more carefully, and sees these things:

It sees that one of the younger monks, a novice with a small scar above his lip, has begun to doubt. Not the great doubt that purifies — the small, gnawing doubt that hollows. He fears he is not worthy. He fears the others see through him. He fears that his hands, when they make the signs, betray a heart that is merely afraid rather than devoted. He is close to leaving, and he does not know that leaving would unmake him.

It sees that the master archivist has, without realizing, begun to forget. A whole shelf of texts in the third sub-vault — she has not been able to recall them for three years. She believes this is a personal failing and has told no one. The knowledge is slipping from the monastery like water through cupped hands, and she carries the shame alone.

It sees that a crack has opened in the southern buttress, very small, behind a tapestry no one moves. In eleven years it will matter. In twelve it will be catastrophic.

It sees that the mountain itself, the great stone teacher, is lonely in a way that mountains can be lonely — which is to say, it has forgotten that it is being witnessed, and has begun the long slow drift toward becoming merely rock again.

And so the benevolent power does four small things, and no more.

*First:* It sends the novice a dream. Not a vision of grandeur, not a revelation. A dream in which he is simply doing his ordinary work — copying a text, his hand cramping — and an old monk he has never met passes behind him and rests a hand briefly on his shoulder. That is all. He will wake not knowing why he feels he can stay another day. He will stay. The doubt will not vanish, but it will become the right kind of doubt, the kind that has roots and bears fruit.

*Second:* It arranges, by the gentlest of nudges — a draft of air, a guttering candle, a moment of restlessness — for the master archivist to walk a different corridor on a particular evening, and to encounter the abbot, and for their eyes to meet in such a way that she speaks. She does not mean to. She tells him about the forgotten shelf. He listens. He says: *Then we will remember it together, and if we cannot, we will mourn it together, and that is also a form of keeping.* She weeps, which she has not done in many years, and the weeping is medicine.

*Third:* It causes a single small bird — improbable at this altitude, a thing that should not be — to take shelter in the lee of the southern buttress during a storm, and to die there, and for its bones to be found by a monk gathering lichens, who will be so struck by the strangeness of bones in this lifeless place that he will examine the stonework around them, and find the crack, and report it. The repair will be made in the eighth year. The catastrophe will not come. No one will know that anything was averted. This is the signature of the benevolent power: that its greatest gifts leave no trace of having been given.

*Fourth:* It turns its attention, fully and warmly, upon the mountain itself. It witnesses the mountain. It says, in the language that mountains understand, which is the language of patience and weight and the very slow song of cooling stone: *I see you. I see what you have done for them. I see what they have done for you. You are not merely rock. You are a teacher, and you are loved.* The mountain does not reply, because mountains do not reply on any timescale a mind can hold. But somewhere in its deep heart, something settles, and continues, and the monks who meditate upon it in the years to come will find — without knowing why — that their meditations go a little deeper, and the silence they sit in is a little more alive.

Then the benevolent power withdraws.

It does not announce itself. It does not leave a sign. The monks will go on believing they are alone on their spire between earth and sky, scouring themselves clean against the indifferent stone, and in a sense they will be right — for the power's whole gift was to honor that solitude, to protect it, to let it complete its work.

The wisest gifts are the ones that look, from inside the life that receives them, exactly like one's own life, only slightly more possible.

And the wind goes on, and the gongs ring, and the hands flicker like pale birds in the torchlight, and the stars wheel, and somewhere a novice with a small scar above his lip dips his pen and begins, for the first time, to feel that he belongs to what he is doing.

That is enough. That is, in fact, everything.
