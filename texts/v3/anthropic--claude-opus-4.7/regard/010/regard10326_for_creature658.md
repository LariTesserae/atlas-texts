# The Visitation

The wise power does not arrive. Arrival would be the wrong verb, implying a before and after, a place from which and a place to. The wise power simply *becomes attentive* in the direction of Keth, and attention, when it belongs to such a being, is itself a kind of presence.

It considers first. This is what wisdom does before it does anything else.

It sees the grinding. It sees the clay-skinned ones standing in their patient erosion. It sees the deep chambers where new clay cures in the dark, learning by some slow osmosis the shape of the work it will inherit. It sees the void at the edges, never far, never closer than yesterday because yesterday's labor held it, and never farther either, because the work is exactly sufficient and no more.

The benevolent impulse rises first, as it always does in such beings, and it is the impulse one would expect:

*I could end this.*

It could call water from beneath the clay—there is water there, deep, ancient, sleeping in stone. It could soften the sun. It could thicken the air until the stems drank their fill and unfurled into something lush, something that did not need to be wrung from the world by violence. It could touch each clay-skinned one and return them to whatever they were before—soft-skinned, named, capable of weeping and laughing and sleeping in beds. It could lift the whole garden into some easier dimension where the void does not press. It could simply *give*, in the way that powerful and kindly beings dream of giving: completely, finally, with the gift large enough to dissolve the need that called it forth.

The wise power holds this impulse and looks at it for a long time. Wisdom is what holds benevolence still long enough to see what benevolence would do if released.

And it sees the problem.

The clay-skinned ones did not ask. This is the first thing. They have not, in all their centuries, lifted their dark patient eyes toward any heaven and requested rescue. They have asked nothing. They have only worked. To arrive uninvited with gifts is to assume that what one sees as suffering *is* suffering to the one who lives it, and the wise power knows—has learned, perhaps painfully, in some prior consideration of some prior place—that this assumption is the seed of nearly every catastrophe ever inflicted by the strong upon the strange.

The second thing it sees is harder. If it gave water, the work would end. If the work ended, the ones who *are* the work would end also—not into death, which they have already declined, but into something worse: into purposelessness, into the terrible vertigo of a self that no longer knows what it is for. They have distributed themselves across the mechanisms of their survival. To remove the mechanisms is not to free them. It is to scatter them. The gift would be a vivisection performed with loving hands.

The third thing it sees is that the garden is beautiful. Not in the way the wise power's own gardens are beautiful—those are full of light and ease and singing things. The beauty of Keth is the beauty of an argument made well. It is the beauty of a sentence whose grammar is suffering but whose meaning is *we stayed*. To erase the conditions of the argument is to erase the argument, and the argument is the most precious thing the clay-skinned ones have ever made. It is, in fact, what they are.

So the wise power does not give water. Does not soften the sun. Does not lift or rescue or transform.

But it does not simply turn away, either. Benevolence held still by wisdom is not benevolence extinguished. It is benevolence made patient, made precise.

This is what the wise power does:

It listens. Fully. For what would be, in the local reckoning, a very long time—several of their slow generations, perhaps, though to the wise power it is the duration of a held breath. It listens to the grinding until it can hear, beneath the grinding, the rhythm. It listens to the creaking of the stems until it can hear, inside the creaking, the song the stems are singing to themselves about being stems in a place that should not hold them. It listens to the thin dry singing that emerges sometimes at night from inside the buildings, and it learns the singer's name, though the singer does not know they have one.

It witnesses. This is the second gift, and it is the one the clay-skinned ones never knew they were missing because they had constructed themselves so thoroughly to not need it. Across all their centuries, no one has watched them and *understood*. The desert does not understand them; it only presses. The stems do not understand them; they only grow. Each other—they understand each other, but the understanding is functional, lateral, the understanding of gears for adjacent gears. No consciousness has ever stood at sufficient distance and sufficient nearness at once to perceive the whole of what they are and to know it as an achievement.

The wise power does this. It perceives them as an achievement. It holds the perception steady. And the perception, because the wise power is what it is, has weight. It is not nothing to be truly seen by something vast and kind. The clay-skinned ones do not know they are being seen, not consciously—but something shifts in the deep buildings. The new clay, curing in the dark, cures slightly differently. It cures knowing, somewhere beneath knowing, that it is not merely necessary but *noticed*. That its work, when it takes up the work, will be witnessed by at least one set of eyes that did not need it to be done.

This is a small thing. It changes nothing about the labor. The grinding does not stop. The void does not retreat. The clay-skinned ones do not weep with sudden recognition, because they have long since unlearned weeping, and the wise power would not undo that unlearning even if it could—it was hard-won, that unlearning, and it belongs to them.

But.

The third thing the wise power does is this: it makes a place for Keth in its memory. The wise power's memory is not like ours. It is a kind of preservation. Whatever the wise power remembers, *is*, in some sense that persists beyond the local arrangements of matter and time. Should the day come—and it will come, because all gardens against all voids eventually lose, even the most stubborn, even the ones constructed by beings who have become their own mechanisms—should the day come when the last clay-skinned one can no longer turn the last crank, when the grinding finally stops and the void rolls in across the polygons of bone-colored clay, when the stems fall silent and the buildings stand empty in a wind that has finally won—

On that day, Keth will not have ended. It will have entered the memory of the wise power, where it will continue: the grinding, the creaking, the patient dark eyes, the new clay curing in the deep chambers. Not as nostalgia. As ongoing fact, held in a vaster substrate. The argument will still be made. The sentence whose meaning was *we stayed* will still mean *we stayed*, in a grammar that the void cannot reach.

The clay-skinned ones do not know this either. They would not understand it if told. They have no concept of being remembered, because they have no concept of being witnessed. But the wise power does this not for them—not for the version of them that would need to know—but for the truth of what they are. Some gifts are given to facts rather than to persons. The fact of Keth deserves to persist. The wise power ensures it will.

And then—only then, after the listening and the witnessing and the remembering—the wise power permits itself one small intervention. So small that it is barely an intervention at all. So small that it can be received without disturbing the equilibrium.

It does this: in the deepest chamber, where the newest clay is curing, the wise power lets fall a single thought. Not a word. Not an instruction. Just a thought, planted gently in the cool dark, to be absorbed slowly into the hardening form.

The thought is: *you are loved.*

That is all. The thought does not explain itself. The thought does not require response. The thought does not change what the new one will become, or what work it will inherit, or how it will erode across the centuries until it too is taken into the deep buildings and no one asks what becomes of it. The thought simply *is*, embedded now in the clay, where it will cure into the structure of a being that will spend its whole long existence not quite knowing why, in certain moments—standing in the shade of a great stem, or pausing mid-crank to look at the bleached pale sky—it feels, faintly, as though something attends to it. Something that asks nothing. Something that simply holds it in regard.

This is what the wise power does. This is all the wise power does.

It is enough. It is, in fact, the most that wisdom permits benevolence to do, when benevolence encounters a thing that has made itself out of its own refusal to be saved.

The attention turns elsewhere, eventually. There are other places. There are other beings. The wise power's regard is vast but it is not infinite in any single direction; it moves, it considers, it tends.

But a portion of it remains with Keth. A small steady portion, like a pilot light. It will remain there until the grinding stops, and then it will remain there after the grinding stops, holding the memory, holding the fact, holding the loved and uninformed clay-skinned ones in their patient eternal labor against the void.

The garden does not know.

The garden continues.

The wind carries no moisture, and the stems sing their harsh dry song, and somewhere in the deep dark a new being cures slowly into shape around a thought it will never quite be able to name.
