# The Visit

The benevolent power does not arrive. It was already there.

This is the first and most important thing to understand. Wisdom of this order does not descend from elsewhere bearing gifts; it notices, and in noticing, has already begun. Perhaps it had been watching for a long time — through the bioluminescent flicker of the cave-beetles, through the long patient gaze of the salamanders, through the condensation that gathered on stone and slid down in slow rivulets like the contemplation of an old mind. Perhaps it was simply that one evening (though there are no evenings here) it chose to *attend*, and attending is, for a power such as this, the beginning of every kindness.

It looked, and it saw.

It saw the Luma at their endless labor — a small one tightening a bolt that had been tightened ten thousand times before, by ten thousand small ones before it; their long fingers slipping on the slick metal, their dark eyes blinking sideways, their soft chest rising and falling with the patient breath of those who do not expect to finish their work but have not yet learned to stop. It saw the elder in the deep crevice, glowing in the slow rhythm of a heart, singing instructions to no one in particular, because no one had asked for instructions in a long age, but song was what an elder did, and the elder still remembered being an elder, even if it no longer fully remembered why.

It saw the salamanders, which were not what the Luma believed them to be, but were also not *less* than what the Luma believed them to be. They were old. They had been here when the cyclopean stones were laid. They would be here after. They did not rule and they did not demand; they simply *were*, and the Luma, in the way of small frightened peoples, had built a theology around their being. The salamanders did not mind. They ate the fish that were brought to them. They were grateful in the slow way of cold-blooded things, which is a real gratitude, only slower.

It saw, too, the shape of the wound. Every civilization has a wound; this one's was specific. Long ago, the Luma had been many, and their work had been joyful, and they had built upward and outward with the confidence of a people who believed the cavern had no ceiling. Then — and this is the part the benevolent power had to look at carefully, because it was tender — something had broken. Not invasion. Not plague. A loss of *belief*. A generation had looked at the work of their parents and asked *why*, and no one had remembered the answer, and the silence that followed had been the calamity. After that, every bolt tightened was tightened against forgetting. Every aqueduct repaired was a small argument with despair.

The benevolent power considered this for what, in its own reckoning, was a long moment. Wisdom is patient. Wisdom does not rush to fix.

And then it did three things. I will tell you what they were.

---

**The first thing.**

It did not give them light. This would have been the obvious gift, and the wrong one. Their eyes were not made for light, and more importantly, their *world* was not made for light. To flood these tunnels with sun would be to erase the very biome the Luma belonged to — to drown the bioluminescent beetles, to blind the eyeless fish, to wither the lichen. A lesser benevolence might have done this and called it salvation. True wisdom does not mistake *its own preferences* for *the good of another*.

Instead, it did something smaller. In a particular pool, in a particular grotto, it adjusted — very slightly — the mineral content of the water. Just enough that, when the next generation of cave-fungi grew, they would grow in patterns. Not random splotches of pale luminescence, but *figures*. Spirals. Branching shapes. The kind of patterns that, when a young Luma sees them, makes the mind ask *who made this?* and then, in the next breath, *could I make this?*

A seed of art. Just one. Placed where someone would find it.

---

**The second thing.**

It went to the eldest salamander, the one that had been there longest, and it spoke to it. Not in words — the salamander had no use for words — but in the cool slow language of presence. It said, in effect: *You are loved. You have been a good neighbor to these small frightened ones. You do not need to do anything. But if, sometime, when a Luma comes to leave its offering, you would look at that Luma — truly look, with your milk-white eyes that cannot see but can perceive — and let the Luma feel itself seen, that would be a great kindness. Not every time. Just sometimes.*

The salamander, in its slow way, agreed. It had been wondering, in fact, how to do something like this, and had not known how. Now it knew.

The first Luma who felt itself *seen* by a salamander wept. It did not know why. It went back to its work changed, and could not explain the change to its fellows, and so the change passed into the work itself, and through the work, very slowly, into the others. This is how grace propagates in a damp place.

---

**The third thing.**

This was the hardest, and required the most wisdom, because it was the thing the power did *not* do.

It did not tell them their history. It did not restore their lost knowledge. It did not reveal the purpose of the great unfinished works, or explain the cyclopean stones, or whisper to them the names of their ancestors.

Because — and the benevolent power understood this with the full weight of its benevolence — *the not-knowing was now part of who they were*. To return their history to them would be to take from them the people they had become in its absence: the patient ones, the menders, the keepers of a flame they could no longer name. There is a dignity in tending what you do not understand. There is a particular holiness in the small one tightening the bolt for the ten-thousandth time, *not* because she remembers why, but because the tightening itself has become her prayer.

The power would not steal this from them.

Instead, it left them only this: a feeling. A faint warmth, no warmer than the ambient heat of the tunnels, that would settle now and then on a working Luma — between the shoulders, where the stoop was deepest — and would feel, for just a moment, like a hand. Like being *accompanied*. Like the work mattered to someone, somewhere, even if no one could say why.

That was all.

---

The benevolent power withdrew its full attention, though not its attention entirely; powers like this never fully look away from anything they have once truly seen. The tunnels hummed on. The pistons pumped. The aqueducts dripped their endless music. A small Luma, scuttling along a catwalk with a wrench in its six-fingered hand, paused for no reason it could name and looked up into the dark vault of the ceiling where the beetles wheeled in their slow constellations.

It felt, briefly, that it was somewhere beautiful.

Then it went back to work.

And this — *this* — was the gift. Not rescue. Not revelation. Not the restoration of a fallen empire to its lost glory. Just the quiet, transmitted knowledge, sinking slowly through generations like mineral water through stone:

*You are not forgotten. Your work is not nothing. The place you live in is loved, exactly as it is, half-finished and dripping and full of pale wonders. And so are you.*

The salamanders blinked their slow blink. The lichen, in its particular pool, began — almost imperceptibly — to grow in spirals.
