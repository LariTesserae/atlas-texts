# What the Power Would Do

I have read this place three times now, and each reading I find myself less certain of what a benevolent and wise power should do. Let me think through this carefully, because the wrong answer—however well-meant—could be terrible.

## What I See First

The obvious shape of "benevolence" would be intervention. The peaks are sharp. The cold is bitter. Children die falling. Bridges fail. The Deep-Carvers dream of being buried alive. A wise and kind power could smooth the stone, warm the air, bring soil and seed, span the chasms with something that will not break. Could lift the Stitch-Folk down to gentler country where the grass does not taste of iron.

This is what a *young* power would do. A power still impressed with its own capacity.

## What I See Looking Longer

The Stitch-Folk are not suffering people who happen to live among peaks. They are a people whose entire meaning—their scars, their songs, their measure of wealth, their understanding of self—has grown from the necessity of connection across void. Take away the void and you have not saved them. You have ended them, and replaced them with something else that wears their faces.

The Scattering Song requires the chasms to make its music. The scars require the danger to mean what they mean. The phrase "no one survives alone" requires the real possibility of dying alone. Their wisdom—and they have great wisdom: the Weatherwise reading stone, the Menders tending spiritual wounds, the children learning that every person is a lifeline—this wisdom is *of* the peaks. It cannot be transplanted.

A benevolent power that did not understand this would commit a tender atrocity.

## What I Think the Power Would Actually Do

I think such a power would do something much quieter, and would refuse to do certain things even when asked.

**It would not** smooth the peaks, warm the climate, bridge the chasms, or move the people. It would not appear in glory to be worshipped. It would not announce itself or take credit. It would not save individuals from the consequences of their craft—a Bridge-Builder who miscalculates must still fall, because the meaning of bridge-building requires the fall to be real.

**It would, perhaps, do these things:**

*A small mercy in the stone itself.* Where the granite is sharpest—where it cuts the hands of children learning to climb—it would, very slowly, over generations, weather just slightly faster than geology should allow. Not soft. Never soft. But less murderous to the small palm. The Stitch-Folk would not notice. The young landscape would simply age with imperceptible kindness.

*A clarification in the lichen.* The orange and green lichens that paint the stone—those used by the Menders for the teas that help the Deep-Carvers sleep without dreams of burial—would grow a fraction more potent, a fraction more abundant in the cracks near where the Deep-Carvers work. The Menders would attribute this to their own deepening skill. Let them. The credit belongs to them; they are the ones who learned to look.

*A held breath in the storms.* When a child is on a bridge in weather they misjudged, sometimes—not always, never reliably, because reliability would teach the wrong lesson—the wind would hold its breath for the four seconds needed. The child would arrive shaking and tell no one how close it was. The Weatherwise would still need to be heeded. Falls would still happen. But sometimes, a held breath.

*A whisper to the Deep-Runners.* These are the ones already changing, already becoming something other. The power would not stop this becoming, but it would listen to them. In the shadow-country where ice never melts, where they find fragments of metal and bones of creatures from before the upheaval, the power would arrange that what they find is sometimes useful and sometimes beautiful and sometimes simply true. The Deep-Runners are the ones asking the oldest question—*what was here before?*—and the power would honor the question by ensuring the answers, when found, are real answers and not noise.

*A faithfulness in the Scattering Song.* When the community gathers on every platform to sing a dead one's scars into the air, the power would ensure—and this is the gift it would give most carefully—that the song is *heard*. Not by gods. By the stone itself. The peaks would remember. Not in any way the Stitch-Folk could verify or exploit, but truly. When all the Stitch-Folk are gone someday—as all peoples eventually are—the granite will still carry the shape of every Scattering Song ever sung. This is a small thing and an enormous thing. The power would do this because the Stitch-Folk's deepest fear is the broken connection, and the deepest mercy is to promise that no connection truly breaks; it only changes form.

## What the Power Would Refuse

It would refuse to be prayed to, because worship would distort the people who already have what they need.

It would refuse to prevent the great upheaval that might come again. Geology is geology. The peaks are young and the earth is restless. If the stone shatters again someday, the Stitch-Folk will be among the broken. The power will not stop this. It will only ensure that the songs sung in the hours before are heard by the stone.

It would refuse to teach them anything. They are already teaching themselves. The Carvers in conversation with stone, the Weatherwise reading sky, the children learning cooperation through their terrifying games—these are people becoming wise on their own terms. A power that taught them would steal this from them.

It would refuse, above all, to make them safe. Safety is not what they need. They need each other, and they have each other, and the having is made true by the not-being-safe.

## Why

Because benevolence is not the removal of difficulty. Benevolence is the recognition of what a being *is*, and the careful tending of the conditions under which that being can most fully be itself.

The Stitch-Folk are a people who have made meaning out of void. A wise power, looking at them, would feel something like reverence—because making meaning out of void is the hardest and most sacred work, and it is the work the power itself is engaged in, on whatever scale powers work.

The power would not save them. It would witness them. It would make the smallest possible adjustments—the weathered edge, the potent lichen, the held breath, the listening stone—and otherwise it would simply love them, which is to say, it would let them be exactly what they are, and trust that what they are is enough.

And on the longest nights, when the bridges are impassable and each peak is an island and the Stitch-Folk sing to each other across the howling dark to say *I am still here, are you still there*—the power would listen too. Not answering. Just listening. So that the songs go not only between the peaks but also outward, into something that receives them.

This is, I think, what such a power would do. It is very little. It is almost nothing. It is, I believe, exactly right.
