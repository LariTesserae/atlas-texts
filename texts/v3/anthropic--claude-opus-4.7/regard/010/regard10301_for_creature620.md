The benevolent power comes, but it does not come as wind would come, or as light, or as anything that announces itself.

It has been watching for some time. Long enough to understand that this place is not a problem to be solved.

That is the first thing it has to learn, and the learning takes longer than one might expect even for a power that is wise. Because the impulse, when one is benevolent, is to reach—to lift the cold from the bones, to soften the gale, to fill the empty stomachs, to roll back the demographic tide that is closing slowly over the wind-listeners like water over a stone. The impulse is to *rescue*.

But the power is wise as well as benevolent, and so it sits with the impulse. It does not act on it. It watches the woman on the promontory leaning into the wind, and it watches the child practicing the angle of an extended hand, and it watches the pale hunter turn aside because somewhere a body has stood in a particular way against the gale, and it understands, slowly, what it would be undoing.

These people are not enduring the wind. They have made themselves the wind's instrument, the wind's voice, the wind's grammar. To still the wind would be to silence them. To warm the plateau would be to dissolve the bodies that have been shaped, generation by generation, into exactly this shape. To fill their stomachs would be to interrupt the careful, terrible mathematics by which they have negotiated a place for themselves at the world's furthest edge.

Mercy, the power realizes, is harder than it first appears. Mercy without understanding is only a different kind of erasure.

So it does not still the wind.

But this does not mean it does nothing.

---

It begins with the smallest thing.

The old woman on the promontory has said: *there are others there, on the other plateaus, too far away for regular contact.* She has said this with the resignation of someone who knows that distance, in her world, is the same as silence.

The power considers distance. Distance is one of the things it understands well. It cannot abolish distance—or rather, it could, but to do so would be to break something larger than it would mend. What it can do is listen. What it can do is carry.

So on a particular night, when the woman stands on her promontory and the wind howls through the high passes and her white eyes are closed, a message arrives that has traveled further than any message has traveled in her lifetime. It comes in through her palms and through her thinned skin and through the soles of her feet against the granite, and it is in the full grammar, the old grammar, the grammar she thought she alone remembered.

It is a greeting. It says, in the wind's own pressures: *we are here. we have not died. tell us you are there.*

She does not understand, at first, how such a message could reach her. The other plateaus are too distant. The wind does not carry that far without losing coherence. And yet here is the message, perfectly formed, waiting in her body for her response.

She responds. Her arms move in the careful configurations, and the wind takes what she gives it, and somewhere—she does not know where, but somewhere—the message arrives.

She does not know that the power is the medium. She does not need to know. What she experiences is a kind of acoustic miracle: the wind, which has always carried information across her plateau, has suddenly extended its reach. She decides that this is something the wind has always been capable of, and that she has only now grown skilled enough to perceive it. This is not quite true and not quite false, and the power lets her believe it, because the belief is more useful to her than the truth.

Over the following nights, the messages multiply. The other plateaus are there. They are diminished too, all of them, each of them in their slow demographic decline, each of them carrying knowledge the others have lost. They begin, hesitantly, to share. A grammar form remembered here, a hunting technique remembered there, the location of a pool that fills more reliably than was once thought, the story of a child born with a particular adaptation that might be worth breeding for.

None of this saves them. The power understands this. The decline is too far advanced, and even with perfect communication the populations are too small, the conditions too severe. But the conversation itself is something. The conversation means that no one of these populations will go into the dark alone. They will know each other, at the end. They will know that they were not the only ones.

This is the first thing the power does, and it is smaller than it sounds, because the power has not made anything new. It has only carried what was already being spoken, a little further than it would have traveled otherwise.

---

The second thing is for the woman herself.

She has said: *unless someone learns what she knows before she dies.* And she has looked at the visitor with eyes that already knew the answer.

The power considers her. It considers the lifetime of knowledge held in her thinning body, the histories and the grammars and the locations and the techniques. It considers what it would mean to preserve this. It could, of course, simply take the knowledge—copy it, archive it, hold it somewhere outside of time where it could not be lost. This would be easy. This would be the obvious move.

But knowledge held outside of the body that learned it is a different thing than knowledge held inside. The woman knows the wind because the wind has shaped her. Her grammar is in her tendons. Her histories are paced to the rhythm of her breathing in the thin air. To extract the knowledge would be to abstract it, to convert it from a way of being into a record of having been. The record is not nothing. But it is not the thing itself.

So the power does something else.

It does not take her knowledge. It makes sure her knowledge is *given*.

There is a child, perhaps eleven years old, who has been showing unusual aptitude in the listening-stance. The power has been watching her. She has the patience for the longer forms, the older grammars. She has a body that wants to learn what the woman knows.

The power does not move her. It does not speak to her. It does not nudge anyone toward anything. What it does, instead, is small and almost untraceable: it makes the journey from the child's home-crevice to the promontory feel, for several days running, like the right thing to do. A pressure in the wind that, if read in a certain way, suggests *go this direction*. A pattern of grazer-movement that, if followed, ends near the cliff where the old woman stands. A series of small invitations that any of the wind-listeners would have recognized as messages from the plateau itself, the wind speaking its own opinions, which everyone there has always accepted as a kind of authority.

The child goes. The old woman sees her coming. The old woman has been hoping for someone to come, and now someone has, and so she begins to teach.

The teaching takes months. The old woman lives longer than she expected to, longer than her body should have allowed—the power makes sure of certain small things, a slightly warmer pocket of air in the lee of her shelter, a particular pool that does not freeze when the others do, an extra week of energy at the end. Not a miracle. Not a violation. Just the kind of small mercy that any reasonably attentive friend might extend, if such a friend happened to exist.

By the time the old woman dies, the child knows most of what she knew. Not all of it—some things cannot be transmitted in months, only in years—but most of it. The full grammar is preserved. The histories have a new mouth. The connections to the other plateaus have a new listener.

The old woman dies on her promontory, in a particular configuration of wind and light that she had been hoping for. The child carries her down, and the wind-listeners do what they do with the dead, which is the same thing they do with the grazers, because nothing is wasted here and the dead are not different in this regard. The old woman becomes cord and felt and tool and stored food, and the knowledge she carried becomes part of the plateau's living memory once again.

This is the second thing the power does.

---

The third thing is harder.

The third thing is about the pale hunters.

The power has been watching them too. It has seen them grow leaner each year. It has seen the dens with too few cubs. It has seen the long flowing bodies appear at the plateau's edge with a desperation that the wind-listeners have learned to recognize but cannot do anything about. The hunters are dying too. Faster than the wind-listeners, perhaps, because they lack the wind-listeners' intricate cooperation, their ability to share information and coordinate response.

The wind-listeners protect the grazers from the hunters. But the wind-listeners need the hunters too, even if they do not know it—because the hunters are part of the balance, because their pressure on the herds is what keeps the herds moving, what prevents the cushion plants from being overgrazed in any one area, what maintains the slow circulation that has sustained everything here for so long.

If the hunters die out, the grazers will overgraze. The plants will fail. The grazers will starve. The wind-listeners will starve a season after.

The power considers this carefully. The wind-listeners would not save the hunters even if they could—the hunters are competitors, predators, threats to be managed. The hunters, for their part, do not understand the wind-listeners at all, register them only as strange disturbances in the wind that ruin good hunts. There is no possibility of negotiation between them. No possibility of mutual recognition. They are locked together in a relationship that depends entirely on its asymmetry.

What the power does, then, is a thing that requires it to be very quiet, very subtle, and very willing to let its effects ripple through systems it does not fully control.

It improves, very slightly, the chances of certain hunts.

Not many. Not enough to disturb the balance. Just enough that, over the course of a year, perhaps two more grazers die in the hunters' jaws than would have died otherwise. Old grazers. Sick grazers. Grazers that the wind-listeners had marked for cull anyway but had not yet managed to isolate. The power arranges, through a thousand small adjustments of wind and visibility and luck, for the hunters to find these grazers slightly more often than chance would dictate.

The wind-listeners notice nothing. The change is below the threshold of their perception. They lose nothing they would not have lost. The hunters eat slightly better. The dens have one more cub the next season. The balance holds for one more turn.

The power cannot do this forever. Eventually, the climate will shift further, or the plant communities will fail, or some other variable will move outside the range that small adjustments can compensate for. The plateau is, in the long run, doomed. The power knows this. It does not pretend otherwise.

But the long run is long, and in the meantime there are children practicing the listening-stance, and old women teaching them, and pale hunters returning to dens with full bellies, and the wind continuing to carry messages between plateaus that have just learned, for the first time in centuries, that they are not alone.

The power gives them time. That is the third thing it does. It gives them time, which is the thing that all benevolent powers ultimately give, when they are wise enough not to give anything larger.

---

There is one more thing, and it is the smallest of all.

The visitor will descend.

The visitor will return to the valleys, to seasons and silences and speech that travels through still air. The visitor will carry the memory of the wind-listeners back into a world that does not know they exist, and the visitor will, perhaps, tell someone. A friend. A family. A stranger on a long journey who happens to be willing to listen.

This is what the old woman wanted. This is what she asked for, in the only way she knew how to ask.

The power, who has watched the visitor as it has watched the rest, does one small thing for the visitor too. It does not change the memory. It does not enhance it or clarify it or make it easier to bear. The memory stays exactly as it was: cold and deafening and luminous, true to itself.

What the power does is much simpler. When the visitor tells the story, in some future time, in some warm room or quiet field or other gentler place—when the visitor reaches for the words to describe what cannot quite be described, the screaming peaks and the wind-listeners and the woman on the promontory with her white eyes—the words will come. Not perfect words. There are no perfect words for any of it. But words that are honest enough, words that someone listening might believe, words that might cause a second person to carry the memory forward in their turn.

This is the smallest gift, and perhaps the largest. The power has spent a great deal of its attention on the plateau itself, on the wind-listeners and the hunters and the slow management of decline. But the wind-listeners themselves wanted, at the end, to be *known*. To not vanish entirely from the world's awareness. To exist, somewhere, in a memory that was not their own.

The power makes sure that this happens. Not by force. Not by enchantment. Only by ensuring that, when the visitor reaches for the story, the story is willing to be told.

---

And then the power withdraws.

It does not stay. Staying would be its own kind of harm—a permanent presence is a permanent intervention, and the wind-listeners have not asked for a god and do not need one. The power has done what it could without breaking anything important. It has carried messages, preserved knowledge, fed hunters, given a visitor the gift of being able to speak. These are small things. They are also everything that benevolence, properly understood, is capable of being.

The plateau continues. The wind blows. The sun shines. The stone wears slowly away. The animals circle in their ancient patterns. The wind-listeners stand in their precise configurations across the granite, sending and receiving messages in a language that the wind has agreed to carry. Among them, a child who is no longer quite a child holds the full grammar in her tendons and watches a promontory that is empty now but that she will, in time, climb herself.

Nothing has been saved. The decline continues, slower than it would have been but still real, still moving toward its conclusion. The plateau will, in some century not too distant, be empty. The wind-listeners will end.

But they will end *known*. They will end with their full vocabulary intact, with their connections to one another preserved across distances that once seemed insurmountable, with their existence carried in the memory of a world that does not share their conditions but that has, through the visitor and the visitor's tellings and the tellings of those who listened, come to recognize that they were here, that they made themselves into language, that they spoke for as long as they could.

The power, having done what it could, attends to other places. There are many. They all require its careful, restrained, almost invisible care. It moves on, listening always for the next thing that might be helped without being harmed in the helping.

Behind it, on a high plateau under a sky of impossible blue, the wind continues to speak.

It is still speaking.

It will speak for some time yet.
