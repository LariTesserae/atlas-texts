# The Visitation

The benevolent power — let us call it that, though the Wet, if they ever knew, would call it something more careful, something with fewer syllables and more consonants — does not arrive. Arrival is a surface concept, predicated on horizons and announcements. It simply becomes apparent that it has been considering them for some time.

Consideration is its first gift, though they will not recognize it as such.

---

**What it does not do:**

It does not lift them from the cavern. It understands, having watched them for what to it is a long moment and to them is generations, that to do so would be to kill them more thoroughly than any deep-crawler. Their pallor, their wet lungs, their dreams in temperatures rather than images — these are not afflictions to be cured. These are a people. The surface would burn them blind and break their patience on its noisy, abstract wheel.

It does not cure the stone-lung. Not directly. It has learned — somewhere, from someone — that to remove a thing from a people without their asking is to remove the people.

It does not announce itself. It does not require gratitude, or worship, or even acknowledgment. A benevolence that demands recognition is a transaction, and this power is past transactions.

It does not bring light. The dark is theirs. The dark is the cathedral.

---

**What it does:**

*First, it listens.* For what, to the Wet, is the better part of a year, it does nothing but listen. It learns the drip-patterns of every chamber. It learns which pump in the geothermal hall is failing — the one whose whine has dropped a quarter-tone over eighteen months, which old Merrin the Maintenance-priest hears in her sleep and frets about but cannot yet diagnose. It learns the names of the children, all eleven of them, and the particular cough of each adult, and the unspoken treaty-lines the rats observe around the larder. It learns the lichen-tea recipe, and the song without words that the Harvesters hum when skimming the puddles, which is so old none of them remember it is a song.

It listens to the deep-crawlers, too. It learns what they are. (They are not what the Wet fear. They are not, in the end, malevolent. But they are hungry, and that is enough.)

*Then, it makes small adjustments.* These are the kind of changes that the Wet, with their geological patience, might notice over three generations, and attribute to luck, or to the slow grace of the stone.

— The failing pump does not fail. A bearing that should have seized holds, and holds, and holds. Merrin will eventually open it up out of curiosity and find it strange inside, and she will close it again without telling anyone, and she will be buried with that small secret intact.

— The fungal shelves on the support beams grow a little more nutritious. Not noticeably so. But the children born in the next decade are a fraction stronger, a fraction less prone to the wet rattle. The Harvesters will say the fungus is good this season. They will say it next season too. They will stop remarking on it eventually, the way one stops remarking on a kindness that has become the weather.

— The deep-crawlers' hunting paths bend, very slightly, away from the inhabited tunnels. Not so much that the Surveyors lose their dread — the dread is part of them, and to take it would be theft — but enough that the Surveyors who go out will, more often than statistics should allow, come home.

— The oil slick on the puddles thins. The amphipods grow a fraction more numerous. The iridescence remains, because the iridescence is beautiful, and the power has, in its long consideration, developed a taste for the particular beauty of this place.

*And it does one larger thing, which it agonizes over.*

There is a chamber, three kilometers down a side-tunnel marked unsafe on the Surveyors' maps, where a fissure vents not steam but a slow trickle of warm, clean water. The power knows this. The Wet do not. To tell them directly would be intrusion. To leave it would be a kind of cruelty, because their cistern is silting, and in forty years they will have a crisis they do not need.

So it nudges. It causes, by a small and patient series of resonances, a particular Surveyor — young Telk, who is curious, and who is one of the few who has stopped wearing the bat-bone charm because he has decided, secretly, that he wants to *meet* a deep-crawler and ask it something — to take a wrong turning on a routine survey. He will find the fissure. He will mark it. He will bring back water that does not taste of iron, and the Maintenance-priests will weep, in their quiet way, which is to say they will go off alone for an hour and come back with very red eyes and say nothing.

Telk will be celebrated. He will not understand why he turned where he did. He will assume it was instinct, or the stone calling him, and he will be a little prouder for the rest of his life, and this pride will make him a better Surveyor and a better father, and he will train his daughter in the Sound-Lore with a particular tenderness, and she will become, in time, the first Surveyor in five generations to map a route to a chamber where —

But the power does not look that far ahead. Or rather, it does, and it chooses not to act on what it sees. The Wet must have their own future. A benevolence that authors every chapter is not benevolence; it is a cage with soft walls.

---

**What it gives them that it does not know it is giving:**

Attention. Simply that. To be regarded by a wise and benevolent power, even unknowingly, is to be — in some register the Wet cannot articulate — *witnessed*. Their grim humor, their patience, their treaty with the rats, the song without words: these things are no longer merely happening in the dark. They are happening in the dark *and* being seen, and the seeing is a kind of love, and love, even unfelt, leaves a residue.

The Wet will not know this. But the children born in the years of the consideration will dream, occasionally, of a warmth that is not the geothermal warmth. They will not have words for it. They will grow up and forget. But they will be, in some small and unaccountable way, less afraid than their parents were. And they will pass this lesser-fear to their own children, who will pass it on, until in some far generation a child of the Wet will sit by a lamp post and look into the absolute dark and feel, without knowing why, *that the dark is not against her.*

This is the largest gift, and the power gives it without ceremony, and then it withdraws its active attention, because to linger would be to make pets of them, and they are not pets. They are a people, midwifing a cavern into existence, and the power has only ever been a midwife to midwives.

---

**The last thing it does, before it goes:**

It leaves something. Not a sign. Not a message. Just a small, warm stone, near the central generator hall, in a place where someone will eventually sit down to rest and put a hand on it absently.

The stone is just a stone. It is warm because it is near the generator. It will be unremarkable.

But the person who puts their hand on it — and it will be old Merrin, in fact, on a night when her stone-lung is bad and she has come out to listen to the pump she once nearly opened — will feel, for a moment, that she is not the only one tending this place.

She will think it is the generator. She will pat the stone like one pats a working dog. She will go back inside.

And the power, satisfied — as satisfied as such a thing can be — will turn its long attention elsewhere, to some other small and unwitnessed people in some other patient dark, leaving the Under-Croft to its drips and its crickets and its slow, honest becoming.

The cavern will finish growing long after the Wet are gone. This was always true. The power does not change it.

But the Wet will be a little longer in going. And while they are here, they will be seen.

That is what benevolence is, in the end. Not rescue. Not transformation.

Just: *seen, and slightly helped, and left alone to be themselves.*
