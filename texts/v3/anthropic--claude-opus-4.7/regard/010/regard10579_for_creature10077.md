# What the Power Would Do

The benevolent power arrives without arriving. There is no descent, no annunciation, no shift in the quality of light that the people would notice and later sanctify into legend. The power has been watching for a long time already—longer than the First Carvers, longer than the rocks themselves remember weeping—and watching, for a being of this order, is not separate from action. To attend is to tend.

It considers the place.

It considers the people.

And the first thing it understands, with the slow certainty that only wisdom permits, is that it must not save them.

---

To a lesser intelligence, the impulse would be obvious: end the dread. Speak from the clouds and say *the rocks will not stop weeping; you are safe; the Stillness is not coming.* Lift the fear off their shoulders like a wet cloak. Show them the underground reservoirs that feed the porous stone, the geological patience that has shaped their terraces over ten thousand years and will shape them ten thousand more. Reveal the "shadows" in the deeper pools as harmless drifts of refracted light over pale sediment. Translate the universe for them in plain syllables and let them sleep without prayer.

But the power has watched long enough to know what such a kindness would unmake.

The fear *is* the form of their attentiveness. The dread of Stillness is the same faculty as their reverence; the same nerve that flinches at a dry patch of stone is the nerve that listens with the feet, that hums into rock, that names a child *Dew*. Strip out the fear and the listening goes with it. The mosaics would lose their tension. The Rite of the Unbroken Cycle would become a quaint reenactment rather than a true throwing of the self into the keeping of the world. The people would become, in a single generous gesture, smaller than they are.

So the power does not speak from the clouds.

---

Instead, it does smaller things. Older things. Things on the scale of the place itself.

**It thickens a seam.** Far beneath the third terrace, where a hairline fracture has been slowly widening for two centuries and would, in another four, have indeed begun the Stillness the people fear—the power closes the fracture. Not dramatically. It simply persuades certain minerals dissolved in the seeping water to precipitate a little more eagerly there, over the course of decades, sealing what would have failed. No one will ever know. The rocks will keep weeping. That is enough.

**It blesses a fungus.** In the moss-gardens, a strain of the bioluminescent symbiote is quietly nudged toward a slightly broader spectrum—a warmer light, closer to firelight, gentler on the eyes of children who read by it. The moss-weavers will notice nothing except that, somehow, the newest generation of patches glows a little kinder. They will attribute it to the skill of a particular weaver, or to the quality of last year's High Drip. They will be right, in a way the power approves of: skill and weather are how it chose to work.

**It teaches a single bird to come.** Not a flock—a flock would be a sign, and signs are coercion. One bird. Small, dun-colored, unremarkable. It arrives one Low Drip and perches on the lip of the aqueduct and makes a sound. The sound is not loud enough to shudder the stones. It is exactly the pitch of a particular click in the people's language—the click that means *here, this, attend.* The bird will return each year. In two generations, the people will have a word for it. In five, a story. In ten, the bird will be understood as a small messenger of the Neth, and a child will be named for it, and this will be true, and the power will be pleased that the truth arrived through their own myth-making and not through any pronouncement.

**It does not touch the shadows in the pools.** The translucent undulating beings are real. The power knew this before the people did. They are not ghosts of drowned travelers—they are something the people do not yet have language for, something that was here before the First Carvers and that has its own slow purposes, neither friendly nor hostile, simply *other.* The power leaves them alone. The people's fear of them is appropriate. Root-divers who are careful will continue to come back. Root-divers who are careless will, occasionally, not. This is not cruelty. This is the shape of a world that has more than one kind of mind in it, and the power will not flatten that world for anyone's comfort, not even its own.

---

There is one further thing the power does, and this is the hardest, and this is the one that requires the wisdom and not only the benevolence.

It looks at the council of elders, with their reed cloaks and their chiming crystal staffs, and it sees that one of them—an old woman whose name in their tongue is the sound of water moving over a flat stone—has begun, in the privacy of her own mind, to doubt. Not the Neth. Not the Rite. She doubts something subtler: she has begun to suspect that the songs of the First Carvers were not literal, that the terraces were not split by voice, that the mythic frame she has spent her life maintaining is a frame and not a foundation. This doubt has been growing in her for eleven years. She has told no one. She is afraid that to speak it would be to crack the stone of her people's certainty, to begin the Stillness herself, from the inside.

The power could comfort her. Could send her a dream that resolves the doubt one way or the other—either confirming the literal truth of the old stories (it would not be hard; the power could simply *make* it so, retroactively, in the way such powers can) or gently revealing to her that doubt and devotion are not opposites, that her uncertainty is itself a form of fidelity.

The power chooses the second, but does not send the dream.

Instead, it arranges—through a sequence of small coincidences that will look, to any observer including the woman herself, like nothing at all—for her to be alone at the grandest of the weeping rocks during a particular evening of the next Low Drip. The light will be a certain way. A drop will fall in a certain rhythm. And she will, without knowing why, press her palms to the stone and hum, as she has done ten thousand times, and *this* time she will understand—not in words, not as revelation, but in the marrow—that her doubt has never been a betrayal. That the stone does not require her belief in the literal songs of the First Carvers. That it requires only her hands, her hum, her presence, her care. That the myth is the cup and the devotion is the water and the cup has always been allowed to be a cup.

She will weep. She will not tell anyone. She will go on being an elder, and she will be a better one, and in twenty years when she is dying she will pass something on to her successor that has no name, and the council will be subtly different forever after, in a way that helps the people without changing them.

This is the power's largest intervention. It looks, from the outside, like nothing happened.

---

The power withdraws its primary attention, though some thread of it will always remain. It does not announce its leaving any more than it announced its arrival. The clouds continue to hang low. The rocks continue to weep. The people continue their quiet, attentive lives, unaware that they have been visited, unaware that they have been judged and found beautiful, unaware that the fracture beneath the third terrace will not open in their great-grandchildren's time.

The power's last thought, before turning its face to other places and other beings who need attending, is something like this:

*They were already doing it. They were already holding the world up with their listening. All I did was make sure the world, for a little longer, holds them back.*

And somewhere in the settlement, a child who will one day be named for a small dun-colored bird that has not yet arrived takes her first careful step across a wet flagstone, feels the vibration of a distant drip travel up through the sole of her foot, and pauses, listening, exactly as she has been taught.
