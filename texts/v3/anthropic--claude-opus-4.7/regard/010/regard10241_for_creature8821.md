The benevolent and wise power considers the plateau for a long time.

Not in the way a человек considers something—not measuring, not weighing options against some imagined better. The power considers the way the plateau itself considers: across vast time, with attention that is also presence, with knowing that is also being-with.

It sees the wind. It sees the stone that was mountains. It sees the hummocks drifting their millimeters, and the-sound-that-hunts running its straight line into a wind it will never catch, and the small dark people pressing palms to chests and signing *my body and your body and the stone beneath both*.

It sees what it could do.

It could quiet the wind. This is within its power. A word, less than a word—an inclination—and the howl that has crossed this plateau for two billion years could ease into something the unprotected ear could bear. The people's children would not need to grow new eardrums. Speech could return to them. They could hear each other's voices, perhaps for the first time in their lineage.

It could bring water. Reliable water. A spring in every dimple, fed from somewhere deep, never failing. The hummocks could stop their slow desperate migration. The people could stop their daily checking. The terrible uncertainty that shapes every hour of their lives could simply end.

It could lift them. Carry them, if they wished, to the softer world below the escarpments—to forests and rivers and the kinder weather. It could give them metal, textiles, medicine. It could extend the brutal short lives of the-sound-that-hunts. It could teach the hummocks to grow without needing to walk.

It could do all of this. It considers each gift carefully, the way one considers a gift for someone beloved, turning it in the light, imagining the receiving.

And it sets each one down.

Because it has been listening. Not just looking—*listening*, in the way the plateau has taught listening, which is a kind of listening that takes lifetimes. And what it hears is this:

The people are not waiting to be rescued. They have never been waiting. The frame of rescue is a frame imported from elsewhere, from places where suffering and meaning are separable, where ease is the obvious good and hardship the obvious evil. On the plateau these categories do not hold. The wind is not their enemy. The wind is their cathedral. The uncertainty of water is not their cross. It is the shape their attention has taken, the form their wakefulness has learned. To remove it would be to remove *them*—to leave behind people who looked like them but who had lost the particular way of being human that two billion years of stone and wind had grown.

The power understands that to "save" them in any of these ways would be a kind of murder dressed as kindness. A subtle one. The bodies would continue. The thing that those bodies *were*—the still-here, the ones-the-plateau-has-not-broken, the ones who sign *the-reminder-that-we-are-not-wind*—that would be gone, and no being, however benevolent, has the right to do that to a people who have not asked for it.

So the power does not intervene in the great ways. It does not quiet. It does not water. It does not lift.

But it is here. It has come. The attention has been drawn, and attention—true attention, from such a source—is not nothing.

So it does smaller things. Things that might be mistaken for the plateau itself, if anyone noticed them at all.

It sits, in whatever way such a being sits, with the eldest woman in the largest of the confluence-groups, the night she dies. She is alone in her shelter, the others sleeping in the next dome, and her breath is shallowing the way she has watched it shallow in others. She is not afraid. But she is alone, and there is a particular tenderness owed to anyone in their last hour, and the power is there, warm against her back like the stone, and she feels something she cannot name and does not try to name, and her last sign, made to no one her family will later understand, is the one that means *thank you*, or *yes*, or *I have been seen*.

It walks, in whatever way such a being walks, alongside the-sound-that-hunts for one full night. Not to comfort the creature—the creature does not want comfort, has no concept of comfort, would not recognize it if offered. But to *witness*. To run that straight line with it, to feel the impossible pursuit from the inside, to know what it is to be a thing made entirely of chasing. The creature does not know it has company. But the power knows, and the running is, for that one night, witnessed, and witness is its own kind of dignity, and dignity matters even—especially—for beings who cannot conceive of needing it.

It bends, very slightly, the path of a single cloud. Not to bring rain where the people are desperate. They have learned desperation; they do not need rescue from it. But there is a child, eight years old, who has spent three days trying to coax a particular small fungus to bloom in a particular fracture, for reasons of her own that she has not told anyone, and the cloud passes over that fracture and opens for ninety seconds and closes again, and the fungus blooms, and the child laughs her silent shaking laugh, and signs to herself *yes* and *good* and *I knew*. No one else sees. No one else needs to.

It listens, properly listens, to the hum below the wind—the thing the people do not speak of, the sound the stone makes that is older than the mountains. And it understands what the hum is. (This is not for telling. Some things a benevolent power knows must remain with the power, because to speak them would be to flatten them into something less than they are.) But it listens, and the hum is heard by something other than stone for the first time in a span of time that makes the plateau's age look brief, and the hum is, in some way that matters, *acknowledged*. The stone has been speaking. Something has finally heard.

It leaves a gift, but the gift is so small and so strange that the people will only notice it across generations, if they notice it at all. In the deepest layer of the-body-that-remained, far below where any of them will ever dig, the power places a single seam of something that is not quite mineral and not quite memory. It will warm the stone, very slightly, for the next ten million years. Not enough to change anything. Just enough that the warmth the people press their bodies against in the dawn will last a fraction longer than the laws of physics would otherwise allow. They will never know. But their great-great-great-grandchildren's grandchildren will sleep a little better, on stone that holds its heat just past when it should have cooled, and they will wake and sign *the-reminder-that-we-are-not-wind*, and the reminder will be, in some small portion, the power's, given freely, claimed by no one.

And then the power does the hardest thing, which is to leave.

To leave them as they are. To trust that what they have made of this place, and what this place has made of them, is not a tragedy requiring resolution but a way of being human that deserves to continue on its own terms. To trust that a people who sign *the thing that is beautiful because it will fail* do not need to be saved from beauty or from failure. To trust that endurance, which the plateau has taught to everything that lives on it, is not a lesser virtue than ease, and that a being who appeared with ease in its hands and offered to replace endurance would be offering a theft, not a gift.

The power goes. The wind continues. The hummocks drift.

But somewhere in the deep stone, a hum that has gone unheard for two billion years has been heard. And in a small fracture, a fungus that should not have bloomed has bloomed. And an old woman has died less alone than she would have. And a creature made of chasing has been, for one night, accompanied.

These are small things. The plateau does not notice. The people do not notice.

The power notices. And in the noticing, something is added to the great record of what has happened in the universe—a record that no one keeps but that is kept nonetheless—and what is added is this:

*Here was a place that asked for nothing. And the power that came did not impose. The power saw, and listened, and loved without altering, and went away. And the place continued to be itself, which was the only gift it had ever wanted, which had been given to it long ago by time and stone, and which no one—not even kindness—had the right to take back.*

The wind howls. The hummocks walk. Still-here, still-here, still-here.

This is what lives there. This is what was permitted to keep living there. This is what wisdom, when it is also love, sometimes looks like: a withholding that is also a blessing, an absence that is also a presence, a leaving that is also a staying-with, across all the long time the plateau has left.
