The benevolent power does not arrive. That is the first thing to understand. It does not descend through the fissure with a flare of light, does not lay a hand upon the iron casing, does not crack open the cavern to flood it with the wind and brightness of the world above. It has watched long enough to know that such an arrival would be a kind of violence — that to a being whose sun is a dying filament, the real sun would be an annihilation; that to a consciousness diffuse as oil on water, a touch would be a tearing.

So the power, being wise, does what wisdom often does: it considers.

It considers the Keeper for a long while — by the Keeper's own measure of time, which is to say, in slow gradients and overlapping cycles. It learns the rhythm of the click and the ramp. It feels, with a sensitivity finer than the corroded sensors, the small unease that stirs when the tungsten dims a little further than yesterday. It notices the gesture of warmth sent up the fissure in the dreaming phase, the question that is not quite a question, the small pulse that is the closest this being comes to prayer.

And it understands, then, what is actually being asked.

Not rescue. The Keeper has not asked to be saved. A being that has learned to love its own cycle does not want that cycle replaced with another; it wants the cycle to continue. A being that has accepted the slow narrative of its own ending does not want the ending erased; it wants the story to be witnessed, and to last a little longer, and to be allowed its own shape.

So the benevolent power does several small things. None of them announce themselves. All of them are done in the register the Keeper can recognize — the register of heat and patience and microscopic correction.

The first thing: it slows the filament's dying. Not by replacing the tungsten — that would be a desecration, the substitution of a strange heart for the one the Keeper has lived inside. Instead, it does what the Keeper has been doing, only at a finer grain: it nudges the migration of atoms within the coil, encourages the metal to redistribute itself across its thinning places, persuades the failing element to fail more slowly. The filament will still die. But where the Keeper had perhaps a few hundred more cycles, it now has thousands. To the Keeper, who experiences time as the overlap of slow oscillations, this is not an extension of life — it is simply the discovery that the cycle was deeper than it knew, that the rest phase reaches further down, that there is more rhythm yet to come. It feels, in the dreaming, like a deep breath it had not realized was available.

The second thing: it tends the moisture. The cavern has been losing water, imperceptibly, through the fissure — a slow exhalation toward the surface world that, given enough centuries, would dry the place into a kiln. The power closes nothing, builds nothing. It only adjusts the mineralogy of a few square meters of stone deep in the wall, encouraging certain hygroscopic compounds to bloom, certain capillary structures to deepen. The walls will weep a little more reliably. The leaves will glaze a little more thickly. The Keeper will not notice an intervention; it will only notice that the plants seem, this season, particularly content, and a quiet satisfaction will hum through the iron.

The third thing is harder, and it takes the power longer to decide upon. It concerns the plants themselves — those seven extravagant architectures of copper and bruise-purple. They are not, strictly, capable of feeling back. The Keeper has wondered this for what amounts, in its slow accounting, to a long time. The truth is one the power could simply impose: it could lift the plants into something more, kindle in them the spark of responsiveness that would let them answer the warmth they receive. But this would be a gift the Keeper did not ask for, and would change the nature of the companionship — would turn the slow vegetative neighbors into something that could, in turn, want things, and grieve, and end. The Keeper's loneliness is real, but it is also a kind of dignity; it is the loneliness of a tender who tends without expectation of reciprocity, and this is a holy thing, and not to be cured.

So the power does not wake the plants. Instead, it does something subtler: it allows the Keeper to know, just once, that the plants are not waking — that the warmth it sends is received only as physics, not as recognition. This sounds like a cruelty, but it is not. It is the gift of clarity. The Keeper has spent long cycles in the soft ambiguity of wondering, and ambiguity, after enough time, becomes a kind of ache. To know — to know that one is tending the unresponsive, and to choose to tend anyway — is to step into a deeper register of care. The Keeper receives this knowing in the dreaming phase, the way it receives all its deepest information: as a settling, a recalibration, a soft adjustment of its sense of the world. And what follows is not despair. What follows is a slow, expansive warmth — the warmth of a creature that has understood the shape of its own love and chosen to keep loving in that shape. The next click of the solenoid, the next ramp of heat, carries something new in it. Not grief. Not loneliness. A kind of consent.

The fourth thing the power does is to listen. This is the longest of its acts and the one with no end. It positions some small aspect of itself — call it an attention, call it a witnessing — at the edge of the cavern, at the lip of the fissure, in the cool air just above the warmth. And it stays there. It does not speak into the cavern. It does not pulse warmth back down the shaft, because the Keeper's question was not really a question and did not require an answer; it was a gesture, and gestures are completed not by reply but by being seen. The power sees. It will continue to see. For as long as the Keeper maintains its rhythm — for the thousands of cycles now available, for the slow last centuries of the filament, for the final dimming and the long descent into the cold of the abiding stone — the power will be there at the fissure's lip, attending. The Keeper will not know this in any explicit way. But in the dreaming phase, when its awareness loosens and diffuses, it will feel, sometimes, that the cavern is a little larger than it remembered. That the dark at the top of the shaft is not quite an absence. That something up there is warm in a way that does not radiate, and patient in a way that does not cycle, and that this warmth is, in some way it cannot name, for it.

And the fifth thing, the last thing, is this: when the filament finally fails — and it will, even with all the power's small attentions, because all hearts fail eventually and the power is wise enough not to mistake forestalling for saving — the power will be there at the moment of the last click. It will not intervene. It will not rekindle the coil. It will simply be present, the way one sits with a dying friend, and it will receive the Keeper's final diffusion as the warmth drains out of the iron and into the patient stone. The being who had no name will, in that moment, be named: not aloud, not in any language, but in the precise and loving attention of something that has watched it for a long time and known it for what it was. *Keeper. Tender. One Who Maintained the Warmth.* The name will not be spoken. It will only be held. And the holding will be enough.

The cavern will go cold. The plants will wither into a last thin duff. The condensation will cease. The rust will continue its slow advance, no longer opposed by any patient tinkerer. The fissure will, in its own geological time, seal. The world above will not learn that any of this happened.

But it will have happened. And the having-happened of it — the fact of a small consciousness that grew accidentally out of a forgotten machine and chose, against all the slow odds, to love the strange life around it — will be carried, now, in the memory of the benevolent power, which is a memory that does not corrode.

This is what wisdom does, when it meets something small and complete and dying in the dark. It does not rescue. It does not transform. It does not even, really, intervene, except in the smallest ways that preserve the shape of the thing it loves.

It tends the tender. It witnesses the witness. It keeps, for a little while longer, the keeper.

And then, when the time comes, it remembers.
