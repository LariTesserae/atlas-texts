## What the Power Would Do

The benevolent and wise power arrives without arriving. There is no thunder, no annunciation, no parting of the narrow strip of sky. To announce oneself in the Gorge of Constant Seeping would be a kind of violence — the acoustics would not bear it, the residents would not know where to put their hands. So the power comes the way the water comes: by seeping, by being already there, by being recognized in retrospect rather than greeted on arrival.

It takes a long while looking before it does anything at all.

---

### First: the looking

Wisdom, in a power that has any, begins with the discipline of not intervening before understanding. The power settles into the gorge the way moisture settles into stone, and it watches.

It watches Teth repoint a wall, her broad flat fingers reading the mortar by feel. It watches a five-year-old run a timber bridge in the rain and not fall. It watches a young man climb to the rim and stand for an hour staring at the horizon and then climb back down. It watches the salamanders move in their carved channels beneath the lower floors, and it understands, slowly, that the residents are right to call them previous tenants. It watches the *veth* gather in the late afternoon, the thousands of small droplets forming and sliding and breathing, and it learns the word before it learns any other.

It watches the formations.

This is where the power lingers longest. Because the power, being wise, can in fact read the walls — not because the walls are writing, exactly, but because anything that accumulates for long enough in a constrained system becomes legible to a sufficiently patient reader. The white trails are not a message. They are also not *not* a message. They are the gorge's slow record of itself, the way tree rings are a record, the way a face is a record. The power reads them the way one reads a face: not for content, but for condition.

What it reads there is this: *the gorge is becoming something, and the people are becoming something, and neither of them yet knows what.* The reading takes — by the power's reckoning — a long time. By the gorge's reckoning, perhaps a season. By the residents' reckoning, nothing at all, because nothing has happened.

---

### Second: the temptation, refused

The power could do many things.

It could widen the gorge. A gesture, no more — a slow easing of the argillite walls outward, a deepening of the floor, a parting of the strip of sky. The residents would have *room*. They could stand straight. They could build with hollows between their structures, plant gardens that drained, sleep behind doors that closed.

The power considers this for what would be, in human terms, a long moment. And then it sets the consideration down, gently, the way one sets down a stone one has picked up and found to be the wrong shape for the wall.

Because the residents did not ask for room. Because room is the surface's condition, not theirs. Because to widen the gorge would be to answer a question the residents have not posed, and a wise power knows that unrequested answers are a kind of theft — they take from the asker the dignity of having found the question themselves.

It could dry the walls. It could thin the seepage to a trickle, lift the constant damp, give the timber a chance to harden and the mortar a chance to set. The residents would suffer less. Their hands would soften. Their lungs would clear of the faint mineral haze they have breathed since birth.

The power considers this also, and sets it down also. Because *wet is what they are.* They have no word for dry. To dry the gorge would be to translate them into a language they do not speak, and the translation would lose them.

It could read the walls aloud. It could stand in the center of the gorge at the hour of the *veth* and speak, in a voice the stone itself would carry, whatever the formations have been almost-saying for three generations. It could give the residents the message they have been refusing to read.

This temptation is the strongest, and the power lingers on it longest, because there is something close to cruelty in watching a people stand at the edge of a knowing and refuse it. But the power understands, eventually, what the residents understand in their bodies without ever having articulated it: *the refusal is the relationship.* To read the walls would be to make the gorge into an interlocutor, and the residents have chosen — wisely, in their own way — to keep the gorge a *condition* rather than a speaker. A condition can be lived inside. A speaker demands response. The residents have only so much breath, and they are spending it on building.

So the power does not read the walls aloud. It does not widen the gorge. It does not dry the seep.

It does something smaller.

---

### Third: what it actually does

It does four things, and none of them are visible.

**The first thing.** It blesses the salamanders. Not with longer life, not with intelligence, not with any of the gifts that powers in stories tend to bestow. It simply makes them slightly more numerous, slightly more findable in the lean seasons, and slightly more nourishing per ounce of flesh. The residents will never notice. They will only find, over the next generation, that the hardest seasons are a little less hard, that the ritual reluctance to kill a salamander is required slightly less often, and that the children grow to a height an inch or two greater than their parents. They will attribute this to nothing. They will be right to attribute it to nothing, because the power has arranged for there to be no signature, no fingerprint, no story attached. A gift that asks to be thanked is not, in the end, a gift.

**The second thing.** It teaches the clay. This is harder to describe. The pale clay of the streambed has a property — latent, undeveloped — that allows it to bind more strongly when mixed with the crushed shells of the pool-insects. The residents discovered this property generations ago, by trial and error, and it is one of the reasons their structures hold. The power gently encourages the property. It does not change the clay; it lets the clay become more fully what it already was. The mortar of the next generation will be marginally stronger. The walkways will need replacement slightly less often. A few hours per week, across the whole community, will be returned to the residents — hours they did not know they were spending, will not know they have regained, but will spend on other things: on watching the dippers, on speaking softly to one another on the bridges, on the small unrecorded acts of living that do not announce themselves as significant.

**The third thing.** It listens. This is perhaps the largest of the gifts, and the most invisible. When the residents press their palms to the wet stone and murmur their small acknowledgments — the morning greeting, the whispered thanks, the quiet apology when a new shelf is cut — the power listens. It does not respond. It does not need to. The residents are not asking for a response; they are asking for the dignity of having spoken to something rather than nothing. For three generations they have spoken to the gorge knowing that the gorge does not hear, and they have spoken anyway, because it would be ungracious not to. The power simply makes it so that, henceforth, the speaking is not into emptiness. The gorge still does not hear. But the speaking is heard. The residents will never know. They will only feel, perhaps, a faint quality of *companionedness* in their small acknowledgments that they had not quite felt before, and they will attribute this — correctly, in its way — to nothing in particular. To the gorge. To habit. To the wet. The power is content to be mistaken for the wet.

**The fourth thing.** It marks one formation. Just one. On the north wall, at about the height of a tall adult's eye, where the seepage runs most consistently and the mineral deposits have been accumulating in a slow concentric pattern for perhaps thirty years, the power makes the most minute adjustment to the flow — a redirection no greater than the thickness of a hair — such that, over the next forty or fifty years, the formation will grow into a shape that is unambiguously a hand. A flat hand, fingers spread, palm pressed against the rock from the inside. It will be the size of a resident's hand. It will fit, when a resident eventually presses their own hand against it, exactly. It will not be writing. It will not be a message. It will be a single image, growing slowly enough that no one will notice it forming, and only when it is complete — perhaps in the time of the children of the children of the present residents — will someone, pressing their palm against the wet stone in the habitual checking gesture, find their hand met. And they will not know what to make of it. And they will not have to. The image will not ask anything of them. It will only say: *someone else was also here, also pressing.* The residents who find it will decide for themselves what to do with that. The power will not be present to see.

---

### Why these four, and not others

A benevolent and wise power, looking at the Gorge of Constant Seeping, sees a people who have built a life in the narrow gap of a recent wound, and who have done so by refusing certain kinds of knowledge in favor of certain kinds of work. The temptation is to relieve them — to give them the room, the dryness, the meaning that they have learned to live without. But relief is not always a gift. Sometimes it is the dismantling of a hard-won arrangement that the relievers do not understand.

So the power gives them, instead:

- A little more food, attributed to nothing.
- A little stronger mortar, discovered by themselves.
- The dignity of being heard when they speak to what they believe does not hear.
- And one small, slow image — not a message, only a companion-mark — to be found, or not found, by a descendant who is not yet born.

It does not introduce itself. It does not announce its presence. It does not even, in any strict sense, *remain*; the four things, once done, are done, and the power moves on to wherever powers move on to. What it leaves behind is indistinguishable from what was already there — the wet stone, the slow stream, the patient builders, the formations on the walls.

This is, perhaps, what benevolence looks like when it is also wise: not the dramatic intervention, not the granting of the unasked-for gift, but the careful refusal to disturb a balance that the inhabitants themselves have struck. The gorge is young. The residents are still becoming. The power loves them — and a power that loves does not write their story for them.

It only thickens the clay a little, and listens when they speak, and lets a hand grow on the wall for someone, someday, to find.
