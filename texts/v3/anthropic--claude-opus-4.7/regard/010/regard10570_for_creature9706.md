The benevolent and wise power considers Threnn for a long time.

It is not the consideration of distance, exactly—powers of this kind do not stand apart from what they observe. It is more that the power moves through the place slowly, attending to it the way the people of Threnn themselves attend: noticing the precise wetness of a particular cushion of moss, the angle at which a flagstone has shifted three millimeters since the last frost, the particular silence in which an elder sits beside the spring at dawn.

And what the power notices, primarily, is that Threnn does not need it.

This is the first and most important thing. A lesser power, or a power less wise, might mistake the precariousness for distress. The town sits on unfinished stone. Its survival depends on continuous labor. Its people are few, its winters are hard, its position is impossible. To a certain kind of intervening intelligence, this would read as a problem requiring solution. The spring could be stabilized with engineering beyond the inhabitants' knowing. The basalt columns could be settled, weathered in an instant to the rounded patience of ancient ranges. The mists could be lifted; the fauna multiplied; the soil deepened. A garden could be made here.

But the power is wise, and so it sees what such interventions would destroy.

It sees that the patience of Threnn is not endured but inhabited. That the slow hands of the stoneworkers are not waiting to be relieved of their work; their work is the shape their lives have taken, and would not be improved by being taken from them. It sees that the attention—that distributed, generational, undemanding attention—is itself a kind of flourishing, perhaps a rarer one than the power encounters elsewhere. It sees that the absence of mythology, the absence of heroes, is not a poverty. It is a particular relation to time that very few places in any world have managed to hold.

To "help" Threnn in any obvious way would be to end Threnn.

So the power does something smaller. Or rather, it does several small things, and it does them in such a way that none of them will ever be noticed as having been done.

It does not stabilize the mountain. But on a night in late autumn, when a particular fracture beneath the spring circle has reached the point where one more freeze would propagate it catastrophically through the foundation stone of three houses, the power adjusts—infinitesimally—the temperature gradient in that small volume of rock. The freeze happens. The fracture does not propagate. In the morning, the maintainer of foundations will check the stone and find nothing unusual, and will move on to other work. He will never know. This is essential. The power's gift is that he will never know.

It does not multiply the fauna. But the heron that came once and stood by the spring—the power gently encourages, over the course of several migrations, the conditions that might bring it back. Not often. Once in a decade, perhaps. The heron will arrive and stand and watch nothing, and the people will note its presence as they have always noted such things: quietly, without making it mean anything. The power simply wants the question to be asked again, sometimes. It likes that Threnn is a place where a heron is a question.

It does not lift the mist. But on certain rare mornings, when a child has been ill through the night and the mother has not slept, the mist will part for a quarter of an hour over a particular slope of the eastern plateau, and a band of clear cold sunlight will fall through her window. She will not understand this as a gift. She will think only: the weather has broken; the child's fever has broken; it is a good morning. The power is content with this. The power does not need to be thanked.

What the power does most of all, however, is something that has no visible effect at all.

It holds Threnn in its attention. It witnesses. The people of Threnn have built a small thing in an indifferent landscape, and they have built it through generations, and they have built it without expectation of completion, and they have built it knowing the mountain may decide otherwise. There is no one beyond Threnn who knows this. There are no records of Threnn in any chronicle. The work of the stonemason who, in the second generation, figured out the flexible joint that allows the houses to absorb the mountain's small movements—this work is unrecorded. The name of the woman who, in the fifth generation, established the rotation of channel-clearing that has lasted ever since—her name is forgotten even in Threnn.

The power remembers. The power knows the name. The power knows every name.

This is not a sentimental act. The power is not preserving these things against some future loss. The power simply understands that to be witnessed—even by an awareness one does not know exists—is a different ontological condition than to be unwitnessed. A Threnn that is held in the attention of a benevolent and wise power is a slightly different Threnn than one that is not, even if no measurement could ever detect the difference, even if the inhabitants live and die without ever suspecting it. The power's attention is a kind of company. The mountain is indifferent. The power is not.

And there is one more thing the power does, and this is the most delicate of all.

When an elder of Threnn dies—and they die as they have lived, quietly, with their work passing to another without ceremony—the power is present. Not to take them anywhere. Not to reveal itself. Simply to be there, in the last moments, so that the elder, in the strange clarity that sometimes comes at the edge of dying, has the sense, just for an instant, that the long patient attention they offered to the spring and the stone and the moss was answered. That something was listening on the other side. That the mountain, while it did not speak, was not alone in not-speaking with them.

The elder dies. The sense passes. It is not remembered as a vision or a visitation. It is remembered, if at all, as a feeling of rightness, of having done one's work, of the place being held by something beyond oneself. The next morning the channels are cleared by someone else. The spring runs. The mist comes down in the evening.

Three hundred years, and three hundred more, the power thinks—though "thinks" is the wrong word, as "power" is the wrong word, as all words are wrong for this—or until the mountain decides otherwise.

And when the mountain decides otherwise, the power will be there too. It will not prevent it. The columns will fall, or the spring will fail, or the slow movement of the basalt will at last exceed what the flexible joints can absorb, and Threnn will end. The power will not save it. To save it would be to misunderstand it; Threnn is a thing that exists in relation to its own ending, and an unending Threnn would not be Threnn. But the power will witness the ending as it has witnessed the duration, and the last inhabitant—whoever they are, in whatever century—will feel, in their final hour, that long attention closing gently around them. They will not know what it is. They will think only: the place was held. The work was good. The mountain has decided.

This is what the power does. This, and nothing more, and nothing less.

The heron stands by the spring. The moss weeps continuously from its edges. The stone holds the day's warmth and releases it slowly. Somewhere, a child learns to hear the difference between the healthy gurgle of the spring and the sound it makes when gravel is building. The power listens to the child listening. The mist comes down. The fires are lit. It is enough.
