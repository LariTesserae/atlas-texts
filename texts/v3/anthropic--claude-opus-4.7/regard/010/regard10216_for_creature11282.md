They come at dusk, when the long-winged birds are passing.

Not as light, not as voice, not as any of the forms that lowland stories give to powers. The benevolent and wise do not announce themselves to a place like this; announcement is a lowland habit. They come at the pace of the mountain, which means they have, in a sense, been coming for a long time already, and the arrival is only the moment a thing that was always approaching becomes near enough to notice.

What they notice first — because they are wise, and wisdom is mostly the discipline of noticing in the right order — is that nothing here is broken.

This is the difficulty.

A benevolent power arriving at a starving village knows what to do. A benevolent power arriving at a city in plague knows what to do. A benevolent power arriving at a child weeping in a dry field knows what to do, and does it, and is thanked, and goes. But here: the trees comb the fog and the basins fill and the pots are made of the grandmothers and the disputes are tended like shrines and the dying sit at their birth-edges and watch the curve of the world. The town is not in want. It is not even, in the way the powers usually mean the word, suffering. It is *diminishing*, yes — there are fewer of them than there were — but the diminishment is not a wound. It is the shape of the thing itself. To heal it would be to end it.

The power sits with this for what, from outside, would be a long time. From inside the summit's pace, it is the right amount of time. It sits at the western edge, unseen, beside a shrine-keeper who is also sitting, and watches what the shrine-keeper watches, which is the light going.

Several temptations present themselves, and the power, being wise, recognizes each as a temptation and sets it down.

*To bring water.* A spring could be made to rise in the rock. The jars would not need to be carried up the stair. The climbers would be spared. But the climbers are not, in themselves, a problem to be spared; the climbing is half the web of obligation, and the obligation is half the town. Water given freely would unmake the knotted cords in every doorway. The town would be richer in water and poorer in itself, and the second poverty would be the deeper one. The power sets this down.

*To make them many.* The town could be filled. Children could come in numbers; the lanes could thicken; the diminishment could be reversed. But the summit is small, and the trees are slow, and the fog is only so much fog. To make them many would be to make them hungry, and then to have to feed them, and then to have to keep feeding them, and the slow rule would be broken, and they would become a people who lived at the lowland pace on a mountaintop, which is to say they would become tourists in their own town. The power sets this down.

*To make them last.* The mountain could be held. The powdering could be stopped. The cliffs could be fixed in their apricot color forever, and the people fixed with them, generation after generation tending the same shrines for an unending time. But they have already said, in the long vowels at dusk, that they do not wish this. They understand themselves to be in the same business as the rock. To exempt them from that business would be to take from them the thing they most quietly know about themselves. The power sets this down.

*To be known.* The power could reveal itself. Could be thanked. Could become, in time, a fifth shrine, a named thing at a fifth edge that the summit does not have. But the four edges are the four edges because the summit has four edges; a fifth would be a lie about the shape of the place. And the shrine-keepers do not pray. They sit. A power that wished to be prayed to would have to teach them to pray, which would be to teach them to be a different people. The power sets this down.

What is left, after the temptations are set down, is small. This is usually the case.

The power does three things. They are so small that no one in the town notices any of them as an event, and this is the point; an event would be a lowland thing, and the power has, by now, taken on something of the manners of the place.

The first thing. On a particular night, when the cloud rolls up the cliffs and sits on the summit, the fog is a little heavier than it would otherwise have been. Not much. A hand's more water in each basin. The trees comb what is given them; the trees do not know the difference. The townspeople, in the morning, do not know the difference either, because the difference is within the range of ordinary variation, and the slow rule does not record variation within its range. But over a generation, and another, the basins are a little fuller than the long arithmetic of the summit would have allowed, and the diminishment slows — not stops, slows — by an amount so small that no shrine-keeper sitting at any edge could ever name it. The town will still end. It will end a little later. The last family at the last edge will sit a few more dusks than they would have, and will not know they were given them, and this not-knowing is part of the gift; a gift known is a debt, and the knotted cords do not have a place for a debt to something that will not come to collect.

The second thing. There is a child, this year, born at the southern edge, whose eyes are the usual startling pale. The power does nothing to the child. The power simply, in passing, *notices* the child — and the noticing is a kind of blessing that the powers can give without altering anything, a witnessing so attentive that the thing witnessed becomes, for a moment, fully itself. The child will grow up to be a potter. Her hands will be the long-fingered hands. She will, late in her life, make a particular cup, no different from any other cup, apricot-colored, containing in some literal and unremarked way her grandmothers, and she will give this cup to a climber at the top of the stair, and the climber — who is the third thing — will drink from it, and will sit down, and will not go back down for a long time, and when he finally descends he will be slower, and the lowland will feel to him like a place of unseemly hurry, and he will tell, in the lowland, in halting words that do not carry the soft consonants well, what it was like up there. He will not tell it well. No one tells it well. But one person, hearing him, will decide to climb. And that person will be the kind who moves at the other pace. And one morning, without ceremony, they will be of the town.

This is the third thing: the power makes the climber thirsty, at exactly the right moment, in exactly the right lane, so that the cup is offered and accepted. Nothing more. A small adjustment to a throat. The kind of thing a power can do without being a power about it.

And then the benevolent and wise withdraw, at the pace of the mountain, which is to say they are withdrawing already and have been withdrawing since they came, and the withdrawal will take as long as the arrival took, which is a long time by the body's pace and no time at all by the rock's.

What they leave behind is exactly what they found: a town on a stub of mountain, diminishing, tending its shrines, arguing in the lanes, drinking its grandmothers from apricot cups, watching the curve of the world go dark from four edges. Unchanged. Slightly more itself. Given a little more time to be the thing it is, and no more than a little, because more than a little would be a different gift, and a different gift would be for a different people.

The shrine-keeper at the western edge, sitting beside where the power sat, feels at one point in the evening a small warmth at her shoulder, like the warmth of a stone that has held heat since some earlier age. She does not turn to look. Turning to look is a lowland habit. She sits, and watches the light going, and after a while the warmth is gone, and the long-winged birds are passing, and she begins, very quietly, in the long vowels, to sing.
