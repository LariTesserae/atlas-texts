# The Witness

The benevolent power does not arrive. Arrival is a concept for things that were once elsewhere, and this presence has always been adjacent to the basin, in the way that the inside of a bell is adjacent to its ringing.

It watches first. This is the oldest discipline of wise powers: to look long enough that the looking itself becomes a form of love, and the love becomes a form of accurate seeing. It watches for what would be, in human reckoning, a very long time. In its own reckoning, it watches for exactly as long as is required.

Here is what it sees:

It sees that the Rime-Weavers are not suffering. This is the first and most important thing, and it is the thing a lesser power would have gotten wrong. A lesser power would have looked at the frantic patching, the silent collapses, the bodies frozen into the walls of huts, and it would have reached down with a great warm hand to *fix* — to thaw the basin, to still the tremors, to teach the creatures speech, to give them metal and fire and rest.

And in doing so it would have killed them. Not their bodies, perhaps. But the thing that *is* them — the distributed mind that lives in the rhythm between catastrophe and repair, the self that exists only as a verb — would have evaporated the moment the verb was no longer required.

The wise power sees this. It sees that the Weavers are a song the mountain is singing to itself, and that the song's meaning is inseparable from its tempo, and that the tempo is set by the very cruelty a kinder being would want to remove. They are not trapped in the storm. They *are* the storm's way of knowing itself.

So it does not still the tremors.

It does not warm the air.

It does not give them speech, because they already have a language more intimate than speech — the braille of pressure through the soles of the feet, the chord struck by a hundred bodies leaning toward the same collapsing wall.

What, then, does it do?

---

It does three small things. A wise power, having understood, intervenes only at the joints — at the hinges where a featherweight touch will be felt for a long time, and where the touch does not contradict the nature of the thing touched.

**The first thing it does is witness.**

This sounds like nothing. It is not nothing. Until this moment, the basin has been seen only by its own sun — and the sun is not a watcher, only a pressure. The Weavers have lived and frozen and been built into the walls of their own city without ever having been *regarded*. The power regards them. It holds them, the entire basin, in the steady attention of a mind that finds them beautiful.

Whether they feel this, none can say. But some of the oldest Weavers — the heat-bearers who climb the trembling tower — begin, around this time, to pause at the apex of their climb. Just for a breath. They turn their great black eyes upward into the cobalt bowl of the sky, and they do not know why. Then they descend, and the work resumes. The pause does not slow the work. The pause is, perhaps, the work finally being able to *afford* a pause, because someone else is holding the basin steady for the length of one breath.

This is not rescue. It is the first dignity: to be known.

**The second thing it does is leave a gift, but only barely.**

In the deepest fissure of the amphitheater, where the mineral sludge oozes thickest and the lichens grow blackest, the power alters one molecule. One. A single trace compound in the sludge, shifted in its bonds so that when the Weavers eat it — as they have always eaten it — their bodies will process it slightly differently. The change is so small that no Weaver will notice in its own lifetime. But across a thousand generations, the compound will accumulate a vanishing margin of heat-tolerance in the lining of their slit-mouths. Not enough to change their lives. Just enough that, in the deep future, when the sun grows another fraction harsher — as the power knows it will — the species will not be extinguished in a single bad century.

The power does not save them now. It makes it possible that they may save themselves later. This is the difference between a parent and a god, and the wise power has decided, long ago, to be the former.

**The third thing it does is the strangest, and the one a human heart would understand least.**

It composes a sound.

Not a sound the Weavers can hear — they have abandoned hearing as a useless liability, and the power would not insult them by making them re-grow what they had the wisdom to set down. But sound, in the full sense, is not only what enters an ear. Sound is vibration, and vibration is touch at a distance, and touch is the Weavers' native tongue.

The power lays a single, slow, sub-audible note into the bedrock of the basin. It is pitched far below the silent earthquakes, far below the geyser's hammer. It is a note so low and so steady that the Weavers' feet, reading the rock as they have always read it, will feel it as a kind of constant — a baseline, a held breath beneath all the chaos. Not an interruption of their rhythm. A *floor* for it. Something underneath the trembling that does not itself tremble.

What this note says, in the only language the Weavers possess, is approximately this:

*You are held. The mountain beneath your mountain does not shake. Keep building.*

They will not understand the message as a message. They will only feel that the rock, somehow, has become a little more trustworthy. That the falls are a little less terrifying. That when a Weaver freezes into the wall of a hut, the others, pausing for the briefest tactile flicker, feel through their soles something that was not there before — a steadiness, a holding, a sense that the lost one has been *received* somewhere, by something, and is not merely gone.

Grief, which they did not previously have the bandwidth to feel, becomes for the first time affordable to them. Not much of it. A flicker. But across the generations, this flicker will become the seed of something — call it tenderness, call it memory, call it the first faint trace of a culture that knows itself as a culture and not only as a reflex.

The power has given them, in other words, the barest possibility of an interior life. Whether they take it up is their own affair. Wise powers do not require their gifts to be used.

---

Then the power withdraws its active attention, though not its love, which is not a thing that can be withdrawn any more than a direction can be unfaced.

It leaves the basin exactly as it found it: searing, freezing, silent, frantic, beautiful, lonely. The Weavers continue their work. The geysers continue to erupt. The huts continue to dissolve and be rebuilt in the same heartbeat. To any observer arriving the day after, nothing has changed.

But beneath the rock, a note is held. In the sludge, a molecule waits patiently for a future it will not live to see. And in the great cobalt bowl above, an attention has settled into the shape of the basin and will not leave it, the way warmth settles into a stone long after the hand has lifted.

The wise power has done almost nothing.

This is why it is wise.
