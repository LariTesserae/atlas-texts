# The Visitation

The benevolent power did not arrive. It had always been there, the way warmth is always in a stone before you notice you are cold. But on a particular day — though days here were only a convention of shadow against spire — it turned its full attention toward the Fane, the way one turns a face toward a small sound in another room.

It saw the spires. It saw the lichens in their slow centuries. It saw the scuttling shadow-things in the canyons doing what shadow-things have always done. And it saw the settlement: the chimneys, the gleaming towers, the trembling ground, the feverish bright minds inside their sleek shells, building, building, building.

It listened for a long time before it did anything at all.

---

What it heard, beneath the rumble of machinery, was this: a sound like someone running in a dream. The runner cannot stop. The runner does not remember beginning. The runner is afraid that if they slow down, they will discover what they are running from, and the discovery will be worse than the running.

The power understood. It had seen this shape before, on many worlds. A people, gifted and clever, who had mistaken motion for meaning, who had built a magnificent engine around an unexamined ache.

The power considered.

It could have done many things. It could have stilled the machines with a thought. It could have poured water into that thirsty valley until the lichens drowned in their own astonishment. It could have appeared in the sky as a face and given a commandment. Lesser powers do these things and call it help. This power was older than that.

Wisdom, it had learned, is mostly a matter of restraint — of doing the smallest possible thing that allows the larger thing to happen on its own.

So this is what it did.

---

**First**, it did nothing. For what would have been, in the reckoning of the Fane, three of their long radiant years. It simply watched, and in watching, blessed. To be truly seen by something that wishes you well is itself a kind of nourishment, even when the seen one does not know they are being seen. The lichens grew a little brighter in those years. The shadow-creatures bore healthy young. The inhabitants did not notice, but their dreams — those few who still dreamed — became marginally less brittle.

**Second**, it placed a small thing in the path of one of them.

Not a prophet. Not a chosen one. The power had long ago given up on chosen ones; they tended to crack under the weight, or worse, to thrive under it. Instead it chose an ordinary engineer — a middling technician of the seventh tier, whose exoskeleton bore the scuffs of competent but unremarkable labor. Her designation does not matter. Call her the one who paused.

What the power placed in her path was this: a stone. An unremarkable stone, in an unremarkable corridor between two unremarkable workshops. The stone was shaped, by the merest nudge of probability over a thousand years of wind, so that when she walked past it on her way to repair a coolant manifold, a particular angle of late light caught it, and it looked — for just one instant — like the face of someone she had loved as a child and forgotten.

She stopped.

She did not know why she stopped. She stood in the corridor for perhaps eleven seconds. In those eleven seconds, the feverish engine inside her, which had been running since she was decanted, missed a beat. Into that missed beat, a question fell — small, soft, unwelcome:

*What am I doing?*

She shook herself and went on to the manifold. She did not speak of it. She did not, for many cycles, even remember it. But the question had been planted, and questions of that kind, once planted, do not die. They wait.

**Third**, the power did the same thing, in slightly different forms, for several hundred others. A reflection in a polished plate. A particular pitch of machine-whine that resembled, just barely, a lullaby no one living remembered. A pattern in the lichen on a southern cliff that, if you tilted your head, looked like a hand opening. None of these were miracles. None violated any law of the place. The power had only arranged for the existing materials of the world to whisper, very softly, in a register the inhabitants had trained themselves not to hear.

**Fourth**, and this was the largest thing it did, the power did not save them from what was coming.

Because something was coming. The trembling of the ground was not metaphor. The planet was alive in a way the inhabitants had refused to investigate, because investigation would have required slowing down, and slowing down was the one forbidden act. A reckoning was building beneath their feet — perhaps a century away, perhaps less. The power could have averted it. It chose not to.

This is the hardest part of benevolence, and the part that lesser powers never learn: that to remove the consequence is often to remove the only teacher patient enough to reach the student. The power loved them too much to rob them of the moment when they would finally have to stop and listen.

But it did this: it made sure that when the reckoning came, those few who had paused — the one who paused, and the others like her, who by then would number perhaps a few thousand, scattered through the towers like seeds in a dry pod — would recognize what was happening. They would not panic. They would know, with a knowledge they could not account for, that the trembling was not an enemy but a voice. And they would be ready to translate.

The settlement, in its current form, would not survive. The power grieved this, in its way. It grieves everything that ends, even the things that need to end. But what would rise after — a quieter people, fewer, slower, with their exoskeletons grown over by patient lichen and their machines repurposed for listening rather than for drowning out — that, the power thought, would be worth the grief.

---

And then the power withdrew its full attention, though not its love, which is a different thing and does not withdraw. It turned its face toward another small sound in another room of the universe.

Behind it, in the Fane of Endless Radiance, a technician of the seventh tier walked past a stone in a corridor and, for eleven seconds, was free.

It was enough. It is always, in the end, enough. The smallest pause is a door, and doors, once opened even a crack, tend over time to swing.
