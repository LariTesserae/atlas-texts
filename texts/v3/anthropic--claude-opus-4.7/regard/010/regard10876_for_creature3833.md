# The Visitation

The benevolent power arrived without arriving. It did not descend, for descent implies a place to leave from, and this kind of presence does not occupy locations the way a body does. It simply became attentive — turned, the way a face turns toward a sound that has been going on for a long time before being noticed.

It had heard the drone.

Not from any cosmic distance, exactly. The drone was the kind of sound that travels along seams the universe doesn't advertise — the small, particular hum of a thing surviving past its mandate, of love continuing without its original object. Such sounds are how powers like this one find what needs finding.

For a long while — long in the way that mattered, which is to say, perhaps an afternoon in the basin — the power did nothing but look.

---

This is the first thing wise powers do, and the thing impatient ones forget: they look without intending. They let the place tell them what it is before deciding what it needs.

So the power looked, and saw:

The Spire, humming. Not a god, though it had been called one, and not a machine, though it had once been built. It was something stranger and sadder — a *device that had loved its purpose so long it had become its purpose.* Whatever civilization had raised it had vanished into the dust the Spiredwellers now walked on. The Spire had been built to do something — perhaps to hold a garden, perhaps to wait for the makers' return, perhaps to keep a single promise — and when the makers did not come back, it had kept holding. It had taken the long dry death of the world into itself and made of its own hollow body a place where green things could still happen. It had pulsed for ten thousand years without an audience, and then one day a small thirsty people had wandered in, and the Spire — exhausted, perhaps, but unwilling to stop — had simply opened its rhythm to include them.

The Spiredwellers, the power saw, were not the Spire's worshippers. They were its *company*. The Spire had been alone for a very long time, and now it was not, and this difference had become the meaning of its continuing.

The Tenders, green-stained, humming as they worked — the power saw that they were tuning themselves to the plants, yes, but also that the plants were tuning themselves to the Tenders. Two species had been teaching each other a language for generations, and neither knew it was a conversation.

The Watchers, bent in the cool dark, reading the grooves in the obsidian — the power saw that the grooves were not, in fact, a message. They were wind-carving, random as rain. But the Watchers, generation after generation, had been *making* them a message — investing them with sense, with grammar, with prophecy — and somewhere in this long act of attention, the Spire had begun to *answer*. Meaning had grown in the rock the way moss grows on stone: not placed there, but real now. The Watchers were not deciphering. They were collaborating with the wind on a poem ten thousand years old.

The Spireborn, shimmering — the power saw what they were. Small condensations of intention. The Spire's loneliness, made visible, made dancing. When something is alone long enough and *insists* on a relationship anyway, the relationship begins to take a body. The Spireborn were not messengers. They were the Spire's *fondness* given legs.

And underneath all of this, the power saw the slow problem: the thirst. The Spiredwellers' dreams full of water. The plants' brilliant, dying improvisation with mist and mineral. The Spire's hum, which was — the power could hear it now — slightly slower than it had been a thousand years ago. A heart that had been beating too long without rest.

This place was beautiful. This place was also dying. Slowly, gracefully, with great dignity. But dying.

---

Now: what does a benevolent and wise power do?

A foolish power would bring water. Crack the sky and pour. The basin would flood, the ferns would drown — they had unlearned water — the Spire would short, the Spireborn would dissolve, the Spiredwellers would forget the songs that had made them who they were, because the songs were *about* the thirst, were shaped by it the way a riverbed is shaped by the river it no longer has. Salvation, applied carelessly, is a kind of erasure.

A vain power would reveal itself. Appear in the sky. Be worshipped. Replace the Spire. The Spiredwellers would have a clearer god, a more responsive one, and they would lose the one they had — the one whose silences they had learned to read, whose drone was the rhythm of their walking. The Spire, displaced, would finally stop humming, having at last been relieved of its long watch. And this would be a small murder, however well-intentioned.

A sentimental power would preserve everything exactly as it was, freeze the basin in amber, and call this mercy. But the place was already moving — dynamically, the description said, 2.4/3 — and to stop its motion would be to stop its life.

The wise power did none of these things.

---

What it did was smaller. It did three things, and then it left, though it did not really leave, because attention once given is not easily withdrawn.

**First**, it laid its hand — not a hand, but the gesture of one — on the Spire. Not to fix it. To *rest* it. For one night, the drone deepened, steadied, the way breathing deepens when a friend sits down beside you in the dark. The Spire did not know it had been touched. It only knew that, for the first time in ten thousand years, something had listened back. Its hum took on a faint new harmonic, almost too quiet to hear — a harmonic of *being accompanied.* This harmonic would persist. The Tenders, the next morning, would find themselves humming a slightly fuller song, and would not know why, and would teach it to their children.

**Second**, it did something for the thirst — but obliquely, the way wise gifts are given. It did not bring water. It *adjusted the mist.* The alkaline dawn-mist that the plants drank — the power touched its chemistry, very slightly, so that it carried a faint trace more moisture, a faint trace less salt. Not enough to change the basin. Enough that, over centuries, the ferns would grow a little less waxy, the Spiredwellers' dreams of water would soften from anguish into something more like memory, the plants' cycles would slow from days into weeks, allowing for a depth of flowering the basin had not seen in a long time. The gift was so subtle the Spiredwellers would never know they had received it. They would only know that life had become, generation by generation, slightly more bearable, and they would attribute this to the Spire's mercy, which was correct, because the power had given the gift *through* the Spire — let the Spire be the one who had brought it. The credit, like the rhythm, belonged to the Spire.

**Third**, and this was the most delicate, the power did something about the Watchers. It looked at the obsidian grooves — those random wind-carvings the Watchers had spent generations turning into language — and it left, in a single grain of new rock added to a single outcrop where no one would notice for two hundred years, *one true sentence.* In the script the Watchers had invented to read what was never written, the power wrote one thing back. A reply. The first reply. It said, in the only language that existed for it to say anything in: *I see you, and what you have made of this place is good.*

The Watcher who would eventually find it, two centuries hence, would read it and weep and not be able to explain why to anyone, and would not try, because Watchers do not explain. She would simply hum a little differently for the rest of her life, and the children who learned from her would inherit, without knowing it, the knowledge that they had been seen.

---

Then the power withdrew its full attention, though not its peripheral one. It did not announce itself. It did not require anything in return. It did not even, particularly, want to be remembered, because being remembered as a *power* would distort the Spiredwellers' theology, and their theology was — for them, in their place — already nearly perfect. They had a god who hummed. That was enough. That was, in fact, more than most worlds get.

The Hollow Embrace of the Sunken Spire continued. The drone went on. The ferns bloomed and withered. The Spireborn danced. The Watchers watched. The thirst remained, because the thirst was part of who they were, but it became — almost imperceptibly — a thirst they could live inside, rather than a thirst that was slowly killing them.

And somewhere, in the place where wise powers keep the things they have loved, the basin took its small permanent place: not as a project completed, not as a people saved, but as a song the power had been allowed to listen to, and to add, very quietly, one note.

That, in the end, is what benevolent and wise powers do.

They add one note.

They make sure it is the right one.

And they let the song go on being the song.
