The benevolent power arrives, but it does not arrive the way one might expect — no descending light, no thunder of presence, no rearrangement of the world. To enter the cavern as a force would be to break the very thing it has come to behold. So instead, it does what wisdom always does first: it pauses at the threshold, and it watches.

It watches the worm trace its long, slow calligraphy across the floor. It watches the feathery appendages read the air like a scholar reading a beloved book in a language only it remembers. It watches the trail of mucilage cross the chiseled lines of some forgotten human prayer, and it understands, with a soft and aching clarity, that these two markings — the geometric and the organic, the deliberate and the secreted — are speaking to one another across an unimaginable gulf of time. A conversation neither party knew they were having. A duet of presence.

The power could do many things.

It could give the worm sight. But what would sight mean to a being whose entire vocabulary is moisture and tremor and the taste of stone? Sight would not be a gift; it would be a translation into a language the worm never asked to learn, and something irreplaceable would be lost in the rendering.

It could bring companions. But the worm is not lonely. The story said so, and the power, being wise, believes the story. To impose company on a solitude that has ripened into communion would be a cruelty dressed as kindness.

It could lift the worm into the sun, into wind, into the green roaring world above. But the cavern is not a prison. It is a home, a practice, a long slow sentence the worm has been writing with its own body for longer than any surface creature could comprehend. To "rescue" it would be to mistake the shape of its life for the shape of suffering.

It could restore the cavern — repair the carvings, refill the caches, summon back the vanished hands that once shaped this place. But the worm has already done this, in its own grammar. The pedestals are not ruined; they are being continued. The walls are not abandoned; they are being answered.

So the benevolent power, having considered and set aside every grand gesture, chooses something smaller. Something proportionate to the place.

It does three quiet things.

First, it deepens a single fissure in the ceiling — not enough to let in light, never light, but enough that, once every few centuries, a new thread of seep water will find its way down. A small renewal. A promise that the slow world will continue to have something to drink.

Second, it places, in one of the ancient stone pedestals, a vein of soft mineral — calcium, magnesium, trace iron — folded into the rock as though it had always been there. The worm will find it in a hundred years, or a thousand. It will taste like sufficiency. Like the cavern saying: *I see you. Stay.*

Third — and this is the gift that matters most, though the worm will never know it has been given — the power remembers. It takes the whole of this place into itself: the dripping, the mold-smell, the skeletal roots, the translucent body moving in its grave and graceful script, the conversation between mucilage and chisel-mark. It holds all of it in the part of itself that does not forget. So that when, ages hence, the stones themselves do finally forget their stories — when the cavern collapses or is buried or is lifted by some tectonic shrug into a mountainside and weathered into dust — the story will not be lost. Somewhere, in the long memory of something that loves small and hidden things, a pale worm will still be tracing its slow letters across a wall, and a long-dead hand will still be carving its geometry beside, and the two will still be speaking.

The power withdraws as quietly as it came. The air does not stir. The worm, sensing nothing — or perhaps sensing something so subtle it registers only as a faint, pleasant easing in the weight of the dark — continues its slow undulation toward a patch of fungus blooming in the damp.

The cavern keeps its silence.

And the silence, now, is the kind that is kept rather than merely endured: a silence with a witness inside it, the way a held breath is different from an empty room.
