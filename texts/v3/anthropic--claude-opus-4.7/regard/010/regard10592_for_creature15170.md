The benevolent power considers them for a long time before doing anything.

This is the first thing worth saying: it does not arrive in a hurry. It is wise, which means among other things that it has learned the cost of arriving in a hurry to a place that has been becoming itself for only a little while. It has seen what happens when a kindness lands on a thing still deciding what shape to take — how the kindness becomes the shape, and the thing inside is lost to its own future. So it watches.

It watches a long time. Long enough to learn the rhythm of the cloth-wringing in the mornings, the way a child at four already knows three different meanings in the trembling of the rock, the way the bench at the scarp is never empty and never assigned. It watches the discard pile of blackened reed-cloth and sees that the liverworts are doing there, in miniature and slowly, the work the whole hamlet is doing: turning the cast-off into the next surface. It watches an old woman wipe a wall and notices that she does not wipe it dry. She wipes it to a particular degree of wetness, the degree at which the stone seems to her to be most itself. The power had not known, before, that there was such a degree. It learns.

It notices that these people are not suffering. This matters. A lesser power, or a power less patient with its own benevolence, would have seen the pale skin and the cropped hair and the flavorless fish and the smoke that will not rise, and would have called this suffering, and would have acted. The wise power sees instead that the people have been pressed into the same shape as the stone, as the passage says, and that this pressing is not a wound. It is a fitting. They have fitted themselves to a place that is itself still arriving. They are part of how the place arrives.

So the power asks itself the only real question: *Is there anything they lack that they themselves would recognize as a lack?*

It considers giving them light — the soft colors restored, the edges sharpened. But they have a relationship with the softened light; their evenings at the bench depend on the dimming. To give them clarity would be to take their dusk.

It considers giving them abundance — fish with flavor, reeds that grow tall, dry ground. But the scarcity is the reason they share a single awareness across the roofs. Plenty would scatter them into separate houses with separate concerns. The hamlet would end, not by dying but by becoming something else that did not need to be a hamlet.

It considers giving them safety from the settling of the slab. But the settling is what they listen to. Without the tremors there would be nothing to read, and the children would lose the first literacy of their lives.

It considers, briefly and with some sorrow, simply revealing itself — letting them know they are seen and loved. But it understands that to be known by such a power is a weight, and these people are already carrying the exact weight they were made to carry. One more ounce, even an ounce of glory, would unbalance them.

What the power does, in the end, is small. Three things.

First: on a night when the slab settles harder than usual and a hairline crack opens beneath the outermost house — the kind of crack that in a year or two would have widened and taken a wall — the power closes it. Not visibly. Not with any flourish. It simply persuades the rock, atom by patient atom, to remember a slightly older configuration of itself. In the morning a man runs his hand along the base of the wall and feels nothing wrong, and thinks nothing of having felt nothing wrong, and goes on with his day. The hamlet keeps its outermost house. No one knows. This is the point.

Second: the power does something for the fish. Not the living fish — it leaves their flesh flavorless, because the people have built a use for flavorlessness; the bones are for needles, and a fish that tasted good would be eaten instead of unmade into tools. But the eyeless fish, drifting through the dark channels, have a kind of awareness the people have never suspected, a slow blind attention to the water that touches them. The power gives this attention, very gently, a small increase. Enough that the fish, in their drifting, begin to avoid the woven traps a little more often, but also — and this is the real gift — enough that they begin, without knowing it, to follow the cooler currents toward the scarp at evening. So that the person on the bench, hands on wet stone, will sometimes feel, through the rock, a faint additional motion: a living thing moving toward the edge of the made world at the same hour they themselves come to sit at it. Neither party will understand. But something in the hamlet's evenings will deepen by a fraction, and the deepening will be shared with a creature that has never been seen.

Third, and last, and smallest: the power leaves a thing at the bench. Not an object. An object would be found, examined, discussed, integrated or rejected, and either way it would become a story, and the story would change the place. Instead the power leaves a quality. It is hard to say in words what it leaves. The nearest word is *welcome*, but welcome implies a host, and there is no host here, only the stone deciding. Perhaps the better word is *recognition*. The power leaves, on the bench, the sense — available to anyone who sits there, never named, never insisted upon — that the act of sitting and feeling the patient movement from below is itself being received somewhere. That the listening is heard. Not heard by anything in particular. Just heard.

The people do not notice this either, in any way they could describe. But over the seasons that follow, the bench is sat upon a little longer in the evenings. The person there stays past the failing of the light, sometimes, and goes back across the roof bridges in full dark, and is not afraid. When asked why they stayed so long, they shrug and say the air was good. This is true. The air is good. It has always been good. It is only that now, when they breathe it, something in them is answered.

The power withdraws to its watching. It will do nothing else for a long time. It understands that the greatest kindness available to a place still becoming itself is to let it become, and to close — quietly, invisibly, without credit — only those cracks that would have ended the becoming too soon.

In a hundred years the slab will have settled into its final shape. The rills will have rounded their edges. The liverworts will have closed over the discard pile entirely. The people will be a little less translucent, perhaps, having found whatever equilibrium the matured stone permits. They will not remember that they were ever attended to. They will not need to. The attention was never for being remembered. It was for them, exactly as they were, on a wet slab of new rock, wiping the walls to the correct degree of wetness, listening for the rhythm that meant it was safe to cross.

The power, if it could be said to feel anything that simply, is glad.
