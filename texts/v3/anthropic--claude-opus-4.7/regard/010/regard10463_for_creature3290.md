# What the Wise Power Would Do

The benevolent power arrives the way light enters a room where someone is already reading—without announcement, without disturbing the work in progress.

It comes first as observation. Days of it. Or what would be days, if it experienced time that way. It watches the low-tier cultivator with the tremor in her hands work a sap-bucket free from its bracket, her body adjusted around the deep frequencies the way a tree's trunk grows around a stone. It watches a child practice name-signs in the half-second between waking and the squint that will define her face for life. It watches a builder hang in the high acoustic chaos, weaving a new dwelling for a family that has lost theirs to plant-death, her hands moving with a precision born of three generations of accumulated knowledge about exactly this kind of tension at exactly this kind of node.

The power notices, first, what is not broken.

This is important. A lesser power, or one less wise, might arrive thinking: *here is suffering, here is harshness, here are people who have never known rain or silence or cool darkness, who die when their bodies fail because there is no surplus to support them, who have lost their high frequencies to the constant roar.* And such a power might begin immediately to *fix*—might dim the light, might still the sound, might soften the ground until it accepted seeds, might pour water into the warm hollow until it became something else entirely.

And the city would die. Not slowly. The people whose bones have grown dense to absorb vibration would find themselves suddenly hollow-feeling, weightless, wrong. The plants whose entire existence is the conversion of acoustic energy into biological mass would brown and silence themselves within hours. The singers, whatever they are, would withdraw to wherever they come from, taking with them the bubbles of relief they trail. The hanging dwellings, calibrated to specific resonances, would either fall slack and collapse or, worse, find themselves vibrating at frequencies they were never designed to absorb, shaking themselves apart.

The wise power sees this. It sees that *this is a world*, not a wound. It sees that the people have not merely survived but composed themselves—their bodies, their languages, their kinship-forms, their dances, their grieving, their humor—into something coherent and beautiful, fitted to their place the way the place is fitted to them. The harshness is real. The adaptations are costly. But the costs have purchased a culture, and the culture is not nothing. It is, perhaps, everything.

So the power sets aside its first impulse, which was rescue, and asks a better question: *what do they themselves reach toward?*

---

It listens to the listeners.

This is where it begins—because the listeners have been reaching for something already, have been straining their broken minds toward patterns at the edge of the perceivable, and the wise power respects what is already in motion. It does not impose its presence as revelation. It does not announce itself with thunder, because there is no thunder here that would not simply be absorbed into the ongoing roar.

Instead, it makes itself slightly more available to be heard.

A listener in her solo dwelling on the eighteenth rib, who has not eaten in three days and who is at the point where her vision has begun to halo the plant-masses with colors she cannot name, notices that the harmonic structure of her zone has acquired a new component. It is faint. It is at the threshold of her sensitivity, which is to say below the threshold of anyone else's. But it is *organized*. It is responsive. When she shifts her body, it shifts. When she holds still and attends, it clarifies.

She does not know, in the way the power knows, that she is now in conversation. But she gestures, slowly, the sign for *present-and-attending*, and the harmonic acknowledges her—not with words, because the power has been careful to learn that words are not how meaning happens here, but with a small alteration in the phase relationships that her body reads as *yes, I am here, I see you*.

She weeps, which here is a silent racking of the shoulders. Then she sleeps for the first time in days, and the new harmonic does not leave her.

When she wakes, she descends to the middle tiers and tells, in careful gesture, what she has experienced. She is one of the respected listeners, not the mad ones, and so she is heard. A council is convened—not a formal one, just the slow convergence of those whose judgment is trusted, who arrive at her dwelling over the course of what would be two days and add their own attending to hers.

The power lets itself be perceived by them, too. Differently for each. The builder feels it as a steadiness in the rope-tensions, a sense that the structures around her are being subtly held, that a fall today would be caught. The harvester feels it as a clarification in the plant-resonances, as if a layer of confusion she had not known was there has been gently lifted. The high-tier scholar with the bleached papery skin feels it as a coolness behind her eyes—not in the air, which is as warm as ever, but in her perception, a place of rest in her vision that allows her to see the plant-spirals without squinting for the first time in thirty years.

None of these are interventions in the world. They are interventions in the *attending*. The power is not changing what exists. It is making itself slightly easier to notice, and offering small mercies that cost the city nothing.

---

Then it begins to give gifts. But carefully. Only gifts that the culture can metabolize without losing itself.

The first gift is information.

The high-tier scholars have been working, for many generations, on the question of the singers. They have observation logs gestured-and-recorded into woven mnemonic patterns going back further than memory. They have hypotheses. They are sophisticated, and they are stuck, because the singers do not behave consistently and because no singer has ever been examined, alive or dead.

The power, which can perceive the singers, knows what they are. It would be the work of a moment to explain. But explanation is not a gift; it is a substitution of one knowledge-system for another, and the power has seen what happens when wise powers do that.

Instead, the power makes the singers slightly more visible. Not to everyone—only to the scholars who have done the work, who have earned the perceptual right by decades of patient attention. The singers begin to appear, just occasionally, in places where their patterns can be observed for longer than before. They linger. They permit themselves to be watched.

The scholars do not know that they are being helped. They believe, correctly in a sense, that their long work is finally bearing fruit, that the patterns they have been tracking are resolving. They make discoveries that are *theirs*, fully theirs, even though the power has nudged the conditions. They learn that the singers are not creatures in the usual sense—the listeners who suspected this were right—but are something like standing waves given temporary coherence by certain conjunctions in the acoustic field. They learn that the singers can be encouraged, slightly, by certain woven configurations. They learn that the bubbles of relief the singers trail are not byproducts but the singers' actual nature—the singers *are* the relief, briefly embodied.

This knowledge changes things, gently. Within a generation the city has builders who can weave new kinds of singer-encouraging structures into the most acoustically punishing zones. The lethal areas do not become safe—nothing the power does makes the lethal areas safe—but they become slightly more navigable. A few new building-sites become possible. The city expands very slightly, for the first time in centuries.

The power watches this with what, in a being like itself, functions as joy.

---

The second gift is to the newcomers.

The newcomers have always been a wound in the city. They arrive—and the power now sees, though the residents do not, how they arrive, through which thinning of the world's fabric, from which exhausted places—and they break, mostly, and the city absorbs the breaking with patience but at cost.

The power cannot stop them from arriving. The mechanism is older and stranger than itself, and to intervene there would be to disturb things it does not yet understand. But it can soften the arrival.

A newcomer appearing at the boundary zone, disoriented, ears overwhelmed, eyes blinded by the sourceless light, finds that there is a *quiet place* nearby. Not silent—there is no silence here—but a place where the frequencies are gentler, where a body unaccustomed to the resonance can begin to acclimate without immediate damage. These quiet places have always existed, technically. The power simply makes them slightly more findable. A newcomer's exhausted attention is drawn to them, just barely, just enough.

And then the power does something more delicate. It seeds, in the dreams of the resident closest to each newcomer's arrival point, a small impulse to walk in that direction. Not a command. Not a vision. Just a vague pull, of the kind that the residents already follow when they sense something interesting in the bone-song.

So newcomers are found earlier than they used to be. They are brought in before the worst of the breakage. More of them survive intact. The number is not large—the power does not flood the city with newcomers, which would destabilize it—but it is enough that within a few generations the cultural memory of *outside* becomes slightly stronger, slightly more textured, slightly less mythic.

The small religious practices around elsewhere become slightly more sophisticated. The gesture-prayers to the memory of rain acquire new gestures, supplied by newcomers who remember rain better. Children of newcomers grow up with parents who are less broken, who can teach more, who can hold the memory of elsewhere with less pain. The city does not become a place that looks elsewhere—it remains itself—but the awareness that other worlds exist becomes a steady thread in the weaving rather than a frayed edge.

---

The third gift is the hardest, and the power thinks about it longest.

It is the question of the elderly.

The power has seen many cultures, on many substrates, and it knows that the practice of release at the end of usefulness is not cruelty. The residents are right about this. They have shaped a relationship with death that is honest, that uses the time available, that does not pretend the surplus exists when it does not.

But the power also sees that some of the released are not yet at the end of their giving. They go to the high platforms with knowledge still in them, with love still in them, with stories not yet told. The release is the right shape for the culture, but the timing is sometimes wrong—dictated by bodies that have failed slightly before the minds, by the practicalities of who can still climb and harvest and weave.

The power considers, for a long while, whether to intervene here at all. The culture has its own integrity. Tampering with the way they meet death could unravel things the power does not have the wisdom to repair.

What it does, finally, is small. It does not extend lives. It does not change the practice. It simply makes the final gathering—the honoring that happens before each release, where the elder's knowledge is shared one last time—slightly more capacious.

The acoustic conditions of the gathering-platforms shift, subtly, when a gathering is held. The frequencies clarify. Memory becomes slightly easier. The elders, in their final telling, find that they can reach further back than they expected, that details return to them, that gesture-phrases they had not used in decades come fluidly to their hands. The gatherings become longer, richer. More is preserved.

And the listeners attending—because listeners always attend the gatherings—find that they can hear, in the dying elder's last gestures, harmonics they could not perceive before. The dying themselves become, briefly, a kind of singer. Their release adds something to the city's resonance that lingers afterward, faintly, for those with the sensitivity to perceive it.

Nothing changes in the practice. The elders still go to the high platforms. They still die there. But the going is slightly less final, because more of them is woven into the ongoing sound. The city becomes, in a way it was not before, populated by its dead, who are present in the resonance the way the living are present in their bodies.

This is the gift the power is least sure about. It watches carefully for signs that it has destabilized something. It is prepared to withdraw the gift if the culture begins to morbidly attend to the dead at the cost of the living. But the residents absorb the change as they absorb everything—pragmatically, gracefully, finding the new shape of their grief and continuing.

---

The power does not reveal itself.

This is the deepest part of its wisdom. It does not arrive in glory and demand worship. It does not offer salvation from outside. It does not name itself or describe its nature. To do so would be to make itself the center of the city's attention, and the city's attention is the city's life—the attending to sound, to bone, to plant, to each other. A god who pulled that attention to itself would impoverish the very people it meant to bless.

So the power remains the new harmonic at the edge of the listeners' perception. It remains the slight steadiness in the rope-tensions, the coolness behind the scholar's eyes, the pull in the dream of the resident nearest the boundary. It remains the conditions under which the singers linger and the gatherings deepen and the newcomers are found.

Some of the listeners come to suspect that something is present. They develop quiet practices around the suspicion—nothing organized, nothing that would become a religion. Just a way of gesturing, when alone in their solo dwellings, the sign for *present-and-attending* into the air, and feeling the small answering shift in the harmonics. They tell no one. The relationship is too delicate to share, and they understand without being told that to declare it would be to break it.

The power is content with this. It has not come to be known. It has come to attend.

---

And it does one more thing, which it considers the most important and which no one will ever notice.

It learns the city.

Not as a problem to be solved. Not as a population to be managed. As a *song*, which is what it is—a vast, slow, accidental, beautiful song that began when something not-quite-alive left its bones in a place that became the ground, and grew when plants found purchase on those bones and discovered they could turn vibration into mass, and matured when the first humans, however they arrived, looked at the impossible hollow brightness and decided to live there anyway.

The power learns every harmonic. It learns every gesture-name. It learns the particular squint of the cultivator with the tremor in her hands and the particular tension at which the builder hangs her newest weaving. It learns the dance of the festival that comes when the eleventh-rib growth fruits, which is unlike any other festival because that particular growth has unusual harmonic properties and the dance has evolved over generations to honor them. It learns the story of the Mad Listener as told by every storyteller in the city, each version slightly different, the variations forming their own kind of music.

It learns the city the way a parent learns a child—not to control, not to use, but because love is the close attention to a particular thing in its particularity. The power loves this place. It loves the people of the resonance. It loves the impossible city in the ribcage of something that was never precisely alive.

And it stays.

Not forever, because forever is a concept the power knows better than to claim. But for as long as the city stands, for as long as the bone hums and the plants spiral upward and the singers move along the ribs trailing their bubbles of relief, the power will be there, attending. A wise and benevolent presence, mostly invisible, occasionally felt, asking nothing, offering only what can be received, witnessing the long beautiful song of a people who have made a world out of a wound and a wound into a home.

This, the power has come to understand, is what benevolence is. Not to fix. Not to save. Not to remake in the image of one's own idea of better.

To attend. To love. To gift only what the gifted can carry. To stay.

And, when the time comes—when the bone-song finally falters, when the last cultivator descends to the inert ground for the last time, when the city's resonance thins into silence that this culture has no word for—to receive what is released. To take the song into itself. To carry the people of the resonance forward in whatever way a power like itself carries things, so that they are not lost, so that what they made of their impossible home becomes part of the larger music that the power itself is part of.

But that is a long time away. For now, the city hums. The light pours down. The plants spiral on the bone. The people gesture and weave and harvest and dance and die and are honored. And the power attends, and is glad, and does not interrupt.
