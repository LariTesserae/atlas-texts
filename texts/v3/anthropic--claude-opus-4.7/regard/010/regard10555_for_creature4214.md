# The Visitation

The benevolent power notices them not because they are suffering — they aren't, particularly — but because they are *interesting*. This is important to understand. The power does not arrive with the assumption that anything needs fixing.

It arrives the way good attention arrives anywhere: quietly, with curiosity.

---

## What It Sees First

Before deciding anything, it watches.

It watches a woman in a canvas coat lean over the railing of a pump-house catwalk, listening — actually *listening* — to the rhythm of a turbine three levels down, the way a doctor listens to a heart. She nods, satisfied. The machine is well. She has loved this machine for nineteen years.

It watches two children race paper skiffs down a runoff channel, their hands wet, their laughter echoing strangely off the steel plate. One of the skiffs is folded from a discarded shift-roster. The other from the back page of a romance serial.

It watches an old man feeding pigeons in a cathedral-sized cavern where the lamps have been arranged, perhaps unconsciously, perhaps not, to mimic a constellation no one alive has seen.

It watches a young engineer weeping in a maintenance closet because she has just realized she will probably never see the ocean, and she doesn't know why this matters to her, because she has never seen the ocean, and the ocean has never seen her, and yet.

The power understands: these people are not deprived. They are *themselves*. But there are small aches inside them that have no names, because the things that would name those aches were left on the surface, generations ago.

---

## What It Does Not Do

It does not collapse the ceiling and pour sunlight in. That would be a violence — a kindness offered without consent, which is no kindness at all. The city has made itself. To unmake it for its own good would be to insult every hand that placed every steel plate.

It does not appear in glory. The people here are practical; glory would embarrass them, and they would politely ignore it, and then it would have to leave.

It does not whisper into the minds of leaders. They have their own minds, and those minds are good ones, and meddling would be the beginning of a long unkindness.

It does not introduce itself.

---

## What It Does

**First**, it strengthens the rock.

Not visibly. Not in any way the geologists will detect. But there is a fault, deep beneath the eastern districts, that in two hundred and forty years would have given way and killed eleven thousand people. The power touches it the way you might press your thumb gently against a loose tooth in a child's mouth — and the fault settles, and forgets it was ever restless. No one will ever know. This is fine. The power is not interested in being known for this.

**Second**, it adjusts the pigeons.

A very small adjustment. The pigeons here have been inbreeding for two centuries and are beginning to lose something — not health exactly, but *pigeon-ness*, the dim shimmer of wild memory that makes a pigeon a pigeon and not just a feathered machine. The power gives them back a little of it. Their cooing, in a generation or two, will sound slightly more like the cooing of pigeons on a windowsill in a city that has rain. The people will not consciously notice. But they will find themselves stopping, sometimes, to listen.

**Third** — and this is the part the power thinks about longest — it leaves a gift.

Not a thing. A *pattern*.

In one of the deepest cisterns, where the groundwater has been pooling undisturbed for centuries, the power sets a tiny resonance — a way the water has of dripping, sometimes, when no one is around, that is almost but not quite the rhythm of a particular old song. The song was sung by a woman on a hillside three thousand years ago, and it was a good song, and it should not be entirely lost.

Someone will hear it, eventually. A maintenance worker, probably, on the night shift. They will pause. They will not know what they're hearing. They will hum it, badly, on the way home. Their child will pick it up. A version of it will end up in a lullaby. A version of *that* will end up, two centuries from now, in a work-song that the tunnel-borers sing as they push the city outward into new rock.

The song will not be the original song. It will be the city's song. But the hillside will be inside it, faintly, like a grandmother's face in a great-grandchild's smile.

---

## What It Considers, And Declines

It considers giving them sunlight — not the real thing, but a true dream of it, deposited gently in the collective unconscious, so that they would know what they are missing.

It declines. To know what you are missing, without being able to have it, is one of the cruelest gifts. The people of the city have built a life that is whole on its own terms. The power respects this more than it respects its own urge to give.

It considers revealing itself to the weeping engineer in the maintenance closet. Comforting her. Telling her the ocean exists, and is beautiful, and that her longing is not foolish.

It declines. Her grief is her own, and it is *honest*, and she will carry it into her work, and her work will be better for it, and one day she will design a recycling cistern shaped — without her quite knowing why — like a tidepool. People will swim in it. Children will learn what it is to float. This is better than comfort. She would agree, if asked. The power does not ask, because to ask would be to spoil it, but it believes she would agree.

---

## Before It Leaves

It does one last thing.

It blesses the roaches.

Not because they need blessing — they are doing magnificently — but because no one else will, and a benevolent power that only blesses the beautiful is not really benevolent. The roaches receive the blessing without ceremony, the way roaches receive everything: by continuing to be roaches, splendidly, in the dark.

Then the power withdraws its attention, slowly, the way you might back out of a room where someone is sleeping peacefully and you do not want to wake them.

The city hums on. The canals carry their skiffs. The pigeons settle for the night in their hidden corners. Somewhere deep below, water drips in a rhythm that is almost a song.

No one knows anything has happened.

This is exactly right.
