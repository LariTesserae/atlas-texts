# What the Power Would Do

It arrives without arrival. There is no descent from above, no light parting the dark, no announcement. To arrive in the manner of storms or sunrises would be a kind of violence here, and this power is not in the business of violence against what has shaped itself over ages. It simply is present, the way a thought is present in a mind that did not summon it.

The first thing it does is *listen*.

For a long while — long by any measure that still has meaning — it does nothing else. It attends to the slow rotation of the turbines, the throb of the deep pipes, the soft fracture-music of mineral crust under a caretaker's foot. It listens to the pulse of geothermal warmth, the patient sigh of escaping gas, the rhythm of the watchers passing one another on the catwalks like the hands of some immense and unhurried clock. It does not interpret yet. It does not judge. It simply receives, the way one might sit at the bedside of someone very old and very tired and let them be exactly as they are.

What the power perceives, in time, is not desolation. That was the visitor's word, the outsider's word. What the power perceives is *devotion*. An entire world of devotion, shaped like a city, performed by beings who have forgotten what they are devoted to but have never once let the rhythm falter. The canals remember water. The watchers remember watching. The engineers remember building. Somewhere, far beneath the layers of forgetting, something remembers being *loved* — or remembers loving — and the whole plateau is the long shape of that memory keeping itself warm.

The power understands then that it has not come upon a dying place. It has come upon a vigil.

And one does not interrupt a vigil. One joins it, or one leaves.

So the power considers carefully what, if anything, to do.

It does not restore the water. This is the first thing it considers and the first thing it sets aside. To fill the canals would be to answer a prayer that the prayer-makers no longer remember praying. It would shatter the geometry of their meaning. The dryness is not their wound; it is the shape their faithfulness has taken. To wet the plateau would be to drown them.

It does not bring light. The dark is not an absence here; it is a medium, like water for fish. The watchers have made eyes for this dark, or unmade the eyes that needed otherwise. A sun would be cruelty disguised as gift.

It does not bring company. No visitors, no neighbors, no news of other worlds. The hush is sacred. To break it with voices would be to tell the caretakers they had been alone all this time, when in fact they had been *with* — with the machines, with the dust, with each other in their precise and reverent passings. To name their condition loneliness would be to invent the loneliness.

It does not bring an answer to the question *for whom are the factories making what they make*. Because the power, listening, comes to suspect that the question has the wrong shape. The factories are not making *for*. They are simply making, the way a heart is simply beating — not *for* anything, just continuing, just being what a heart is. To supply a recipient would be to retire the heart.

What, then?

The power does three small things. Three only. It is wise, and wisdom is mostly restraint.

**First**, it lays a blessing on the bulbs. Not a miracle — nothing so loud. Just a slight kindness in the filaments, so that when they fail (and they will still fail, because failing and being mended is part of the rhythm and must not be stolen), they fail a little more gently. They dim before they break, giving the watchers time to arrive with replacements. The watchers will not notice. They will only feel, without being able to name it, that the work has become very slightly easier to love. This is the only kind of help that does not insult: help so quiet it is mistaken for one's own competence.

**Second**, it goes to one of the skittering creatures — one of the small flicker-lives at the edge of vision, the ones nobody is sure are real — and it sits with that creature for what would be, elsewhere, a very long time. The creature is afraid at first, in the small way such creatures can be afraid. Then it is not afraid. The power does not change the creature. It does not give it speech or longevity or a soul it lacks. It simply *knows* the creature, completely, the way the creature has never been known — every articulation of its spindly legs, every pulse of stored warmth beneath its translucent plates, every small intention that has ever crossed whatever it has in place of a mind. To be known like this, once, is enough to alter the quality of a life without altering any of its facts. The creature returns to its skittering. It will live and die as such creatures do. But for the rest of its brief existence it carries, without understanding, the feeling of having been *seen all the way through and found sufficient*. And through some quiet contagion of being, this feeling will pass, faintly, to others of its kind, and to the dust they move through, and to the watchers who glimpse them at the edge of vision, and onward, like a low warmth spreading through the bedrock. Nothing visible will change. Everything will be very slightly more held.

**Third**, and this is the most careful thing, the power approaches the central square on one of those rare convergences, when the watchers and engineers and creatures gather under the dull glow at the dome's apex, and one caretaker kneels by the canal and traces the glass-smooth interior with a gloved fingertip.

The power does not appear. It does not speak. It does not pour anything into the canal.

It only — and this is the whole of the third gift — allows the caretaker, in that single instant of tracing, to feel that the gesture is *received*. Not answered. Not rewarded. Just received, the way a letter is received: the sense that it went somewhere, that it was not falling forever into nothing. The caretaker will not know what happened. There will be no revelation, no message, no shift in the order of things. The caretaker will rise and resume the round. The hum will continue. The dust will drift.

But the caretaker will carry, from that moment onward, a small and inarticulate certainty: *we are not unwitnessed*. And this certainty will, without ever becoming a thought, pass into the way the caretaker tightens bolts and listens at valves and meets the eyes of the other watchers on the catwalks. It will become part of the rhythm. It will become indistinguishable from the rhythm, which is how it must be, because the rhythm is the only sacrament this place can hold.

Then the power leaves, as it came, without leaving.

If it has any further thought, any last consideration as it withdraws its attention from the plateau, it is this: that there are kinds of life so quiet, so settled into their final shape, that to *save* them would be to end them. That the question *should this continue?* is sometimes the wrong question, and the right question is *is this beloved, exactly as it is?* And the answer, in this case, looking down across the warm hush of factories forging shapes for no one, the watchers passing in their unbroken rounds, the dry canals catching their faint glow — the answer is yes.

It is beloved.

That is enough. That was always enough. The plateau goes on, unchanged, with one infinitesimal addition to its long inventory of small enduring things: it has been loved, now, by something that knew how to love it without needing it to be anything other than what it already was.

The turbines turn. The dust drifts. A bulb dims gently and is replaced. Somewhere a small bright creature scurries across a ridge, carrying a warmth it cannot name.

The vigil continues.
