# What the Power Would Do

The benevolent power considers the Vertan Mountains for a long time before acting. This is important — that it considers, rather than rushing to bestow.

It sees the railways cutting through valleys, the smokestacks, the factories. It sees what a less wise power might see: an intrusion to be cleansed, a primal land to be restored to some imagined purity. And it sees what a more sentimental power might see: a noble people to be preserved in amber, frozen in their hardihood forever.

It rejects both impulses.

Because the wise power understands something that powers often forget: the mountain-dwellers built those railways. They laid the track. They fired the furnaces. The industrial development is not a violation of who they are — it is also who they are. To "save" them from it would be to refuse to see them as fully as they deserve to be seen. They are not artifacts. They are people, and people make trains.

So the power does something quieter.

---

**First, it simply attends.**

For a season — though seasons mean little to such a being — it does nothing but watch. It learns the name of the woman in the highest settlement who keeps the seed-stock of a barley variety no one else remembers how to grow. It learns the boy in the rail-town who sketches the mist on the back of waybills. It learns which of the gnarled trees in the eastern canyon is the oldest, and what the wind sounds like inside its hollow. It learns the grief of a mother whose son went down to the lowlands and did not write. It learns the small corruption of the foreman who skims wages, and also the reason — his daughter's illness — without excusing it.

The power learns the place from the inside, the way a guest learns a house: by accepting hospitality, not by rearranging the furniture.

---

**Then it makes three small interventions.**

*The first is a clarification of water.* In a dozen high streams, the power adjusts something subtle in the way the mist condenses. Not more water — the mountain-dwellers do not need a miracle, and a miracle would warp them. But cleaner water. The traces of metal leached from old mining tailings settle out, bind to stone, become harmless. No one notices for years. When a child in a downstream village does not develop the slow tremor that her grandmother had, no one connects it to anything. This is as the power wishes it.

*The second is a dream.* The power gives a single dream to a single engineer — a woman who designs the boilers for the valley factories. In the dream she sees, with perfect clarity, a different geometry of combustion: cleaner, more efficient, achievable with the materials and tools she already has. When she wakes she thinks she has solved it herself, and in a real sense she has; the power only opened a door she was already walking toward. Over the next decade, the smokestacks of the Vertan Mountains breathe lighter. The mist becomes mist again, and not something else wearing mist's clothing.

*The third is a witness.* The power finds, in a lowland city far away, a young scholar with a careful heart, and arranges — by the most mundane of coincidences, a missed train, a borrowed book — for her to become curious about the mountain people. She will spend her life among them, learning their language, recording their songs, writing down the barley-keeper's knowledge before it is lost. She will not romanticize them. She will write them as they are: hard, kind, petty, magnificent, ordinary, irreplaceable. Her books will be read in the lowlands and, eventually, by the mountain-dwellers' own grandchildren, who will be glad someone troubled to look.

---

**And then the power withdraws.**

It does not announce itself. It does not ask to be worshipped or even known. It understands that to reveal itself would be the cruelest gift of all — it would turn a self-reliant people into supplicants, would make their triumphs feel borrowed, would replace the dignity of their struggle with the smaller comfort of being chosen.

The mountain-dwellers will go on believing they did it themselves.

And the wise power smiles at this, because in every way that matters — they did.

---

What the power does *not* do is perhaps the truer measure of its wisdom:

It does not stop the hard winters. It does not prevent the rockslides. It does not keep the young from leaving for the lowlands, nor compel them to return. It does not make the work easier. It does not preserve the old ways against change, nor accelerate change against the old ways. It does not appear in visions to the dying or grant prayers at shrines. It does not make the mountains smaller or the air thicker.

It loves them too much to do any of that.

Love, when it is wise, mostly looks like restraint. Like clean water no one traces to its source. Like a dream that feels like one's own idea. Like a stranger who shows up to listen, and writes things down, and goes home.

The mist still clings to the ravines. The trains still chug through the valleys. The gnarled trees still hold their stubborn ground. And somewhere above it all, attending without intruding, a benevolent power keeps its long, patient watch — content to be, forever, the unfelt warmth at the edge of a hard and beautiful world.
