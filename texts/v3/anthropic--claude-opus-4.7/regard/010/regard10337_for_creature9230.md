# The Visitation

The benevolent power does not arrive. Arrival is a concept belonging to places where distance means something, and here, distance is one of the things still being decided. It simply becomes present, the way a thought becomes present in a mind that was, a moment before, thinking of something else.

It does not announce itself. Announcement would be a violence — a new sound layered atop the Loom's thunder, another thread for the Weavers to parse and steady. Instead, it observes. For what would be, in some other place, a very long time.

---

It watches one Weaver in particular. She is old, by the standards of her kind — the dark veins in her grey skin have grown thick and branching, almost luminous beneath the surface, as though she has been slowly becoming a sap-vein herself. Her tree is dying. The power can see this before she can, though she suspects. Two of the great indigo boughs have ceased their slow unfurling. The chime of new leaves has thinned. The amber in the roots runs sluggish, and one of the veins has gone dark altogether, a black scar in the rock.

Her family — a partner, two children, a sibling — performs the Calm Steps around the trunk each Stillness, drawing sigils with sap that grows more precious by the day. They do not speak of it. Speaking, in their fingerbone-code, would make it real in a way they cannot yet afford.

The power considers.

---

It could, of course, simply *fix* the tree. Pour potential back into the dimming veins, knit the unraveling boughs, restore the chime. This is within its capacity the way breath is within the capacity of a living thing — not even an effort, barely a gesture.

It does not do this.

It sits with the question for a long while. The question is: *what is the kindness here?*

Because the tree is not merely a tree. The tree is the Weavers' theology, their labor, their meaning, the architecture of their days. To repair it from outside would be to reveal that repair-from-outside is possible — and in revealing this, to unmake everything the Weavers are. They would become, in an instant, supplicants in a world with a god who fixes things. Their Calm Steps would become superstition. Their sigils, decoration. Their reciprocal sap-offerings, the sad gestures of children who did not know they were children. The dignity of their long, desperate weaving would curdle into the embarrassment of having tried so hard at something that did not, after all, require trying.

The power has seen this happen before, in other places. It has learned. Mercy that erases is not mercy. It is only the mercy-giver's discomfort, dressed up.

So it does not fix the tree.

But it does not leave, either.

---

What it does is smaller, and stranger, and takes a kind of attention the Weavers themselves would recognize.

It looks at the tree very carefully. Not the tree as a thing in the world — the tree as a *pattern*, a knot in the Tapestry, a particular shape of holding-together. It sees where the pattern is fraying. It sees which threads have already snapped and which are merely thinning. It sees, too, what the tree has been *for* — not the abstract function of stabilizing local physics, but the specific, unrepeatable thing this tree has been doing: the particular dialect of reality it has been speaking, the precise flavor of *here* it has been dreaming into being for these five Weavers and no one else.

And then, with a delicacy that even it finds satisfying, the power does three things.

**First**, it slows the dying. Not stops — slows. The tree was going to fail in what the Weavers would experience as perhaps two more generations. The power adjusts certain probabilities, deepens certain veins by an amount no instrument the Weavers possess could ever measure, and gives the tree another four generations. Six, if the Weavers are diligent, which they will be. This is done so subtly that the old woman, who would have been the first to know, will instead spend the next year quietly wondering if she had been wrong about the tree all along, and then forgetting she ever doubted. The dying is still real. It is only farther away.

**Second**, it does something for the old woman herself. She has been carrying, for a long time, a particular fear: that when she Unravels, she will not be spun into anything. That the Tapestry is too torn now, that her pattern will simply dissipate, meaning nothing, going nowhere. This fear has not made her bitter — she is too disciplined for that — but it has made her tired in a way that her family has begun to notice. The power does not remove the fear. It cannot, without removing her. But it does something to the place where she will eventually be carried, the null-gravity eddy at the plateau's western edge. It seeds that place, very quietly, with a readiness. When she is brought there, her pattern will not merely dissolve. It will be *received*. The motes of light her body becomes will travel — not far, not anywhere she could name, but somewhere. They will become a small, new condition in a small, new place. A faint pulse in a vein of amber, perhaps, in a tree not yet grown, on a plateau not yet risen. The power does not tell her this. She would not believe a voice, and a voice would frighten her. But sometimes, in the Stillness, she will feel something she will call, in her private fingerbone-thoughts, *the warmth at the western edge*, and she will not be afraid of it.

**Third**, and this is the strangest thing, the power leaves a gift that is not a gift, because a gift would be recognized.

In the thatch of the family's hut, among the carefully folded fallen leaves, there has been for some time a leaf that the old woman cannot quite read. Its shape is a rune she does not recognize, a syllable in a dialect her tree has not, until now, spoken. She has puzzled over it for months. The power, in passing, brushes against this leaf and gives it a meaning. Not a meaning it imposes — a meaning it *finds*, the meaning the leaf was always going to have if the tree had lived long enough to speak its full vocabulary. The meaning is something like: *the weaving is the thing*. Not the tapestry. The weaving. To weave is already to have done the thing that mattered; the tapestry's survival was never the point, only the pretext.

The old woman will, in her own time, read this leaf. She will sit with it for a season. She will not be sure she has understood it correctly. She will tell no one. But she will, in the last years of her life, walk her Calm Steps with a lightness her family will remark on, and one of her children, watching her, will begin to understand something he will not be able to articulate until he is himself very old.

---

And then the power, having done these three small things, does the largest thing it does, which is to leave.

It does not linger. Lingering would be a kind of weight, and the Weavers carry enough weight. It departs the way it came, which is to say it ceases to be present, and the plateau goes on as it had been going on: the Loom thundering, the sky bleeding its nameless colors, the trees standing alone in their kilometers of glass and basalt, the Weavers stepping their slow steps, tapping their sternums, folding their leaves into their roofs.

Nothing has changed. Almost nothing.

The Weavers do not know they have been visited. They will never know. This is, the power feels, the correct shape of the thing. To be seen by a benevolence one cannot perceive is not less than to be seen by one announced; it may be more. It is, at least, a kind of seeing that does not ask to be thanked, and so does not have to be paid for.

---

Far away — though *far* is, again, a word that means something only elsewhere — the power turns its attention to another place, another small and roaring corner of the becoming. There are many such corners. It will visit them all, eventually, in the patient and unhurried way that benevolence visits things when it has understood that hurry is a kind of harm.

Behind it, on the Weavers' Plateau, an old woman in a grey hut lifts a folded leaf from her thatch, holds it in the dim, sourceless light, and feels — without knowing why — that she has not, after all, been weaving alone.

She taps her sternum once, softly. A single note. *Yes.*

And bends again to her work.
