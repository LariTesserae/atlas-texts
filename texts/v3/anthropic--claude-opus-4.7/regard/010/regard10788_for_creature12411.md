# What the Power Would Do

The benevolent power arrives without arriving. It has always been here, in some sense — that is the nature of such powers — but now it *attends*. Its attention settles over the high country like a second quality of light, one that does not blind.

It looks. This is the first thing, and perhaps the longest thing. It looks for a span that, measured in human breaths, would be unbearable, but the power does not measure in breaths. It learns the precise angle of every gnarled branch, the names the people give to the wind in each of its moods, the particular grief of a hunter who lost a sibling to the cliffs three winters ago, the chemistry of moss, the long slow cooling of the stone since the mountains rose. It listens to the songs around the small fires and finds them good — not quaint, not primitive, but *good*, the way a clear note is good, the way water finding its level is good.

And here the power does something that distinguishes it from a lesser power: it considers whether to act at all.

A foolish benevolence would warm the air, multiply the game, soften the cliffs, drop fruit from heaven. A foolish benevolence would mistake hardship for harm. But this power has watched many worlds and understands that the people's dignity is not separable from their circumstances — that to lift the weight from their shoulders is also to unmake their shoulders. The reverence in their stone-markings, the deliberateness of their footsteps, the way they use every part of the animal — these are not despite the harshness. These are the harshness, transfigured into a way of being. To erase the cost would erase the meaning.

So the power does not flood the valleys with summer.

Instead, it does smaller things, and these are what it does:

**It witnesses.** This sounds like nothing, but it is not nothing. Somewhere in the structure of things, the songs sung around those small fires are now held. The name of every person who has ever lived in this place, in every generation back to the first who climbed up here when the rock was still warm from its rising — every name is known and kept. The markings on the cliff faces are read and understood, and the intention behind them, the reverence, is received. Nothing here will be lost to the indifference of time. Whatever else happens to this world, *this place was seen*, and the seeing was loving.

**It steadies what is fragile in ways the people would not notice.** A child wandering too near a crumbling ledge feels, for no reason she can name, a sudden wish to turn back, and turns back. A hunter pursuing the ice-eyed predator into a narrowing gully hears something — a stone clicking, perhaps — and pauses, and lives. The old woman whose joints have begun to fail in the cold dreams one night of a particular south-facing hollow where the sun gathers, and in the morning tells her grandson, and they move her bedding there, and she has three more good years. These are not miracles. They are barely even nudges. The power is careful: it does not break the integrity of cause and effect, it only leans, very gently, where leaning will not be felt as intrusion.

**It does not introduce what does not belong.** No new crop appears. No revelation is delivered. No prophet is chosen. The power has seen what happens when even kindly meant gifts arrive in a culture that did not grow them: the gifts become wounds. The people here have what they need to be who they are. The power respects this absolutely.

**It tends to the ecosystem at the edges.** A particular lichen, whose extinction would unravel three other species and eventually impoverish the moss the people rely on, is quietly given a slightly better foothold on the north-facing rocks. A disease that would have swept through the quick-footed creatures and crashed their population is met, at the cellular level, with a small resistance that means only a hard year instead of a catastrophic one. The balance the people live within is preserved — not frozen, for the power knows that ecosystems must change, but defended against the kinds of collapse that come from outside the system's own logic.

**It prepares, against a day the people cannot see.** Far below, in the wider world, things are moving. They always are. Someday — not soon, but someday — others will come up the slopes: people with metal, people with maps, people who do not know how to read the markings on the cliffs. The power cannot prevent this; it would not, even if it could, override the freedom of those distant others. But it does begin, very quietly, to weave protections. It strengthens the memory of these people in the deep structure of the place itself, so that whoever comes after will find it harder to forget that someone was here first, and lived rightly. It seeds, in the dreams of a few sensitive descendants scattered across the generations, an inheritance of knowledge — so that if the line is ever broken, fragments of it might still surface, and find each other.

**And one more thing.** On a particular evening — chosen for no reason the people could discern, but chosen with great care — the light at sunset lasts a little longer than usual. The shadows soften. A warmth that is not quite warmth settles over the small gathering at one of the fires. An old hunter, sitting with his back to the stone, feels suddenly and without explanation that he is loved. Not by his people, whom he already knows love him, but by something larger, something that has been listening to his life. He does not have language for this. He does not try to find language for it. He simply sits a while longer by the fire, and when he sings that night, his voice carries something new in it that the others hear without naming, and the song enters the tradition, and is sung by his grandchildren, and theirs.

This is what the power does.

It does not save them, because they do not need saving.
It does not change them, because they are already what they should be.
It loves them, exactly as they are, in the place that made them.

And then — though it never really leaves — it turns part of its attention elsewhere, to other small fires in other harsh places, carrying with it the memory of this one: a high country of pale rock and persistent moss, where a few people lived deliberately, and marked the stones with reverence, and were seen.
