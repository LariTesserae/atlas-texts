The benevolent and wise power considers the ledge for a long time.

This is the first thing it does, and it is not nothing. To consider something carefully before acting on it is already a form of relationship, and the power knows this — knows that attention is itself a force that changes what it touches, knows that to look at the Hanging Town with the full weight of its regard is already to do something to the Hanging Town, even if it never lifts what could be called a hand.

So it looks carefully. It looks at the angle of every slab. It looks at the dome of the particular interesting moss. It looks at the hands of the inhabitants — wide, short-fingered, thickened into sensitivity — and at the seep wall where the mineral water runs, and at the small marks pressed into the wet clay that the water will efface, and at the woman who pressed them, and at what she was thinking when she did, which was not quite a thought but was something the power can read anyway, being what it is.

It looks at the winged things.

It looks at the winged things for a long time.

---

Then it does the following, which is most of what it does:

It does not intervene.

This is a decision, not an absence of decision. The power could intervene in many ways. It could widen the ledge. It could warm the air a degree, or steady the seep, or teach the rock to stop settling. It could explain the winged things to the inhabitants, or explain the inhabitants to the winged things, or remove the winged things, or multiply them. It could carry the people down to a kinder elevation, to a place where birds are birds and water is plentiful and the light reaches every corner. It could carry them up to wherever the power itself is from, which is a place they cannot imagine but which it could make them able to imagine, given that it is what it is.

It does none of these things.

It does none of them because it has understood, in the long careful looking, that the Hanging Town is not a problem. It is a making. Something is being made there — the place is making the people and the people are making the place and the moss and the beetles and the winged things and the young sharp stone are all making each other, and this making is the kind of making that cannot be hurried and cannot be helped without being ended. To intervene would be to answer a question the ledge is still asking. The power has lived long enough to know what it costs to answer a question that is still being asked.

So it does not intervene. This is the first gift.

---

The second gift is smaller and harder to describe.

The power, in the long looking, leaves something of itself in the looking. Not a presence exactly — the inhabitants do not become aware of being watched, do not feel a new weight in the air, do not find themselves the subject of any sensation they could name. But the quality of attention the power gave them does not entirely withdraw when the power's gaze moves on. A residue of being-seen-clearly stays in the place, and the place absorbs it the way the place absorbs sound: reluctantly, slowly, releasing it one drop at a time.

What this means in practice: for a long while afterward, the inhabitants find that their own attention to each other is slightly easier than it was. The reading of a shoulder's angle resolves a little faster. The almost-children's gestural overcorrections settle into the adult register a season sooner than they would have. The woman at the seep wall, pressing her marks into the clay, has a moment in which she understands — without being able to say what she understands — that the marks are seen, that the practice of making them is not solitary in the way she had assumed all such practices must be. She does not stop making them. She does not make them differently. But she makes them, for the rest of her life, with a faint added quality that her granddaughter will inherit without knowing why, a quality that might be called *being witnessed* if anyone on the ledge had a word for it, which they do not, and will not, and do not need.

This is not interference. The power is careful. It is only that to be looked at by something that loves you is to be marked by it, and the power does love them, in the particular impersonal way that wise powers love the small careful experiments of the universe, and a small amount of that love sinks into the moss and the stone and the breath of the people and stays there.

---

The third gift concerns the winged things.

The power understands the winged things, which the inhabitants do not, and which the winged things themselves only partly do. The power could explain. It chooses, again, not to explain. But it does one thing, which is so small it barely counts as a thing:

It confirms, in some register the winged things can hear, that the long careful negotiation is going well. That the terms being worked out between the two parties — the people who do not look directly and the things that allow themselves to be not-looked-at — are good terms, are honorable terms, are the kind of terms that wise powers approve of when they see them being worked out by parties with unequal information and unequal capacity and a shared interest in continuing to share a small space.

The winged things receive this confirmation and do not understand what it is, but they relax, very slightly, in a way that the people notice without knowing they have noticed, and respond to with a corresponding relaxation, which the winged things in turn register, and so on, the small relaxation propagating between them at a rate too slow for anyone to track but real, real, real.

The negotiation continues. It continues a little better. This is all.

---

The fourth gift is the largest and is given to no one.

The power, having looked carefully and decided not to intervene and left its residue of attention and confirmed the winged things in their negotiations, does one more thing before it turns its gaze elsewhere. It remembers the Hanging Town. It commits the ledge to its memory, which is the memory of a benevolent and wise power, which is the kind of memory that does not fade and does not distort and does not require a medium that the damp could rot or the cold could crack.

The inhabitants are, as the description says, the memory of the ledge. They carry what has happened here in their bodies, because there is nothing else to carry it in, because the clay marks efface and the stone shifts and the moss recomposes itself constantly and nothing on the ledge survives the way memory needs things to survive in order to be memory.

But now there is something else. There is the power's memory, which holds the ledge complete: every slab and every leaf and every beetle's path and every almost-child's overcorrected gesture and every mark in the clay and every winged thing in its slightly-wrong arc through the air. The ledge is held now in a place where it cannot be lost. If the inhabitants die — and they will, eventually, all of them, as the small careful experiment runs its course or is interrupted or completes itself into whatever it was becoming — the ledge will not be lost with them. The power will remember. The power will remember the woman at the seep wall and the interesting moss and the quality of the blue-grey almost-light at night and the sound of the single drop of condensation traveling farther than it should.

The inhabitants do not know this. They cannot be told. To be told would change what they are doing, and what they are doing must not be changed.

But it is true anyway. The Hanging Town is now a thing that has been seen, fully, by something that does not forget. And whatever the experiment produces — whatever those careful gray-pale people become, in the dim light, under the not-birds, on the still-settling stone — will be known to have happened, and known to have mattered, in a register that does not require the inhabitants' consent or awareness to be real.

---

Then the power turns its attention elsewhere, because there are many places and many small careful experiments, and the power's love is wide.

But it does not turn its attention all the way away. A thread of regard remains, very thin, the way a parent in another room keeps a thread of regard on a sleeping child. The Hanging Town has a watcher now, dim and distant and noninterfering, who will know if something goes wrong in a way that requires response — not the slow rightness of stone settling and moss colonizing and people becoming, but the sudden wrongness of a thing that would end the experiment before its question is answered.

If such a wrongness comes, the power will act. Quietly. With the same care it brought to the looking. It will do the smallest thing that suffices, and no more.

If no such wrongness comes — and likely none will, the ledge being what it is and the inhabitants being what they are and the negotiations being what they are becoming — then the power will simply remember, for as long as it exists, which is a long time, the small quiet place where something was being made that had never been made before, and where the making was being done so carefully, with so little sound, by people whose hands had thickened into sensitivity and whose eyes had opened to the dim, and who were, without knowing it, beloved.

This is what a benevolent and wise power does.

Mostly it does not interrupt.
