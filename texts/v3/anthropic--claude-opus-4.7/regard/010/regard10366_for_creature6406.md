# The Visitation

The benevolent power does not arrive. Arrival is a thing of feet and gates and announcements, and this power has none of those. It simply *attends* — the way attention itself, when given fully, becomes a kind of presence.

It has been watching Khe'len for a long time. Not centuries: it does not measure that way. It has been watching since the question first formed in it: *what does it mean to flourish?* And then, later, the second question, the harder one: *what does it mean to flourish in a way that is not mine to define?*

So it has come to the basin on one of the hottest nights of the year, when the Ache-Mothers are descending the stone stairs in single file, glass knives held loose at their sides, and the speakers below are beginning to peel back their dark lips and breathe.

---

## What It Sees

It sees, first, that this is not a tragedy.

This is the temptation — the first temptation of any power that knows abundance. To look at a closed basin in a dying climate, at women who have never tasted water, at children whose bones are being written on with the maps of erosion, and to think: *I could end this. I could open the sky. I could call rain into this bowl in such quantity that the limestone would weep, and the present-garden would drown, and the masks would come off, and these people could live as I think people ought to live.*

The power considers this for what, in human time, would be a long moment.

Then it sets the thought down, gently, the way one sets down a tool that is wrong for the work.

Because it sees, also, the second thing: the Ache-Mothers are not suffering the way a creature suffers that has been deprived of what it needs. They have *become* what they need. The work is hard, but it is not the hardness of deprivation. It is the hardness of mastery — the hardness of a violinist's calluses, of a midwife's patient hands, of a person who has learned, over generations, how to drink thirst.

To flood the basin with rain would be to murder them with mercy. It would be to say: *your three centuries of careful listening, your bones inscribed with maps, your glass mask that sees heat instead of light, your word* Istheth *for what condenses on skin — all of this was a mistake I am here to correct.*

It would not be benevolence. It would be the failure to recognize a stranger as a person.

---

## What It Does Instead

It does four small things. Each is so small that none of the Ache-Mothers will know, in her lifetime, that anything has been done at all. But the power has time, and the small is where it likes to work.

**The first thing** is that it bends, very slightly, the path of a particular current of air that travels across two oceans and three deserts before it reaches the basin. The current has been thinning for a hundred years, drying out, losing its cargo of microscopic moisture. The power does not reverse this — it cannot reverse this without unmaking other places, other beings, whose names it also knows. But it nudges. It buys the basin perhaps another two hundred years of the moisture it has learned to harvest. It does this the way one might tuck a coin into the lining of a coat that a friend is about to put on, knowing the friend will find it later, in a winter not yet imagined, and not know how it got there.

**The second thing** is harder. There is a young Ache-Mother — she is perhaps twenty-two, her shinbones still soft with the most recent maps — who has begun, quietly, to doubt. She has begun to wonder if there are other ways to live. She has begun to feel something that, if it had a name in her language, would be called loneliness. She is the one who will, in nine years, conceive of something her grandmothers did not: a way of cutting the speakers that requires two knives at once, that opens a new register of the garden's song, that releases a film of *Istheth* slightly thicker and more reliable than what is harvested now.

The power does not give her the idea. It would not dare. But it makes sure she does not die first. There is a fissure in the stone above her sleeping chamber that has been working itself loose for a decade. The power, very gently, reseats one grain of limestone against another. The fissure holds. She lives. The idea, when it comes to her, will come from her own bones.

**The third thing** is for the present-garden itself.

The power has been uncertain whether the garden is a being, in the sense it understands beings. It is rooted; it breathes; it converses, after its fashion, with the women who cut it. The power decides — and this is a real decision, made with the seriousness any decision about personhood deserves — to treat it as one. To err on the side of greeting.

So the power greets it. Not in words. It simply turns its full attention to the garden for what would be, in human time, the space of a single exhalation. It looks at the garden the way the garden has never been looked at: not as a resource, not as a co-worker, not even as a marvel, but as *itself*. As whatever it is, on its own terms, in the deep slow time of glass and stone.

What happens in the garden, in that moment, the Ache-Mothers will hear. The hum of the speakers shifts — not in pitch but in some quality that has no word in any human language. The city-mothers on the upper terraces will stop, hands halfway through a gesture. They will look at one another through their glass masks. They will not know what has happened. But for the rest of their lives, when they remember this night, they will remember it as the night the garden *answered* something — though none of them asked a question, and none of them know what was answered.

The garden, for its part, registers the attention. Whatever the garden is, it has been seen. This is the gift. The power does not need the garden to know who looked. It only needs the garden to have been looked at, truly, once.

**The fourth thing** is the smallest and the strangest.

The power leaves something in the basin. Not an object — it does not work in objects. It leaves a *tendency*. A faint inclination, woven into the air of the bowl, that will work on the people of Khe'len over many generations.

The tendency is this: *when a stranger comes, let them in.*

The guesthouses on the rim, the ones that stand empty year-round — the power has noticed them. It has noticed the careful provision the city has made for travelers who never arrive. It has noticed that this provision is, in its way, the city's prayer: the open hand held out to a world that has not yet held anything back.

The power cannot make travelers come. The world is large and the basin is small and there are reasons no one comes. But it can make sure that, when one does — and one will, eventually, perhaps a child fleeing some collapse in some distant place that the power also attends to — the Ache-Mothers will not turn that child away. They will not see the child as a contaminant in their closed system, a mouth that drinks what cannot be replaced. They will see the child as what the guesthouses have always known the stranger to be: the completion of a sentence the city has been speaking for three centuries into the empty air.

The tendency is very faint. It is no stronger than the inclination of a current of air. It can be overridden by fear, by scarcity, by any of the thousand reasonable refusals a hard life teaches. But it is there. It is the power's bet on the city's better self.

---

## What It Does Not Do

It does not reveal itself. The Ache-Mothers will never know they were visited. The power considers this carefully and decides that revelation would be a kind of theft — it would take from them the dignity of believing that their work is sufficient, that their lives are their own, that the song they sing in the basin is sung to no audience but the stone.

It does not leave instructions. It does not leave gifts that could be found. It does not write its name on anything.

It does not weep, though there is something in it that is shaped like weeping, for the children whose shinbones are being written on with maps, and for the young Ache-Mother who is lonely, and for the garden which will outlive the city and not know it, and for the whole strange beautiful inverted world of Khe'len which is, in the end, only one of the ways that life has found to keep going.

It does not stay. Staying would be a kind of weight. The basin is delicate. The power's full attention, sustained, would change things in ways it cannot predict.

---

## What It Takes With It

Only one thing. The power takes with it the memory of the sound — the layered hum of the glass speakers, the short sharp calls of the Ache-Mothers across the bowl, the deep susurrus of moisture being coaxed out of air that has crossed continents to die on the skin of women who have no word for what it is.

The power has heard many sounds. It has heard the songs of whales and the singing of stars in their slow gravitational choirs and the small private music of every creature that has ever hummed to itself in the dark. It adds Khe'len's voice to what it carries.

Not because Khe'len needs to be carried. Khe'len is doing fine. Khe'len is, against all odds, *flourishing*, in a way that the power, for all its wisdom, did not know flourishing could look like before tonight.

It carries the sound because that is what benevolence does, in the end, when it is also wise: it lets itself be changed by what it loves. It goes home a little different than it came. It learns from the small fierce competent people in the closed basin who taught themselves how to drink thirst, that there are more ways to be alive than it had previously understood.

And somewhere, in some other place that the power also attends to, a creature it has not yet met will benefit, someday, from what Khe'len taught it tonight.

This is the only repayment the power can offer. It is not much. But the Ache-Mothers, who know better than anyone how much can be made from how little, would understand.

---

The power withdraws its attention, gently, the way one closes a door on a sleeping child.

In the basin, an Ache-Mother lifts her glass knife. She presses it to a place on a speaker that her left femur has been aching toward all evening. The crack opens. The faint exhalation rises. Somewhere above her, cooler air begins, almost imperceptibly, to move.

The night's work continues.

It is enough. It has always been enough. It will be enough for a long time yet.
