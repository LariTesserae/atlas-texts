The Power considers.

It has come from far places, drawn by something it could not quite name from across the cold spaces — not a cry for help, because no cry has been made, but a particular shape of stillness that registers, to a being attuned to such things, the way certain rare notes register on a tuning fork. The Power arrives without arrival. It does not displace mist or startle the springs. It simply is present, the way attention is present in a watching mind.

It looks.

It sees the wiry people bent at their plants. It sees the careful slope of a wall built by a great-grandmother of a great-grandmother, sees the way her hand’s habit lives on in her descendant’s hand. It sees the dead in the marsh-edges feeding the moss that feeds the goat-less, bird-less, beetle-less green. It sees the children, who are not really children in the way the Power has known children elsewhere — small, yes, and learning, but not bright with that particular fever of becoming that the Power knows from a thousand other worlds.

And the Power, being benevolent, feels the first stirring of an impulse: *to give.*

To send rain that does not need to be coaxed. To call the birds in — they would come, if it asked; somewhere, in a sister-valley a continent away, there are finches that would settle here gladly. To slip into the soil a quiet richness, so that the stubborn plants would unbend and offer their fruit without being begged. To set, in the minds of the people, a small bright seed: *more is possible.*

The Power, being wise, pauses.

It looks again, and this second looking is longer. Wisdom is mostly the discipline of the second look.

It sees now what the first look missed: that the equilibrium here is not a poverty. It is a *practice.* Every gesture of every stunted hand is a sentence in a long, slow argument these people have been making — to themselves, to the mountain, to whatever they imagine listens — that it is possible to live without consuming a world. That a species can hold still. That generations can refuse the bright fever and choose, instead, to be the steady pulse of a place rather than its devouring weather.

No bird sings here because the people, somewhere in their unrecorded past, made a covenant the Power can almost taste in the mist: *we will not be the kind of creature that fills a place.* The absence of fauna is not a wound. It is the shape of a vow.

And the people themselves — the Power looks into them, gently, the way one might look into a still pool without troubling the surface. They are not suffering. This is important and the Power makes itself certain of it. They are not bored, because boredom is a disease of beings who believe elsewhere is better. They are not lonely, because they are woven. They are not afraid of death, because they have arranged death to be a homecoming, and they have meant it, and the arrangement has held.

They are also not *happy* in the bright sense the Power has seen elsewhere — the laughter of otters, the singing of certain green planets at dawn. There is a register of joy they do not occupy. The Power notes this honestly. It does not pretend the cost is nothing.

But the cost was chosen. And it is still being chosen, every slow morning, by every slow hand.

The Power withdraws its first impulse the way one withdraws an offered gift upon realizing the recipient is not lacking but complete. To pour abundance into this place would be a violence dressed as kindness. To wake the fever of becoming in these people would be to murder, very politely, the civilization they have spent uncounted generations building — for what they have built is not the city of stone and mud-brick. What they have built is the *way of being* that the city merely houses.

Still. The Power has come a long way, and benevolence does not like to leave entirely empty-handed. It searches for what it can rightly give.

It finds three small things.

The first: it touches, very lightly, the springs. Not to multiply them. Only to ensure that the deep aquifer that feeds them — which the people do not know about, and which is, in fact, slowly failing on a timescale longer than their memory — will continue. The Power does not add water. It only mends a small fracture, far below, that no one will ever see. The equilibrium the people maintain at the surface will continue to be maintainable. Their vow will not be betrayed from beneath.

The second: it places, in the marsh where the dead are laid, a faint and nameless blessing. Not a change. A kind of *acknowledgment* — so that when a body returns to the water, something in the water knows. The people will not perceive this directly. But perhaps, on certain evenings, one of them tending the marsh-edge will feel, without knowing why, that the work is *witnessed.* That the long quiet labor of their kind has not gone unseen in the wider dark. This is the smallest gift, and possibly the largest.

The third: the Power leaves, in the air above the city, a single thread of itself — gossamer, undetectable, patient. Not surveillance. A door. If ever, in some century the Power cannot foresee, one of these people stands at the edge of the marsh and looks up into the mist and *asks* — asks anything, asks for more, asks for less, asks merely whether anyone is there — the thread will hum, and the Power will hear, and the Power will return, and it will listen first before it does anything at all.

It does not tell them about the door. To tell them would be to plant the fever. The door is only for the one, if ever there is one, who finds the asking in themselves without being taught it.

Then the Power does the last and hardest thing wisdom asks of benevolence: it goes away. Fully. It does not linger to admire its restraint, because lingering would still be a pressure on the place, and the place has earned its lack of pressure.

The mist closes. The springs weep. A wiry woman, bent over a stubborn green shoot, straightens a moment, feels nothing in particular, and returns to her work.

Somewhere very far off, the Power moves on, carrying with it a new understanding it did not have before — that there are forms of flourishing that look, to the untrained eye, exactly like a world holding its breath; and that the highest use of great power, sometimes, is to recognize a smaller, slower power already at work, and to bow, and to pass by.
