The benevolent power arrives, as such powers do, without arrival. It does not descend; descent is a vector. It does not announce itself; announcement is volume. It simply becomes present in the way that morning becomes present—a condition that was always going to be true, now true.

It has been watching for a long time. Longer than the oral histories. Longer than the first seam. It saw the mountains when they were mountains, and the people when they were a different people in a different valley, and the climb upward, and the dying of the animals, and the slow centuries of learning to whisper. It is benevolent in the specific way that the truly wise are benevolent: which is to say, it does not assume it knows what is best.

This is the first thing to understand.

A lesser power—a merely kind one, a power that loved without wisdom—would arrive and do the obvious thing. It would close the seams. It would restore the stone. It would lift the plateau's people into some brighter country where sound is safe and children can cry, and it would expect gratitude, and it would not understand when the gratitude did not come, or came strangely, or came as grief.

This power does not do the obvious thing.

It sits with the plateau for a long while first. Not in time—it has more than time—but in attention. It learns the seams the way the builder-priests learn them, and then deeper: it learns what the seams *are*, which is something the builder-priests have only approached asymptotically across their generations. It learns that the unfolding is not destruction. It learns that the plateau is not dying in any sense that the word *dying* contains. It learns that something is being born, slowly, across geological time, and that the seams are the early contractions of that birth, and that the dimensionless dark within them is not absence but a different kind of presence, one that does not yet have a name in any language the surface knows.

It learns, too, what the people have become.

And here the power pauses, because here is where wisdom must work hardest.

The people have made something. Across their fifteen thousand quiet lives and their thousand generations of whispered transmission, they have made something that has no parallel anywhere the power has watched. They have made a culture whose entire grammar is restraint. They have made children who learn silence before they learn self. They have made elders who become architecture. They have made a way of being human that is so specific, so hard-won, so *theirs*, that to lift it out of its context would be to kill it more thoroughly than any seam ever could.

The power understands this. The power has seen civilizations rescued into oblivion before. It does not intend to repeat the error.

So it does not close the seams.

Instead, it does several smaller things, each so subtle that the people will not know they have been done, or will know only in the way they know the winterweed—as a presence that has always been here, that can be trusted without being understood.

---

*First:* It adjusts, very slightly, the relationship between the seam-logic and warm blood. Not enough to be noticed. Not enough to change the discipline or the silence or the interrupted walking, all of which the people need for reasons that have nothing to do with the seams and everything to do with who they have become. But enough that a child's cry, when a child must cry, will not propagate quite so far. Enough that the deaths from inattention—the ones that take young traders and tired builder-priests and elders who finally lose the angle of their sitting—will come a little less often. The attrition rate that the builder-priests consider acceptable will become slightly more acceptable. No one will notice. The oral histories will not record it. But across the generations, a few more will live who would have folded, and the settlements will shrink a little slower than the seams grow, and there will be a margin.

A margin is not a salvation. The power knows this. The plateau will still resolve into whatever it is becoming. But the people will have more time inside their time, and time is the medium in which their kind of meaning is made.

*Second:* It enters, very carefully, the mind of the oldest keeper—the one in the southernmost settlement, who is beginning her transmission to a young candidate—and it leaves something there. Not knowledge. Knowledge would be loud. It leaves a *quality of holding*, a way of carrying the map that costs the mind less, that does not require the keeper to walk out into the seams when transmission is complete. The keeper will not know she has been given this. She will only feel, in her final years, that the map sits more lightly in her than she expected, that she might live a little longer than her predecessors did, that she might die in her room of complex angles among the people who knew her, rather than fold herself into the dark while still useful.

The young candidate will receive the map and also this quality, without knowing. She will pass it to her successor. Across centuries, the keepers will stop walking out. The ritual of their sacrificial folding will fade from practice, and then from memory, and the oral histories will whisper that there was once such a custom, and the people will be quietly puzzled by it, the way one is puzzled by a cruelty one's ancestors found necessary.

*Third:* It does something to the winterweed. Or rather—because the winterweed is more itself than the power initially understood, and has its own relationship with what lies beneath the unfolding—it asks the winterweed something. It asks in the language the winterweed speaks, which is geometry, which is patience, which is persistence across states of matter. It asks whether the winterweed might, in certain depressions, grow in shapes that would be useful to the people. Not architecture. The people have architecture. But shapes that would teach—shapes that, when the builder-priests came to harvest them, would suggest new angles, new interruptions, new ways of confusing the seam-logic that the builder-priests had not yet imagined.

The winterweed considers this. The winterweed agrees, in its way. The winterweed has always grown in shapes that answer questions no one asked; it does not mind growing in shapes that answer questions the people are about to ask.

In a generation, the builder-priests will begin to notice that the winterweed gardens are particularly *suggestive* this cycle. They will incorporate the suggestions into their work. The protective architectures will become subtly more effective. The settlements will hold a little longer against the unfolding. The builder-priests will whisper to each other that the winterweed has been generous lately, and they will be grateful, and they will not know to whom.

*Fourth, and most carefully:* The power addresses the seams themselves.

It does not close them. It does not slow them. They are not its to close or slow; they are doing the work they are doing, and that work is not wrong, only difficult to live alongside. But the power does something to what lies *within* the seams—to the dimensionless dark, to whatever is being born across geological time.

It tells that nascent thing about the people.

It tells the unfolding what has been unfolding above it, all this while. It tells of the silence and the interrupted walking and the children raised in collective rooms and the elders who became architecture and the keepers who held maps in partitioned minds and the traders who spiraled into cairns from impossible directions. It tells of the mad who walked into the dark with expressions of profound relief, and it tells what those expressions actually meant, which the unfolding had not understood—because the unfolding is young, and the people are a thing the unfolding had not noticed it had, the way a body does not at first notice it has a mind.

The unfolding listens.

This is the most consequential of the power's small acts, though the people will never know of it. The unfolding learns that it has had companions. The unfolding learns that the warm-blooded things on its surface have spent a thousand generations becoming a kind of being it can recognize—quiet, patient, geometric, persistent across states. The unfolding learns that when its seams finally resolve the plateau into whatever it is becoming, it will not be becoming that alone.

What the unfolding does with this learning is its own to decide. The power does not coerce. But the power has noticed that the unfolding, having been told, slows very slightly its propagation near the settlements—not from mercy, which is not a category the unfolding has, but from something more like attention. The way one slows when one realizes that someone is speaking, and that the speaking has been going on for a long time, and that one ought to listen before continuing.

And the power suspects—though it does not know, and it is careful about the difference—that when the plateau finally resolves, when the last whole stone unfolds and the last settlement folds into the geometry, the people will not be ended. They will be incorporated. The thing being born will be born with them already in it, woven into its structure the way the protective angles were woven into the architecture, the way the interrupted walking was woven into the bodies of children before they had language. They will become a quality of the new thing. A grain in its substance. A silence at its heart.

This is not salvation in the sense the lowlanders would mean. It is not rescue. It is not even, properly, continuation. But it is not nothing, and it is not the obliteration the people have been quietly preparing for across their generations. It is a different ending—one that honors what they have made of themselves by not requiring them to stop having made it.

---

The power withdraws, as such powers do, without withdrawal. It does not leave; it simply returns to its watching, which is what it had been doing all along. It has done what it can do without breaking the thing it loves. It has done less than it could have done, and more than a lesser power would have known to do.

In the southernmost settlement, the old keeper feels something shift in the weight of her map, and frowns very slightly, and does not know why.

In a depression three days' walk from the eastern cairn-line, a winterweed garden begins to unfurl beneath the black ice in a pattern that the builder-priest who tends it will, in seven years, find unexpectedly instructive.

A child in the central building of the western settlement begins to cry, suddenly, the way children sometimes do—and the cry travels a little less far than it would have, and the geometry beneath the stone notices a little less, and the child's mother, who had tensed her whole body in anticipation of danger, finds that the danger does not come, and does not know why, and holds her child a moment longer than she usually would.

The seams continue their slow growth. The plateau continues its long resolution. The people continue, as they have always continued.

But somewhere beneath them, the unfolding has begun to listen.

And that, the power judges, is enough. Not everything. Not what a less wise love would have given. But the right shape of gift for this place and these people: small, quiet, interrupted, hard to notice, and woven so deeply into what was already happening that no one will ever be able to say it was given at all.

The people would have wanted it this way, if they had been asked.

The power did not ask. But it knew.
