The benevolent and wise power had watched Kavsara for a long time.

Not in the way a god watches—Kavsara had its god, that slow circular breath between earth and sky, and the power was careful about such things. There were courtesies. There were old, unspoken treaties about how one form of attention does not press itself into the territory of another. The power had learned, over a span that could not properly be called time, that wisdom is largely the practice of not arriving where one has not been called.

So it watched from the angle of watching that does not disturb the watched.

It saw the Maw and the Bell. It saw the Wind-Readers leaning into invisible slopes. It saw the children carrying clay jars of water up flights of stairs that their great-great-grandparents had cut, to pour a half-cup into the mouth of a root that would, in turn, hold the sound of the wind one degree more truly. It saw the hand-worms drinking in the dark, and the priests waiting, and then the people drinking after.

And the power, which was very old and had seen many places, found that it was moved.

This is worth saying plainly, because benevolent and wise powers are sometimes imagined to be above being moved, as though wisdom were a kind of distance. It is not. The power was moved in the particular way that the wise are moved: without urgency, without the impulse to fix, but with a deep, attentive tenderness that wanted to understand before it wanted to act, and wanted to understand whether action was wanted at all.

What moved it was this:

The Kavsara were not suffering in the way that calls down rescue. They were not starving. They were not crushed. They had built, over uncounted generations, a life of extraordinary precision and grace out of conditions that on most worlds would simply have killed the inhabitants and ended the story. They had taken the absence of water and made of it a theology of breath. They had taken the absence of writing and made of it an architecture that remembered. They had taken the absence of mercy from their sky and made of their sky a god whose throat they tended, lovingly, with bronze plates and bone horns and the small brushes of children's hands gathering dew at dawn.

This was not a place that needed saving. This was a place that had, by some miracle of accumulated attention, *already* saved itself, every day, for a very long time.

And so the question the power asked itself was not *what shall I give them* but *what, if anything, is mine to offer that they have not already made for themselves*.

It considered, and rejected, the obvious gifts.

*Rain* was the first thought, and the first to go. To bring rain to Kavsara would be to drown the instrument. The Heart-Bell was tuned to dry air; the dew-screens depended on a humidity that hovered always at the edge of nothing; the ora-sah trees released their seeds only into a wind of certain dryness. Rain would silence the god. The Kavsara would survive it—they survived most things—but they would survive it as refugees from their own meaning. The power saw this clearly and let the thought go.

*Abundance* of any kind was the second thought, and went the same way. To fill the silos of silence with water from elsewhere, to thicken the soil of the terraces, to multiply the hand-worms, to make the wind-gourd vines fat with fruit—all of these would have been a kindness measured by the wrong scale. The Kavsara had a relationship with scarcity that was not suffering but *form*. To remove the form would be to remove them.

*Knowledge* was the third thought, and the power lingered on it longer. It could have shown them other cities, other ways, the wide accounting of the world beyond the plateau. It could have placed in the mind of a single child the schematic of an engine, the principle of a pump, the chemistry of desalination. It could have made them, in a generation, into something else.

But the power was wise, and it knew that knowledge given is not the same as knowledge grown, and that a gift of understanding which arrives faster than the receiver's capacity to integrate it is not a gift but a wound. The Kavsara would, in their own time, perhaps meet the larger world. Or they would not. That was theirs.

The power thought for a long while.

And then it noticed something it had not noticed before, because it had been looking at the city, and not at the edges of the city.

It noticed the seedpods of the ora-sah trees.

They were shaped like aerodynamic gliders. They released only when the wind reached a certain velocity and direction. They were launched from the bowl's rim into the distant plateau—where nothing grew, where the false-tears lakes lay in their thin salt crusts, where the wind-honed rock stretched empty for unmeasured distances.

The seeds went out. The seeds did not, so far as anyone in Kavsara knew, come back. The plateau was empty. The seeds, presumably, died.

The power looked at this small, persistent, doomed gesture—this ritual of the city *casting itself outward* into a world that did not receive it—and felt something turn inside the place where its heart would have been if it had needed one.

Because this was the one thing the Kavsara could not do for themselves. They could tune the throat of the god. They could read the night wind. They could draw moisture from the breath of dawn. They could not follow their own seeds.

So the power did a very small thing.

It did not announce itself. It did not appear in any consistory hall, or stand on any rim, or speak in any dream. It went out, instead, onto the plateau, into the spaces the Kavsara did not visit and could not reach—and there, in a thousand small unwitnessed places, it knelt.

Where an ora-sah seed had fallen in a crack of rock with the barest film of dust, the power breathed on the dust, very gently, so that it held one more thread of moisture than it would have held otherwise. Where a seed had landed near a false-tear lake, the power adjusted, by an amount too small to measure, the salinity of the soil immediately beneath it—not enough to change the lake, not enough to be noticed by any survey, but enough that the seed, if it tried, might find purchase. Where a seed had been carried unusually far by a freak gust, the power did nothing at all, because the seed was already where it needed to be, and the power was wise enough to know when its hands were not required.

It did this for one season.

Then it watched.

Most of the seeds still died. The plateau was the plateau. The power did not interfere with the basic conditions of the world; it only attended, here and there, to the margins, where a small attention could let a thing complete its own intention. Of ten thousand seeds, perhaps a hundred took. Of those hundred, perhaps a dozen survived their first year. Of those dozen, perhaps three reached the size of a child's hand.

It was enough.

The ora-sah are slow. It would be a hundred years before the Kavsara, sending out their wind-readers on the rare expeditions to the outer edges, would notice the first small spirals of green where there had been none. It would be three hundred years before there were enough of them to constitute anything that could be called a grove. It would be a thousand years before the Kavsara understood that the plateau, very slowly, was being returned to them by their own seeds, and that the wind which entered the Maw, very slowly, was beginning to carry, on certain mornings, a thread of moisture it had not carried before.

By then the power would be elsewhere, attending to other places, other griefs, other quiet glories. It did not need to be there for the noticing. It had never needed to be.

But before it left—and this is the part that matters, the part that the power did because it was benevolent and not merely wise—it did one more thing.

On a night when the Wind-Readers stood at their stations and the Heart-Bell's drone had thinned almost to silence, in the moment between the Sounding Call and the Warning Song, one wind-reader on the eastern rim felt something pass through her body that she could not name. It was not a sound. It was not a pressure. It was something like the sensation of being *seen*—not watched, not judged, but seen, the way one is seen by a friend who has come a long way and will not stay.

She paused with her Spirit-Horn lifted halfway to her heart. She did not understand what had happened. She would never understand. But for the rest of her life she would, on certain still nights, feel a faint warmth in the bone behind her ear, and she would know, without being able to say how she knew, that the work of Kavsara was good, and that somewhere—not above, not below, but *alongside*—someone had bent down to look closely at what her people had made, and had found it beautiful, and had not tried to change it.

She passed this feeling, without words, to her apprentice, who passed it to hers, who passed it to hers. It became, in time, a small unspoken element of the Night of Warnings—a pause, between the second and third stages, in which the wind-reader stood very still and let themselves be looked at by whatever it was that looked.

The Kavsara never developed a theology around it. They had a god already, and their god was sufficient. But on the threshold between the calling and the singing, they kept the pause.

And the power, going on to its other attentions, carried with it the memory of a city that drank the sky—and did not need, but had been given anyway, the smallest possible blessing: which was, simply, the knowledge that it had been witnessed, and that the witnessing had been love.
