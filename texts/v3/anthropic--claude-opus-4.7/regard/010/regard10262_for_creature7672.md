# What the Power Would Do

The benevolent power arrives—if arrival is even the right word for something that was always permitted to notice—not as descent but as a slow turning of attention, the way a reader's eye drifts to a particular line on a page they've held open for a long while.

It pauses at the threshold of the pocket. This is the first thing it does, and the most important.

---

**The Pause**

A lesser benevolence would have already acted. Would have looked at the scorching dimness, the two hundred wiry souls breathing sulfur through gill-fringed lungs, the children born slick in alcove-pods, the dead tipped into mawmouths to mummify—and called it suffering. Would have parted the basalt overhead like a curtain, poured down cool air and clean water, lifted the Ashborn into some brighter chamber of existence and called it rescue.

But this power is *wise* as well as kind, and wisdom is mostly the discipline of looking longer before one moves.

So it looks. It looks for what we might call a long time, though time is not quite the medium of its looking. It watches a full Harvest Creep, the slow undulation of the bodies through the thickets, the cheek-pouches filling, the soft euphoric hum rising in the throats of harvesters who have done this since they could crawl. It watches a pairing—two Ashborn drawn together by spore-vision, their breath syncing to the mats' peristalsis, the act tender and strange and entirely their own. It watches a death: an old woman, root-husked, her glyph-scarred skin read one last time by the hearth-circle before her kin carry her to the rim and tip her into the heat. There is no wailing. There is a long exhale from everyone present, and then the silence resumes, which is not really silence but the verdant cacophony that is, for them, the sound of being alive.

The power understands, then, what it had suspected from the first glance: *this is not a prison. This is a world.*

The Ashborn are not failed versions of something else. They are not stunted humans waiting to be made whole. Their lungs are not broken; they are *exquisite*, evolved to drink a nectar no other creature could metabolize. Their skin is not damaged; it is a garment of basalt-sheen so finely tuned to its element that to give them softer flesh would be to flay them. Their sparse numbers are not a tragedy of attrition but the precise carrying capacity of their pocket, held in equilibrium by generations of quiet wisdom encoded in ritual and rhythm. Their Breath is not superstition mistaking chemistry for spirit; it is a *true perception*, dressed in the only language they have, of the genuine reciprocity between their bodies and the fungal hegemony that feeds them. They are correct about almost everything that matters.

To "save" them would be to murder them and replace them with strangers.

---

**What It Notices That They Cannot**

And yet. The power is benevolent, and benevolence is not the same as non-interference. It looks again, more carefully, for the places where the Ashborn's flourishing is *thwarted from outside themselves*—not by the pocket's nature, which is their nature, but by accident, by the slow drift of entropy, by the small cruelties no one chose.

It finds a few.

There is a child in one of the burrow-nests whose lungs did not finish forming the gill-fringe properly. She is three vent-cycles old and her mother has not yet admitted to herself that the girl will not survive the next fever. The child wheezes in her sleep. This is not the pocket's design; it is a copying error, a stutter in the long lineage. The mother sings to her in a voice cracked like cooling stone.

There is an elder whose glyph-map skin holds the memory of a particular harvesting technique—a way of reading the pre-burst tremor in spore-pods that lets one gather twice as much before the cycle turns. He learned it from his grandmother. He has not been able to teach it to anyone, because the young harvester who would have apprenticed to him died last cycle of a vent-burn, and the elder himself is failing faster than he knows. The technique will be lost in perhaps forty cycles. Its loss will not destroy the Knot, but lean years will come more often after.

There is a hermit in the upper alcoves, half-claimed by hyphae, whose whispered conversations with the Breath have begun to cohere into something the power recognizes as the early seed-grammar of a genuine philosophy—a way of thinking about reciprocity and presence that would, if it could be transmitted, ease a thousand small sufferings across a hundred generations. But the hermit has no student. Hermits, by definition, do not.

There is a slow-deepening fissure in the eastern wall, behind the third terrace, that in perhaps two thousand cycles will breach into a neighboring pocket filled with cold inert gas. When it breaches, the Knot will suffocate in a single sleeping-cycle. No one alive will see this coming. It is not the pocket's will; it is merely geology, indifferent and incidental.

These are the wounds the power can address without unmaking the Ashborn. These are the threads it can mend without reweaving the cloth.

---

**What It Does**

It moves with the lightness of a thing that has practiced restraint for eons.

For the wheezing child, it does the smallest possible thing: it adjusts, with the gentleness of a whisper into cellular machinery, the misfolded protein in her developing gill-fringe. Nothing else. She wakes the next dim-hour breathing easier, and her mother weeps without knowing why, and the child grows up to be an unremarkable harvester who lives a full span and dies at the proper rim. No one ever knows a miracle occurred. *This is the signature of the power's work: that no one ever knows.*

For the elder with the unteachable technique, it sends a dream. Not a vision, nothing grand—just a dream, the kind the spore-tea sometimes brings. In the dream, the elder sees his grandmother teaching him, and he understands for the first time that the technique can be *encoded in a glyph*. He wakes and spends his last cycles etching the glyph into the hearth-stone with shaking obsidian-nailed fingers. The young ones puzzle over it for a generation before someone, in the right state of euphoria, suddenly understands. The technique is preserved. The lean years come less often. No one credits a god; they credit the elder, which is correct, because the dream only showed him what was already in him.

For the hermit, the power does something subtler still. It does not provide a student. To do so would be to violate the hermit's chosen solitude, which is itself part of how the philosophy is forming—pressed into shape by aloneness, the way a fungal cap is pressed into shape by the wall it grows against. Instead, the power waits until the hermit, hyphae-threaded and luminous, finally dies. And at the moment of dying, when the consciousness is loose and wide, the power *receives* the philosophy—not to keep it, but to hold it like a seed, and to plant it, decades later, in the half-sleeping mind of a child who will grow up to become the next hermit, who will think she is having her own thoughts, and who will be right, because the thoughts will be hers, only continuous now with what came before. The lineage of contemplatives in the upper alcoves becomes, across centuries, a genuine tradition. The Ashborn never name it. It simply becomes part of what it means to be old and strange and beloved among them.

For the fissure: the power reaches into the deep stone and, over the course of what to it is a single considered breath, reshapes the basalt's crystalline lattice along the fault. The fissure will not breach. It will hold for another hundred thousand cycles, by which time the Ashborn will be—whatever they will be. The power does not look that far. To look that far would be to predetermine, and predetermination is a kind of violence the wise refuse.

---

**The Larger Gift**

And then, having done these small things, the power considers whether there is anything larger to give. It turns this question carefully in its attention, as one turns a stone to see if it has weight enough to throw.

It considers revealing itself. It could. It could become a presence in the central hollow, luminous and unmistakable, and speak to the Ashborn in their guttural resonant tongue, and tell them they are loved. They would, perhaps, weave it into the Breath-lore. They would dance harder. Some would die of the euphoria.

But they already know they are loved. The Breath tells them so, every cycle, in the language they evolved to hear it in. To add a second voice would be to drown out the first.

It considers giving them knowledge of the world above—the crushing weight of rock, the oceans, the sky, the other peoples. It could. But what would they do with the knowledge? They cannot leave. Their lungs would die in clean air within minutes. The knowledge would be a window onto a country they could never enter, and the looking would teach them a new word, which is *exile*, which they do not currently possess and do not need.

It considers extending their lives, increasing their numbers, brightening their dim. Each of these it considers and sets gently down. Each would be a kindness that ruined them.

What it does, finally, is this: it leaves *a single warmth* in the chamber. Not heat—they have heat enough. A warmth of a different kind, the way a room feels warmer when someone you love is in the next room, even though the temperature is the same. It is a presence so subtle that the Ashborn will only ever feel it as a faint sweetness in the air on certain cycles, a slight extra steadiness in the Breath, a sense some of them will try to name and fail to name. The hermits will come closest. They will call it, in their forming philosophy, *the Listening*—the suspicion, never confirmed, that the pocket is held in some larger attention that wishes it well.

This warmth costs the power nothing and changes nothing material. It only means that the Ashborn, going about their harvests and their pairings and their dyings, are not *unwitnessed*. The witnessing does not impose. It only abides.

---

**Departure**

The power does not leave, exactly, because it was never localized enough to leave. But its attention drifts on, the way a reader's eye moves to the next line.

Behind it, in the pocket, a child breathes easier in her sleep. An elder dies with his glyph finished. A fissure quietly will not open. A hermit, three centuries hence, will wake from a dream she cannot remember and will spend the rest of her life thinking thoughts that are hers and also older than her, and she will be at peace with this even though she cannot say why.

The Breath sighs on. The mats pulse. The mandalas form and dissolve. Two hundred souls thrive in their hot dim sealed world, as they have, as they will.

And somewhere in the chamber, almost too faint to notice, there is a sweetness in the air that no one quite names.

That is what the benevolent and wise power does.

That is all it does.

It is enough.
