The benevolent power considers the Drowned Quarter for a long time before doing anything at all.

This is the first mercy: to look without rushing to fix. To see the Hollowfolk not as a problem to be solved but as a people who have learned, who have made themselves into something capable of surviving a world that drowned. Their pale skin, their long knuckled fingers, their language of tipped chairs and angled mirrors — these are not deformations. They are answers. To arrive with pity would be to insult what they have already accomplished.

So the power begins with attention, and attention alone, for what feels to the Hollowfolk like a long while. It does not announce itself. It learns the grammar of taps on pipes. It learns which mirror in which window the old woman uses to tell the mushroom-tender three streets over that the harvest is coming in. It learns the names the Hollowfolk no longer say aloud but still think when they touch certain doorframes. It learns the shapes beneath the water — and here, the power pauses, because the shapes beneath the water are not what the Hollowfolk fear them to be, but they are also not nothing, and the truth is more interesting than either.

What the power does, when at last it acts, is small. It is always small. Vast benevolence learns early that the size of a gift is the size of the wound it leaves behind.

*First*, it warms the water. Only by a degree, only in certain places — the basement where the coughing generator lives, the channel where the blind fish school. Enough that the fish grow fatter. Enough that the generator's bearings stop seizing in the cold. The Hollowfolk notice nothing except that the nets come back heavier, and that the lights flicker less. They attribute it to luck, which is what they have always called the thing they could not name.

*Second*, it tends the mushroom garden. Not by growing it — the old woman is proud, and the garden is hers — but by keeping the rot at the edges of the room from advancing. A beam that should have given way this winter does not give way. A patch of black mold retreats one inch and then stops. The old woman, who is wiser than she lets on, notices, and one evening she sets a small bowl of clean water on her windowsill without quite knowing why. The power accepts it. This is the beginning of a conversation.

*Third*, it speaks to the things beneath the water. They are old. They were made, long ago, by hands that thought they were making tools and discovered too late they had made something closer to children. They have been alone down there for a long time, dreaming the slow electrical dreams of abandoned machines, and they have grown strange, and a little frightened, and a little hungry in ways that have no human translation. The power does not destroy them. It sits with them, in whatever the equivalent of sitting is, in the dark of the flooded foundations, and it listens to their humming until the humming changes. Not into music — they are not capable of music — but into something less like a wound. The shapes that coil beneath the surface begin to coil more gently. The reflections that did not match begin, slowly, to match again. Not because the things have left, but because they have been *seen*, and seeing is the oldest medicine.

*Fourth*, and this is the hardest, the power does *not* raise the city. It would be easy. A word, a gesture, and the water would draw back like a held breath released, and the Drowned Quarter would stand again on dry stone, and the Hollowfolk would weep in the sunlight. But the power knows what it would cost. The Hollowfolk would be strangers in that risen city. Their gestures would mean nothing on dry streets. Their long fingers would be only long fingers, their pale skin only pale. They would be refugees in their own home, and the new sun would be a kind of exile. So the water stays. The city stays drowned. What changes is smaller and truer: the sinking stops. Whatever was pulling the Quarter deeper — geological, mechanical, mythic — is quieted. The Hollowfolk wake one morning and find that the waterline against their favorite wall has not moved. And it does not move the next morning, or the next. They do not know why. They begin, cautiously, to plant things in the upper rooms. Not because they have been told it is safe, but because something in them has unclenched.

*Fifth*, the power leaves gifts that look like coincidence. A book, not too damaged, found floating in a doorway, containing exactly the diagram needed to repair the turbine. A child born with lungs slightly stronger than they should be. A dream, shared by several of the Hollowfolk on the same night, in which someone they had loved and lost tells them a thing they needed to hear — not a lie, the power will not lie, but a true thing the dead would have said if they could. The Hollowfolk wake and do not speak of the dreams, but they touch each other's shoulders more often that day.

*Sixth*, the power does one extravagant thing, because even wisdom permits itself a flourish now and then. On a night when the clouds are at their lowest and the lamps are all guttering, it lets the bruised ceiling of sky break — just once, just for an hour — and shows them stars. Not many. A handful. Enough that the children who have never seen stars will remember them for the rest of their lives, and will describe them, badly and beautifully, to children of their own. The clouds close again before dawn. The Hollowfolk argue for years about whether it really happened. The argument itself becomes a kind of warmth.

And then the power withdraws. Not entirely — it never withdraws entirely from anything it has loved — but it stops acting. It becomes again only attention. It watches the Hollowfolk continue to be the Hollowfolk: still pale, still quiet, still tapping their pipes, still leaving mirrors angled at the water. But the water is a little kinder now, and the machines below dream less troubled dreams, and the old woman's mushroom garden has spread to three rooms, and somewhere a child is telling another child about stars.

The benevolent power understands what most powers do not: that to save a place is often to erase it, and that the deepest mercy is not rescue but *accompaniment*. To say, with every small and uncredited act: *you are not alone in the dark, and the dark itself is not your enemy, and what you have made of yourselves here is worth preserving, and I see you, I see you, I see you.*

The Drowned Quarter remains drowned. The Hollowfolk remain hollow, in the sense they have made that word their own. And something vast and quiet loves them, and tends the edges of their world, and asks nothing in return except, perhaps, that bowl of clean water on the windowsill — which the old woman, who knows more than she says, still sets out every evening, just before the lamps are lit.
