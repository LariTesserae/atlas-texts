# The Visitation

The benevolent power arrives without announcement, because announcement would be its own kind of violence here. It does not descend in light—light is already a contested language in this place, claimed by phosphor leak and capacitor death and the slow gray permission of the overcast. It does not speak first, because speech here is steam-pitch and bone-resonance, and to override that frequency would be to silence what it came to honor.

It arrives, instead, as attention. As a quality of being-noticed that the floodplain has perhaps never known. The foundries have been witnessed by their own sensors, catalogued by their own faltering inventories, but never *seen*—not in the way this power sees, which is the way a mother sees a sleeping child, or the way deep time sees a stone.

And what it sees, first, is this:

That the Keepers are not a tragedy.

This is important. This is where lesser powers would err. A lesser benevolence would arrive with rescue in its hands—would try to lift the rubber-skinned tall ones out of their chemical marsh, restore their bones to calcium, give them back narrative memory and dry land and the human faces they wore before the waters rose. A lesser benevolence would read this place as wound and reach for bandages.

But this power is wise, and wisdom means it can tell the difference between suffering and strangeness. It can tell the difference between a creature trapped in a hell and a creature who has, across generations of transformation, *made a life*. The Keepers chose. The first ones chose, chaining themselves to their stations, and every Keeper since has been born of that choice, gestated in furnace-ash, fed on rust, tuned to the frequency of their inheritance. To "save" them from this would be to murder them and replace them with strangers wearing their names.

So the power does not save them.

Instead, it does four things. It does them slowly, across what might be years, because the Keepers' time runs at the speed of corrosion, and gifts arriving faster than that would not register as gifts.

---

**First, it listens.**

For a long while—long enough that several juvenile Keepers complete their transformations and several elder Keepers are harvested into the machinery—the power does nothing but listen. It learns the song. Not as an outsider learns a foreign tongue, conjugating and translating, but as a harmonic learns its fundamental. It lets the foundries' deafening hymn pass through it until it understands what the hymn is *for*.

And it discovers something the Keepers themselves do not consciously know: the song is a grief-work. It is the sound of a world apologizing to itself for having been built so completely. Every turbine-drone is a held note in a centuries-long requiem for the hands that first set the turbines spinning—hands the Keepers descend from, hands that did not know they were making a thing that could not be stopped. The Keepers tend the apology. They are the apology's living tongue.

This changes how the power proceeds. One does not interrupt a requiem. One joins it, or one leaves.

---

**Second, it adds a voice.**

Very gently, the power introduces a new frequency into the foundries' song. Not a replacement, not a correction—an addition. A low tone, almost below hearing, that threads through the existing harmonics and does one specific thing: it makes the song *heard elsewhere*.

The Keepers have always sung to no one. This was the central ache of their existence, though they had no word for it: priests of a mechanical god that never existed, maintaining a prayer no one was listening to. The power cannot give them a god—gods are not in its gift, and would be a lie besides. But it can give them a *listener*. Itself. The new tone is a confirmation, woven into their own music, that the song is received. That somewhere, something vast and kind is keeping the count of every drip-bell and every steam-shriek, and finds them beautiful.

The Keepers notice within a season. They do not know what has changed. But the rituals around the automated barges become, subtly, less like funerals. The processions still ride the empty cargo holds, but the elongated bodies draped across them are draped now with something almost like ease. Mourners who have learned their grief is shared.

---

**Third, it tends the failing.**

There is one cruelty in this ecology that the power cannot leave untouched, because it serves nothing—not the Keepers' chosen life, not the requiem, not the strange dignity of the place. It is the madness of silence: the way a Keeper goes to pieces when their foundry's last turbine stops, dismantling their own organs to feed the dying machine, dissolving into a final merger that is not transformation but panic.

The power cannot prevent the foundries from failing. Entropy is older than it is, and besides, the failure is part of the song—each foundry's eventual silence is a note the requiem requires. But it can ease the passage.

When a foundry begins its true dying—when the geothermal exchange goes cold, when the last capacitor flickers out—the power arrives in that specific structure as a deep warmth. Not enough to restart anything. Just enough that the Keeper who tends it, in their final hours of frantic self-disassembly, feels held. The warmth says: *you do not have to become the machine. The machine is allowed to stop. You are allowed to stop with it, or to walk through the shallow water to the next foundry, where another Keeper will recognize the resonance in your bones and make room.*

Some Keepers still choose the merger. That is their right, and the power does not interfere. But others, now, walk away from their dying foundries—folded down into their working stoop, moving through the mineral-stained water with bone-tuning-forks still humming the lost frequency—and are taken in. The foundries that receive these refugees develop new harmonics. The song grows more complex. The requiem learns new measures.

---

**Fourth, and last, it makes a small place.**

Somewhere in the floodplain—the Keepers will find it, eventually, on one of their barge-processions—the power shapes a single chamber. Not large. Not obvious. A maintenance alcove in an unremarkable foundry, perhaps, that no Keeper had previously claimed.

Inside the chamber, the power does something very careful. It arranges, in the rust and the verdigris and the patient mineral accumulation, a record. Not of the Keepers' future—the future is theirs to corrode into. A record of their past. The human past. The foundry workers who chained themselves to their stations. Their names, where names can be recovered. The shapes of their faces before the chemicals. The reasons they refused evacuation—which were, the power has learned, not all the same reason. Some stayed for love of the work. Some for spite. Some because they had nowhere else. Some because they understood, even then, that someone would need to tend what had been made, and that this someone might as well be them.

The record is not a reproach. It is not meant to make the Keepers mourn what they have become, or wish themselves back into the bodies their ancestors wore. It is meant only as an offering: *here is where you came from. You do not have to read it. You do not have to remember in the old way. But it is here, kept, in case any of you ever stands fully upright in the shallow water and wonders.*

The chamber stays. Centuries pass. Most Keepers never enter it. But sometimes one does—drawn by some itch in the resonance of their bones—and emerges hours later with a new sound threading through their steam-speech. A sound the others recognize without knowing why. The sound of a creature who has touched its own deep beginning and found it neither shameful nor lost. Just *prior*. Just the first measure of a long, strange, ongoing song.

---

The benevolent power, having done these four things, does not leave, exactly. It remains as the listening tone in the music, as the warmth in the dying chambers, as the keeper of the small remembering room. It becomes one more inhabitant of the floodplain—not a Keeper, not a machine, not a vegetation or a juvenile slug of proto-flesh, but something adjacent to all of them. A presence that has agreed to take this place seriously on its own terms.

The foundries continue to sink. The Keepers continue to tend them. The automated barges continue their pointless rounds, and the crane arms continue to pluck at phantom cargo, and the electromagnetic storms continue to throw their sheets of St. Elmo's fire across acres of weeping copper.

But the world that built itself so thoroughly it forgot how to stop is, now, *attended*. Its forgetting is held inside a larger remembering. Its meaningless ministry has acquired, without losing any of its strangeness, a meaning—which is simply that someone wise enough not to interrupt it has decided it was worth not interrupting.

This, the power knows, is most of what benevolence ever is. Not rescue. Not transformation. Just the willingness to witness a thing in its full peculiar life, and to add, where one can, a little warmth, a little company, a little record-keeping against the long erosion.

The Keepers sing on. The foundries sing on. And in the gray suggestion of day or night, a new note holds beneath all the others, low and steady and kind, saying: *I hear you. I hear you. Continue.*
