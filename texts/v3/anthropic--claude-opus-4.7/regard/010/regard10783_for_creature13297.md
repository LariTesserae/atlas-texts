# The Watching

The benevolent power notices them, as such powers do, not all at once but slowly — the way one becomes aware of a sound that has been present for some time. It has been moving through the long galleries of existence, attending to things, and now it pauses over this high cold plateau and considers.

What does it see?

It sees a people who have made a cathedral of their entire world. It sees hands thick as stone mauls patiently fitting a single block into a wall begun by someone's great-great-grandmother. It sees a child — broad-faced, solemn — being taught the song that names every stair of a particular stair, three thousand stairs, each with its own syllable. It sees an old one die in a high room and the family gather not to weep loudly but to sit, breathing slowly, until the body has cooled to the temperature of the room and joined, in some quiet sense, the stone.

It sees that they are not suffering. This is the first and most important thing the power notices, and it considers this carefully, because beings of its kind are sometimes too quick to mistake hardship for suffering, and slowness for stagnation, and cold for cruelty. The Endurers are not waiting to be rescued from their lives. Their lives are not a problem to which the power is the solution.

And so the power does not do what a lesser, more anxious power might do. It does not warm the air. It does not gift them metal, or fire-engines, or the wheel-driven plough. It does not lift their burdens, because their burdens are also their meaning — the colossal stones they move are how they know each other, how a son knows his father's strength and his mother's patience, how a community knows itself across generations. To take the weight away would be to take the shape away.

But the power is not nothing, and it is not indifferent, and it has been moved.

So it does these things, quietly:

**It listens.** It bends some portion of its vast attention toward their epic songs, their resonant baritones echoing in the canyons of stone, and it learns them. It learns the name of every stair. It learns the names of the dead going back further than the singers themselves know — recovering, from the deep memory of the world, syllables that had been lost in rockfalls and avalanches and the simple forgetting that comes with time. And then, very gently, on certain still nights, when a singer reaches for a name that has slipped from the tradition, the name comes back to them as if it had been there all along. They do not know why. They feel only that the song is whole in a way it had not quite been, and they are content.

**It steadies one stone.** There is a particular keystone, in a particular arch, in a particular high city, that would have failed in four hundred years and brought down a quarter of a district and killed perhaps two thousand of them in their sleep. The power looks at this stone and, with something less than a thought, alters the pattern of its crystalline structure by an amount no instrument they will ever build could detect. The stone will now hold. No one will ever know. This is the kind of gift the power prefers: invisible, uncredited, indistinguishable from the ordinary mercy of the world.

**It speaks to one of the wide-winged birds.** Not in words — the bird has no use for words — but in the language birds actually use, which is shape and updraft and the geometry of hunger. It tells the bird, in this language, where a herd of the shaggy herbivores has wandered, far from any hunting party, in a season when the city of Vehl-Karruk is close to a hungry winter. The bird, who already had a loose understanding with certain hunters of Vehl-Karruk, flies low over their watch-cairn three mornings running, in a particular pattern. The hunters read the pattern. They find the herd. The winter is not hungry. No one in Vehl-Karruk will ever compose a song about this, because nothing unusual happened. A good winter is not a song. The power is pleased that it is not a song.

**It does one thing that is harder to describe.** Among the Endurers there is a young one — call her Thuun, though that is not quite her name — who has been having thoughts that frighten her. She has been looking at the angle of a particular ramp and the way the counterweights move and she has been *almost* understanding something her people do not yet understand: that force and distance trade for one another in a ratio, and that this ratio is a number, and that the number is the same number whether you are lifting a stone or a mountain. She does not have the words. She has only the feeling of standing at the edge of a vast clear space inside her own mind, and she is afraid of it, because no one she knows has stood there, and she does not know if she is allowed.

The power considers Thuun for a long moment.

It does not give her the words. The words must be hers, or her children's, or her children's children's. It does not tell her she is right. To be told would be to be robbed.

What it does is this: it arranges, by the smallest possible nudge to the day, for her to be sitting in a particular sheltered courtyard at a particular hour when her grandmother passes through. Her grandmother, who is very old, and who once had similar thoughts and put them away because there was no one to share them with, sees the look on Thuun's face. The grandmother sits down beside her, slowly, with the great ponderous grace of her people, and says nothing for a long time. Then she says, in their slow tongue: *"What are you looking at, child?"*

And Thuun, who would not have spoken to anyone else, speaks to her grandmother. And the grandmother, who has carried this unspoken thing for sixty years, listens. And something passes between them that will, in three generations, become a school of thought, and in thirty, a discipline, and in three hundred, perhaps, the beginning of a different relationship between the Endurers and the stones they love.

The power did not cause this. The power only made certain the two of them were in the courtyard at the same time. Everything else was theirs.

**Finally,** the power does one small thing for itself, which benevolent powers are also allowed to do. It commits the plateau to memory. Not a casual memory, but the deep kind — the kind reserved for things one does not wish to lose. It memorizes the saw-tooth horizon and the brittle light and the slow percussion of stone on stone. It memorizes the way the Endurers move, the way their broad hands close around a chisel, the particular gravity of their patience. It does this because the universe is very large and contains many things, and the power has learned that it must choose, sometimes, what to hold close, or all of it becomes equally distant.

It holds this place close.

Then it goes on, because there are other places, and other beings, and the work of a benevolent power is never done. But some attention remains. Some small fraction of its regard stays here, attending, the way a parent half-listens for a child in another room while doing other things. If something terrible were to happen — a comet, a plague, some catastrophe beyond the slow strength of their stone-laying — the power would return. It has, in a sense, left the door open.

The Endurers do not know any of this. They go on lifting their stones. The hammering continues, slow and rhythmic, like the beat of a geological heart. The wind sighs through the canyons. Somewhere, a singer finds a name she did not know she knew, and adds it to the song, and the song is a little more complete than it was yesterday.

This is enough. This is, in fact, exactly the right amount.
