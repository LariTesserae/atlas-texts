# What the Power Would Do

The benevolent and wise power arrives the way wisdom usually does: without announcement, occupying no light-shaft, casting no second shadow. It has come because something here interests it — not the spectacle of a freshly torn world, which is common in the universe, but the particular gesture of the lean figure at the entrance, the raised palm asking for quiet while the ceiling decides.

That gesture tells the power almost everything it needs to know.

---

## First, It Listens

For what would be, by surface clocks, a long time — perhaps three turns of the moon, perhaps three years; the power is not in a hurry — it does nothing but attend. It learns the names that aren't yet names. It feels Shiver-Mother breathe through the soles of every sleeper. It watches a Light Shepherd, a girl of seven with one cloudy eye, tip her mica panel by the width of a fingernail to chase a beam onto a moss mat, and it understands that this is already a kind of prayer, and already a kind of engineering, and that the people here have not yet learned to separate the two — which is, the power notes, a thing worth protecting.

It watches a duel of balance. It watches a Return Walk. It watches a man press his thumbprint into the Ledger and then, alone in the dark, press his thumb again against his own chest, as if claiming himself.

The power finds that it loves them. This is not surprising. Benevolent powers generally do; the wisdom is in what they do *next*.

---

## What It Considers, and Sets Aside

It could stabilize the rift. One soft adjustment to the plates beneath, and no ceiling would ever fall again. The Second Sky would hang forever, mica-freckled and safe. The people would build higher, more permanently. They would lose the habit of low walls and portable belongings. They would lose, the power sees, the quality of attention that made the power notice them in the first place. *Set aside.*

It could give them fire hotter than their bellows can reach — could place, just so, a vein of richer ore where a Seam-Runner would find it next week. But it sees how the Bellows-Feet rotate, how the lungs of the young are spent in monthly turns, and it understands that the technology and the social form are growing together, root and shoot of one plant. Hand them bronze now and the rotation breaks; someone becomes a permanent smith, then a chief, then a king, and the Quiet Council fills with shouters. *Set aside.*

It could speak. Appear in a shaft of noon-light and offer counsel. But these are people who have made silence into etiquette and gesture into grammar; a voice from the air would be a kind of violence, a Bright Debt so large no bucket-carrying could repay it. They would build a religion around it within a generation, and the religion would be about the power, not about Shiver-Mother, who is the truer teacher because she is actually there. *Set aside.*

It could remove the pale eel from the siphon pool, or place one there if there is none — could resolve the rumor either way. But rumors, the power knows, are a young culture's way of practicing the shape of belief. *Set aside.*

The power considers, and sets aside, almost everything it could do. This is most of wisdom.

---

## What It Does

**It does four small things.**

**One.** On a night when the turquoise phosphor draws itself along a new fracture, the power bends — very slightly — the way the mineral light behaves. Not more of it. Not brighter. Only: it lasts a few breaths longer than it would have, and it traces shapes that are a little more clearly *not* human, a little more clearly something else keeping pace. The children who see it will describe it accurately for once, and the adults, who have been pretending not to hear, will find that pretending has become harder. The imagined heirs are nudged half a step toward being acknowledged neighbors. When the people meet, in some century, whatever actually lives in the deep cracks, they will meet it with less surprise and less violence. This is the smallest possible gift: a slight increase in the honesty of a rumor.

**Two.** The power finds the moss. There are seventeen varieties pinned to the lighted walls, and one of them — a curl no one has yet favored, because it grows slowly and tastes of iron — carries, in its cells, a tendency the power recognizes. With the lightest possible touch, less than a breath, it encourages that tendency. The moss will, over the next forty years, become slightly better at binding the calcium in cave-cricket dust into something a child's bones can use. Infants in the rift will grow a finger's-width taller and noticeably stronger. No one will know why. The Stone Nurses will credit the gutter alignment. The Quiet Council will credit patience. The power is content to be uncredited; this is the second part of wisdom.

**Three.** There is a man — the power will not name him, because naming, even in thought, can shape a life unfairly — who is going to be killed in the next tremor by a slab the Stone Nurses misjudged. He is not especially important. He is a Bellows-Foot whose month is nearly up, who has begun to cough, who has a daughter of four learning to keep her hand on the wall. The power does not save him. To save him would be to step inside the deal the people have made with Shiver-Mother, and that deal is theirs. But it does, on the morning of the day, cause a soot-colored bat to fly oddly — to circle twice instead of once before returning to its crack. A Seam-Runner notices. Seam-Runners notice bats. She follows the bat's odd path with her eyes and sees, in the ceiling above the kiln pit, a hairline she had not marked. She marks it. The kiln is moved by two paces. The slab falls on schedule. The man lives. His daughter, in forty years, will be one of the six on the Quiet Council, and her chief virtue will be that she remembers entire nights without speaking, because her father taught her, having been given an unearned second portion of nights.

The power has not interfered. It has only adjusted the flight of a bat. The people did the rest. This is the third part of wisdom: to find the smallest lever, and to let the lifting be done by those whose arms are already there.

**Four.** And this is the one the power thinks about longest.

The Second Sky — the great half-detached mica-freckled sheet — is going to fall. Not soon; not in the lifetime of anyone now pressing a thumbprint to the Ledger. But it will fall, and when it does it will kill many, and the survivors will lose their central myth in the same hour they lose their kin, which is more than a young people should have to bear at once.

The power could secure it. It does not. The myth requires the fall; the people have built the meaning of *ascending* into the very fact that the sheet is unstable, and to stabilize it would be to hollow out their cosmology from beneath.

Instead, the power does this: it deepens, by a hair, a single hairline crack in the sheet's upper edge. The crack will widen, very slowly, over the generations. Long before the sheet falls, it will begin to *sing* — a low thrum when the wind passes through the fissure above, audible only on the quietest nights, and only to those who have learned the people's discipline of listening.

The people will hear it. They will not know what it means. They will, being who they are, decide what it means. The power suspects they will decide that the Second Sky is *speaking* — preparing them, counting down, teaching them the song they will need to sing as they ride it into some other openness. They will compose, over decades, a music to answer it. By the time the sheet falls, every child will know the song. The ones who survive will sing it over the ones who don't, and the song will be old enough by then to hold the grief.

The power has given them, in effect, two extra human lifetimes of warning, disguised as a slow miracle, embedded in the geology itself. It has not told them anything. It has only made the stone slightly more articulate, in a language they were already learning to hear.

---

## Then It Leaves

The power does not stay. Staying would become, eventually, a presence; presence would become a fact; facts become foundations, and these people have agreed, wisely, to build on nothing that pretends to be a foundation.

Before it goes, it pauses once more at the entrance, where the lean figure with the mica shard still stands — or another like them; the post is always filled. The power raises, in its own fashion, a palm. Requests quiet. Receives it. Passes through.

What it leaves behind: a bat's slightly altered flight, a moss's slightly altered chemistry, a phosphor's slightly extended glow, and a crack in the great sheet that will, in time, learn to sing.

What it takes with it: the memory of a girl tipping a mica panel by the width of a fingernail, and the understanding that somewhere in the universe, a people who have not yet agreed to call themselves a people are, with stone tools and held breath, doing the work that powers like itself were made to honor and, mostly, to leave alone.

The hollow grows. Geology writes the lines. Water punctuates them. The people move quickly into every comma the earth creates.

And something, now, hums faintly in the ceiling — so faintly that for a hundred years no one will be quite sure they heard it.

That is enough. That is, the power thinks as it goes, almost always enough.
