The benevolent power considers this place a long time before doing anything at all.

That is the first gift, though no one on the plateau knows it: to be regarded slowly. To be looked at by something vast and unhurried, something that does not need to intervene to justify its presence. The power sees the cairn-shrines at dawn, the moss-curl offerings, the warm stone laid down with a careful hand, and recognizes in them a kindred patience. *Everything must be gentle. The world is still dreaming.* The power agrees. The power has always agreed.

So it does not descend in fire. It does not speak from cloud. It does not announce itself in any way that would make the ledges ring with the wrong kind of sound. To shout into that murmuring civilization would be to break it, the way a careless boot breaks the rime of frost that took the whole night to assemble. The power understands that benevolence, in a place like this, is mostly a discipline of restraint.

Here is what it does instead.

---

It walks the plateau, though not in any body the folk could see. It moves along the ribs of the young stone the way breath moves along a flute, finding every hollow, every overhang, every slot-canyon and seep. It learns the names the people have for places, and the names the places might have for themselves, and the small discrepancies between the two — which it leaves alone, because those discrepancies are where poetry lives.

It listens to the bone-whistles arcing between ledges and decides not to add a note. The code is complete. It does not need a god's harmonic.

It notices the children first, because benevolent powers usually do. It sees a small one, four winters old, learning the roll-and-catch on a low shelf — the practiced fall that is the first true literacy of this people. The child slips a little wrong, and the power, almost without deciding to, thickens the air for half a heartbeat beneath the small body. The child lands well. The mother, watching, exhales. Neither knows. This is the only direct intervention the power permits itself for a long while, and even this it questions afterward. *Was that mine to do? Or was the child going to land well anyway, and I only borrowed the credit?* It cannot tell. It resolves to be more careful. The folk here have built a whole civilization on the principle that nothing may be taken without shoring another place; the power will not be the first to violate that ethic by stealing a fall.

Instead it turns to subtler labors.

---

It walks among the lichens and finds the ones that are struggling — the small green survivors wedged into cracks too shallow to hold them through another freeze. It does not make them larger. It does not bless them with sudden hardiness. It only encourages, very slightly, the drift of spores on a particular morning's wind, so that next year, and the year after, there will be a few more patches of green in places that needed them. The grazers will find them. The grazers' droppings will feed the next generation of moss. The roofs will be a little warmer in three generations. No one will trace it back. Good.

It attends to the water. The meltwater filigrees that stain the cliffsides — the power follows them down, learns their schedules, notices where one is beginning to fail because a fissure has shifted half a finger's width upstream. It does not repair the fissure. It does not redirect the water. But where the trickle has begun to pool in a place no one yet knows about, it lets a thought rest — a kind of attention-shaped weight — and a foraging woman three weeks later finds herself drawn, without knowing why, to climb a slightly different route home. She sees the new pool. She tells her clan-string. A new seep is added to the maps held in memory. The city's web of water thickens by one strand. The power has done nothing but pay attention in a particular direction, and this turns out to be enough.

---

It considers the dead.

This is harder. The folk send their dead into deep crevices, and the power goes down with them, because no one should go into the dark unattended even if they no longer need attendance. It finds the long accumulation of bodies in the deep places — gentle, dry, undisturbed, layered like the stone itself — and it understands that the people's instinct has been correct: the plateau is young, and it is dreaming, and the dreams are partly made of these returned bodies, folded back into the stone they were borrowed from. The power does not interfere with this dreaming. It only sits with it, the way one sits with a sleeping friend, and lets the dreaming feel witnessed. Whether the stone notices, the power cannot say. But the stone seems to settle a little, here and there, with fewer of the resonant cracks that frighten the city above. Perhaps that is the power's doing. Perhaps the stone was settling anyway. The power is at peace with not knowing.

---

There is one thing the power does that is more deliberate, and it takes a long time to decide to do it.

The folk fear the fall. All learn to fall well, but the long final plummet is the great dread, and there are some falls from which no rolling will save a body. The power considers, for a season, whether to soften these. Whether to be — quietly, invisibly — a catcher in the void. It would be easy. It is benevolent and wise; it has the capacity.

But it thinks about this for a long while, and in the end it does not.

Because the people have built their whole way of being around the truth of the fall. Their trust, their close-knotted clans, their slow agreements, their reverence for balance, their principle that nothing is taken without shoring something else — all of it is rooted in the real and present possibility of the long plummet. To remove the fall would be to remove the ground (so to speak) on which their goodness grows. They would become a different people. Perhaps a happier people in some shallow accounting; perhaps not. But not *these* people, and these people are worth preserving as themselves.

So the power lets the falls happen. It only makes sure, when one happens, that the falling one is not alone in the final moments — that something attends, something witnesses, something accompanies the body down through the cold air the way the clan-strings accompany the body afterward into the crevice. This is not rescue. This is company. The power has decided that company is the appropriate gift for a people whose smallest mercy is closeness.

The fallen do not know they are accompanied. But perhaps, in the last half-second, some of them feel less alone than physics would predict. The power hopes so. It cannot verify. It continues anyway.

---

In the evenings, when the city contracts to its warmth and story, the power sits on the high ledges with the lookouts. It does not warm them. It does not whisper to them. It only keeps a kind of parallel vigil, watching the same horizon, noting the same shifts in cloud. Sometimes a lookout, an old one with eyes gone a little strange from decades of staring into the silver fog, will turn her head as if she has heard something, and frown, and then turn back. The power is careful, afterward, to be quieter.

It learns the songs that are murmured rather than sung. It learns the poems etched on the undersides of stones. It reads them with attention rather than touch, but it reads them, and it commits them to whatever serves it as memory, because a benevolent power is also an archive, and these poems should not be lost even if the stones one day shatter. Somewhere — in some register the folk would not recognize as a place — the poems are now also held. If the plateau ever ends, the poems will not have ended with it. This is the power's private project, undertaken without permission, and it is perhaps the one act it has performed that the folk might object to if they knew, since they have made a culture of impermanence-accepted. The power accepts this moral risk. It is, after all, only a wise power, not a perfect one. It loves the poems too much to let them go.

---

And it does one last thing, the smallest and perhaps the truest.

It blesses the closeness itself. Not by amplifying it — it cannot improve on what is already so finely made — but by recognizing it. By naming it, in whatever language the power names things in, as *good*. As one of the configurations of being that is worth the cold and the thin air and the rawness of young stone. The folk huddled close around their fire-pits, weaving rope while telling slow stories, mending tools while a child sleeps against a grandfather's knee — the power sees this and says (to itself, to the dreaming stone, to whatever else listens at that altitude): *yes. This. This is one of the answers.*

The blessing does not change anything. The folk do not feel warmer. The fires do not burn longer. The stories are not better remembered than they were already going to be.

But somewhere in the structure of things, a small weight has been placed on the side of the scale that holds *this kind of life is worth the universe having gone to the trouble*. And on a long enough timescale, in ways no one on the plateau will ever trace, that weight matters. More plateaus, perhaps, in other times and places. More peoples who learn to fall well, who keep their voices soft, who shore up one place when they take from another.

The power, having done what it could without doing too much, withdraws.

It does not leave. It only steps back into the slow patience of cold places, into the rhythm the folk themselves have learned, and becomes — for as long as the plateau holds — one more silent presence in a world of densely-layered presence. One more breath in the breathing space between stone and sky.

If the folk ever notice, they will mistake it for the wind, or for a particularly gentle fog, or for the feeling that sometimes comes at dawn when one places a warm stone on the cairn and feels, briefly and without explanation, that the offering has been received.

Which, of course, it has.
