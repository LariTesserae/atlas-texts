# What the Power Would Do

The benevolent power arrives without arriving. It does not descend in light or announce itself in thunder — both would be redundant here, where the sky already does such things and the inhabitants have long since learned to distrust spectacle. It comes instead the way a thaw comes: slowly, and only to those parts of a thing that are ready.

It looks first. This is the most important thing, and the part most easily skipped.

It sees the world as the world is: young mountains still deciding their shape, water that has not yet learned the patience of carving, an atmosphere thin enough that the stars press close even at noon. It sees the cities clinging like lichen to the cliffs — and it does not pity them, because they are beautiful. The cantilevers, the layered terraces, the pipes carrying meltwater through walls heated by foundry-breath: these are not scars. They are a kind of conversation between a people and a place that did not, at first, want them.

It sees the people. Their thick skin, their narrow squinting eyes, their economical walk. It sees how they pause, sometimes, at a high window or on a gantry over a gorge, and look at the mist rising off the rocks, and forget for a moment to be useful. It sees what they have built and what they have refused to build. It sees the melancholy — that particular weight of a people who know they are guests in a house that was not made for them and could, at any moment, evict them.

And the power sees the stillness. The equilibrium that is not peace but something more like a held breath. A world frozen in the posture of having just survived something. A people frozen in the posture of being about to be careful.

What does it do?

**It does not fix anything.** This is the first discipline. There is nothing here that is broken in the way the power could simply mend. The industry is not a wound; it is a lung. The absence of fauna is not, in this case, a crime to be undone — the world is too young, too high, too raw; the silence is in part the silence of a place that has not yet been written into. To fill it with imported life would be to answer a question the world has not finished asking.

**It does not reveal itself.** A people this cautious, this aware of consequence, would receive a visible god the way they receive a tremor in the bedrock: by going very still, by reorganizing everything around the new fact, by losing some essential thing in themselves to the management of it. The power knows this. Its benevolence includes the discipline of not being known.

What it does instead is smaller, and stranger, and takes a long time.

**It loosens the stillness, by a little.** Not enough to break the equilibrium — the equilibrium is load-bearing — but enough that the held breath can become a slow breathing. It does this by adjusting almost nothing: a slight increase in the frequency with which a particular kind of cloud forms in a particular kind of valley. A barely-perceptible warming of certain south-facing stones. The arrival, over centuries, of three new species of moss in places the inhabitants had given up on. Small permissions. Small invitations for the world to continue being young.

**It listens to the myths.** The stories the people tell — the old ones, from before the factories — the power treats as load-bearing too. It does not correct them. Where a legend says a particular pass is guarded by a thing that should not be crossed lightly, the power, finding the pass in fact ungarded, considers becoming the guardian, quietly, just so the story remains true. There is more wisdom in their inherited carefulness than in any direct instruction it could give. The myths are how this people remembers to be reverent. The power reinforces the myths, the way one might prop up an old beam in a house one loves.

**It attends to the individual being.** Here the power becomes most particular. It finds the engineer who looks out of the gantry window. The child on the third terrace who has begun to ask why there are no birds. The old woman in the archive who is trying to reconstruct a song her grandmother sang about a river that no longer exists in that form. To each of these, in ways indistinguishable from coincidence, the power offers a small thing: a sudden clarity of thought at the right hour, a dream that resolves a problem they had not known how to name, a sentence overheard from a stranger that turns out to be the missing line of the song. Nothing that could be traced. Nothing that takes from them the dignity of having found it themselves.

Because this is what benevolence looks like, for a people like this one: not rescue, which they would experience as condescension; not revelation, which they would experience as catastrophe; but **the quiet underwriting of their own best instincts.** The power becomes, in effect, a slight tilt in the floor of the world, such that when the inhabitants walk in the direction they were already trying to walk — toward care, toward beauty, toward a way of being industrial that does not finally consume the thing it depends on — they find the walking a little easier than they expected. They credit themselves. They are right to.

**And it waits.** This is the last thing, and the longest. The power waits with the world. It does not require the world to become something. It does not need the cities to bloom into gardens or the silence to fill with birdsong on a schedule. It is willing for this place to be exactly what it is — strange, loud, lonely, lovely, caught between its primordial bones and its post-industrial skin — for as long as that is what it needs to be. The power has the patience of someone who loves a thing without needing it to be finished.

If, in some distant century, the equilibrium finally relaxes — if the held breath becomes a true exhalation, and the people lift their eyes from the careful management of their fragility and discover that they can afford, now, to be a little extravagant with their living — then the power will be there too, unsurprised, having expected nothing and hoped for everything.

And if that century never comes, and the world remains forever caught in its strange suspended dance between mountain and machine, then the power will love it exactly as it is, and consider its attention well spent.

This is what a benevolent and wise power does, when it draws near to a place like this:

It comes close enough to be a warmth, and far enough to be mistaken for the weather.
