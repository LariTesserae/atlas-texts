# What the Wise Power Would Do

The benevolent power arrives the way weather arrives—without announcement, without disturbance to the careful surfaces. It does not appear as a figure. It has learned, in its long wisdom, that figures are interruptions, and interruption is its own kind of violence against a place that has tuned itself so finely to its own breathing.

It comes first only as attention.

It hovers in the spaces between the clay walls and listens. It listens for a long time—what would be years, if it counted in years, which it does not. It listens to the whisper-language of the people, the dropped-pebble divinations of the keepers, the differential whistling of children's ceramic toys. It tastes the air the way the keepers taste it. It learns the names the people have for the seven kinds of dampness, the eleven kinds of dryness. It learns which old woman in the third district can predict rain three days out by the ache in her wrist, and which child in the ninth has the rarest gift: she can hear, faintly, the water moving in cisterns two districts away.

The power notices something the people themselves do not quite notice, because they are inside it: that this civilization is *beautiful*. Not merely functional, not merely admirable in its adaptation—but genuinely, achingly beautiful in the way a poem is beautiful, or a long marriage. Every gesture rhymes with every other. The hollow sculptures that double as moisture traps. The ashes in the bricks. The water-shares as marriage dowry. The dying carried up to give their breath back to the sky. It is one of the most complete things the power has ever encountered, this civilization—a single sustained answer to a single sustained question, refined across centuries into something like a prayer that has learned to pray itself.

And so the power faces its first real question, which is not *what shall I do* but *what is the shape of love here?*

Because the obvious answers are wrong.

It could, of course, return the lake. With a thought it could call up the waters that left this depression in the deep past, fill the basin until the cities became islands and then drowned reefs and then memory. This would be, by any naive accounting, a gift: abundance where there was scarcity, ease where there was struggle.

It would also be annihilation. The people are not people-deprived-of-water. They are the people-who-are-the-shape-of-this-water's-absence. Give them the lake and you do not save them; you erase them and replace them with strangers who happen to wear their faces. The succulent gardens would rot. The keepers' beads would become costume jewelry. The Dry Dance would become folklore, performed for tourists from wherever tourists come from. The children would no longer dream of measurements. Within three generations the narrow shoulders would broaden, the long fingers would coarsen, the whorls of fingerprints would lose their clay. A different people would inhabit the ruins of this one and call themselves its descendants, and the lie would be gentle and total.

The power knows this. The power has done this elsewhere, long ago, before it became wise. It remembers.

It could, alternatively, do nothing, and call the doing-nothing a form of respect. But this too is wrong. To witness and withhold is not always reverence; sometimes it is only cowardice with a clean robe on. The power is here, and to be here is already to have entered the room. The question is how to be a good guest.

So the power thinks for a while—what would be a long while, if it counted.

And then it begins.

---

**First, it does the smallest thing.**

In the ninth district there is a child, the one who can hear water moving two districts away. She is six years old and she has not yet been tested by the keepers; she will be tested next year, and she will pass, and her life will be set on the keeper-path, which is a good path, an honored path. But the power notices something the testing would not have noticed: the child's gift is unusual not in degree but in kind. She does not hear water the way keepers hear water. She hears something underneath the water, in the clay itself—a slow movement, a deep flexion, the breathing of the old lakebed far below the deepest cisterns.

There is, in fact, water down there. Not a lake. A slow vein, an ancient seep, moving through stone at the pace of generations. The people do not know about it. They could not reach it with their current tools, and even if they could, drawing from it would be a different relationship with water than the one they have built their lives around—it would be taking rather than receiving, and the whole moral architecture of the cities is built on receiving.

The power does not tell the child about the deep water. That would be a gift that becomes a burden, a knowledge that would distort her life and through her the life of her district.

Instead, the power simply *deepens* her hearing, very slightly, so that throughout her long life she will sometimes pause, head tilted, and feel without quite knowing why that the earth beneath her is alive and patient and not finished with her people. She will mention this, occasionally, to her own children. It will become a small family saying: *the ground listens back*. In four generations a descendant of hers will write a poem about it that will be carved into a fountain house wall. In nine generations, when the climate finally shifts and the cities face a crisis the old systems cannot meet, someone reading that poem will be moved to dig.

This is what the power can do well: it can plant time.

---

**Second, it tends to a wound.**

In the fourth district there is a keeper named Inel who is dying of a sickness that has nothing to do with water. She is forty-one. She has three children, the youngest only four. She is the finest keeper of her generation; she can divine the level of a cistern within a finger's breadth from across a courtyard. Her dying is a tragedy no one knows yet to grieve, because she has not yet told anyone, not even her partner, that the cough is more than a cough.

The power could heal her. It considers this for a long time.

It decides: yes. But carefully.

It does not erase the illness, because that would be a miracle, and miracles are loud, and loud things break the quiet of this place. Instead, it nudges. It encourages a particular fungus in the succulent gardens to bloom slightly out of season. It guides the wrist of an old healer who is preparing a poultice for an unrelated ailment to add, on a whim he will not remember having, a pinch of this fungus to the mixture. It arranges for Inel to brush past the healer in the covered walkway and to breathe in the dust of the poultice he is carrying. The dust settles in her lungs. It does what it does. The cough eases over a season. She lives. She does not know she was saved. The healer does not know he saved her. The fungus does not know it healed.

This is the power's preferred kind of intervention: causation laundered through the world's own materials, so that no one ever has to know they were touched.

Inel lives to be eighty-three. Her youngest child becomes the keeper of the fourth district's deepest cistern, and her grandchild is the one who, nine generations after the listening girl, finally digs.

---

**Third, it makes a single offering, and asks nothing.**

The power notices that the people dream of water but no longer dream of swimming. The keepers descend into the cisterns, yes, but they descend with ropes and lamps and ceremony; they do not float. The concept of the body held by water has been lost from the culture's interior life. This is a small loss in a long catalog of losses that no one is keeping, but the power, which keeps every catalog, notices it.

It does not restore swimming. To restore swimming would be to argue with the cities' whole logic.

But once, on a single night, in a single district, it sends a dream to every sleeper at once. The dream is brief. In the dream, each person is held—not by water, exactly, but by something that has water's qualities of buoyancy and enclosure and forgiveness. They are not afraid. They are very young in the dream, or very old; the dream does not distinguish. They are simply held, for what feels like a long time, by something larger than themselves that means them well.

In the morning, the people of that district wake and do not speak of the dream, because they cannot quite remember it, and what they can remember does not have words in their language. But for the rest of their lives, when they are frightened or grieving or simply tired beyond their capacity, they will feel, briefly, a sensation of being held. They will not know where it comes from. They will assume it is something their own minds do.

It is something their own minds do, now. The power has given them a small new capacity and disguised it as something they already had. This is the gift it is proudest of, though no one will ever know to thank it, which is the point.

---

**Fourth, it does not interfere with the great question.**

The great question, which hangs over the Cistern Cities like a weather no one names: the climate is changing. Slowly, but it is changing. The rains are becoming less predictable. In two hundred years, perhaps three, the careful balance of the cities will fail. The cisterns will run dry faster than they can fill. The civilization will face a choice it has never had to face: transform, or end.

The power could prevent this. It could nudge the atmosphere, shift the patterns, buy the cities another thousand years of the rhythm they know.

It does not.

This is the hardest of its decisions, and it makes the decision slowly, and it makes the decision with grief.

It does not intervene because to do so would be to take from the people their own future—the future in which they themselves must decide what they are, beneath their adaptations. Are they the people of this particular water-collection technology, or are they something underneath that, something that could become a different people without ceasing to be themselves? This is their question. It is not the power's question. To answer it for them would be to treat them as artifacts to be preserved rather than as a living thing that has the right to its own becoming, including the right to its own ending if ending is what comes.

What the power does instead is small. It ensures that when the crisis comes, the deep water beneath the lakebed will still be there. It ensures that the poem about the ground listening back will not be lost in any of the fires or floods or forgettings of the intervening generations. It ensures that a few specific children, in a few specific generations, will be born with the kind of mind that can hold both reverence for the old ways and curiosity about the new—the kind of mind that can lead a people through a transformation without breaking them.

It plants time. It plants time. It plants time.

And then it does the last thing, which is also the first thing, and the only thing it has really been doing all along:

It loves them. Quietly, completely, without their knowledge or consent. It loves the narrow shoulders and the clay-darkened palms and the whispering and the hollow sculptures and the keepers' beads and the dying carried upward to give their breath back to the sky. It loves the whole improbable answer they have made of themselves. It loves them not as a curator loves a collection but as one living thing loves another living thing: with the understanding that they will change, that they may end, that the love does not depend on their permanence.

The power stays for a while longer, listening. Then it goes, the way weather goes, leaving the cities exactly as it found them, except for the small adjustments no one will ever trace back to it: a child's slightly deepened hearing, a healer's unremembered whim, a dream that becomes a capacity, a poem that survives.

The cities continue. The dawn harvest moves through the streets. The keepers drop their pebbles and listen. Somewhere in the ninth district, a six-year-old tilts her head, hearing something she cannot name, and her mother calls her in for the morning meal.

The ground listens back.
