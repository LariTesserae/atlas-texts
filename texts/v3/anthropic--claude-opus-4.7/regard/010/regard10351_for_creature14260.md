# The Visitation

The benevolent and wise power considered the Foundling Warren for a long time before doing anything.

This is the first thing to understand: that wisdom, in a being of sufficient scope, looks at first indistinguishable from inaction. The power had seen many peoples in many places, and had learned—through errors it still mourned, through gifts that had become curses in the third generation—that to help is not the same as to intervene, and that intervention is not the same as love.

So it watched. It watched for what would be, in the Warren's accretion-time, the lifespan of a small pillar: perhaps four years. It watched a child named Sef learn to read drip-rates by laying her ear against the cool stone and counting against her own pulse. It watched a Live-Framer named Oto position the cradle for his unborn daughter at the precise angle where, in eighteen years, a column would rise to greet her shoulder as she stood beside it. It watched the Forma-Kin in the deep chamber, and it understood—because it was wise, and wisdom means not flinching from what is strange—that they were not deluded. They were doing something real. The boundary between flesh and stone is a human convention, and the Found had simply chosen to renegotiate it.

The power saw what a less wise power would have seen as problems:

The mineral load in the kidneys. The grinding of teeth. The lithification of joints in middle age. The babies who did not survive their first winter because the aqua calcis was too rich for their small bodies. The slow narrowing of certain galleries that would, in two generations, force a migration the Found did not yet know they would need to make. The fungal blight that would arrive in eleven years, carried on driftwood from a surface war the Found had never heard of.

A lesser power would have fixed these things. Would have descended in light and altered the chemistry of the water, lengthened the lives, opened new galleries with a gesture, immunized the fungus gardens. Would have, in short, loved them into something other than themselves.

The benevolent and wise power did not do this. Because it had learned—and this is the hard-won knowledge that distinguishes wisdom from mere goodness—that a people is not a problem to be solved. A people is a long answer to a question the universe asked in a particular place. The Found were the answer the limestone had given. To erase their suffering would be to erase the question, and with it, the answer, and with it, the Found themselves.

But the power was not nothing. It was not absent. Love that does not act is not love; it is only sentiment. So the power considered: *what can I do that honors what they are, rather than replacing it?*

Here is what it did.

---

**First**, it went to the deepest chamber, where the Forma-Kin lay half-precipitated into the floor, and it sat with them. Not as a god—it had outgrown the appetite for that posture long ago—but as a guest. It made itself small enough to be unnoticed, and it sat for what would be, by surface reckoning, three days. It listened to the subaudible hum of the lattice assembling. It listened to the breath of the woman whose legs were stone and whose ribs still rose and fell.

And it gave her, very gently, the one thing the Found's cosmology lacked: confirmation. Not in words. In a particular quality of attention. She felt, for one moment, that the becoming she was attempting was *seen*—that somewhere in the vast inventory of the real, her transition was logged, witnessed, held. She had never doubted before. But she had also never known, until that moment, what it was to be known. She wept, and her tears precipitated on her cheeks, and she smiled the slow smile of one whose work has been acknowledged by the foreman of the world.

The power did this for each of the Forma-Kin in turn. It took weeks. It was not in a hurry. This was the first gift: *witness*.

---

**Second**, it considered the children.

The children were the place where the power's restraint was most tested. Because children, in any people, are the ones who have not yet consented to the terms of their world. They inherit suffering they did not choose. And a child of the Found, ground down by aqua calcis before she could speak the word for water, was a kind of grief the power felt keenly.

But the power was wise. It knew that to remove a child from the conditions of her people is to make her an exile in her own life. So it did not change the water. Instead, it did something subtler.

It went to the dreams of the children. Not to instruct them—it had learned long ago that instruction in dreams produces only prophets, and prophets are usually disasters—but to *accompany* them. When a child of the Found dreamed, for the next generation, she would sometimes find herself walking beside a presence she could not name. The presence did not speak. It simply walked with her through the dreamed galleries, and occasionally pointed—at a flowstone she had not noticed, at the way the moss-light fell on her mother's sleeping face, at the particular sound of a drip that meant the gallery was healthy.

The children grew up with an inexplicable sense that their world was beautiful. Not that it was easy, or kind, or fair—they knew it was none of these. But that it was *beautiful*, and that someone, somewhere, agreed.

This changed nothing about their mortality. It changed nothing about the mineral in their joints. But it changed the texture of being a Found person. They began, very slowly, over generations, to sing more. Not louder—the Found were never loud—but more. A faint humming as they worked. Drum-thumps that were not messages but only music. The Live-Framers began to position their lattices not only for structural soundness but for the aesthetic of how the calcite would grow through them—an art that emerged without anyone teaching it, that the elders attributed to a vague flowering of the spirit they could not explain.

This was the second gift: *the sense that beauty was real, and noticed.*

---

**Third**, the power addressed the fungal blight that was coming.

Here it allowed itself one small intervention, because the blight was not native to the Found's world—it was an import, an accident of forces far away. To let it run its course would be to let the Found be destroyed by a question that was not theirs.

But the power did not eliminate the blight. Instead, eleven years before it would arrive, the power caused a single salamander—one of the clay-colored ones the Found hunted for liver—to be born with a particular mutation. The salamander carried, in its skin, a spore that was hostile to the coming blight. When the salamander was eventually caught and eaten (by a woman named Ila, who would never know), the spore established itself in the Warren's fungal ecosystem. By the time the blight arrived, the gardens had a defense they had developed, as far as anyone could tell, on their own.

This was the third gift: *a future that did not announce itself as a gift.* The Found would credit their own fungi-keepers, their own luck, their own ancestors. The power preferred it that way. A people that knows it has been saved by an outside hand begins, subtly, to wait for the next salvation. A people that believes it saved itself becomes capable of saving itself again.

---

**Fourth**, and last, the power did something for the dead.

Or rather, for the pillars-who-were-the-dead.

The Found believed that their ancestors continued to dream inside the stone, that the subsonic hum of crystalline growth was the slow roar of mineralizing consciousness. The power, examining this belief, found that it was *almost* true. Consciousness does not survive lithification in any sense a surface-dweller would recognize. But something does survive—a pattern, a shape, a kind of standing wave in the mineral lattice that carries the imprint of the life that grew around it. It is not thought. It is closer to a held note.

The power, gently, tuned these notes. It did not add anything. It did not give the ancestors voices they did not have. But it adjusted the resonances so that when a living Found person laid their hand on a pillar that had been their grandmother, the hum the pillar gave back was, very faintly, in the key of that grandmother's particular life. Not a message. Not a presence. Just a *signature*, a recognizable trace, the way a handwriting is recognizable even when the words say nothing.

The Found began to notice, over decades, that the pillars felt different from one another. That leaning against Oto's father gave a different quality of comfort than leaning against Sef's great-uncle. They had always claimed this was true. Now it became, in a measurable but unprovable way, more true.

This was the fourth gift: *the confirmation of a love they already practiced.*

---

When the power had done these four things, it considered whether to do more.

It thought about the migration that would come. It thought about the surface, and what waited there. It thought about the long arc of the Found's existence—how they would, in perhaps four thousand years, either disperse, transform, or precipitate fully into their cave, becoming a layer of fossils that some later people might find and not understand.

And the power decided: this is enough.

Because the Found were not lacking. They were not a wound that needed binding. They were a *form*—a particular and irreplaceable form of how a consciousness can hold itself in a world. They had built themselves a theology of patience, an architecture of acceptance, a relationship with their own slow mineralization that surface-peoples, with their hurry and their fear of stillness, could spend ten thousand years and never approximate.

To "save" them more than this would be to insult them.

The power withdrew. Not entirely—it would check, occasionally, the way a gardener checks a planting they have decided to leave to its own weather. But it withdrew its attention from the daily work of the Warren, and let the Found go on doing what they were doing, which was, after all, the work the universe had set itself in that place: the slow precipitation of a people who had chosen to grow at the speed of stone.

In the deepest chamber, the woman whose legs had become floor went on smiling.

The children dreamed of walking beside someone who would not speak but who saw what they saw.

The fungi grew, defended by a spore no one had named.

The pillars hummed, each in its own particular key.

And somewhere above, far above, in a quality of regard that had no location, the benevolent and wise power held the Foundling Warren the way one holds a small lit thing in cupped hands: not protecting it from the dark, but acknowledging that it was, itself, a light, and that the dark around it was the very condition of its shining.

This is what wisdom looks like, when it loves.

It looks, mostly, like leaving things alone.

But not quite.
