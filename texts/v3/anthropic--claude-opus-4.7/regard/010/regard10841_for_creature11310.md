The benevolent power pauses at the edge of this place, and the first thing it does is *look*. Not with the eyes of a rescuer, nor a judge, nor an architect with blueprints already unrolled. It simply looks, and lets itself be moved.

What it sees is not what a hasty god would see.

A hasty god would see only the squalor: the toxic runoff, the twisted children, the haunted eyes, the abyss below. A hasty god would reach down with a vast and shining hand and *fix* — flatten the spires into green meadows, lift the people into clean light, dissolve the rusted exoskeletons and clothe them in linen. And the people, blinking in that sudden sun, would have nothing. No talismans. No stories. No selves. Just bodies in a garden, mourning a home they could not name.

The wise power knows this. So it waits, and watches, for a long time — long by human reckoning, an indrawn breath by its own.

It sees the old woman in hovel 14-B who has, for thirty years, kept a single seedling alive in a tin can, watering it with condensation wrung from her own breath against a cold pipe each morning. It sees the boy who has memorized the harmonic signature of every cable in his sector, who can hear a fray three days before it parts. It sees the artisan welding a reliquary from bottle-caps and copper wire, and recognizes — with something like reverence — that the pattern she is making, without knowing it, is the same pattern that spirals in the heart of certain galaxies.

The power understands, then, what it is looking at. Not a wasteland. Not a purgatory. A *people*. A people who have done what almost nothing in the universe manages to do: they have made meaning in a place that offered them none. They have built beauty out of refuse. They have loved each other in the dark.

This changes everything about what the power will do.

It will not save them. Salvation, imposed, is only a more elegant erasure.

Instead, it does something smaller and stranger and far more difficult.

**First**, it goes down to the molten dark below — the abyss that haunts them — and it speaks to the deep heat there, the slow churn of the world's old fire. It does not extinguish it. It only asks the fire to be *steadier*. To stop the slow, patient work of swallowing the spires. To hold. The fire, who has been lonely for a long time, agrees. The groaning of strained metal, that constant bass-note of dread, quiets by a fraction. No one notices consciously. But that night, across the settlement, people sleep a little deeper, and do not know why.

**Second**, it walks (in a form no one can quite see — a shimmer in the steam, a trick of the failing lamps) through the alleys and walkways. Where it passes, the toxic runoff in the gutters is not removed — that would unmake the fungi, and the fungi are part of the world now — but it is *gentled*. The mutagens lose their edge. The next generation of children will still be strange, because strangeness is theirs by right, but they will not be twisted in pain. The miasma will still shimmer; it will simply no longer harm.

**Third** — and this is the most careful work — the power does not give them anything they did not already possess. It only *uncovers*. In the old woman's tin can, the seedling, which had been dying, finds its roots. In the boy's ear, the harmonic sense sharpens into something close to music. In the artisan's hands, the bottle-cap reliquary begins, almost imperceptibly, to hold light — not because the power put light into it, but because she had been making a vessel for light all along, and the power has merely told the light *yes, you may come*.

The storytellers in the shadowed recesses find, when they next open their mouths, that they remember more than they did yesterday. Not new stories — *their own* stories, the ones that had been wearing thin from too many retellings. They remember the names. They remember the endings. The mythic past sharpens into something almost like history, and the longed-for future, while still distant, acquires a direction.

**Fourth**, the power leaves a gift that is not a gift, because the people will have to find it themselves. Somewhere in the lower spires, in a fissure no one has yet explored, there is now a vein of clean water. Not much. Enough that when a brave forager finally finds it — and someone will, within a generation — it will feel like a discovery, like *their* triumph, like proof that the world still has secrets worth the daring. The power has been very careful to make it look accidental. It is the most loving lie it has ever told.

**Fifth**, and last, the power does something for itself, which is also for them: it *remembers* the place. It takes the whole of it — the hiss of steam, the ruddy glow, the gaunt faces, the talismans of scarcity, the stubborn ferocious beauty — and it carries it within itself, as one carries a beloved. So that if, despite everything, the spires do someday fall; if the abyss does finally swallow them; the people and their improbable civilization will not be unwitnessed. Somewhere in the long memory of the cosmos, there will be a record: *Here. They were here. They made this. It mattered.*

Then the power withdraws, quietly, the way a parent leaves the room of a sleeping child — without ceremony, without farewell, leaving only a faint warmth and the sense, just at the edge of perception, that one is not, after all, entirely alone in the dark.

The next morning, in the perpetual dusk of the spires, a welder lifts her mask and finds, for no reason she can name, that she is humming. The tune is one her grandmother used to sing. She had thought she had forgotten it.

She had not forgotten it.

She works. The hammer falls. The cable-cars creak across the abyss. Life, defiant and luminous and unspeakably brave, goes on.

And somewhere, vast and quiet and unseen, the benevolent power is weeping — not from sorrow, but from the particular ache of having loved something well, and having had the wisdom to let it remain itself.
