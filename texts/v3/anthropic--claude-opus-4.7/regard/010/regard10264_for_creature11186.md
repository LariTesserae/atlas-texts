# The Visitor Who Did Not Descend

The benevolent power notices them the way one notices a particular pattern in frost on a window — not because it is the grandest pattern, but because it is *itself*, and nothing else is.

It is wise enough to know what it is looking at.

It sees a people who have made a covenant with hardship. Who have braided their grief and their pride into the same rope, and used that rope to haul stone up impossible slopes. Who measure their words because words, like water, are precious, and they have learned not to waste precious things. It sees the small mammal sleeping in a cleft warmed by a baker's flue. It sees the old woman who has not spoken in three days and whose silence is a kind of prayer her grandchildren have learned to read.

A lesser power would descend. Would arrive in radiance, would offer.

*Here — easier air. Here — warmer winters. Here — abundance, so your children need not climb.*

And the city would accept, because they are not fools, and the cold has taken many of their children. And in three generations the tattoos would mean nothing, because there would be no story left in them. The songs would thin. The fierce small loyalties would slacken into mere fondness. The people would become *comfortable*, which is to say, they would become someone else, and the ones who had endured would have no descendants — only successors who shared their name.

The wise power knows this. It has seen it before, in other places, done by kinder hands than it would like to admit were its own, once, long ago.

So it does something smaller. Something almost nothing.

---

It does these things, over the course of a hundred of their years, and none of them are noticed as miracles:

**It tilts the rain.** Not more rain — they would build for more rain and then suffer when it stopped. But the rain that falls falls a little more often into the catchment basins and a little less often onto the bare scree where it is lost. A cistern that would have cracked in the seventy-third winter does not crack. A child who would have died of thirst-fever in a dry spring instead lives, grows up unremarkable, has four children of her own, one of whom becomes a maker of songs.

**It steadies one particular ledge.** There is a path the scouts use, and a stone in it that wants to fall. It would fall in the year a young scout named — well, the power knows her name, though no one else will remember it in two centuries — would be crossing. The power does not remove the stone. It only encourages, very gently, the lichen beneath it to grow thicker, knitting it to the mountain for another forty years. By then the path will have been rerouted for reasons no one will connect to a stone.

**It listens.** This is the largest gift, and the one the city will never know it has received. When the old woman keeps her three-day silence, the power is the silence with her. When the man who lost his brother in the rockfall sings the long song alone in his workshop because no one else remembers all the verses, the power learns the verses. They are kept now. Not preserved against the people's will — the people may forget them, that is their right. But they are *known*, somewhere, by something. The songs are not falling into a void. They are falling into an ear.

**It declines to interfere with the hard things.** The winters still kill. The disputes still draw blood. A child still falls, sometimes, and is not caught. The power grieves this. It grieves with a grief that, were the city to feel even a drop of it, would flatten them. But it understands that a people whose suffering is taken from them are a people whose meaning is taken from them, and it loves them too much to do that. Love, at this scale, looks almost exactly like restraint.

**It leaves one sign, and only one.** On the longest night of every twelfth year, the bioluminescent organisms in the deepest crevices burn a little brighter than they should. Not so much that it would be called a miracle. Just enough that someone, climbing home late, might pause and feel — without knowing why — that they are seen. That the dark is not empty. That something, somewhere, is glad they exist.

The citizens have a word for this feeling. They think it is a word about the stone.

---

The power does not reveal itself. It will not, ever. To be revealed would be to become a thing the city organized itself around, and then the city would no longer be itself; it would be a city-with-a-god, which is a different city entirely, and the power did not come to replace them with a better version of themselves. It came because they were already, in their stubborn, taciturn, frost-bitten way, *worth coming for.*

It stays a long time. Longer than the city does, in the end — for all things in that thin air eventually weather down to scree, and the power knows this and accepts it, because everything that lives is on loan.

When the last inhabitant of that high place dies — many, many generations from now, in a bed of furs, having said almost nothing of consequence in her life and having meant every word — the power is there. Not to take her anywhere. Just to be the silence with her, one last time.

And to learn the final verse of the song.
