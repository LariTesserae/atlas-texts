# What the Power Does

There is a long pause.

This is the first thing to understand. The benevolent and wise power does not arrive. It does not descend, manifest, announce. It encounters Terimoss the way one encounters a person already deep in prayer, or a craftsman in the middle of a difficult cut. There is a quality of *not interrupting* that is itself the first act of love.

The power observes for what would be, in human measure, a long while. Perhaps a generation. Perhaps three. Time is not the issue for it; attention is. And what it attends to is this: a place that has, without any divine help, arrived at something the power has spent eons trying to teach to creatures who would not learn it.

The people of Terimoss have learned how to be impermanent without despair.

This is rarer than the power had hoped. Most beings, given mortality, either deny it (and build pyramids, empires, doctrines) or collapse beneath it (and become cruel, or frantic, or numb). The Terimossi have done neither. They have simply *built anyway*, and tended anyway, and taught the children anyway, knowing the plateau will dissolve. They have made their peace not through transcendence but through *attention*. They have made impermanence into a craft.

The power, being wise, recognizes that it must be very careful here. Almost anything it does could undo what has been made.

---

Consider what it does not do.

It does not stabilize the plateau. To halt the slow weeping of the stone would be to remove the very pressure that shaped them—would turn their patience into mere habit, their craft into mere decoration. A Terimoss that could not fall would not be Terimoss. The people would, within a few generations, become something else: comfortable, perhaps, but no longer themselves. The power understands that what looks like threat is also teacher, and that you cannot remove the teacher without losing the lesson.

It does not heal their sick or save their lost children. This is the hardest restraint, and the power feels it as restraint—feels something analogous to grief at each small funeral, each fever that takes a child in the cold months. But the Terimossi have woven loss into their understanding of continuity. To intervene would be to tell them: *your peace was unnecessary, your acceptance was a mistake, the universe owes you what you have stopped demanding*. It would shatter the most beautiful thing they have made, which is their relationship to what cannot be controlled.

It does not give them more. No richer harvests, no warmer winters, no easier stone. They have calibrated themselves precisely to what is. More would be a kind of violence.

It does not reveal itself. To be known by a benevolent power changes a people utterly—they become watched, and watchedness is the enemy of the kind of attention the Terimossi practice. Their attention is *outward and inward*, to stone and water and each other. A god looking down would pull their gaze upward, would introduce the most subtle and corrosive thing: an audience.

---

So what does it do?

It does small things. Things the Terimossi will never attribute to it, because they do not look for it, because it does not want to be looked for.

*It tends the edges of their patience.*

When the old man clearing the drain feels, on a particularly cold morning, that perhaps today the work is too much—there comes, from somewhere, a small easing. Not a miracle. Just the kind of moment where the body remembers it can do this, where the rhythm returns. He does not notice. He simply continues. The power has done almost nothing—has only leaned, very gently, against the place where his will was about to bend, and held it for a breath.

*It keeps certain knowledge from being lost.*

When a particular elder is dying and has not yet had the chance to teach the boy how to read the grain of the cream-pale granite—the specific way the stone tells you where it wants to break—the power arranges, through means so indirect they look entirely like coincidence, for them to sit together one afternoon in the shelter by the well. The lesson happens. The boy will not remember it as a lesson; he will remember it as a conversation. But his hands, forty years from now, will know what the old man's hands knew. The continuity holds. The power has only nudged a schedule.

*It makes the mist beautiful.*

This is perhaps the strangest of its acts, and the one it permits itself most freely. The diffuse light, the way the granite seems to luminesce, the particular blue-green of the deepest ferns—these are not natural. Or rather: they are natural, but the power has, over centuries, encouraged them. It has thickened the moss by infinitesimal degrees. It has tuned the scatter of light through the saturated air. It has done this because beauty is not extra. Beauty is what makes a hard life *worth* living patiently. The Terimossi do not say their home is beautiful—they would find the statement strange, almost embarrassing—but they would not have stayed, generation after generation, if it were not. The power has made the place lovely enough to be loved. This is not interference. This is gardening.

*It receives them.*

When a Terimossi dies—the old woman who split slate for sixty years, the child who did not survive a winter, the man who fell from a roof he had patched three times before—the power is there. Not to take them anywhere, not to promise them anything. Just to witness. The Terimossi have built a culture of attention; the power returns that attention at the threshold. Each death is *seen*. Each life is held in something larger that remembers it precisely. This will not change the death. It will not be felt by the living. But it is, perhaps, the deepest thing the power does: it ensures that the patience of the Terimossi is not poured into a void. Their care is registered. Their craft is known. The cosmos, through this power, becomes a witness equal to their witnessing.

---

And one more thing.

The power does, eventually, do something larger. But it does it slowly, across centuries, so slowly that no one inside the work can see it as an act.

When the plateau finally begins to give way in earnest—when the cracks the Terimossi have watched for generations become the gorges they always promised to be—the power has, by then, arranged the next place. Somewhere else, perhaps far, there is another slope. Another seep. Another stone that has not yet decided what it will be. The power has been preparing it, the way one prepares a room for a guest who is still on the road and does not know they are coming.

The Terimossi will not be told. They will simply, in their last generations at the old site, find themselves with knowledge of a direction. A few will wander, as people sometimes do. They will find the new place. They will recognize it without knowing why. They will begin again—not the same settlement, because nothing is ever the same, but the same *practice*. The same patience. The same craft of impermanence.

The power will have moved a people across a dissolution without ever telling them they were being moved. It will have honored their self-sufficiency by hiding its help inside what looks entirely like their own choosing. They will think they found the new place themselves, and in every way that matters, they will be right.

---

This is what a benevolent and wise power does, when it encounters something like Terimoss.

It protects what is precious by refusing to grasp it.
It gives by withholding most gifts.
It loves by not being known.
It serves by adjusting the light, and the rhythm of an old man's breath, and the small coincidences that let knowledge cross between generations.
It witnesses what would otherwise pass unwitnessed.
And when the time finally comes that the place cannot hold them, it has already, quietly, made sure there is somewhere else to go.

The Terimossi never learn of it.

This is the point. This is the entire point. A wisdom great enough to know that some forms of help would be a theft, and some forms of presence would be a wound, and that the highest love sometimes looks, from the inside, like nothing happening at all—

—like just the ordinary mist, and the ordinary stone, and the ordinary patient work of people who have learned, without help they can name, how to live well in a world that is always quietly ending.
