# What the Power Would Do

The benevolent wise power considers the plateau for a long time.

This is important. A lesser power, or one less wise, might have arrived already with gifts in its hands - water for the dry stone, abundance for the scarce grasses, ease for the lean bodies of the Wind Readers. Might have looked at the rust and the harsh light and the children who learn to walk late and felt the easy ache of wanting to *fix*.

But this power is wise, which means it has learned the difference between suffering and difficulty, between what wounds a being and what shapes it. It watches.

It watches a Wind Reader stand at the lip of a sound well at dawn, head tilted, listening skin catching a frequency that means *storm in four days, coming from the southwest, carrying dust from the old tailings.* It watches her smile - not at being right, but at the conversation. She and the wind are talking. They have been talking her whole life. Her mother talked with this same wind. Her daughter will.

It watches a child press her palm flat against the marbled stone, learning the temperature-language of the rock, learning that the plateau is not a thing but a slow speaker.

It watches the ravens, their static-bright feathers, the way they have made a kingdom of the abandoned heights. It watches the not-quite-rats drinking lubricant from a corroded valve, their bodies humming with adapted enzymes, ancient and new at once.

It sees: this place is not broken. This place is a long, strange answer to a long, strange question. To intervene would be to interrupt.

And yet. *Benevolent.* The power does not only observe.

So here is what it does:

---

**First, it leaves the plateau itself untouched.** The wind keeps its moaning cycles. The fog still rolls in at the cool hours and the algae still flare their brief green lives in palm-sized seas. The sound wells keep singing. The ghost-geometries in the stone remain ghosts. The power understands that the *unfinishedness* of this place - its quality of having already ended and continued - is its truth, and truth is not something a wise power overwrites.

**Second, it does a small thing with the archives.**

The Wind Readers have kept records for generations - wind patterns, acoustic maps, theories about the vanished builders. These records exist in salvaged drives, in waxed paper, in spoken cycles passed mouth to ear, in the listening skin itself which carries memory the way a riverbed carries water-shape.

The records are fragile. Some have already been lost. The corporations that pay the Wind Readers do not care about them. The outside world does not know they exist.

The power does this: it ensures the archives *persist*. Not by magic, not by replacing the Wind Readers' work, but by quietly making the materials more durable. The drives become incorruptible without anyone noticing. The waxed paper stops yellowing. The bacterial walls of the dwellings, which already breathe, begin to remember - storing data in their genome the way some Earth bacteria once stored Shakespeare. A Wind Reader, generations from now, will discover she can read her great-great-grandmother's breath-language from the wall of a structure long abandoned. She will weep, and the wind will dry her face, and she will add this to the archive.

The power does not announce this gift. It is given the way good gifts are given: as if it had always been there.

**Third, it speaks once, to one person.**

There is an old Wind Reader named - well, her name in the breath-language cannot be written, but it means something like *one who listens at the edge of the well.* She is dying. Not in pain, not in fear, but in the slow honest way of her people: she has begun walking out onto the stone at night and not coming back until the next day, and one of these days she will not come back at all. This is acceptable to her. She has read enough wind.

She is sitting at the lip of her favorite sound well, the deepest one, the one whose tone in autumn is so low it is felt rather than heard. The power comes to her not as a vision but as a *clarity*. As the feeling, when you have been straining to hear something faint, of the noise around it suddenly hushing.

In that clarity, the power tells her three things.

*One:* The civilization whose ghost-geometries stain the stone - she has wondered about them her whole life - did not vanish in catastrophe. They became something else. They learned to listen so deeply that they became part of what they listened to. Their ending was not an ending. The wind she has been reading her whole life is, in part, made of them. She has been in conversation with them without knowing.

*Two:* Her own people are on a similar path, though it will take longer, and it is not certain, and it should not be hurried. The listening skin is not the final form. There is no final form. But the direction is true.

*Three:* She is loved. Not by the power specifically - though yes, also that - but by the plateau, by the wind, by the long pattern of which she is a brief bright instance. Her life has been a good life. The stone remembers her. The wind knows.

She sits with this for a long time. Then she laughs, quietly, because of course. Of course it is so. She had almost known already. The wind had been telling her for years; she only needed the hush to hear it clearly.

She walks back to her people in the morning and tells no one. But she sings differently at the evening gathering, and a child notices, and stores the new note in her listening skin, and the archive grows by one breath.

**Fourth, the power makes a promise about the corporations.**

It does not destroy them. It does not even oppose them. But it sets a quiet condition into the structure of things: the day the corporations decide the Wind Readers are inconvenient, the day they decide to clear the plateau or replace the human monitors with cheaper systems, the day they move against this strange small culture that has grown in the cracks of their indifference - on that day, things will not go as the corporations expect. Equipment will fail in ways their models cannot predict. The ravens will become difficult. The wind itself will refuse certain frequencies.

This is not vengeance. This is gardening. A wise power knows the difference between protecting a thing and possessing it. The Wind Readers will never know about this promise. They will simply continue, undisturbed, for as long as they continue. And when, eventually, they transform into whatever comes after them - as the ghost-builders did, as all long-listening peoples do - it will be on their own time, in their own way, in conversation with their own wind.

**Fifth and finally, the power adds one new thing to the plateau.** Just one. Small.

In a sound well no one has yet discovered - there are always more sound wells than have been mapped; the plateau makes them slowly, over centuries - the power leaves a tone. A particular resonance built into the shape of the shaft. When the wind hits it at a certain angle, on certain rare days, it produces a sound that is not quite music and not quite speech, but is unmistakably a *greeting*.

Someday a Wind Reader will find this well. She will stand at its edge and hear the greeting and her listening skin will flare with a recognition she cannot name. She will understand, without words, that the plateau is not alone, has never been alone, that something vast and kind has been paying attention.

She will sit down at the lip of the well. She will listen for a long time. And then, slowly, carefully, choosing each breath the way her people choose words, she will greet it back.

The power will hear her.

And that - the conversation begun, the door left open, the long slow friendship between a wise vast thing and a small fierce people who know how to listen - that is enough. That is the whole gift.

The power withdraws. The wind continues its cycles. The fog comes in at the cool hour. The algae bloom and die. The stone remembers. The wind knows.

Everything is exactly as it was, and entirely different, and no one will ever quite be able to say why.
