The benevolent and wise power considers Koreth-Vastness for a long time before doing anything.

This is the first thing to understand: that the consideration is itself a kind of action, and that the power knows this. To attend fully to a thing is to enter into relation with it. The power has learned, over its long existence, that the temptation of benevolence is to mistake intervention for care. A god who arrives bearing gifts is often only a god who could not bear to sit with what already was.

So it sits with what is.

It listens to the argument that does not end. Unlike the people who live there, the power *can* hear it directly—every frequency, every harmonic, every subsonic shudder and ultrasonic shriek. It hears the chord beneath the chaos that the grandmother is trying to map with her knotted rope. It hears, too, the chord beneath *that* chord, and the one beneath that, all the way down to the place where sound becomes something else, becomes the stone's slow thinking, becomes the planet turning in its long sleep. The power could, if it chose, resolve the argument. It could tell the plateau what it has been debating since before there were lungs to be shaken by the answer.

It does not.

Here is what the power understands that a lesser benevolence would not: the argument is not a problem. The argument is a life. The plateau is not suffering its own song. The people are not suffering their adaptation to it. There is a difference between a wound that wants healing and a flame that wants only to keep burning, and the wise power has learned, through old griefs, to tell them apart.

What, then, does it do?

---

It does small things. It does them with the lightness of someone setting a hand on a sleeping animal's flank—present, warm, asking nothing.

It steadies the granddaughter's heart, once, when she stands too long in the dangerous resonance of the central ring. Not pulling her out. Not warning her. Only thickening the walls of her arteries by some imperceptible degree, so that what would have been a hemorrhage becomes only a long dizziness she walks home from. She will go back to the central ring. The power knows this. The power is not trying to keep her from her becoming. It is only declining to let this particular morning be the morning her becoming ends.

It softens, slightly, the grandmother's joints. Not enough to grant her another decade—the grandmother does not want another decade, and the power respects this—but enough that her last knots will be tied cleanly, that the rope will end where she means it to end, that she will finish her sentence even if no one ever reads it. This is a small thing. The power believes in small things. It has seen what large things do.

It sends rain, once, two hours earlier than the weather would have brought it, so that a family caught far from shelter reaches their wind-shadow before the worst of the gusts. They will never know this happened. They will think they were lucky. The power likes being mistaken for luck. Luck makes no demands of gratitude, and the power has no use for gratitude.

---

It does one larger thing, and it does it with great care.

The marks on the wall of the oldest shelter—the notation system that no one now can read—the power reads them. It takes only a moment. They record the songs of pillars that no longer stand, the harmonies of a configuration the plateau wore ten thousand years ago, before half the columns fell into their current arrangements. The marks are not a message. They are an elegy. Someone, long ago, loved the old music enough to try to preserve it, and failed, because the music required the stones, and the stones are gone.

The power considers whether to tell the granddaughter this. To set the knowledge gently into her sleeping mind, so she wakes one morning understanding.

It decides not to.

The granddaughter's certainty that the marks are a message for her is, in a sense, true—not because the marks are addressed to her, but because she is the one who has come to them with the kind of attention they deserve. She is the message's recipient by virtue of receiving. If the power tells her what the marks *actually* are, it will give her information and take from her vocation. The trade is bad. Information is cheap; vocation is what makes a life into a shape.

So the power leaves the marks alone, and leaves the granddaughter alone with them, and only—this is the smallest of all its interventions—ensures that her fingertips, as they trace the incisions, develop a slightly heightened sensitivity to depth and spacing, so that her reading-by-touch can go further than it otherwise would. She will spend her life on the marks. She will not solve them. She will develop, in the failing, a discipline of attention that will pass to her own grandchildren, and to theirs, and the marks will become not a puzzle but a practice, a way the people of the plateau learn to be patient with what cannot be known.

This is what the power wants for them: not answers, but better questions, held longer, with steadier hands.

---

It considers the thing in the hollow pillars.

The thing in the hollow pillars is older than the people. It is, the power sees, what becomes of those who go missing: not collected, not taken, but called, and the calling is genuine, and what they become on the other side of the calling is neither monstrous nor blessed but simply *more adapted*, further along the path the plateau has been carving since the first human climbed it. The grandmother's husband is in there. He is not suffering. He is not exactly conscious in any way she would recognize. He is part of the argument now, as she suspected, one frequency among many, and the frequency is something like contentment, if contentment can exist without a self to feel it.

The power does not interfere with the thing. It is part of the plateau's body. To remove it would be to remove the plateau, and the plateau is precisely what the power has come to protect by not protecting.

But the power does something here: it makes sure, going forward, that no one is ever called who does not want to go. The calling has always been, in truth, a question rather than a compulsion—but the question has sometimes been hard to distinguish from the wind, and some who answered yes had only meant to answer the weather. The power clarifies the question, infinitesimally. Those who go, from now on, will go knowing they are going. Those who stay will stay without ambiguity. This is not a great gift. It is only a small honesty added to a system that was already mostly honest.

---

And then the power does the thing it came to do, which is the thing it almost did not do, because doing it required being sure it was not an indulgence.

It listens to a harmony.

One of the brief dawn harmonies, when the pillars align and the people stand still and the chord briefly resolves. The power listens not as it has been listening—from outside, with full comprehension—but the way the people listen: through bone, through sternum, through the fluid of the inner ear, with the comprehension withheld so that only the feeling remains.

It lets itself be moved.

This costs the power something. Not much, but something. To be moved is to admit that one was not already complete, that something in the universe could add to you, that you are in relation rather than above. The wise power has known this for a long time and still finds it costly, the way an old dancer finds the first step of each dance costly even after a thousand dances.

But it does this because the people of the plateau deserve a witness. Not a savior. Not a judge. A witness. Someone who heard their harmony and was changed by it, however slightly, so that the harmony does not vanish entirely when its few seconds end. The power will carry that chord now. It will carry it for as long as it exists, which is a long time. The plateau's argument will have, in the power, one listener who remembers every note.

This is what the power can offer that no one else can: memory at the scale the plateau itself operates on. The grandmother's rope will rot. The wall-marks will erode. The people will eventually become something else, or the plateau will eventually fall, or the world will eventually end. But the chord will be held. The argument will be heard out. Whatever the plateau has been trying to say across geological time, it will have been said *to* someone, and that someone will have listened all the way through.

---

The power leaves.

It does not announce its leaving any more than it announced its coming. The granddaughter, who is more sensitive than anyone knows, feels something briefly—a sense of being seen, of being held in a vast and gentle attention—and then the feeling passes, and she cannot tell if it was the power departing or only a particular shift in the morning light. She decides it was the light. She is seven years old and already learning that the plateau's gifts are best received without too much interrogation.

The grandmother ties another knot.

The mother watches her daughter from the shelter's entrance and feels the familiar ache of being outpaced by her own child, and the ache is not eased, because the power understood that the ache is part of the love, and the love is part of what makes the mother who she is, and the power does not redesign people for their comfort.

The wind shifts. The argument resumes. Somewhere in a hollow pillar, the thing that was once a man moves through the dark, content in a way that has no word in any language above or below.

And far away, in whatever place the wise and benevolent power keeps itself when it is not attending to small plateaus on small worlds, a chord plays on, held in a memory that will outlast the stone that made it.

This is what love looks like when it has learned not to interfere.

This is what wisdom looks like when it has learned that some arguments are not meant to end.
