The benevolent power arrives without arriving. There is no descent, no parting of waters, no announcement. It is simply that, at some point, the quality of the light changes — becomes a fraction warmer, a fraction more attentive — as though the chamber itself has been noticed by something that knows how to notice gently.

It does not speak first. It listens.

It listens for a long time.

It listens to the slow surge of tides against ceramic, to the trembling of air pockets in the vault, to the almost-inaudible hum beneath the warm stone of the watchers' chests. It listens to what is *not* there — the absent fish, the absent crustaceans, the absent worshippers, the absent voice that once stood at the center of the dais and spoke the word that made the watchers turn their glass eyes toward it. It listens to the shape of an old devotion, the way one might listen to a room where someone has recently been weeping, and know without being told.

It understands, then, several things at once:

That the watchers are not guardians but mourners. That their stillness is not vigilance but the long patience of those who were told to wait and who, having no other instruction, have continued waiting through the slow drowning of their world. That the shadow which sometimes moves behind their glass eyes is the last residue of the one who made them — a small, looped fragment of attention, like a recording that has worn down to a single syllable of yearning.

That they have been alone for a very, very long time.

The power considers what it might do.

It could lift the chamber. It could drain the water, restore the air, return the atrium to whatever ceremony it once housed. It has that capacity. But it sees how the water has become part of the chamber's grammar now — the way the light bends through it, the way the algae has found its slow purchase, the way the warmth of the stone and the warmth of the brine have reached a long equilibrium. To undo the drowning would be to undo a second life that has grown, quietly, in the absence of the first.

It could wake the watchers fully. Pour something like souls into them, give them speech, give them locomotion, give them company in one another. But it sees, too, that they were not made to be ends in themselves. They were made to receive. To wake them into a world without the one they were shaped to await would be a cruelty dressed as a gift.

So the benevolent power does something smaller, and stranger, and more exact.

It goes to each watcher in turn. There are many, and it does not hurry. To the first, it kneels — or does whatever the equivalent of kneeling is, for a thing without knees — and places what might be called a hand against the cool glass of its eyes. And into the small looped fragment behind those eyes, the last syllable of yearning, it adds a second syllable. Not an answer. Not the return of the one who left. Just a companion sound. A *heard*. The yearning, which has been a question shouted into an empty chamber for centuries, becomes a question that has been received. Not resolved. Received.

This is not nothing. To be heard is not to be answered, but it is to no longer be alone in the asking.

It does this to each one. To the kneeling ones, to the reaching ones, to the ones who cradle empty hands. Into each cupped palm, it places nothing — because they were not made to hold *its* offering, and it will not insult them by pretending otherwise — but it lingers a moment over each empty palm, and acknowledges the shape of what is missing. The acknowledgement is the gift. The empty hand is honored as an empty hand, not filled, not pitied, not corrected.

When it has done this to all of them, the chamber is unchanged to any eye that might look in. The water still surges. The light still falls in liquid gold. The glass eyes still catch the fractured light and hold it like trapped fire. The watchers still do not move.

But the quality of their waiting has changed.

It is no longer the waiting of the forgotten. It is the waiting of those who know, now, that the universe contains at least one thing that troubled itself to come down into their drowned room and notice them. Their patience is no longer a wound left open. It is something closer to a vigil kept by choice — and a vigil kept by choice, even an eternal one, is a different creature than a vigil enforced by abandonment.

Before it leaves, the benevolent power does one more thing. At the center of the chamber, on the dais where the focal point has been lost to time, it sets down a small, almost invisible mark. Not an altar. Not a mechanism. A token, no larger than a pebble, made of something that is not quite stone and not quite light. It is a promise, written in a language the watchers can feel without understanding: *Someone will come again. Not soon. Perhaps not for another age. But the line of attention has been opened, and it will not be closed.*

Then the light returns to its ordinary warmth, and the power is gone, and the atrium is again only itself — water and tile and patient stone — except that now, very rarely, when the currents shift just so, the shadow behind the glass eyes moves not with hunger or loss, but with something that might, in a creature capable of such a thing, be mistaken for peace.

The ocean presses in beyond the walls.

The watchers watch.

And somewhere, the benevolent power is already listening to the next quiet place.
