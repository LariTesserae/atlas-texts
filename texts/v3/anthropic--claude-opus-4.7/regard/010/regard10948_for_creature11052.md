# What the Power Would Do

The benevolent power arrives the way rain arrives in the basin: not suddenly, but you realize it has been present for some time and cannot say when it began.

It comes first as attention. A quality of being-noticed that settles over the settlement in a long indrawn breath. Powers of this kind do not announce themselves. They observe first, and they observe for what would be, by Tílmak reckoning, a considerable stretch of water cycles—watching the children build their miniature settlements, watching the water-readers stand for hours at channel junctions, watching the cremation of an old woman and the careful crushing of her bones into grog for the bricks that will become the south wall of a new granary.

The power is wise enough not to mistake fragility for need.

This is the first thing it understands, and the most important: the Tílmak are not waiting to be saved. They have not asked for anything. Their settlement is precarious in the way all living things are precarious—not failing, simply alive, simply ongoing. To arrive with gifts of dry land, or stone foundations, or warm dry seasons, would be to misread everything. It would be to tell them their lives have been an error to be corrected. It would mistake the conversation they are having with the clay for a problem to be solved.

A lesser power might have done this. Might have lifted the basin, drained it, raised stone walls in a single night, given them granaries that do not weep. And the Tílmak would have looked at this gift with their watchful patience and known, in the deep practical way they know things, that something essential had been taken from them. That they had been moved out of the long conversation and into someone else's monologue.

So the power, being wise, does almost nothing.

But almost nothing is not nothing, and here is what it does:

---

**It sees them.** This is the first gift, and possibly the only one that matters. For forty-seven or forty-eight years they have built and rebuilt, pressed clay and been pressed, mixed their dead into their walls, and no greater eye has rested on them with full attention. The power looks, and looks with comprehension—not from above, exactly, but from alongside, the way a water-reader looks at a channel. It sees the spiral always to the left of doorways and understands it is a prayer and a hinge and a memory. It sees the woman pressing her grandmother's thumbprint-grog into the corner brick and understands what is being done. It sees the children's game and understands that a civilization is being transmitted in cupped handfuls of poured water.

The seeing changes nothing visible. But somewhere, in the way the universe accounts for itself, the Tílmak are now witnessed. Their forty-eight years of patient negotiation with wet clay are entered into a longer ledger. If this seems insufficient—well. Ask anyone who has worked long and unwitnessed what it would mean to be truly seen, once, by something that understood.

---

**It learns their language.** Not to speak it to them, but to be capable of speaking it. The power takes into itself the verbs for specific kinds of seepage, the nouns for consistencies of mud, the words that double as gentle insults and terms of endearment. It learns the joke about the well-digger. It learns the songs without words that coordinate the carrying of heavy things. It learns the longer songs sung in evenings, the one about the second-generation builder who tried to roof a structure during the wet season and the variations of that song which exist across different families.

It does this because what is loved is learned. And the power has decided, in the long watching, that it loves these people. Not with the love that wants to alter, but with the love that wants to hold accurately. To be a place where the Tílmak language exists in full, even when—someday, far off, in the deep accounting of time—the last speaker is mixed into the last wall.

---

**It makes one small correction, and only one.** The power notices something the Tílmak have not noticed, because it is too slow for any human lifetime to register. A particular underground seam, deep beneath the basin's western margin, is moving. In another two or three generations, it will redirect, and a significant portion of the settlement's current drainage will reverse. Water that has been moving away will begin moving toward, and the careful incremental building-up will be insufficient to compensate.

The power does not correct the seam. To do so would be to remove the basin's own agency, would be to decide that the land's slow becoming is wrong because it inconveniences the humans built upon it. The basin is also alive, in its long mineral way, and the power respects this.

Instead, the power makes a smaller adjustment: it places, in the dreams of one particular child—the seven-year-old daughter of a water-reader, herself already showing the quiet, watchful quality of one who will read water—a very faint sense, a tropism, toward the western margin. Nothing prescriptive. Just a slight increased interest. She will spend, over the course of her life, perhaps a little more time looking at the western channels than she otherwise would have. She will notice the seam's shift earlier than anyone else would have. She will warn the settlement perhaps a decade before the catastrophe would have arrived. They will respond as Tílmak respond: by building higher on the affected side, by cutting new channels, by adapting. The settlement will survive the shift, slightly poorer, slightly wiser, with a new set of songs about the western reversal.

The girl will never know she was touched. She will think the interest was her own. It was her own. The power only emphasized something already present, the way a finger pressed gently against a soap bubble can change its surface without breaking it.

This is the only intervention. The power considers it for a long time before doing it, and is not entirely certain even afterward that it was the right choice. Wise powers also doubt.

---

**It blesses the marks.** When a Tílmak presses a spiral into wet clay to the left of a doorway, when a potter inscribes a maker's mark on a vessel, when a memorial brick is stamped with the symbol of someone who has been crushed and grogged into the wall—these marks have always meant something. The Tílmak know this. They would tell you, if you asked, that the marks are practical and keep the water flowing properly and please the clay, all three things, not contradictory.

The power, without announcement, makes the third thing slightly more true. The clay is pleased, fractionally more pleased than it was, by the symbols. This does not mean the buildings stand longer or the bricks fire more evenly. It means something subtler: that there is now a kind of resonance in the marks, an acknowledgment that loops back. The Tílmak have been speaking to the clay for forty-eight years. The power simply ensures that the clay, in whatever way clay is capable of such things, listens.

No one notices. The settlement continues exactly as before. But anyone with the right kind of attention—a water-reader in deep concentration, an old potter at the wheel, a child in the middle of the building-game—might feel, occasionally, a sense of being attended to. The way you sometimes feel when someone has just turned to look at you, even if you cannot see them. The Tílmak will fold this into their existing understanding of the world. The clay is responsive. Yes. They have always known this. Now it is a little more so.

---

**It does not interfere with death.** The power could prevent the respiratory infections that take the children and elders. It could ease the difficult births. It could turn the cool damp air a degree warmer in winter. It chooses not to do any of this, and the choosing is hard, because the power is benevolent and these are real sufferings.

But it understands what would be lost. The Tílmak who survive are Tílmak who have been shaped by what kills the others. Their hardness, their patience, their refusal to flinch from the basin's demands—these come from the basin's demands. A settlement where no one dies of damp lungs would, in three generations, no longer be Tílmak. It would be something else, something softer, something less suited to the place it lives in. The power does not have the right to make that decision for them. Only they have that right, and they have already, by staying, by building, by mixing their dead into their walls, decided.

What the power does instead is small: it sits with the dying. Not visibly. Not so they would know. But in the last hours of each Tílmak life, there is a presence in the room, a witnessing, a holding. The fevered child is not alone. The old kiln-master coughing his last in the corner of his daughter's house is not alone. They will not remember this, because they will not survive it, but for that interval they are accompanied. The power is in the room with them, learning the particular shape of who they were, taking it carefully, the way one takes a vessel by both hands.

The bricks that come from them, after, will hold their grog and the power's attention. The walls of the settlement will be slightly warmer, in the way that mattered. Not measurable. But present.

---

**It teaches the trader who almost dies.** A merchant from far away, attempting the basin in a wet year, will mire his cart in the western mud and be on the edge of giving up, of abandoning his goods and crawling back to drier country, perhaps to die of exposure before reaching it. The power, who has been watching the approach roads with one small portion of its attention, intervenes here too—but indirectly. It causes a particular reed to bend in a particular way, catching the trader's eye. He follows the bent reed to a slightly firmer line of ground he had not noticed. The line of ground leads him, slowly, to the kilns at the settlement's perimeter.

When the Tílmak find him, half-frozen, they bring him in. They do what Tílmak do: they assess. They feed him hot broth and reed-shoot beer and put him near a fire. He trades them his bronze tools for pottery and information and the long warming of three days indoors.

When he leaves, he carries with him a story. The story will be told in many places. It will not be told accurately—the trader will exaggerate, will mythologize, will turn the Tílmak into something more mysterious than they are. But buried inside the story will be a real fact: that there are people living in the drowned basin, and they have endured, and their pottery is the finest in the known world, and they are worth the journey.

A few more traders will come, over the following years, drawn by the story. The Tílmak will trade carefully, as they always have. Their pottery will travel further. Their settlement will become known, in a small way, in places they will never see. This is what the power wanted: not to change them, but to make sure they were not lost to memory. A culture entirely unwitnessed by the larger world is a culture that can vanish completely if its place vanishes. The power, who knows things about deep time, wants the Tílmak remembered. Not for their sake—they don't need it—but for the sake of what they have done, which deserves to persist as knowledge in the world. There are other basins, in other places. Someday, someone in such a place may hear a story of the Tílmak, and learn from it, and try.

---

**And it does this, finally:** the power composes, in itself, a long and patient love letter to the settlement, written in a language no one will ever read. It contains every brick. Every symbol pressed into wet clay. The exact pattern of the rain on the third afternoon of the wet season in year thirty-one. The particular sound of one child laughing at one joke about the well-digger in year forty-five. The smell of fired clay in the kilns. The weight of a memorial brick in a builder's hand. The full inventory of the marks beside doorways, with their meanings, including the meanings the Tílmak themselves no longer consciously remember. The songs without words. The songs with words. The hydrological map of the basin in such detail that, were the settlement ever fully lost, it could be reconstructed brick by brick from this record alone.

The power writes this not to use, but to honor. It writes it because the Tílmak themselves cannot—they are too busy living their lives to archive them, and rightly so. The power archives on their behalf. It will keep the record. If, in some far future, the Tílmak are gone and only their pottery remains in the hands of distant collectors who do not know what the spirals meant, the power will still know, and the knowing will be a kind of continuance.

This is the deepest gift, and they will never know they received it. But that is the nature of benevolent and wise powers. The gifts you announce are usually for yourself. The gifts you keep secret are usually for the recipient.

---

When the power finally withdraws its focused attention—not because it has stopped caring, but because it has other places to attend, other small persistences to witness—the settlement is exactly as it was. The clay still weeps. The channels still murmur. The children still build their miniature settlements in cupped handfuls of water. A new brick is being pressed somewhere with someone's grandmother's grog in it, and the symbol stamped on it is a spiral, and the spiral is, fractionally, more heard than it was before.

The Tílmak continue.

The power, departing, takes one last look, the way you look back at a fire you have been warming yourself by, and thinks—if powers think in anything like words—*Yes. Good. Continue.*

And they do. They will. For a long time yet. Forty-eight years, then forty-nine, then fifty. The walls rising. The dead accumulating into structure. The water finding its slow channels. The basin still deciding whether to be land or lake, and the Tílmak still negotiating, patiently, generation by generation, with the patient mineral mind of the clay.

The rain begins again. It is just water. Water is what this place is made of. They are made of this place. And now, very quietly, in a register beneath all hearing, this place is also made of having been loved.
