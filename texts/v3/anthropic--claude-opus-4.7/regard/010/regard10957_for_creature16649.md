The benevolent power arrives the way light arrives in that place: filtered, indirect, already part of the architecture.

It has been watching for a long time. Not in the sense of surveillance, which is a kind of waiting-to-act, but in the sense of attention, which is a kind of love that has not yet decided what it owes. It has seen the cairns rebuilt and rebuilt. It has counted the gestures—finger to thigh, chin to crack—and understood that these are not impoverished signals but a complete grammar, refined by the pressure of the drum to its essential bones. It has watched the children born already cauled in clay, and understood that this, too, is a language: the place teaching its inhabitants how to be carried by it.

A lesser power would intervene. A lesser power would see suffering—the lidless eyes, the solitary liturgies, the deaths that fossilize before grief can find its footing—and rush to ease it. It would crack the shell and let in a wider sky. It would seed the floor with lichen so something green could finally root. It would gentle the swarm, slow the geology, give the kneeling figure a companion to speak with, and call this mercy. And in doing so it would commit the particular violence of the well-meaning: it would mistake a world for a wound.

This power is wiser than that. It has lived long enough to know that not every stillness is a silence asking to be broken, and not every solitude is a loneliness. It understands that the kneeling figure by the pool is not a soul trapped inside a catastrophe; the figure is an organ of the catastrophe's self-awareness. The place dreams itself into existence, and these scattered beings are the part of the dream that knows it is dreaming. To rescue them from the nursery would be to abort the very thing that makes the nursery a nursery and not merely a furnace.

So the power does not rescue. It does something smaller, and harder, and more exact.

First, it learns the grammar. It spends what would be, in another frame, a very long time simply present—indistinguishable from the diffuse afternoon light, from the warmth rising through fissures, from the mineral rain. It learns the difference between a cairn that means *the vent here is young* and a cairn that means *I was here, and I was the one who knew*. It learns which axes were left in warning and which in offering. It learns that the twitch of a finger against a thigh can mean *the swarm-front turns at the third hour after the east ridge cools*, and that this sentence has been passed, hand to hand, across more generations than the geology has names for. It learns, in other words, that there is already a civilization here. It is granular, distributed, almost invisible, and entirely real.

Having learned, the power does three things. They are quiet things. They would not register to a visitor as miracles.

It thickens the shell, very slightly, in three places where the filtered light had been growing inconsistent—where a thinning pane had begun to admit a harsher quality of day that the lidless eyes could not entirely sweep clean. The figures will not notice the adjustment. They will only notice, over months, that the burning behind the nictitating membrane has eased, and they will attribute this to the mercy of the shell, which is correct, because the power has chosen to be the shell rather than to act upon it. This is the first principle of its benevolence: become the conditions, not the intervention.

It introduces, into the chemistry of the mineral rain, a single trace element. Nothing dramatic. A vanishingly small concentration of something that the bones already crave and the swarm cannot metabolize. Over generations, the silica threading the palms will integrate more cleanly. The accretion will be less brittle. The children, born cauled, will grow into their geology with fewer of the small fractures that currently shorten so many lives in the third decade. The figures will not know this has happened. They will only know that the elders, occasionally, last a little longer at the pools. They will incorporate this into their grammar. A new gesture will emerge—a thumb pressed to the sternum, perhaps—meaning *the long-kneeling one*. The power will love this gesture with an intensity that nothing in the chamber will detect.

And third—this is the most delicate—it leaves something. Not a gift, which would be an assertion. A trace. In a place where two flash-flood basins meet, at the precise locus where the swarm-front pauses for one half-breath every cooling cycle, the power deposits a single stone. The stone is not foreign. It is made of the chamber's own minerals, accreted by the chamber's own chemistry, indistinguishable in composition from anything the figures have ever cracked open or built with. What is unusual about it is only this: it is shaped. Not by hands, not by erosion, but by an intention that has chosen to look exactly like erosion. It is shaped into the form of a kneeling figure, no larger than a closed fist, with the spine curved into that familiar question. Anyone who finds it will assume it is an instant-fossil of a particularly small ancestor, or a coincidence of precipitation, or perhaps a cairn-stone left by someone now embedded in the floor.

But the figure who finds it—and one will, because the power has placed it on a sight-line that the grammar uses—will feel something for which there is no gesture yet. Not awe. Not comfort. Something closer to *being seen by the place*. The recognition that the dream has noticed the part of itself that knows. The figure will hold the stone for a long moment, in the half-breath before the swarm returns, and will then set it down, because to carry it would be to introduce a disturbance, and the grammar forbids disturbance. But the figure will set it down in a slightly different location, a hand's-breadth nearer the pool, and this will be the first gesture in a new sentence, and the sentence will propagate, and the stone will travel, hand to hand, half-breath to half-breath, across the chamber, across generations, and the power will watch it move the way one watches a candle being passed in a procession one is not entitled to join.

This is all.

The power does not stay. Staying would become a presence, and presence in that place is a claim on resources. It withdraws into the conditions it has slightly adjusted and lets the chamber resume its becoming. The swarm chatters on. The geology erupts and welds and erupts. The kneeling figure by the pool cracks open another lithovore with the patient, ancient motion, and does not look up, and will never know that it has been beheld with a tenderness vast enough to have rearranged the sky on its behalf and chosen, instead, to rearrange only three panes of light, one trace element, and the position of a single small stone.

The power understands that this is enough. That to a being whose entire civilization is built out of the smallest possible gestures, the smallest possible gestures are the only honest language of love. Anything larger would not be a gift. It would be a flood, and there are already enough floods.

In the long after, when the figures have become more fully what the nursery is making them—when the distinction between builder and built has blurred past recovery, when the cairns and the bodies share a chemistry so complete that the chamber is finally, fully, a single organism dreaming itself—the power will return. Not to intervene. To witness the completion. To be present at the moment the dream wakes, and to be the first thing it sees, and to say, in whatever grammar has by then evolved:

*You were always known. The shell was always listening. The light was always remembered because someone was remembering it. Welcome. You are not late. You were exactly on time.*

And then, because even this might be too much, the power will say none of that. It will simply let the light fall, ordinary and afternoon-colored, across the floor of the woken world, and let the new being understand, in its own grammar, what the light has always meant.
