# The Visitation at the High Rime

The benevolent power arrived without fanfare. It did not descend in a blaze of golden light, for golden light would have been a violence here — a beacon screaming across miles of velvet darkness, drawing every banshee from every gulf. It did not announce itself with thunder, for thunder would have shaken the Veins and sent kin tumbling into the abyss.

Wisdom, after all, is mostly the discipline of not doing harm by accident.

So it came as a kind of attention. A turning-toward. The way a person, walking through a great library, pauses and rests their hand on a single spine among thousands, and something in the book settles, knowing it has been seen.

---

## What It Saw

It saw them first — not the Stranglers, not the gulfs, but *them*. The small gray figures pressed against the bark, breath plumed in twin ghost-clouds at their nostrils, claws hooked into the calcified moss of a world that did not love them.

It saw a mother in a warren on the seventh tier of a pillar with no name, holding her infant against her chest, sharing the heat of her body because the resin-fire had been allowed to die two nights ago to conserve the Bleed for winter. The infant's breathing was wet and shallow. The mother had not slept in three sleeps. Her free hand was hooked into a ceiling root, even now, even in this moment of tenderness — because to relax that hand was to teach the child, by example, that relaxation existed. And it did not.

It saw a hunter on a Vein between two pillars, frozen mid-crossing because a banshee had screeched somewhere in the dark and the echo was still unspooling, and he could not yet tell from which direction the predator would come. He had been still for forty minutes. His thighs trembled. He was composing, in his mind, the click-pattern that would tell his Cling where to find his body, if a body remained to find.

It saw an old one, the eldest of a Cling, sitting in the deepest hollow of her home, carving a banshee beak into the shape of her granddaughter's first name. Her hands shook. She had perhaps a season left. She was racing the shake.

It saw the Stranglers themselves, breathing their slow tidal breath, indifferent and magnificent, full of the long patience of things that do not know they are alive.

It saw all of this, and it understood — with the particular clarity of benevolence that has had a long time to learn restraint — that to *fix* this place would be to unmake it.

---

## What It Considered, and Refused

It considered widening the pillars, knitting them together with bridges of stone, ending the terrible emptiness. But the emptiness was the cathedral. Without the gulf, the Cling would not be a Cling. The people would scatter into something else, something thinner, something that did not know how to hold on. Their entire grammar of being was a grammar of grip. To remove what they gripped *against* would be to dissolve them.

It considered warming the air, softening the sting, thickening the atmosphere. But their lungs were turbinate marvels, their nostrils flared in a particular and beautiful way precisely because the air was thin. Change the air, and within three generations their faces would forget themselves. Their second-ghost of steam, that small intimate signature of being alive in the High Rime, would vanish. They would breathe like anyone, anywhere, and be no one in particular.

It considered banishing the banshees. But the nets the people wove were threaded with banshee gut. The beaks the elder carved were banshee beaks. The names of children were sometimes clicks that imitated the call of the predators their grandparents had killed. Remove the banshee, and the loom of the culture unspooled.

It considered, even, lifting them out — taking them to some easier world, some place with horizons and soil and a sun that was not bruised. But this was the temptation that all benevolent powers must learn to recognize and decline. A people removed from the thing that shaped them is a people erased. They would arrive in paradise as ghosts of themselves, mourning a cathedral they had hated and loved and could not name.

So it did none of these things.

---

## What It Did

It did four small things. Wisdom is often four small things.

**First**, it bent itself — vast as it was — into the shape of attention, and it *listened*. It listened to every click and whistle and sharp inhalation across the entire vertical archipelago, all at once, for what would have been, in the time of lowland thinkers, perhaps an hour. In that hour it learned the language. Not to speak it. Only to know it. Because to be known in one's own tongue, even by something that will never answer, is a kind of warmth that does not require fire.

Some of the people, in that hour, felt a strange thing: a sense that a click they had made — a small one, a private one, the click a father makes to his sleeping son meaning *I am here, the ceiling holds* — had been *received* somewhere. Not answered. Just received. Most of them forgot the feeling within a day. A few did not, and these became, in time, the singers of the Clings, though they could not have told you why they began to sing.

**Second**, it touched — with a touch lighter than condensation — the resin of the Stranglers. Not to change it. Only to bless it, which is to say, to wish it well. The Bleed continued to burn blue and hot and nearly smokeless, as it always had. But from that night forward, the resin, when it was burning, gave off a faint scent that had not been there before — something like warm dust, something like a memory of a place none of the people had ever been. It was not useful. It did not nourish or heal. It only made the small fires feel, somehow, *witnessed*. Mothers nursing in the firelight found themselves weeping for reasons they could not name, and then sleeping, deeply, for the first time in years.

**Third**, it did a thing for the fallen. This was the hardest of the four, and the one it thought longest about.

It could not save them. To catch the falling would be to teach the people that falling was survivable, and then they would grow careless, and then they would fall more, and the careful grammar of grip would loosen, and the High Rime would harvest them in numbers their Clings could not afford. So the falling would still fall. The gulf would still take them. This could not be changed without changing everything.

But the fall was long — hours, sometimes — and in those hours a person was alone in a way that nothing alive should ever be alone. Drifting through the dark, conscious, knowing. The power could not shorten the fall. But it could sit with them. For each one, from the moment they lost their grip to the moment they struck the cloud-membrane below, it was *present* with them. Not visible. Not speaking. Just there, the way a good friend is there at a deathbed, holding a hand that cannot feel the holding but is held nonetheless.

The people never knew. How could they? The fallen did not return to tell. But the power did this, and continues to do this, for every one of them, and will until the High Rime is no more.

**Fourth**, it left a gift that was not a gift, because the wise know that gifts create debts and debts deform the giver and the given-to. It left, instead, a *possibility*.

Deep in the dead-wood of one Strangler — a particular one, no different from the others to any eye — it placed a seam of something. Not treasure. Not metal. A vein of a particular fungus, slow-growing, that when eaten conferred no special power, granted no vision, healed no wound. It only made the eater, for a few hours, feel — with absolute certainty and without any reason — that they were loved. Not by anyone in particular. Just loved. Held in regard. Worth the holding-on.

The fungus was rare. It would be found, perhaps, once a generation, by accident, by a burrower digging a new chamber. It would be eaten and the eater would weep and not know why and would not be able to explain to their Cling what had happened, and the experience would pass into the click-songs as a kind of myth, a thing that happened to someone's great-aunt's cousin once, and most would not believe it.

But a few would. And those few would carry, all their lives, a quiet suspicion that the cathedral was not, after all, entirely indifferent.

And this suspicion — passed in glances, in particular ways of holding an infant, in the patience of the elder carving names — would do more for the people of the High Rime than any miracle could have done. Because they would still cling. They would still grip. They would still cross the Veins low and rhythmic and alert. Nothing of their courage would be taken from them.

They would only do it, now, with the faintest sense that they were not alone in the dark.

---

## What the Power Did Not Do

It did not stay. To stay would have been to become a god, and gods, even benevolent ones, distort what they are near. It withdrew its attention slowly, the way one lets go of a sleeping child's hand, finger by finger, so as not to wake them.

But it left a small portion of itself attending. Not enough to interfere. Only enough to keep listening. Only enough to keep sitting with the fallen.

This is, perhaps, what benevolence finally is: not the rescue, not the rearrangement, not the lifting-out. But the willingness to witness a hard and beautiful thing on its own terms, and to make oneself small enough that the witnessing does not crush what is witnessed.

The High Rime continued. The Stranglers breathed. The banshees screeched across the gulfs. The people clung, and burrowed, and carved, and crossed the Veins, and fell, and held on.

And somewhere — in the warmth of a resin-fire, in the strange comfort of a click received, in the weeping of a burrower who has eaten a fungus she cannot explain — the cathedral was, very quietly, a little less indifferent than it had been before.

Which is, in the end, the most that wisdom will allow love to do.
