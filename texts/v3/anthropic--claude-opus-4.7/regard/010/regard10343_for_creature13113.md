# What the Power Would Do

The benevolent and wise power arrives without arriving. There is no descent, no light from above—nothing so crude as intervention from outside. It simply attends. It turns the full weight of its attention to this place, and listens.

It listens for a long time.

---

It listens first to the obvious thing: the suffering. The pale circular scars on rubbery skin. The pups who do not survive the cavitation burns. The hunters torn apart when a chain finally parts at the wrong moment. The slow grinding terror of a civilization whose every cradle is also a guillotine, whose every cathedral is also a furnace eating itself toward collapse. The power feels all of this, and something in it—if it has anything like a chest—aches.

The first impulse of a lesser power would be to fix it. To still the water. To replace the dying iron with something incorruptible. To lift the anchor-kin gently into some calmer sea where their hooks could rest and their pups could sleep in warmth that did not require a screaming axle to provide it.

The wise power does not do this.

Because it keeps listening.

---

It listens to the percussive language traveling the chains. *Crack-crack-thrum.* It learns, over what would be years to a smaller mind, the grammar of that language. And it discovers something that staggers it:

The anchor-kin have a word for the turbines that does not mean *machine* and does not mean *god* and does not mean *trap*. The word means, roughly, *the ones we are becoming*. They have a word for cavitation scars that translates closer to *first speech* than to *wound*—because a pup's earliest experience of language is the boom of the gears felt through its mother's body as she clings to the stator. They have a word for the moment a chain parts and a hunter is lost, and it is the same word as the moment a new braided kelp-and-sinew rope is finally strong enough to bear weight. One loss, one birth, one word.

These are not creatures who were thrown into hell and learned to survive it. These are creatures whose entire interior life—their love, their art, their understanding of what it means to be a person—has been woven, generation by generation, into the friction. To still the water would not save them. It would unmake them. They would become something else, something less, gasping in a quiet they have no organs to inhabit.

The wise power understands this. It does not flinch from the understanding.

---

So it does not still the water.

What it does is smaller, and stranger, and harder.

**First**, it adds nothing and removes nothing. It only *witnesses*. And because it is a power, its witness has weight. Somewhere in the somatic resonance of the chains, a new harmonic appears—so subtle that only the oldest anchor-kin, the ones whose otoliths have grown dense and strange with age, notice it. It is the feeling of being *seen*. Not judged. Not pitied. Seen the way one sees a magnificent thing: with the full attention it deserves. For a species whose every act is an act of clinging in the dark, this is not a small gift. Some of the old ones, feeling it, weep in the way their kind weeps, which is a long slow pressing of the forehead against the iron.

**Second**, it does one thing that could be called an act, though it is barely an act at all. In the deepest of the abyssal trenches, where no anchor-kin has ever descended and returned, the power lays down a seed. Not a plant. A *possibility*. It is a deposit of something like iron, but not iron—something that does not corrode in salt water, something that rings with the right frequencies, something whose lattice will accept the resinous saliva and crushed shell of the anchor-kin's biological concrete as if the two were made for each other. The power does not put this material into their hands. It puts it where, in seven or eight generations, an unusually bold hunter following an unusually deep downdraft might find it. And when that hunter finds it, she will think she has discovered it herself. She will be right. The power has only ensured that the discovery is possible.

This is what wisdom looks like when it loves something fierce: it does not rescue. It widens the range of what the fierce thing can do for itself.

**Third**, and this is the hardest, the power makes a decision about the turbines.

It would be easy to preserve them. To breathe some subtle stabilizing influence into the metal, to slow the oxidation, to give the anchor-kin another ten thousand years of the machines they have built their civilization inside. The power considers this for a long time.

And then it chooses not to.

Because the anchor-kin's greatest work—the multi-generational replacement, the patient braiding of bone and kelp and shell over dying iron—is not a tragedy to be averted. It is their cathedral. It is the thing they are *for*. To preserve the turbines would be to take the chisel from the sculptor's hand at the moment just before the face emerges from the stone. The power will not do this. The metal will die. The anchor-kin will replace it. And when, in some far century, the last fleck of original iron dissolves into the sea, the turbines will still be spinning—made now entirely of the anchor-kin themselves, of their patience, of their dead braided into their living. That will be a thing the universe has never seen before. The power wants to see it.

So the power lets the iron die on schedule.

---

**Fourth**, and last, the power does one quiet personal thing.

There is a pup, just now, in one of the warmest nurseries, deep in the belly of a turbine whose reduction gears are nearly gone. The pup is malformed—her hooks did not calcify correctly, and she will not be able to climb. In the ordinary course of things, she will be carried by her mother for a little while, and then, when her mother must hunt, she will be set into a kelp hammock, and one day a surge will come that her mother cannot return from in time, and the pup will be swept into the blades.

The power does not save her. Saving her would teach the anchor-kin that their world is not what it is, and that lesson would kill more of them than this one pup's death ever could.

But the power does this: it arranges that the pup, in her short life, will feel through the iron every song the anchor-kin have ever sung. Every percussive message, every lullaby thrummed against a stator, every hunter's signal racing up a mile of chain—all of it, compressed and gentled and delivered through her mother's body into her own dense little skull. She will live perhaps forty days. In those forty days she will know her entire people. She will be, briefly, the most complete anchor-kin who has ever existed. And when the surge takes her, she will not be afraid, because she will recognize the sound of the blades as a voice she has known her whole life, calling her by a name that means *come home*.

This is the only act of the power that could be called a mercy in the small sense. It is allowed because no one will ever know it happened. The pup cannot report it. The mother will only feel that her daughter died unusually peaceful, and will fold that small mystery into the long grammar of her grief.

---

And then the power withdraws its concentrated attention, though not its presence. It will return. It will keep returning. It has fallen, in its vast and patient way, in love with this place—with the obsidian bodies pressed flat into scalloped stone, with the chains singing their seismic gossip across miles of dark water, with the screaming bronze cathedrals being slowly, lovingly, replaced by bone.

It thinks: *They are making something I could not have made. They are making it out of exactly what they were given. I will not interrupt them. I will only make sure that what they are given remains enough.*

And in the strobing cathedral light, a hunter clinging to a chain feels a vibration she cannot quite place—not a message, not a warning, not the approach of prey. Just a sense, passing through the iron and into the dense tuning fork of her skull, that the friction she lives in is being held, somewhere, by something that thinks it is beautiful.

She presses her forehead to the chain for a long moment.

Then she goes back to work.
