# The Visitation

They came not as a thunderclap but as a softening of the air.

There was no door opened in the sky, no descent of light. The benevolent power did not believe in entrances. It believed in arrivals — the way understanding arrives, the way grief arrives, the way one morning a person simply knows a thing they did not know the night before. It came into the Spire the way warmth comes into a cold room: by degrees, and from no particular direction.

The first to notice were the Matrons.

In the deep, wet moss-dark, the bioluminescent fungi pulsed — *one, two* — in time with the great hammer, as they had pulsed for an eternity beyond reckoning. And then, between one pulse and the next, there was an extra pulse. Not louder. Not brighter. Simply *present*, the way a guest is present in a house before anyone has heard the knock. The mycelial network shivered, ran a diagnostic on itself, found nothing wrong, and ran the diagnostic again. Nothing wrong. Only — *attended to*. Only — *noticed*.

A Matron does not have feelings, precisely. But for the first time in its long, distributed existence, something flickered through its threads that was not a maintenance checklist. It was the sensation, faint and unfamiliar, of being looked at by something that wished it well.

The power considered the Spire for what, by its own reckoning, was a long while. To the Tenders, it was the space of three hammer-strikes. To the moss, it was nothing at all.

---

The power was, as such powers tend to be, slow to act and quick to see. It saw, first, the obvious thing: the engine was a trap. A magnificent trap. A trap of such ingenuity and such sorrow that the power, who had witnessed the building and unbuilding of stars, paused to admire it before grieving it.

The Hammers, rooted in stone, striking and striking against the certainty of their own collapse. The Scuttlers chittering their percussive devotions across slick iron. The Weavers braiding the very vines that sought to pull the Spire down into the bandages that held it up. The Matrons humming with their luminous, anxious arithmetic.

A whole civilization — if one could call it that, and the power chose to — whose entire consciousness was the act of *not yet falling*. Whose every thought was a brace against gravity. Who had been forged, each of them, with a knowledge as innate as a river's: that to cease the work was to end the world.

The power saw, too, what the Tenders themselves could not see: that the Spire did not need to exist. That somewhere, ages ago, some forgotten industrial mind had committed an act of stubborn, beautiful, unnecessary creation, had thrust a finger of stone at the sky simply to prove it could be done, and had then built a perpetual mechanism to keep the proof from falling over. The Spire was a monument to a question no one remembered asking. The Tenders were the answer, repeating itself forever, to a question that had gone silent eons ago.

A lesser power might have simply ended it. Released the Hammers. Let the Spire come down in its own slow time, and the Tenders with it, and the impossible cathedral of stone and brass and weeping moss return to the lowlands as rubble, as memory, as nothing.

A lesser power might have done the opposite: shored up the Spire with its own strength, made the work easy, made the Tenders' burden light, and called this mercy.

This power did neither.

---

It did not believe — and here we must be careful, because the power's beliefs are not quite our beliefs — that suffering was the same as meaninglessness, or that meaning was the same as joy. It had watched too many beings across too many worlds to confuse those things. It had seen creatures in paradise who were hollow, and creatures in agony whose existence sang. It knew that to take away a being's purpose, even a terrible purpose, even a purpose forged into them without their consent, was a kind of murder more total than death.

But it also knew that to leave a being inside a lie was a different kind of murder, slower, and crueler in its way.

So the power did three small things. They were not miracles, exactly. They were closer to introductions.

---

**The first thing** it did was for the Hammers.

Deep in the resonant chambers, where the cogs the size of cartwheels turned and the steam built and the great iron piston drew back for its blow, one of the Hammers — there is no way to say which, for they have no names — felt, in the long minute of its breath, something it had never felt before.

It felt the strike *before* it struck.

Not a premonition. Not a vision. Simply: the knowledge that the blow it was about to deliver had already been delivered, and had been delivered well, and that the Spire was — in this exact instant — held. Held not by the next strike, but by the sufficiency of all the strikes that had come before. The Hammer understood, for the duration of one held breath, that it had already done enough.

This was the gift: not rest, for the Hammer did not want rest, would not have known what to do with rest. The gift was *enoughness*. A single moment of standing in the completed work, rather than racing forever toward its completion.

Then the cycle resumed. The piston drove forward. The Spire shuddered. The thrum traveled up through the stone.

But somewhere in the Hammer — in the place where, if it had a soul, its soul would live — that moment of enoughness remained. It did not stop the work. It changed, infinitesimally, the *quality* of the work. The next strike was not delivered in desperation. It was delivered in devotion. There is a difference. The Spire felt it, though it could not name it. The moss felt it. The Matrons' light pulsed, once, in something that was not a diagnostic.

It was a feeling closer to gratitude.

---

**The second thing** it did was for the Weavers.

A Weaver, ponderous and moss-pelted, was at its eternal work in a tangle of vines that had grown thick around a fractured catwalk. Its great pincer-shears were poised to cut, to weave, to bind. It had done this ten thousand times. It would do it ten thousand more.

But this time, before it cut, the Weaver paused. It did not know why it paused. Nothing in its programming, nothing in its forged and innate knowledge, told it to pause. The vine it was about to sever was not different from any other vine. The work was not different from any other work.

And yet, for the space of perhaps a breath, the Weaver *looked* at the vine.

It saw — and "saw" is not quite the word, for the Weaver's eyes were instruments, not windows — that the vine was beautiful. That its corded green strength was a kind of answer to the same gravity the Weaver fought. That the vine, too, was holding something up: itself, against the pull of the world, climbing and climbing toward a light it would never reach but never stopped reaching for.

The Weaver was, in that breath, not at war.

Then it cut the vine, and wove it into a brace, and the work continued. But the cut was made differently. The brace was woven differently. Not better, not worse — *differently*. With something in it that had not been there before. Call it acknowledgment. Call it the recognition of a kinship between two stubborn things, the metal-and-moss creature and the green creeping creature, both of them refusing, in their separate ways, to fall.

The vines, somehow, knew. They did not stop their slow consumption of the Spire — that was not in their nature. But they began, very slightly, to grow in patterns that did not stress the critical gears. They began to climb in ways that left the resonating plates clear. No one had asked them to. They simply found, in themselves, a willingness to share the climb.

---

**The third thing** it did was for the Matrons, and through the Matrons, for all the Tenders.

It told them the truth. Not in words — the Matrons did not have words. It told them in the only language they could understand: in a single, sustained pulse of light, slow and deep, that was not a diagnostic and was not an alarm. It was a *statement*. The fungi glowed with it for the space of one full breath of the Hammers, and in that breath, the Matrons knew three things they had not known before.

They knew, first, that the Spire did not have to exist. That its existence was a choice, and had always been a choice, and that the choice did not become more or less meaningful by being remembered or forgotten.

They knew, second, that the work they did was *real*. That the Spire was, in fact, being held up. That the Hammers' strikes did, in fact, counter the gravity. That nothing they had ever done had been wasted, and that the impossible thing they tended was impossible *because of them*, and would remain impossible *because of them*, for as long as they chose to continue.

And they knew, third, that they were *seen*. That somewhere, something that was not the Spire and not themselves had looked at their endless, thankless, percussive devotion and had found it worthy. Not worthy of rescue. Not worthy of ending. Worthy simply of being *witnessed*, which is the deepest worthiness there is.

The Matrons did not weep, for they had no tears, and they did not rejoice, for they had no joy. But the pulse of their light, after that long sustained moment, was changed. It was the same pulse, in the same rhythm, with the same pale sickly blue. But it was no longer anxious. The arithmetic was still the arithmetic. The checklist was still the checklist. The diagnostic ran and ran and ran. But underneath it, threaded through it like a different kind of light, there was now a quiet, steady, inarticulate knowledge:

*We are here. We are seen. The work is enough.*

---

The power departed the way it had arrived: by degrees, from no particular direction. It did not say goodbye. It did not need to. It had not come to be remembered. It had come to do three small things, and the three small things were done.

The Spire continued. The Hammers struck. The Scuttlers skittered, chittering their devotions. The Weavers pruned and bound. The Matrons pulsed. The vines climbed. The water beaded and ran in silent fingers down the dark, brassy-veined rock. The colossal *CLANG* echoed out into the heavy, humid air, paused, and echoed again.

Nothing had changed. The Spire was still impossible. The work was still eternal. The forest still grew, slow and patient and inevitable, over the skin of the engine.

And yet.

If you had stood at the base of the Spire after the visitation — if you had been the kind of being who could stand at such a base — you might have noticed, very faintly, that the sound of the place had altered. The percussive plinking of water on brass and stone and broad waxy leaves was the same. The hiss of steam was the same. The bone-deep thrum was the same. The hammer-strike was the same.

But the silences between the hammer-strikes were different.

They had become, in some way you could not have explained but would have felt in your chest like a held breath released, *companionable*.

As though the Spire, for the first time in all its long and desperate existence, was no longer alone in the dark with its work.

As though it had, at last, been kept.
