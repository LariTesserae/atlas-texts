# The Visitation at Everweep

The power came not as light, for light would have been a violence to that place. It came as the mist itself thickened by one shade, as the rain remembered something it had forgotten, as the pulse beneath the peat skipped — just once — and then resumed in a rhythm subtly truer than before.

The Wetfoot did not see it arrive. They felt it the way they felt weather coming: a tension behind the sternum, a question the fen was asking of itself.

---

**What the power considered, before it acted.**

It looked first, and looked long. This is the first mercy of the wise: to refuse the seduction of doing.

It saw a people who had not been broken. It saw a land that had not been saved, because it had not asked to be. It saw the rusted engines turning in the dark and understood at once that they were no longer machines — they were organs. The fen had eaten them and made them its own slow heart. To still them would be a wounding. To restore them to their original purpose would be a desecration. They were already in their right relation.

It saw the Wetfoot at their work: the polishing of gears whose function none remembered, the oil poured like libation, the prayers murmured into pipes. The power understood that this was not ignorance. It was a more difficult knowledge than the engineers who built the engines had ever possessed. The engineers had known *how*. The Wetfoot knew *that it must continue*, and *that it is holy*, and *that we are kin to it now*. This is the older science.

It saw, too, the small griefs. A child coughing wetly in a stilt-house at the fen's eastern edge. An old woman whose hands had begun to forget the knots. A hunter who had drowned three winters past and whose name was still spoken at dusk, softly, as though calling him home from somewhere just out of sight. A slow seep of something acrid from one of the deeper engines — a wound the fen was working to close but had not yet closed.

And it saw what was coming, far off: not soon, but not never. Others would find this place. Others always do.

---

**What the power did.**

*First*, it did nothing for a long while. It let itself be witnessed by the fen — for the fen was alive, as the Wetfoot believed, and it deserved the courtesy of introduction. The black pools held the power's reflection and did not trouble themselves. The bioluminescent fungi pulsed once in acknowledgment. The thrum beneath the peat made a small adjustment, the way an old dog shifts to accommodate a guest by the fire. *You may stay,* the fen said, in the only language it had, which was the language of staying.

*Second*, it touched the acrid seep. Not to remove it — removal was not within the grammar of this place — but to teach the surrounding moss a slightly better way of metabolizing it. The moss would not know it had been taught. In a generation it would simply be true that a certain species of sphagnum near the eastern engines grew a little thicker, and drank a little deeper, and the water downstream ran cleaner by a measure no instrument the Wetfoot possessed could detect. The fen would do the work. The power only whispered the recipe.

*Third*, it went to the coughing child. It did not heal her, because healing-from-outside is a kind of theft; it takes from a person the story they were going to tell with their body. Instead it sat beside her through one night — invisible, weightless, present only as a slight warming of the damp air near her chest — and it listened to her breathing until her breathing remembered how to be breathing. In the morning her mother would say the fever had broken in the night, and would leave a double offering of moss at the black pool, not knowing why, only knowing that thanks was owed.

*Fourth*, it went to the old woman whose hands forgot knots. Here the power paused, because this was harder. To restore her hands would be to lie to her about time. Time was passing; this was true, and truth was the fen's first law. So instead the power sat with her, and she — being old, and being Wetfoot, and being therefore unsurprised by presences — felt it, and offered it tea, which it could not drink but accepted in spirit. They sat together. The power gave her, instead of restoration, a small lucidity: a clear hour in which she taught her granddaughter three knots that had been close to being lost. The knowledge crossed. The hands were allowed to keep forgetting. This was the right shape of the gift.

*Fifth*, it considered the drowned hunter, whose name was still called at dusk. The power could not return him. Even benevolence has its grammar, and the dead are a sentence already finished. But it could do this: it could carry, once, the sound of his voice back across the threshold — not as ghost, not as haunting, but as a single clear note in the wind one evening, recognizable to those who had loved him, deniable to those who had not. His widow would weep. His brother would laugh once, startled, and then weep also. The name would be said one last time at dusk that night, and then — gently, gently — released. The fen would receive it the way it receives all things. The dusk-calling would stop. This was not a loss. This was a completion.

*Sixth*, and this was the longest work: it laid something into the deep peat, far below where any Wetfoot foot had ever pressed. A kind of *attention*. A watchfulness woven into the substrate, slow as the peat itself, that would wake — not soon, but not never — when the others came. It would not repel them. The power did not believe in walls. It would only make the fen, at that hour, *more itself*: mistier, more labyrinthine, more patient, more honest about what it was. Those who came with reverence would be permitted to find the Wetfoot, and would be changed by the finding. Those who came with extraction in their hearts would find that the paths did not hold them, that the peat had opinions, that the silent predators in the shallows had been waiting a long time for exactly this kind of meal. The fen would defend itself in the fen's own way. The power only sharpened, very slightly, the fen's intuition about the difference.

*Seventh*, before leaving, it did one thing for itself, which the wise are permitted: it learned the Wetfoot word — the clicked, glottal, almost-unsoundable word — that meant *the way water remembers where it has been*. It carried this word away with it. Somewhere else, in some other work, in some other century, it would need that word. It would have it.

---

**What the Wetfoot knew.**

They knew something had come and gone. They could not have said what. The eldest among them, the ones who tended the engines, noticed that the thrum was a half-tone deeper, as if the fen had drawn a slow breath and let it out more completely than before. The lanterns of fungi pulsed, for three nights, in a rhythm that almost resembled a heartbeat that was not their own. Then they returned to their ordinary glow.

A child was well who had been sick. An old woman taught three knots. A name was released into the dusk. The eastern moss grew, over years, a little thicker.

The clan made no special story of it. They had no word for *miracle* any more than they had a word for *silence*; both concepts assumed an absence of the ordinary holiness in which they were already drowned. They simply added one more shape to their carved symbols — a spiral that closed not quite all the way, leaving a single gap through which something could enter, or leave, or both — and they pressed it into the peat at the edges of the black pools, and they did not explain it, even to themselves.

---

**What the power understood, leaving.**

That the best help is often nearly indistinguishable from no help at all.

That a place which is already whole asks only to be recognized, not improved.

That reverence is a technology older than engines, and outlasts them, and may in the end be what the engines were always reaching toward.

That the Wetfoot were, in their own slow patient way, doing the same work the power itself was doing — tending, witnessing, keeping faith with what had been left behind — and that to meet them was therefore not to descend to them but to find a colleague in the long labor of the world.

It went out the way it had come in: as one shade of mist among many, as a slight truing of rhythm, as rain remembering. The fen wept, as it always wept. The Wetfoot moved through the reeds, as they always moved. The engines turned in the dark.

Everything continued.

Which was, of course, the gift.
