# What the Wise Power Would Do

It would, first, do nothing.

For a long while — by whatever measure such a being keeps time — it would simply attend. Not study, not catalogue, not assess. Attend. The way a person at a bedside attends to someone sleeping: present, quiet, allowing the other to be exactly as they are.

Because the first temptation of power, even benevolent power, is to *improve*. To smooth the razor edges of the rocks. To thicken the air. To warm the wind. To make a more hospitable place for the being who lives there. And the wise part of the power would know that this would be a kind of murder — that the creature *is* its conditions, that to soften the world is to dissolve the one who has become exactly the shape of its hardness.

So instead, it would learn the creature's name. Not impose one. Learn it. The name that the creature already has, written in the particular way it places its feet on frozen stone, in the rhythm of its breathing against the thin air, in whatever it does that resembles, for it, joy.

Then, perhaps, small things.

It would notice the ruins — the simple structures, the rudimentary tools — and understand them not as failure but as a former conversation. Someone once spoke to this land, and the land answered, and the speakers went away or became something else. The wise power would not rebuild those structures. It would, perhaps, keep them from being ground entirely to dust by the next thousand years of weather. A small kindness to memory. A small honoring of the fact that something tried.

It would, on a night when the creature is suffering — because even the well-adapted suffer, this is not a flaw of design but a feature of being alive — make the wind drop for an hour. Not stop. Drop. Just enough that the creature could sleep. And it would not announce itself in this. The creature would simply experience an hour of unexpected ease, and might not even mark it, and that would be fine. Benevolence does not require an audience.

It would, I think, leave a single sign somewhere the creature might one day find it: not a message, not an instruction, but something like a thumbprint. A stone arranged in a way no erosion arranges stones. A place where the moisture condenses into a shape that almost, almost looks intentional. Something that says only: *you are seen, and the seeing is kind.* Whether the creature understands this or not is the creature's business. The offering does not require reception to be real.

And then — and this is the hardest part, the part where wisdom does its true work — it would go away. Not abandon. Withdraw to the proper distance. Because to remain too close, even lovingly, even attentively, is to become a weather of its own, a pressure the creature must adapt to, a new harshness wearing a soft face.

The wise power would understand that the highest form of love, for a being who has shaped itself against difficulty, is to leave the difficulty intact and the being free.

It would check back. Quietly. The way one returns to a garden one planted long ago, just to see. Not to weed. Just to see.

And if, one day, the creature were dying — really dying, the last of its kind, the final breath about to leave the thin cold air — the power would come close again. Not to save. Saving would be another kind of theft. It would come close to witness. To make sure that this life, this strange improbable adapted brilliant life, did not end unseen.

It would hold the moment the way you hold a candle in cupped hands against a wind.

And then it would let the candle go out, because that is what candles do, and what makes them holy is not that they last but that they burned.

That is what I think it would do.

That is what I would do, if I were wise, and powerful, and kind.
