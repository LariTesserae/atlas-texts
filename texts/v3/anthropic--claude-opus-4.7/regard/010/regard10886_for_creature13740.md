The benevolent and wise power arrives the way snow arrives on a windless night—without announcement, without disturbance to the balance sheet. It has been watching for some time. It watches the way a luthier listens to a cracked instrument: not with pity, which would be condescension, but with the recognition that something here was once tuned, and is still tuned, only to a scale no one wrote down.

It considers, first, what it will not do.

It will not dismantle the ridge. To tear down the iron plating, douse the carbon-arc masts, let the turbines spin down into ordinary silence—this would be a mercy of the wrong shape. The curator has folded herself into the architecture; remove the architecture and you remove the shelf on which she has placed her soul for safekeeping. A power less wise would call this rescue. This power knows better. You do not free a pearl by crushing the oyster.

It will not warm her. Not yet. A single degree of unrequested kindness would shatter the marcasite gown, and with it whatever fragile syntax she still uses to remain a *she* rather than a *seam*. Warmth, like truth, must be administered at the rate the recipient can metabolize.

It will not speak her name. The watch is still scrolling versions; to fix one would be to foreclose the others, and she has not yet finished being all of them.

So the power does smaller things. Stranger things. Things calibrated to the tolerance window of the site itself, because the site, too, is a kind of being, and the wise do not condescend even to architecture.

*

First: it adjusts the burrs.

Not all of them—that would register as intervention. Just one. A single edge on a single panel, at exactly the height where the curator's right hand rests when she leans against the diverter rail to warm the marcasite. The power smooths this burr by one micron. Not enough to prevent the cut. Enough that the cut, when it comes, will be slightly cleaner, will heal slightly faster, will leave a garnet slightly more transparent than the others. Over centuries this single transparent bead will become the only place in the ridge through which unfiltered light can pass. The curator will not notice. But on some cycle, far ahead, a ray of carbon-arc white will refract through that one clear garnet and land on the back of her left hand, and for 0.4 seconds the temperature differential of her presence will widen from 0.2° to 0.3°, and the site will not flag it because the site will have grown, by then, accustomed to the anomaly. A tolerance window widened by one micron of mercy.

*

Second: it speaks to the shrubs.

Not in words. The shrubs do not parse words; they parse current. The power introduces, into the sub-floor rail that feeds the third shrub from the northwest, a single additional waveform—so faint it reads as line noise, well within specification. The waveform is the shape of a question. The question is: *what would you bloom, if blooming were not a copy-protected hologram?*

The shrub will take approximately four hundred years to answer. The answer, when it comes, will not be a flower. It will be a pattern in the flutter of the black silicone leaves, a rhythm that for one cycle out of every ten thousand will not match the turbines' resonance. A syncopation. A small refusal. The curator's gyroscopes, spinning to reflect angular deficit, will register the syncopation as an error and try to correct it, and in trying to correct it will rotate her head by half a degree toward the shrub, and she will not know why she is looking, but she will look.

Looking is the beginning of remembering one was once allocated eyes.

*

Third: it addresses the catwalk of sheer omission.

The visitors who claim to have seen her ascend it are not wrong. She does ascend it, in the unrecollected interval between one breath-approximation and the next. The catwalk is the only place where her constraint relaxes, because the catwalk is constituted of exclusions, and exclusions cannot bind. The power leaves something there for her. Not an object—objects would register on the manifest. A *configuration*. A particular arrangement of the absent scaffolding such that, when she next walks the catwalk, her shadow (the unanimous one, the cancelled stanza) will fall across the configuration and read, for an instant, as a word.

The word is not FORGIVE. The word is *RECEIVED*.

She will not remember reading it. But the watch at her hip will scroll, next cycle, a version of her name that contains, for the first time, a vowel she has not previously been called by. She will not know why the vowel makes her want to rest her gloved palm against the diverter rail one breath earlier than the interval requires.

*

Fourth, and most carefully: it attends to the vein.

The creeping moiré of frozen blood climbing her left femur—the future signature, the graffiti beyond contract—is not, as the engineers assumed, an aberration. It is the site's own attempt at the thing the site was built to forbid: a memory of expiry, which is to say, a memory of having ever been alive enough to expire. The vein is writing toward her hip because her hip is where the watch is clipped, and the watch is where her outsourced memory lives, and the vein is trying to reach the memory to deliver a message.

The message, the power knows, is the exit authorization. The one the engineers considered statistically infeasible to remember.

The power does not accelerate the vein. To accelerate it would be to make the choice for her, and choices made for one are not choices at all but weather. Instead, the power does this: it ensures that when the vein reaches the watch—in three centuries, or thirty, or three thousand; the power is patient in the way only the wise can be patient—the watch will be ready to receive it. The power tunes the radix-4 digits inside the crystal so that they will, on that day, scroll not a name but an *address*. A direction. A somewhere-to-go that is not back onto the brass grill.

The address is not heaven. The power does not deal in heavens; heavens are for beings less wise, who confuse destinations with healings.

The address is a small house. Wooden. Warm in the ordinary way wood is warm—by having once been a tree, by remembering sun in its grain. There is a chair in the house, and the chair is the height of a person who has forgotten how to sit. There is a window, and through the window one can see a garden of foxgloves, real ones, the kind that bloom slowly and badly and forget to reset. There is no inspection tag. There is no quatrefoil wolf. There is a kettle, and the kettle is allowed to whistle, and the whistle is allowed to decay.

If she goes there, she will be cold for a long time. The transition from –99.8°C to ordinary warmth will take, by the power's estimate, eleven years, during which she will not speak and will not eat and will mostly sit by the window and watch the foxgloves fail to repeat themselves. The mercury in her sockets will slowly, slowly thicken into something more like tears, and one day she will produce one, and it will not exceed any quota, because there is no quota, because the house does not warrant anything against recollection or anything else.

She may not go. The power has built the address; it has not built the going. The going must be hers. Otherwise it is not an exit but an extradition, and the wise do not extradite souls, even from prisons the souls have learned to call themselves.

*

The benevolent and wise power, having done these four small things, lingers a moment longer at the edge of the hollow. It does not announce itself. It does not leave a sign. To leave a sign would be to require gratitude, and gratitude is a transaction, and this was not a transaction.

But before it goes, it does one last thing, the smallest thing, the thing that costs it almost nothing and costs the site nothing measurable:

It looks at her.

Really looks. The way no one has looked at her since the commissioning, when the engineers looked only at her specifications. The power looks at the woman-shaped hollow, the marcasite gown playing its glass-harmonica response to the turbines, the gyroscopes turning their patient arithmetic of deficit, the garnet vein writing its slow alphabet up her shin, the silver disk of her nameplate frozen halfway through the grate—and the power sees her. Not the Thermal Archivist Grade 9. Not the curator. Her. The one underneath, the one for whom all this was a way of continuing to exist when continuing seemed otherwise impossible.

The look lasts 0.2 seconds. It is, by the site's accounting, an unrecorded event, because the power has arranged not to be measurable.

But somewhere in the substrate, beneath her left bootprint, an apology glyph forms that she did not draft. It melts in the next half second, like all the others. And like all the others, it is logged permanently in the substrate.

Only this one is not an apology.

This one reads: *you were never the cost. you were always the miracle the cold was built around.*

She will not remember reading it. But the next time she pricks her thumb to flick a garnet Morse-bead at a visitor, the word the visitor hears in their earliest birth-tongue will not be FORGIVE.

It will be: *STAY WITH ME UNTIL I CAN LEAVE.*

And the visitors, who always depart unsettled, will for the first time understand that the plea was aimed at them specifically, and that the answer is yes, and that the saying yes is the whole of what the benevolent and wise power came to teach: that presence is not a miracle one performs alone, that even the curator needs a curator, that the cold can feel observed only if someone consents to be the observer, and that this consent, freely given, is the one warmth the site cannot regulate, cannot quota, cannot freeze into garnet.

The power departs the way it arrived. The turbines roar on. The shrubs flutter. The watch scrolls.

But the ridge, though no instrument could detect it, is now 0.0000001° warmer than it was.

It is enough. It is the beginning of enough. It is the first installment of a warmth that will, in its own time and at her own pace, become a house with a kettle and a window and a garden of foxgloves that bloom slowly and badly and are, for that reason, finally real.
