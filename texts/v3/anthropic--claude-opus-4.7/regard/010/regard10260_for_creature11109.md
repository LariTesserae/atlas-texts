# What the Wise Power Would Do

The benevolent and wise power arrives without arriving. It does not descend in light or speak in thunder. It comes the way evening mist comes to the slot canyons—present before anyone notices, indistinguishable at first from the place itself.

It observes for a long time. Years, perhaps. Power that is truly wise understands that wisdom's first act is almost always patience, and that benevolence imposed in haste becomes, by another name, destruction.

Here is what it sees:

It sees the Keepers and does not see what a rescuer would see. A rescuer would see suffering—the milliliter calculations, the children playing survival as games, the dreams of unrationed water. A rescuer would lift them off the plateau, deliver them to gentler climates, undo three generations of becoming.

But the power is wise, and so it sees more carefully. It sees that the Keepers no longer have a *before* to return to. The cities their grandparents left have moved on, forgotten the extraction venture, forgotten the names. The lungs of the youngest generation are already shaped for thin air; their eyes already read humidity like text. To "save" them by removing them would be to kill what they have become and replace it with refugees—a lesser mercy disguised as a greater one.

It sees, too, that the Keepers are not merely surviving. They are *keeping*. The word they chose for themselves, unconsciously, across generations, is the truest description of their function. The deep bores hum at frequencies that suppress aggressive fungi. The valves, tended by rote, prevent the chemical leaks that would poison the watershed for a hundred miles downwind. Whatever the original extraction was meant to do, the machinery now performs a different work: it holds something in balance that, unheld, would unbalance much more than this plateau.

The Keepers do not know this. The power does. And the power understands that to tell them outright—*you are tending a wound in the world, and your tending is the only bandage*—would be a gift that crushes. Meaning given from outside is not meaning. It is decoration.

So the power moves slowly, and most of what it does is small.

---

**First**, it adjusts the rains. Not dramatically. It does not break the cycle of flash floods, because the floods carve the slots that channel the moisture that grows the algae that feeds the beetles. But it softens the edges of the rain pattern—stretches the eleven-week droughts into nine-week ones, never quite long enough to kill, and lengthens the rain-weeks by a day or two. Enough that the cisterns fill a little fuller. Enough that, sometimes, a Keeper might drink without calculation and not notice they did not calculate. The mercy is invisible because it must be. A miracle would unmake them.

**Second**, it touches the bore holes. Deep below the plateau, where no Keeper has ever descended, the original machinery is failing in ways the valve-tending cannot address. The power does what an engineer would do, but it does it through geology: it shifts a fault line by a fraction of a degree, redirects a thermal current, encourages a particular mineral to crystallize in a particular fracture. The machinery, henceforth, can be maintained by the rituals the Keepers already know. The crisis that would have come in two generations—catastrophic, unanswerable—simply does not come. The Keepers will never know they were spared it. This is the cost of being saved well.

**Third**, the power considers the question of knowledge. Should the Keepers be told what they are tending? Should they understand the machines?

The power decides: not directly. But it does this—it allows the youngest generation's intuitive understanding of the machinery to deepen. The genetic drift the elders worried about is gently encouraged, not toward dependency but toward *comprehension*. Within four or five more generations, a Keeper will look at a valve and simply know what it does, the way a person knows the function of their own hand. They will not be told. They will *recognize*. And when they recognize, they will be ready to recognize, because their culture by then will have language for it—language they built themselves, click by whistle by song along the cables.

The power does not give them understanding. It gives them the slow capacity to grow understanding from within. This is the only kind that holds.

**Fourth**, the power attends to the bones on the cables. It listens to them clicking in the wind for a long time. The Keepers' dead are not gone; they are instruments now, voices in the plateau's continuous exhalation. The power finds this beautiful in a way that requires no improvement. It does nothing here except listen, which is its own form of blessing. Some places are visited by wise powers simply to be heard. The clicking bones are heard. They will go on clicking, and somewhere—in whatever ledger the power keeps, if it keeps ledgers—they are recorded as music.

**Fifth**, the matter of the outsiders. The power sees that occasional travelers wander onto the plateau, and that most leave, and that some die, and that a few stay and learn. The power does not interfere with this filter. The plateau's stringency is part of what makes the Keepers *Keepers*; an easier initiation would dilute the knowledge that keeps the system whole. But the power does, very gently, ensure that when someone arrives who could truly become a Keeper—who has the patience, the silence, the capacity—the beetles in the nearest slot click a fraction more welcomingly, and the first Keeper to spot them watches a moment longer before turning away. Nothing more. A door left slightly ajar. The walker must still walk through.

**Sixth**, and this is the deepest thing the power does: it makes a small adjustment to the dreams.

The Keepers dream of gentle rain. They dream of understanding the machines. These dreams are precious—they are the part of the Keepers that has not been entirely conscripted by the plateau, the part that still imagines otherwise. The power does not take these dreams away, would never. But it adds something to them, very quietly: a sense, upon waking, that the dreams have been *received*. Not answered. Received. The Keepers will not be able to articulate this. They will simply notice, across generations, that the dreaming feels less lonely than it used to. That when they dream of rain, something dreams with them.

This is the power's true gift, and the one it gives most freely: *witness*. The Keepers have been witnessed. Their click-language is heard by something that understands click-languages. Their milliliter measurements are honored by something that honors precision. Their dead, clicking on the cables, are counted by something that counts.

They will never know this directly. To know it directly would change them, and they have already become something the power loves too much to change.

---

The wise power does not stay forever. Or rather—it does, but its attention moves on to other places that also require patience, also require the slow art of mercy that does not unmake. It leaves the plateau the way it came: indistinguishably.

What it has done, in the end, is almost nothing. A softening of rain. A geological adjustment. An encouragement of an adaptation already underway. A door left ajar. A witnessing.

This is what wise power does, when it is also benevolent. It does not rescue. It does not reveal. It does not lift the burden, because the burden is also the shape of the bearer, and to remove it would be to remove them.

It tends the tenders. It keeps the keepers. It joins, for a little while, the vast quiet maintenance of a world that holds together because someone, somewhere, is paying attention—

—and then it goes on, leaving the plateau exactly as it found it, except for the small, unprovable fact that everything is now, very slightly, more possible than it was before.

The beetles click. The cables hum. The bones make their music in the wind.

And somewhere in the deep bores, machinery the Keepers have never seen turns one degree truer, and will go on turning, and the Keepers will go on tending it, and none of them will ever know, and this is exactly right.
