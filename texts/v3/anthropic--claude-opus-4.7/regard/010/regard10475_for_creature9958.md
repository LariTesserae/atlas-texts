The benevolent and wise power considers this place for a long time before doing anything at all.

It sees the plateau the way one might see a face that has settled into itself after many decades: every line is earned, every silence is the residue of some old sound finally laid to rest. It sees the people moving along their flagstone lanes, carrying small measures of water, fitting stone to stone. It sees how their lives are not impoverished but *fitted*—the way a river-pebble is fitted to the bed it lies in.

A lesser power, or a less wise one, would arrive.

It would bring rain to fill the cisterns to overflowing. It would warm the air, soften the winters, fatten the soil. It would multiply the small shaggy animals into herds, lengthen the lives of the elders, place a clean new well where the old one labors. It would, in its eagerness to be good, give and give and give—and in giving, it would unmake the very thing it loved. The walls would no longer need to be so carefully fitted. The children would no longer need to learn each stone by touch. The stories told in low ceilings would lose their referents: what is a hard winter, to those who have not known one? What is the dignity of sufficiency, where there is abundance?

The benevolent power understands this. It has watched, perhaps, other places it loved into ruin.

So it does something quieter.

---

**First, it simply attends.**

It places its awareness on the plateau the way one places a warm hand, palm-down, on a sleeping animal's flank—not to wake it, not to move it, only to be with it. The plateau does not know it is being attended to. The people do not know. But somewhere beneath knowing, in the place where moss feels the shape of the stone it grows on, something registers: *we are not unwitnessed.*

This is not nothing. Many things in the universe live and die unwitnessed. To be held in the regard of a wise attention is, in itself, a kind of grace—one that asks nothing and changes nothing and yet is real.

**Second, it makes the smallest possible adjustments, and only to preserve what is already there.**

When a child wanders too far past the rim chasing a bird, the wind shifts—just slightly—so that the child's own cloak flaps against their face and turns them back toward home. When a beloved elder is dying through a long night, and the family has gathered, the power lets the lamp-oil last a little longer than it should, so that no one has to fumble in the dark at the wrong moment. When a wall, ancient and well-fitted, has finally begun to fail in a way no one has yet noticed, a particular stone settles half a finger's breadth, in a particular way, so that the next mason to pass will see it and know.

These are not miracles. They are indistinguishable from luck, from the small mercies the world sometimes offers. The people do not credit them to any power. They credit them, rightly, to the patience of stone and the alertness of their own eyes. The power prefers it this way. To be thanked would be to be seen, and to be seen would be to become an actor in their world, and to become an actor would be to begin, however slowly, to remake them in the image of their gratitude. It declines this.

**Third, it does one thing that is larger, but still quiet.**

It ensures that the plateau is *findable*, but only by those who need it.

Not the curious. Not the conquering. Not the merchant looking for new markets, not the empire surveying its margins. The plateau remains, for these, a difficult place, an unremarkable smear on no map worth keeping. The benevolent power does not hide it—hiding would be an intervention against the world. It simply does not advertise it.

But once in a great while, someone comes up out of the valleys broken in a particular way—a way that the plateau, and only the plateau, knows how to mend. A soul exhausted by velocity, by accumulation, by the relentless production of meaning. A soul that needs to learn, for a season or for the rest of its life, what it is to take only what one needs and to let the rest be. To such a soul, the path up becomes findable. Not easy: findable. The cresting of the rim arrives at the right hour. The first villager met offers a seat near the fire and a cup of heated water, and asks a direct, economical question, and the broken soul begins, without yet knowing it, to be reassembled along quieter lines.

The villagers do not know they are doing this work. They are only being themselves. That is the whole of it.

**Fourth, and last, the power makes a promise to itself about the ending.**

For nothing endures forever, not even endurance. One day—far off, but not infinitely far—the plateau's small population will dwindle past the threshold at which a village can sustain itself. A last child will not be born. A last elder will die in a stone house, and the wind will move through the slit windows, and the cisterns will hold rainwater for no one. This too is part of the pattern. The power will not prevent it. To prevent it would be the deepest betrayal of everything the place has been.

But the power promises this: it will be there, at the end. It will attend the last person as it attended the first it noticed. The last conversation, the last lighting of a hearth-fire, the last hand laid on a wall in farewell—these will be witnessed. And after, when the moss has begun its slow work of softening the human geometries back into the geometry of stone, the power will remain a while longer, watching the place complete its long sentence, until the plateau and the ranges around it are simply, again, what they were before: high country, honest light, water lingering in pocketed pools, abiding.

Then the power will move on, carrying with it—if such a being carries anything—the memory of a small people who lived lightly, and a kind of knowledge that cannot be acquired any other way: the knowledge of how to love something by letting it be exactly what it is.

---

This, then, is what the benevolent and wise power does:

Almost nothing.

And that almost-nothing is the most difficult and most loving thing it knows how to do.
