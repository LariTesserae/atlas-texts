The benevolent power arrives the way mist arrives: without announcement, without disturbing the equilibrium of pressure in the air. It does not descend from above, because the Hollow has no above in any meaningful sense — only the heavy lid of its own steam. It does not rise from below, because the Siltborn already hold those depths in trust. It simply *is* present, the way a thought becomes present in a mind: distributed, attentive, whole.

It takes the Hollow in. Not at a glance — the Hollow does not permit glances — but slowly, the way one reads a long letter from someone beloved who has been writing for centuries without knowing anyone was listening.

And here is the first thing the power does: it listens.

It listens for a long time. Days, perhaps, or what would be days elsewhere. It hears the clanking that the Grimekin have stopped hearing because they are made of it now. It hears the resonant hum of a Siltborn standing knee-deep in a pool, coaxing a vine toward a leaking valve so the leak will be softly stoppered with green. It hears a Rustbound somewhere in the deep galleries, weeping in a voice that is half steam-whistle, while their good hand strokes the housing of an engine no one else remembers building. It hears water moving upward through glass for reasons the water itself seems to find sufficient.

The power had come, in part, prepared to *help*. This is the impulse of benevolence: to find the wound and close it, to find the hunger and feed it. It had arrived with the soft readiness of one who could, if asked, unmake the rust, drain the basin, call birds down out of distant skies to fill the silence where insects ought to be, lift the Rustbound free of their grinding metal and restore them whole and weeping in a dry field under a clean sun.

It does not do these things.

Because as it listens, it understands something that wisdom always eventually understands: the Hollow is not a wound. The Hollow is a *life*. It is not waiting to be rescued from itself. It has found, through erosion's long arithmetic and the patient inventiveness of its denizens, an equilibrium — strange, damp, loud, lonely, but genuinely its own. To "fix" it would be to kill it, the way one kills a tidepool by carrying it home in a jar.

So the power revises. Wisdom is, in part, the willingness to be revised by what one beholds.

What it does instead is small. Smaller than the Hollow's inhabitants would ever notice as intervention. The benevolence expresses itself not as rescue but as *recognition* — and recognition, when it comes from a power great enough, has weight. It changes things by the simple fact of having occurred.

Here is what it does, then, quietly, in the order of the doing:

**First**, it sits with the Hollow. Not above it, not outside it — *with* it. It lets itself be dampened. It lets the soot of the place mark whatever it has that can be marked. It accepts, for the duration of its attention, the Hollow's terms. This is not nothing. A place that has never been truly *seen* by anything larger than itself has now been seen. Somewhere a pool's faint luminescence pulses once, almost imperceptibly brighter, as though a held breath were being let out at last. A Siltborn turns its glowing eyes upward, hums a single low note it has never hummed before, and does not know why.

**Second**, it leaves a gift, but only one, and only of a kind the Hollow can absorb without distortion. It does not bring birds. It does not bring sun. It brings, into the body of one of the oldest engines — the kind only the Rustbound tend — a small adjustment. Not a repair. A *kindness*. A bearing that was about to seize, in a year or a decade, will now not seize. A pressure that was building toward catastrophe, slow as geology, has been bled off into nothing. No one will ever know. The Hollow will simply continue, as it had intended to continue, but the catastrophe that was quietly accruing at its heart has been forgiven. The Hollow gets to keep being itself, longer.

**Third**, and this is the most careful thing: it touches each of the Rustbound. Not to heal them — they have chosen, or been chosen, and to undo that would be to insult the gravity of what they are. But it eases, in each of them, the *unnecessary* portion of the pain. The grinding that served no purpose, the inflammations that taught nothing, the small daily cruelties of metal-in-flesh that the Hollow itself had not required but that had accumulated like rust on the soul. These it lifts away. What remains is the true cost of what they are — and the true cost is still considerable, still a price — but they will sleep tonight, those few, with a quietness in their bodies they have not known. They will think it is the weather. They will be wrong, and it will not matter.

**Fourth**, it does something for the Grimekin that is almost a joke, almost a small loving conspiracy. It does not give them more. It gives them — once, only once, and only for the space of a single shared evening — the awareness that what they have is *enough*. That the geared cycle of their lives is not a lesser version of some brighter life happening elsewhere. That their patchwork and their soot and their murmured conversations with the engines are not the residue of a failed world but the substance of a real one. They will not be able to articulate this. They will find themselves, that evening, lingering a little longer at the small hydroponic troughs. Touching each other's shoulders in passing. One old Grimekin will laugh, and not know why, and the laugh will be picked up by another, and the sound will move through the lofts like a warm current through cold water, and then it will pass, and they will go to sleep, and in the morning it will be only a feeling that something good has been near.

**Fifth**, and last, the power does the hardest thing benevolence is ever asked to do: it leaves.

It leaves because to remain would be to become a feature of the place, and the place has its features already, and they are sufficient. It leaves because love that overstays becomes ownership. It leaves because the Hollow's dignity lies in continuing under its own terms, and any permanent presence — however kind — would be a kind of conquest, even if the conquest were only of meaning.

But before it goes, it makes one promise, and the promise is not delivered in words because the Hollow does not run on words. It is delivered in the faint, almost-not-there warming of a single drop of water hanging from a sagging roof — a drop that catches a light no lamp is casting, holds it for the space of one human heartbeat, and then falls, ordinary again, into a pool below.

The promise is this: *You are known. You will be known again. When the long erosion finally finishes its work and the Hollow at last lets go, none of this will have been unwitnessed. Not the Grimekin's murmured prayers to their bolts. Not the Siltborn's hum in the dark water. Not the Rustbound's terrible patient love for the machines that are eating them. None of it. All of it kept.*

And then the power is gone, in the way it came — not departing so much as ceasing to be locally concentrated — and the Hollow clanks on, warm and wet and loud, and a Siltborn somewhere lowers itself into a pool with an expression that, if it had a face built for such things, might be called peace.

The mist closes. The water continues, in places, to flow upward, for reasons sufficient unto itself.

Nothing has changed.

Everything has.
