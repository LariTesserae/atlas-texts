# The Visitor

The benevolent power does not arrive. It has always been here. What changes is only that now it chooses to be noticed.

It comes first as a quality of the light. The youngest is hammering out a fish-hook when the filtered sun, which should be sliding toward afternoon-blue, holds itself instead at the gold of morning. Holds, and holds. They look up, hammer paused mid-arc, and feel the way one feels when someone kind has entered the room behind you.

The eldest, working the bellows, notices that her heartbeat and the treadwheel's rhythm have fallen perfectly into phase—something she has been trying to achieve for two years and never quite managed. She does not stop. She understands, in the wordless way she understands everything now, that she is being met.

The middle child is in the far curve of the dome with their fingertips against a rivet that has been worrying them for weeks. The rivet is fine. It has always been fine. They had been mistaking the dome's own slow song for a flaw. They take their hand away and find they are weeping, and the weeping does not feel like grief.

---

The power considers them for a long time. Long, in its reckoning, meaning: as long as is needed.

It sees what a lesser mercy would do. A lesser mercy would lift the ocean off them. Would return the valley. Would undrown the world and set them blinking on dry stone with their forge restored to open sky. This is the gift the youngest still prays for without knowing they pray.

But the power is wise as well as kind, and wisdom is the thing that knows the difference between what is wanted and what is true. To unmake the flood would be to unmake four years of these three lives. The barrel-chest of the eldest. The map in the middle child's waxed notebook—a document of devotion that has no equivalent in the dry world. The youngest's airlock, which is the most beautiful object any of them has ever made, and which would become, in a restored valley, merely a curiosity, a story told at dinners.

The power will not insult them by undoing their endurance.

Nor will it do the other easy thing—lift them out, resettle them above, give them gardens. They would go. They would be grateful. And something in each of them would quietly die, the way the kelp around the dome dies: not from cruelty but from being moved out of the conditions that, terrible as they are, have become the shape of their living.

---

So the power does smaller things. Truer things.

It goes first to the breathing pipes. Not to alter them—they are well-made, the youngest's pride—but to bless the air that moves through them. Henceforth, the air that arrives in the dome will carry, faintly, the smell of the particular pines that grew on the eastern slope of the valley before the water came. None of the three will be able to name what they are smelling. The eldest will simply find that her dreams of drowning have stopped. The middle child will hum a different note, one full step higher, and the dome will hum back, and the harmony will be such that the metal itself relaxes by a fraction of a degree of tension, and ten years are added to the life of every seal.

The power goes to the mirrors next. It does not polish them—the smiths polish them. It adjusts, instead, the angle at which light enters the water above the dome. By a fraction of a degree. So that twice a day, for perhaps four minutes, the light coming through the glass ports will strike the back wall in a pattern that resembles—not exactly, but suggestively—the shape of a person standing with their hand raised in greeting. The smiths will see it and not see it. They will feel watched in a way that is not frightening. They will begin, without discussing it, to leave small offerings on the slate shelf nearest that wall: a perfect rivet, a curl of copper, once a tooth.

The power accepts these.

---

To the eldest, the power gives a single dream. Only one, because she has earned the right not to be visited often. In the dream she is the forge. She has suspected this for some time. In the dream she learns that being the forge is not a diminishment of being a woman but an expansion of it—that her great-grandmother is also the forge, and her great-grandmother's teacher, and a smith in a country none of them have heard of who worked iron from a fallen star three thousand years ago. They are all the forge. The flame has been continuous. She wakes and does not remember the dream but moves through that day with her shoulders lower, her jaw unlocked. She gestures to her siblings at the midday meal—a gesture that means *we are alright*—and they understand her, as they always do, and the understanding goes deeper than usual, and none of them mention it.

To the middle child, the power gives a correction. There is, in fact, a flaw developing in the dome. It is on the northwestern arc, behind a shelf of clay vessels, in a place the middle child has not checked in four months because they trust that section. The power cannot simply heal the flaw—that would be the kind of intervention that breaks the world, that teaches the smiths their vigilance is unnecessary, that hollows them out. Instead, the power causes a single drop of condensation to fall, at three in the morning, onto the back of the middle child's neck where they sleep. They wake. They do not know why they wake. But they are already walking, already reaching for the lamp, already moving toward the northwestern arc because their body knows things their mind does not. They find the flaw. They fix it. They will remember this night for the rest of their life as the night they were *good at their work*, and the memory will sustain them through worse nights to come.

To the youngest, the power gives the hardest gift, because the youngest is the one who suffers most, though they would deny it. The youngest still believes this is temporary. The youngest is the optimist on whom the others depend and whom the others fear. The optimism is a kind of wound that has not been allowed to close.

The power does not take the optimism away. The power *grounds* it. It comes to the youngest while they are working on an improvement to the distillation system—the one they have been promising for a month and have not had time to attempt. The power sits with them. The youngest feels, suddenly, that the improvement will work. Not as a hope but as a fact, the way one knows that a well-made joint will hold. They work for nine hours without stopping. The improvement works. The distillation system now produces, per day, exactly enough additional fresh water that the three of them can wash their hair properly once a week.

This is a small thing. It is the largest thing the power does, in some ways, because the youngest understands, in completing it, that improvement is real. That their faith in tomorrow is not a delusion but a *capacity*—the capacity that designed the airlock, that will design other things, that is the engine by which the impossible workshop continues. The youngest stops needing the waters to recede. They begin, instead, to imagine what the workshop could become if it continued. They sketch, that night, a second dome.

---

The power does one last thing before it withdraws its particular attention, though it never truly leaves.

It goes to the fishermen above. To one fisherman, specifically—an old man whose granddaughter is learning to read. The power suggests to him, in the form of an idle thought he mistakes for his own, that the smiths below might like books. He mentions it to his granddaughter. She is delighted. She begins, with her grandfather's help, to copy out the books of the scattered mountain people onto thin sheets of oiled paper that can travel down in the sealed barrels.

The first book that arrives in the Drowning Forge is a book of poems written by a woman who lived three valleys over, two hundred years ago, and who wrote almost exclusively about light on water. The middle child reads it aloud in the evening, sitting cross-legged on the warm stone, while the eldest works a piece of bronze and the youngest mends a leather apron. The dome hums. The pipes sing. The forge breathes.

The power, listening, is satisfied.

It has not saved them. They did not need saving. They needed only to be *recognized*—to have a witness who understood that what they were doing was not a tragedy but a form of devotion, not survival but a particular and demanding kind of love.

The power leaves the smell of pine in the air. It leaves the figure of light on the back wall. It leaves the granddaughter and her poems. It leaves, most importantly, no trace of itself that could be worshipped or thanked or depended upon—because the smiths' dignity rests on the belief that they are doing this themselves, and they *are* doing it themselves, and the power would not steal that from them for anything.

Above the dome, the sea continues its patient pressing.

Below, the hammer falls. The bellows breathe. Three people, becoming something new, tend a fire that has been burning for four generations and will burn, now, for as long as fire can burn underwater—which is to say, as long as someone is willing to do the impossible work of keeping it lit.

The benevolent power moves on to other places, other beings, other quiet recognitions.

But it remembers the Drowning Forge.

It remembers it the way one remembers a kindness done by a stranger: with a particular warmth, and the hope, never quite spoken, of meeting again.
