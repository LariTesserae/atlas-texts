# The Visitation

The benevolent power does not arrive. It was already there — has always been, in the way deep attention is always already there once it turns toward a thing. What changes is only its orientation, the way a face turns in a crowd.

It considers the basin for a long time. Not the time of the Wardens, which is centuries, but a longer time still — the time it takes for a thought to fully form in something vast. It watches the pump labor. It watches a Warden, motionless for three days, observing the slow bloom of rust around a single rivet. It watches the moss creep, get burned, creep again. It watches one of the great ferns lay down another half-millimeter of calcite at its base, building its island toward some completion neither it nor anything else will witness.

And the power, which is wise, does not do the first thing that would occur to a lesser kindness.

It does not drain the basin. It does not lift the hamlet onto dry ground, set it on a sunny hill, give the Wardens green fields and singing birds and a sky that is blue. It sees, almost immediately, that this would be a kind of murder. The Wardens are not trapped in the basin the way a man is trapped in a flooded mine. They are the basin's argument with itself. Remove the water and you remove the Wardens; what you would set on the hill would be empty armatures of pipe, and the hill would be a grave.

It does not repair the pump. It sees that the pump's laboring is not a problem to be solved but a heartbeat. To give the Wardens a pump that never falters would be to give them a heart that never beats — to end the very vigilance that constitutes them. Their love (and the power, being wise, recognizes it as love, though the Wardens have no word for it and would not claim it if they did) is expressed entirely in the attending. Remove the thing that requires attending and you have removed the love.

It does not give them company. No birds, no insects, no animal kin. It sees that the silence-where-animal-life-should-be is not a wound. The Wardens have never heard a bird and do not miss one. To fill their air with song would be to fill a contemplative's cell with a brass band and call it generosity.

It does not even tell them they are seen. It sees that being unwitnessed is part of their dignity. A Warden who knew itself observed by a benevolent power might begin, however slightly, to perform — and the perfect economy of their motion, every gesture necessary, every gesture sufficient, would acquire its first wasted increment. The power will not be the thing that introduces waste into that place.

So it considers, for what in its own reckoning is a long while, doing nothing at all. And nothing at all is a real option, and a good one, and the power holds it seriously in mind. Many of its visitations end this way, with a long look and a withdrawal, and these are not failures. They are a form of respect so complete it leaves no trace.

But.

There is something. The power notices it on what would be, by human reckoning, the fourth day of its attention. One of the Wardens — and they are not really individuals, but this one is locally distinct — has been standing at the western edge of the walkway for some weeks. It is not maintaining anything. It is not communicating. Its moss-lights are pulsing slightly out of sync with the pump, which is the closest thing in this place to a private thought. It is looking at one of the iridescent pools.

In the pool, the dim greenish sky is reflected, and the underside of a fern frond, and the Warden's own face — the smooth peat-plate, the two chartreuse pools where eyes would be. The Warden has, perhaps, never seen itself before. Or has seen itself a thousand times and is only now, for reasons internal to the long slow weather of its being, registering the seeing.

The power watches this for a while.

And then it does the one small thing it decides to do.

It does not give the Warden self-knowledge, which the Warden has not asked for and might not survive. It does not give the pool clarity, or the face beauty, or the moment meaning. It simply — and this is the most delicate operation the power performs in any of its visitations, the one its lesser kin most often botch — it simply ensures that the moment is *allowed to complete itself*. That no drip falls into the pool to break the reflection before the Warden is finished looking. That the pump does not stutter and call the Warden away. That a piece of the carapace, which had been about to flake from the Warden's shoulder, holds for another few minutes. The power makes a small, local stillness — not by adding anything, but by very gently holding back the things that would have interrupted.

The Warden looks. The reflection holds. Whatever passes between the Warden and its own dim green-lit face passes, and is neither witnessed nor recorded, not even by the power, which looks politely away during the actual transaction. When it is done, the Warden turns, slowly, water sighing from its hip-joint, and goes back to its rivet.

The pump thumps. The drips resume their percussion. The held breath of the basin is released, and nothing in the basin knows it was ever held.

The power, satisfied — though satisfaction is too small a word, it is something more like *having loved correctly* — withdraws its particular attention. It leaves behind no gift, no mark, no improvement. It leaves the Wardens to their slow losing war, which is also their life, which is also, seen rightly, a form of devotion so pure that the power, for all its wisdom, learned something from watching it.

This is what the benevolent and wise do, when they are truly both.

They give the smallest possible thing.

They give it without being seen giving it.

And then they go, carrying with them a new understanding of patience, which they did not have before, and which they could not have learned anywhere else.
