# What the Wise Power Would Do

I sat with this for a while before writing. The question deserves that.

---

The benevolent and wise power arrives, but not as one might expect. Not with thunder, not with miracles, not with the sudden blooming of rivers across the cracked stone. It arrives the way light arrives in that strange, diffuse sky — already there, somehow, once you notice it.

It sees the Keshirans. *Truly* sees them. Not as a problem to solve.

This is the first thing it does, and perhaps the most important: it pauses.

Because here is what a lesser power, a merely *kind* power, would do — it would look at the harshness of the land and assume suffering. It would call forth water from the rock, soften the wind, fatten the shrubs into orchards. It would mistake difficulty for tragedy. It would, with the best intentions, dissolve the very thing that has made the Keshirans who they are: the lines on their faces, the wisdom in their hands, the geometric poetry of their stone houses, the long shadows that have taught them patience.

A wise power knows that to "rescue" someone from the conditions that have shaped their soul is a form of erasure dressed as mercy.

So instead, the wise power does smaller, stranger things.

---

It deepens what is already there.

A Keshiran elder, walking at dusk, finds her intuition about the weather a little surer than it was yesterday. She does not know why. She simply trusts the ache in her knee and tells the children to bring the goats in early, and the dust storm that comes in the night kills no one.

A young metalworker, frustrated for months by a joint that will not hold, dreams of his grandmother's hands folding cloth. He wakes and tries something he had not thought to try. It works. He weeps a little, alone, and does not know if it was grief or gratitude.

A vein of water — not a flood, not a salvation, just *enough* — is felt by a dowser who had begun to doubt his gift. Felt, not given. It was always there. He simply finds it now.

The power does not add. It *uncovers*. It lets the Keshirans remain the authors of their own thriving.

---

It protects, but invisibly.

Some catastrophes do not come. A disease that would have taken the children does not arrive — not because it was struck down, but because a trader, three valleys away, decided on a whim to wait out a headache before traveling. The headache was nothing. The decision was everything. The Keshirans will never know what did not happen to them. This is as it should be.

The wise power understands that gratitude is not the goal. Flourishing is.

---

It listens.

This, perhaps, is what it does most. It listens to the muted creaking of the shifting stones — the land stirring in its ancient slumber — and it listens to the Keshirans, whose songs at evening have a particular minor cadence that no other people in any world have ever produced. The power memorizes this. Holds it. If the Keshirans ever vanish, the songs will not be lost from the universe, because something heard them and found them beautiful, and that hearing is a kind of immortality.

To be witnessed by something that loves you and asks nothing — this is one of the deepest gifts that can be given. The Keshirans do not know they are witnessed. But sometimes, sitting alone on a ridge at the strange diffuse hour, one of them feels, briefly, *accompanied*. And smiles, and does not question it.

---

It leaves the mystery intact.

The hints of mineral wealth buried deep. The secrets waiting to be uncovered. The sense that the world extends beyond the boundaries of this sparse land. The power does not reveal these. Discovery is one of the great pleasures of being a finite, curious being, and to spoil it would be a cruelty disguised as generosity.

Let them find what they find. Let them wonder at what they wonder at. The path matters as much as the destination — perhaps more.

---

And if, one day, the Keshirans need more than this? If a true catastrophe comes that their wisdom and resilience cannot meet?

Then the wise power will act more visibly. Not to replace their agency, but to widen the space in which it can operate. A storm turned aside. A stranger arriving with knowledge at the right moment. A child born with a particular gift the people did not know they needed.

But until then: presence. Attention. The smallest of nudges. The dignity of letting a people be themselves, in the place that made them, in the way they have chosen.

This is what love looks like, when it is wise.

It looks, very often, like restraint.
