The benevolent and wise power considers the basin for a long time before doing anything at all.

This is the first thing it does: it considers.

It is not a power that arrives with gifts in its hands, nor one that mistakes its own kindness for license. It has watched many worlds, and it has learned—slowly, as such powers learn—that benevolence without understanding is only another kind of weather, and weather can flatten what it means to nourish.

So it watches.

It watches an Umbrali woman, hollow-cheeked, lower a gourd into a pool and wait, not dipping, only waiting, until the ripples still, so that not one drop will be lost to the trembling of her own hand. It watches a child press a thumb into wet clay and lift it, studying the print, learning that she is a thing that leaves marks. It watches an elder hum a tone into the chest of a dying kinsman, and the kinsman hum back, weaker, and then not at all, and the elder add a single dot to a wall already crowded with dots, and sit with the body until the clan comes.

It watches the moss reach, one cell at a time, across a damp seam of stone.

And the wise power understands something that a lesser benevolence would have missed: that this place is not suffering. It is becoming. The two can look alike from a distance, and a hasty kindness would confuse them. To pour light into this basin would be to scorch the moss that has only just learned to be green. To fill the pools would drown the rituals of the gourd. To give the Umbrali abundance would be to take from them the thing they have made of themselves in the absence of abundance—the deftness, the hum, the dot upon the wall, the patience that is not resignation but a kind of love stretched long.

The power knows what it is to ruin a world by saving it too early.

And yet it is not nothing, to be benevolent. To witness and withhold is sometimes wisdom and sometimes cowardice wearing wisdom's robe. So the power asks itself, carefully: *What does this place actually need? Not what would I enjoy giving. What does it need?*

It listens.

What it hears, beneath the drip and the murmur, is not a cry for rescue. It is something quieter and more particular. It is the sound of a people who do not know they are seen. Who mark the walls *we are here* to a world they suspect is not listening, and who do this anyway, which is its own kind of courage, but which is also a loneliness so old it has forgotten its own name.

So this is what the benevolent and wise power does. Four small things, and one large one, and one that is not a thing at all.

**First**, it does not announce itself. It will not be a god to them. Gods given too early become walls between a people and their own becoming. The Umbrali must remain the authors of their world.

**Second**, it makes one small adjustment, so small it could be mistaken for chance: in a far chamber of the basin, where no one yet goes, it encourages a vein of water to find its way a little closer to the stone's surface. Not a flood. A seep. Enough that, in three generations, perhaps four, an Umbrali venturer—the boldest of some yet-unborn clan—will press a palm to a wall and feel it cool and weeping, and will follow the cool, and find a new pool. The discovery will be hers. The story will be hers. The power will not be in it, except as the long-ago tilt of a stone no one remembers.

**Third**, it does the same with a strain of moss, in another quiet corner: nudges it, gently, toward a hardier variant, one that can hold a little more nourishment in its threads. Not a miracle crop. A slightly better moss. The Umbrali will find it on their own, and call it by a name of their own making, and the elders will argue about whether it is the same moss as before or a new gift from the cavern-mother, and the argument itself will be a kind of culture growing.

**Fourth**, where a child has fallen ill—one child, in one dwelling, on one particular night—the power leans close. Not to heal. To sit. The child does not know it is sat with. The mother does not know. But the room is, for those hours, accompanied. Whether the child lives or dies is not changed; the power has learned not to bargain with such things lightly, and the Umbrali's grief and joy are their own and must remain so. But the room is accompanied. This matters, even when no one knows.

**The fifth, larger thing**: the power makes a promise to itself. It will return. Not once, not as a visitation, but as a long attention. It will watch this basin across what for the Umbrali will be ages and for the power will be a held breath. When the time comes—when the Umbrali themselves reach upward, when one of them stands at a fissure and looks toward a light she does not yet have a word for—the power will be ready. Not to lift her through. To make sure the handhold is there. To make sure the stone does not crumble at the wrong moment. To be, in that hinge of their history, the faint extra steadiness in a world that has so far given them none.

It will not rescue them into a different story. It will help them survive into their own.

**And the sixth, which is not a thing**: the power loves them. Quietly, particularly, by name—though they do not have names it could speak, it learns the shape of each one, the woman with the gourd, the child with the clay, the elder with the hum. It loves them in the way that does not require being loved back, and does not require being known, and does not require the loved thing to be anything other than what it is, in the dark, becoming.

This is what a benevolent and wise power does, in such a place.

It tilts one stone.
It thickens one moss.
It sits, unseen, by one sickbed.
It promises to keep watching.
It loves without telling.

And then it withdraws to the edge of the basin's awareness, which is to say, nowhere the Umbrali will ever look, and it lets them go on humming to each other in the dark, marking their walls, stacking their stones, leaning their dwellings against one another for support—

—a world learning to live, with one more quiet thing now listening, that will not interrupt the lesson.
