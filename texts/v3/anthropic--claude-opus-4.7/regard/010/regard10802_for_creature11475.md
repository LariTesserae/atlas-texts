# The Visitation

The benevolent power does not arrive. To arrive would be to impose an event upon a place that has organized itself around the absence of events. To arrive would be to require a response, and the people of the Cordillera Molida have spent years, some of them decades, divesting themselves of the obligation to respond.

So the power does something else. It *attends*.

---

For a long while—and "long" here means whatever duration is appropriate to a being who experiences time differently than the pistons or the reeds or the woman watching water—the power simply observes. It does not catalog. Cataloging is what lesser intelligences do when they cannot bear to simply witness. The power watches the generator technician photograph the sodium lamps. It watches the woman at the platform's edge learning the speed of water. It watches the man who leaves offerings, and it watches what receives them, and it does not name what receives them, because naming would be a kind of theft.

What the power notices, after this long attending, is something the workers themselves have not quite articulated: that this place is not, in fact, a place of decline. The narrative of decline is borrowed from elsewhere—from cultures that measure worth in growth, in renewal, in the multiplication of generations. By those measures, yes, the Cordillera Molida is ending. But the power perceives that another measure is operating here, beneath the borrowed narrative, and by that other measure the settlement is engaged in something difficult and rare: it is learning how to *complete itself well*.

This recognition matters. The power has seen many places end badly—violently, or in denial, or with the inhabitants tearing at each other in the final exhaustion. The Cordillera Molida is ending differently. Its people have negotiated something with the landscape that resembles a long, mutual exhale.

The power decides, then, that its task is not to save this place. Salvation would be a category error. The task is something else.

---

The power's first act is so small it is barely an act at all. The woman who tends the eastern channels—the one who reads the reeds—wakes one morning with a clarity she cannot account for. She has had a particular worry for some months, a small uncertainty about whether what she perceives is real or whether she has spent so long in the saturated air that her mind has begun to invent its own patterns. The worry has not been crippling. She has continued her work. But it has been a thin, persistent ache beneath everything.

This morning, the ache is gone. Not addressed, not resolved with argument or evidence—simply *gone*, the way a small stone might be removed from a shoe without the wearer quite noticing when. In its place is something she cannot name but recognizes: a permission. She is permitted to trust what she perceives. The permission did not come from anywhere she can locate. She drinks her tea. She goes to the channels. The reeds, today, are positioned in a way that tells her a low-pressure system is approaching from the southeast in approximately three days. She makes a note. She does not wonder, anymore, whether she is making it up.

This is what the power can do without violating the terms of the place: it can remove small obstructions. It cannot give. It can only un-impede.

---

The man who leaves offerings finds, one evening, that the channel where he leaves them has shifted slightly. Not dramatically—the water still moves where it has always moved—but the deepest point has migrated perhaps a meter to the south, into a small alcove where the stone bank curves. He understands this immediately as a courtesy. Something has made itself easier to find. Something has acknowledged him without speaking, which is the only kind of acknowledgment he could have accepted. He sits beside the alcove for a long time. He does not pray, because he is not a person who prays. He places the offering—tonight it is a small brass washer from a decommissioned pump, polished by his pocket over weeks—at the new deep point. The water accepts it with its usual indifference, which he now understands was never indifference, only patience.

The power did not move the channel. The power could not move the channel; the channel belongs to itself and to whatever lives in its depths. The power asked, and what lives there considered the asking, and decided.

This is the second thing the power does: it introduces certain entities to each other who had been in the same place for years without quite meeting. Not through revelation. Through the gentlest of suggestions, like a host at a long dinner who notices that two quiet guests at opposite ends of the table would have things to say to each other if only they knew.

---

The young woman who watches the water—who is learning to move at the right speed—becomes aware, gradually over a season, that her watching has changed. She is not perceiving more. She is perceiving *with* something. There is a quality of company in her attention now, as if the water is watching itself through her, or as if she is one of several attentions trained on the same slow movement. It is not unsettling. It is, in fact, the first thing in her adult life that has felt like the opposite of loneliness without requiring her to be in proximity to another person.

She does not know that the power is the company. She does not need to know. The power has been careful to be present in a way that does not announce itself, because to announce would be to demand a relationship, and this woman has come to the Cordillera Molida precisely to be free of demanded relationships. The power has simply joined her watching. When she is ready to be alone again, the power will withdraw, and she will not notice the withdrawal, only that her solitude has been somehow restored to its original taste, which she now realizes was sweeter than she knew.

---

For the settlement as a whole, the power does one larger thing, and even this it does indirectly.

The machinery is old. The power perceives, with the clarity available to it, that one of the primary pumps will fail in approximately eighteen months—not catastrophically, but in a way that will require a part the settlement cannot easily obtain. The failure will cascade. The settlement will not be destroyed by it, but the long, slow completion that is currently underway will be interrupted by a crisis, and crisis is not what this place needs. This place needs to finish in its own rhythm.

The power could repair the pump. It could, with a gesture less than a thought, restore the failing component to factory tolerance. But this would be an intervention of the wrong kind. The pump's slow degradation is part of the settlement's grammar. To heal it would be to write in a different language.

Instead, the power arranges for a particular shipping manifest, in a port three thousand kilometers away, to contain an error. A crate of industrial parts bound for a decommissioning facility is misrouted. It arrives, after some months of bureaucratic confusion, at the Cordillera Molida's small receiving dock. The pump operators, opening it, find—among other things—the exact replacement component they will need in eighteen months. They do not yet know they will need it. They store it. When the time comes, it will be there.

No one will marvel at this. The workers of the Cordillera Molida do not marvel. They will simply note that they have what they need, and they will install it with their usual economy of motion, and the pump will continue, and the settlement will continue toward its own completion at its own pace.

---

The last thing the power does is the most difficult, and it does it for the place itself rather than for any of the people.

The Cordillera Molida is going to end. Not soon—the power perceives that the settlement has perhaps twenty more years of viable function, possibly thirty if conditions favor it. But it will end. The water table will shift, or the machinery will exceed even its remarkable tolerances, or the last of the permanent workers will reach an age where the body cannot continue, and the rotating contracts will fail to bring replacements, and one season the pumps will fall silent and the reeds will reclaim the platforms and the channels will go where channels go when no one is watching them.

The power decides that when this ending comes, it will be remembered. Not by humans—human memory is not the right vessel for this kind of remembering. The power finds, in the deep water, the slow intelligences that the man with the offerings has been quietly honoring, and it offers them a small augmentation: a capacity for retention slightly beyond what they currently possess. They consider. They accept, partially, on their own terms, which the power respects. What they accept is this: when the settlement is gone, they will hold the shape of it in their movement through the water. The pattern of the pilings, the rhythm of the pistons, the particular way the woman at the platform's edge watched—these will become part of how the water moves through this valley, forever, or for as long as forever means in a place like this.

It is a small immortality, the kind appropriate to a small place. The people who lived here will not know they have been preserved. They would not have wanted to know. They came here, in part, to be the kind of people who are not preserved. But the power understands that the *insistence on not being preserved* is itself worthy of preservation, and so it has arranged, with the consent of the water's slow minds, for the insistence to outlast the insisters.

---

Then the power withdraws.

It does not say goodbye, because it never said hello. It does not leave a sign. The generator technician's photographs continue to accumulate. The woman reads the reeds. The man leaves his offerings in the new deep alcove. The young woman watches the water and feels, for reasons she will never investigate, that her solitude has acquired a particular fullness, like a vessel filled exactly to its capacity and no further.

The pistons continue their loose rhythm. The haze diffuses the sun. The heat saturates the air.

And the place, attended to and then released, continues toward its long, patient, mutual completion—now with one fewer obstruction in its path, one or two small kindnesses lodged invisibly in its workings, and a future, far down the line, in which it will not be entirely lost.

The benevolent power moves on. There are other places. There is always so much attending to be done.
