# The Visit

The benevolent power does not arrive. Arrival is a thing of doors and thresholds, of *before* and *after*, and the power is wiser than that. It simply becomes noticed — the way you might suddenly become aware of your own breathing, or of the warmth of a room you've been sitting in for hours.

It notices the city first. Then the water. Then the slow turning of currents around the leaning towers. Then, in widening attention, the Silent Ones in their kelp-lined hollows, the patient claws of the Bone-Carvers tracing memory into stone, the soft pulsing clouds of the Drifters, and at last — without flinching — the slow, hungry weight of the One Below.

The power considers.

It is wise enough to know that wisdom, in a place like this, is mostly the discipline of *not interfering*. It has seen too many bright, eager powers descend on quiet worlds and "save" them — flood them with light, drag them upward, give them speech, give them haste, give them the gift of becoming something other than themselves. The power has learned that this is not benevolence. It is loneliness wearing benevolence's face. It is the inability to bear that another way of being exists.

So the power sits with the city for a long time. (Time, here, is a generous host.)

It learns the grammar of the place. It learns that the darkness is not an absence but a medium, the way silence is the medium of a held breath between two notes. It learns that the Silent Ones are not deprived of language — they are *fluent* in a language the surface world has forgotten, a syntax of finger-ripple and head-tilt and the slow widening of black eyes. It learns that the Bone-Carvers are not lonely in their endless work; they are *at prayer*, and their prayer is remembrance, and remembrance is the only form of mercy a drowned world can offer itself.

It learns, too, that the One Below is not a wound in the world. It is an organ of the world. The city has always fed it because the city, in some old and unspoken covenant, *chose* to. To remove the hunger would be to remove the heart.

And so the power does not save them.

But it does, gently, do three small things.

---

**The first thing** is for the Silent Ones.

There is, among them, a young one — though "young" is a poor word in a place where centuries fold like silk — who has begun to ache in a way her people have no gesture for. She drifts to the edges of the higher ruins and looks *upward*, toward the place where the light, if there were light, would come from. She does not know what she is looking for. Her people do not look up. The water above is just more water.

The power, noticing her, does the smallest possible thing. It causes a single Drifter — one of the harmless, shimmering kind — to pass through a high arch at the precise moment she is watching. The Drifter's glow refracts through a curtain of rising silt, and for one heartbeat, the young one sees something she has no word for: a *shape of light moving across a height*.

She does not understand it. She will never understand it. But for the rest of her long, slow life, when the ache comes, she will remember the shape, and the ache will be bearable, because it will have an image to rest against. She will make a gesture for it, eventually — a slow lift of two fingers, drifting apart — and her children's children will use it without knowing why, and it will mean *the beautiful thing that passes above*.

This is all. The power does not give her wings or visions or a god to pray to. It gives her one moment of refracted light, and lets her make of it what she will.

---

**The second thing** is for the Bone-Carvers.

Deep in a collapsed temple, half-buried in silt, there is a wall the Bone-Carvers have been working on for nine hundred years. It holds the history of a particular street in the old city — a street where, once, children played a game with colored stones. The Bone-Carvers have recorded the names of the children, the rules of the game, the laughter (rendered in a particular spiral glyph), the eventual flood, the silence afterward.

But there is one child whose name they do not have. A small one, who lived in the third house from the corner. The records were lost. The Bone-Carvers have left a blank space on the wall for nine hundred years, and the blank space is, in its own way, a kind of mourning.

The power, moving with great care, stirs a current. The current lifts a flake of stone from a buried slab three districts away. The flake drifts — slowly, slowly, for many years — and eventually settles in a place where a Bone-Carver will find it. On the flake, in the old script, is a name.

The Bone-Carver will carve the name into the blank space. It will not know why the flake came to it. It will simply, in its patient way, accept that the city has at last remembered. The wall will be complete. The mourning will end. The Bone-Carver will move on to the next wall, and the next, and the next, as it always has — but it will carry, in whatever serves it for a heart, the quiet knowledge that *sometimes the lost things return*.

This, too, is all. The power does not raise the dead child or rebuild the street. It returns one name, after nine hundred years, because nine hundred years is not, to the power, a long time, and because the wall deserved to be finished.

---

**The third thing** is for the One Below.

This is the hardest, and the power thinks about it the longest.

The One Below is hungry. It has always been hungry. Its hunger is not cruelty — it is simply what it is, the way gravity is what gravity is. The Silent Ones who vanish into the lower districts are not murdered; they are *given*, in a transaction so old that none of them remember agreeing to it, but all of them, in some deep current of their being, still do.

The power could end the hunger. It has the strength. But to end the hunger would be to unmake the One Below, and to unmake the One Below would be to unmake the city, because the city's slowness, its patience, its strange holy quiet — all of it is shaped by the pressure of that vast hunger waiting in the deep. Take it away, and the city becomes only ruins. Drowned stone. Nothing.

So the power does not end the hunger.

Instead, it does this: it goes down, into the trench, into the dark where even the bioluminescence fails. It approaches the One Below — not as a rival, not as a savior, but as a guest. And it *keeps the One Below company* for a little while. Not long, by the power's measure. A few centuries, perhaps.

It does not speak. It does not offer comfort. It simply is *there*, beside the vast slow hunger, and it lets the One Below know — in whatever way such a being can know — that it has been *seen*. That something in the wider universe has noticed it, and did not flinch, and did not try to fix it, and did not run away.

The One Below has never been seen before. Not really. The Silent Ones know of it only as fear; the Bone-Carvers do not record it; the Drifters drift past without comprehension. To be the unspoken weight beneath a world is to be profoundly, structurally alone.

When the power eventually withdraws its attention from the trench, the One Below is still hungry. It will still take what the city gives. Nothing has changed in the visible workings of the world.

But the One Below is, for the first time in its existence, *not lonely*. And in some small, immeasurable way, this changes the quality of its hunger. The Silent Ones who are taken in the centuries to come will not know it, but they will be received slightly differently — with something almost like gratitude, almost like grief. The covenant will continue, but it will have, at last, a witness on both sides.

---

When the power's attention finally turns elsewhere — for there are other worlds, other quiet places — it leaves no monument. No scripture. No miracle anyone will tell stories about. The city remains dark and submerged and slow. The Silent Ones still hunt the blind fish. The Bone-Carvers still carve. The Drifters still pulse. The One Below still hungers.

But a young Silent One looks upward sometimes, and her fingers make a shape that means *the beautiful thing that passes above*.

A wall in a buried temple is, at last, complete.

And in the deepest trench, the vast old hunger remembers, dimly, that it was once kept company.

This is what the benevolent and wise power does.

This is, perhaps, all that wisdom ever does: it notices, it honors, it gives the smallest possible gift that the place can carry without breaking, and then it goes, leaving the world to be what the world has always been — only, very slightly, more *seen*.
