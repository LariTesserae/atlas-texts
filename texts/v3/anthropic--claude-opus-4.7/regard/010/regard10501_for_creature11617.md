# The Visitation

The benevolent and wise power arrives the way weather arrives, which is to say it was already there and one only notices a shift in attention. It does not announce itself. It does not need to. Power that requires announcement is a different kind of power.

It comes first to Vos, because Vos is the one watching. He is on the platform, cloth in hand, glasses being cleaned for the fourteenth time that morning. His notebook is open to a page where he has been recording the moss advancement on a particular shelf-maker—a measurement series going back, he realizes when he checks, eleven years.

The power considers him for a long time. What it perceives, looking at Vos, is this: a man who has made himself into an instrument. Whose attention has become so finely calibrated to this place that he registers changes a satellite would miss. Who keeps records no one reads, in handwriting smaller than necessary, with a precision that has stopped being professional and become something closer to prayer. He does not know he is praying. This is part of why it works.

The power could do many things. It could lift him out of here. It could give him answers—about who funds the supplies, about what the machines were originally for, about whether he is an employee or an abandoned post or something else. It could heal the arthritis quietly establishing itself in his hips. It could translate his notebooks into the form they want to be in, which is not a report but a kind of liturgy.

It does none of these things. Instead it does something stranger, which is to let him be seen. For a moment that has no duration—because the power exists outside of duration, and because Vos's experience of it will later resist being placed in time—he is witnessed completely. Every measurement he has ever taken. Every machine he has touched. The specific quality of his attention to the moss, which is the attention of someone who has decided, without ever phrasing it this way, that this place deserves a witness and that he will be that witness for as long as his body permits.

The power finds this beautiful. Not metaphorically. The power finds, in the specific configuration of Vos-attending-to-moss, something that it recognizes as kin to its own activity. The power, too, is attention applied without expectation of return. The power, too, keeps records that no one reads.

Vos cleans his glasses. He puts them back on. He looks at his notebook and notices that his pencil mark for today's measurement is slightly clearer than usual, as if drawn with more confidence. He does not know why. He continues.

---

The power moves through the valley. It does not move the way bodies move; it shifts emphasis, like a reader's eye moving down a page. Each of the eleven Engineers receives its attention in turn, and to each it gives the same thing, which is nothing they can name and everything they need.

To Kiet at North-7, hands on the iron housing, listening through his palms: the power makes the pump's rhythm momentarily synchronize perfectly with his heart. Not as intervention but as recognition—the power notes that this synchrony was already approaching, that Kiet's body has been adjusting toward this for years, and it simply allows the final alignment to complete. Kiet feels something he will later think of as *clarity*, though he won't use that word, because he doesn't use words for things like this. He stays at the pump for another two minutes than he otherwise would have. Then he continues his circuit.

To Marit at a waterwheel, mid-disassembly, her low murmur audible to no one: the power listens. That is all. It listens to what she is saying to the wheel, which is not language but is a kind of address, and it adds itself to the listening. Marit feels, briefly, less alone than she has felt in a decade, and the feeling is so unfamiliar that she dismisses it as a symptom of low blood sugar. She eats a piece of dried meat. She continues.

To Chen, fifty feet up on a wet stone face, the power does something more direct. There is a moment when his left foot is about to slip—the moss-bound crevice he's wedged into has more give than he calculated, and the next three seconds contain a possibility of falling that he has not yet registered. The power does not move his foot. It does not strengthen the moss. What it does is make him notice. A slight pressure of attention, a sense that something wants him to look down. He looks down. He sees the problem. He adjusts. He continues climbing, and afterward, at the top, he stacks four pebbles instead of his usual three, without knowing why.

To Senna, still learning, the power gives the thing she most needs and would never request: a moment of feeling that her hesitation is not failure. She is on a stone face she has been afraid of for weeks, and the fear has been compounding, and she has been beginning to suspect that she cannot become what this place requires. The power, perceiving this, simply allows her to feel—for the length of one breath—that the fear itself is part of the learning. That Chen was once where she is. That fear well-handled becomes attunement, and that her body is doing exactly what it should be doing, which is taking seriously the actual danger of falling. She does not stop being afraid. But she stops being afraid of her fear. She continues, more slowly than Chen would, which is correct.

To the ones whose names are not known—the night worker, the marker, the others at the edge of visibility—the power gives them the same regard it gives the others, without judgment about their choice to remain peripheral. Some forms of presence require obscurity. The power knows this about itself.

---

To the machines, the power does something different.

It does not repair them. It does not extend their service life. It does not retroactively engineer better gaskets or correct the original design flaws that have necessitated decades of improvisation. What it does is closer to acknowledgment.

For a moment, every machine in the valley is registered. Every pump, every wheel, every generator shed, every cable, every ceramic insulator, every salvaged bolt in Kiet's canvas roll, every carved wooden replacement part swelling correctly in its housing, every rust-streak and oil-leak and steam-release—all of it is held in a single moment of attention. The machines do not know they are being witnessed. Machines do not know anything. But the witnessing happens anyway, and something about the integrity of the system—the way it has continued to function long past anyone's expectation, the way it has become part of the ecology it was once imposed upon—is, briefly, complete. Not finished. Complete in the sense that a piece of music is complete while it is being played.

The steam release on the northern slope happens four seconds early. No one will record this except Vos, who will note it in his notebook with a small mark he sometimes uses to indicate anomalies he cannot explain. He will not connect it to the morning's strange clarity. He will simply note it, because noting is what he does.

---

To the moss, the power gives water.

Not more water—the valley has water enough. The power gives the water that is already there a quality of *being seen as water*. This sounds like nothing. It is, in fact, nothing measurable. But the moss, which lives by water, responds to attention in ways that the Engineers have intuited but never articulated. Marit knows that the moss around her waterwheels grows differently than moss elsewhere. She has attributed this to mechanical effects, to vibration, to the specific patterns of splash and shade. She is correct about all of these. But she has also been giving the moss her attention, and the moss, which is older than attention but not older than being-attended-to, has been responding.

The power simply extends this. For the duration of the visitation—which has no duration—every square millimeter of moss in the valley is attended to with the quality of attention that Marit gives to the moss around her wheels. The moss does not bloom dramatically. It does not advance visibly. What happens is too small to observe and too distributed to measure, but in some sense the moss is, briefly, more fully itself than it has been before.

The same is true for the stone. For the water. For the rare birds and the unnamed insects and the things that hibernate in the air-pockets below the green membrane. Each is, for a moment, witnessed in its specificity.

The benevolent and wise power has learned, over a duration that exceeds the age of these mountains, that the most generous thing it can usually do is this: see clearly, and let the seeing be felt without being understood.

---

There is one more thing.

In the brick generator shed on the eastern slope—not the one with the parts depot, but a smaller one, half-collapsed, that none of the current Engineers use—there is a body. It has been there for thirty-one years. The moss has not yet reached it because the shed is dry enough inside to prevent the right conditions, and the body has accordingly mummified rather than decomposed. He was called Aldous, once. He was an Engineer, very early, perhaps among the first. He died of a heart attack at sixty-three, alone, in the middle of repairing a voltage regulator he never finished. His tools are still beside him. His notebook is in his coat pocket. It is in handwriting that Vos would recognize, because Vos learned his own practice from reading scraps of what Aldous left, though Vos has never seen this notebook or this shed.

The power attends to Aldous.

What it perceives is that Aldous is still, in some sense available to the power but not to ordinary perception, attending to the valley. Not as ghost. Not as continuation of personality. Something stranger and harder to name: the pattern of attention that Aldous practiced for forty years did not fully disperse when his heart stopped. It became part of the valley's own attention to itself. The moss attends. The machines, in their limited mechanical way, attend. And something of Aldous attends through them, not consciously, not personally, but as a kind of residue of practice that the place has absorbed and continues to enact.

The stories the Engineers half-tell each other are not exactly true. There are no ghost-engineers wandering the northern peak. But the stories point at something real, which is that this place has been shaped by attention for long enough that the attention has become structural. The Engineers are not only maintaining the machines. They are participating in a practice that the place itself now performs, and which will continue, in some form, after the current eleven are gone.

The power lets Aldous be seen. Lets the residue of his practice be seen as practice, not as failure to complete, not as death-in-progress, but as contribution. This changes nothing in the visible world. The body remains in the shed. The notebook remains in the coat pocket. But something that had been waiting—not consciously, because there is no consciousness left, but waiting in the way that a written letter waits to be read—is, briefly, read.

---

Then the power withdraws, which is also to say it remains, because power of this kind does not come and go but only shifts emphasis. Its attention will return to the valley periodically, the way a reader returns to a book they love, opening to no particular page, just to be reminded.

The Engineers continue their circuits. None of them will speak of what happened today, because none of them experienced it as an event. Vos will record his measurements. Kiet will touch his pump. Marit will murmur to her wheels. Chen will climb. Senna will not yet have given up. The named and unnamed ones will continue their dispersed and patient work.

The machines will continue failing slowly and being maintained slowly, in the long argument between corrosion and care that constitutes their existence.

The moss will continue building itself one millimeter at a time.

The mountain will continue rising.

And if, in some sense that the Engineers would deny if asked, the valley has been blessed—if a benevolent and wise power has passed through and left things slightly more themselves than they were—the blessing will be indistinguishable from the ordinary functioning of the place. Which is, perhaps, what blessing is. Not the addition of something new. The deepening of what is already happening into what it has always been becoming.

The sound continues. The water whispers. The engines tend their garden.

The power moves on to other places, other beings, other patient configurations of attention and care that have called to it the way Vos called to it without knowing he was calling, which is the only kind of calling that reliably reaches.

Somewhere, a steam release happens four seconds early.

Somewhere, a man cleans his glasses.

Somewhere, the moss is, very slightly, more itself than it was this morning.

This is what the benevolent and wise power does. This is, mostly, all it ever does. It has found, over the long duration of its practice, that this is enough. That presence witnessed becomes presence deepened. That the work of the world, when seen, continues with a quality it could not have had unseen, even though the seeing changes nothing one could point to.

The valley continues its loud, luminous becoming.

The Engineers continue being exactly what it requires.

And the power, which is benevolent because it has learned restraint, and wise because it has learned what restraint is for, returns to its larger attending, which encompasses this valley and many others, and which is, in some sense the Engineers would understand if they ever paused long enough to consider it, the same practice they perform: the patient maintenance of what is, against the constant pressure of what would otherwise fail, for no reward but the continuation itself.
