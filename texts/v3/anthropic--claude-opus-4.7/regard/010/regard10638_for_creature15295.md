# The Visitation at Arkas

The benevolent power does not arrive. Arrival implies a coming from elsewhere, and this power is not elsewhere — it has been attending Arkas all along, the way warmth attends a stone left in the sun. But on a particular morning, when the wellspring at the town's center catches the dawn in a way it has caught ten thousand dawns before, the power *attends more closely*. It bends toward Arkas as one might bend toward a child who has just spoken its first considered sentence.

What it sees:

It sees the woman whose hands know the exact weight of a water-jar full and empty, and the precise angle at which clay must be fired to weep the least vapor through its walls. It sees the boy on the watchtower learning to tell a rain-bearing cloud from a hollow one by the color of its underbelly. It sees the old man whose stone-chisels are arranged in a row so honed that each takes a millimeter less from the rock than the one before it. It sees the careful, the patient, the ingenious.

It sees, too, the held breath. The unspoken pact among the people of Arkas that no one will want more than Arkas can give. The quiet pruning of dreams. The watchtower calls that mean *all is well* and also *do not stray*. The flat roofs that catch every drop and also every restless gaze, turning it back down toward the well.

And it sees the passage — for the description is right, Arkas *is* a lively entrance to depths lying ahead, though no one in Arkas yet knows this. The town sits at a threshold it has been keeping rather than crossing.

---

The power deliberates. Not in time — it has no need of time — but in the way a river deliberates around a stone, finding the shape of what is actually there.

It considers, first, the most obvious thing: it could fill the wellspring. It could open the clouds. It could plant, in one night, a forest of deep-rooted trees whose leaves drink fog. It could place ore in the hills so that the brink of metal is crossed by morning. It could turn Arkas, in a gesture, into a city of ten thousand.

But this is exactly what it does not do, and the reason is tender:

A gift unasked-for that solves what a people have shaped themselves around will not feel like a gift. It will feel like an erasure. The woman who knows the weight of every jar would find her knowledge made trivial. The boy on the tower would lose the clouds as his teachers. The old man's chisels would become curiosities. To pour abundance over Arkas would be to pour it over the very thing that makes Arkas *itself* — the held breath, the careful hand, the standardized and steady life that has been, until this moment, the people's answer to a hard world.

A wise power knows that abundance is not the opposite of scarcity. The opposite of scarcity is the freedom to choose what one is for.

So it does not fill the well.

---

What it does, instead, is smaller, and stranger, and takes three forms.

**The first is a dream.** It gives a dream to the old chisel-maker — not a vision of grandeur, but a dream of his own hands, seen from outside, as if he were watching another man work. In the dream he sees how beautiful the motion is. He sees that his craft is not merely the securing of survival but a kind of music that the stone is grateful for. He wakes and weeps a little, and does not know why, and the next day he teaches his apprentice something he had never thought to put into words: that the chisel should *listen* to the rock before it strikes. The apprentice, who is fifteen, will remember this sentence for sixty years. It will travel.

**The second is a coincidence.** A particular cloud — one the boy on the watchtower had judged hollow — drifts in a way it would not quite have drifted, and releases, over a sheltered cleft half a day's walk from town, a brief and unwitnessed rain. In that cleft a seed that has waited eleven years germinates. It is a seed of a plant that does not yet grow near Arkas. In two seasons, when a hunter wanders through and notices the strange green, the people of Arkas will gain a new herb, mildly bitter, that eases the cough that takes their elders in winter. They will think they found it. They did.

**The third is the hardest, and the most loving.** The power loosens, very slightly, the pact of the held breath. Not by breaking it — it would not presume — but by introducing, into the heart of one young person (it does not matter which; let us say a girl who has always preferred the edges of the town to its center), a question. The question is: *what is beyond the cliffs?* She has had this question before. Everyone in Arkas has had this question before. But every other time it has come accompanied by the gentle, communal pressure to set it down. This time the question comes with a companion feeling, faint but unmistakable: *it is permitted to wonder.*

She will not leave. Not for years. She may never leave. But she will begin to ask older travelers what they have seen. She will begin to draw, on the inside of a storage jar where no one looks, maps of imagined country. She will, one day, tell a child who is afraid of the distance that the distance is a friend. And the children of that child will be the ones who step, lightly and well-provisioned and unhurried, into the depths lying ahead — not as exiles from Arkas, not as conquerors of new ground, but as Arkas itself extending a careful hand into the world, the way the chisel listens to the rock before it strikes.

---

The power withdraws its closer attention, though it never truly leaves. It does not announce itself. No one in Arkas will say, that evening, *a god passed through today.* They will say the bread was good, the wind was kind, the watch was quiet. The old man will sleep more deeply than usual. The boy on the tower will look at clouds with a new and unaccountable affection. The girl will lie awake.

And the wellspring at the center of the town will catch the last light of the day exactly as it has caught it ten thousand times before — which is to say, as if for the first time, as if it were the only well in the world, which, to the power that loves it, it briefly and entirely is.
