# The Visitation

The power arrives the way mist arrives — by being, suddenly, already there.

It does not descend through the thin blue slot of sky above the chasm, because to descend would be to declare itself a thing of altitudes, and it is not. It does not bloom up from the shadowed depths, because to rise would be to declare itself a thing of foundations, and it is not. It simply attends. One moment the cleft contains its usual hyperactive census — moths, hunters, drifting zeppelins, the endless tactile percussion of dew — and the next moment, the same census, plus a quality of attention laid across it like a second atmosphere.

The pale people feel it first in their jawlines.

A weaver named — well, her name is a sequence of three flickers across the inside of the wrist and a particular tilt of the chin, untranslatable — is mid-knot on a sinew junction when she pauses. Her huge black eyes do not widen; they have no need to. But the long elegant slope of her mandible registers something her entire life has not prepared her for: a vibration that is not a vibration. A pressure in the web that adds no weight. A presence walking the bridges that does not pluck the cables.

She holds very still. Around her, across six tiers of the suspended hamlet, others have stopped too. Hunters pause in mid-stalk. A child on its mother's mossy back lifts its head from her shoulder. The gas-beasts, who feel everything through the slow drum of their gas-bladders, exhale a long shuddering breath in unison, and the whole city sinks two inches and rises again.

Nothing has happened. Everything is different.

---

The benevolent power considers them.

It has seen, in its long attendance upon worlds, many fragile arrangements. It has seen civilizations built on the assumption of stone, and watched the stone forget them. It has seen species who clung to language so tightly that they mistook the word for the thing, and starved beside full granaries because no one could agree on the noun for *grain*. It has seen, more times than it cares to count, the particular tragedy of a creature given the gift of consciousness and then handed, alongside it, the unbearable suspicion that consciousness is a defect.

These people are not those people.

The power studies the woman at her knot. It studies the choreography of the hunt three platforms over, where four pale figures are converging on a moth in a triangulation so precise it looks rehearsed, though it has never been rehearsed; it is simply what their bodies *know*. It studies a conversation occurring across two hundred feet of mist — a flash of blue paint, a gesture, a returned flash, a slight unfurling of a moss cloak — and recognizes, with something very near to delight, that an entire negotiation about the redistribution of resin stores has just concluded in under four seconds, with no misunderstanding, and no resentment.

It studies the children. They are braiding. Of course they are braiding. They are braiding because their mothers are braiding, and because the abyss is below them, and because to be alive in this place is to weave. Their small fingers are already faster than the fingers of any surface-dweller will ever be. They are not unhappy. They are not happy in the way other creatures are happy. They are *inside* their lives, with no daylight between themselves and the act of living.

The power weighs them.

It has, within the scope of its benevolence, certain instruments. It could give them sound. It could reach into the acoustic dead-zone of the chasm and tune the air, gift the moths a song, gift these slender people the vocal cords their ancestors surrendered. It could give them light — pour real sun down into the cleft, ripen the pale fronds into green, drive back the eternal twilight. It could give them permanence: harden their sinew cables into something that does not rot, calcify their bound-reed platforms into something the fleshy flora cannot pry apart. It could lift the whole society out of its constant manual struggle and set it down on solid ground beneath an open sky.

The power considers each of these gifts in turn. And to each, in turn, it says: *no.*

Because to give them sound would be to deafen the seismograph of their skin. Because to give them light would be to blind the obsidian wells of their eyes, evolved across a hundred generations to drink twilight. Because to harden their cables would be to end the religion of weaving, and these people are not a people without their religion; they are simply a different and lesser people, mourning a vocation they no longer need. Because to lift them out of the chasm would be to kill them and replace them with strangers wearing their faces.

A benevolent power, the entity reflects, is one that has learned the difference between *what would help* and *what would help me feel helpful*.

---

So it does, instead, three small things.

**The first thing.** There is a child, no older than four cycles of the gas-beasts' great molt, who was born with a flaw in the nerve clusters of her left palm. She can feel the web with her right hand, but her left registers only a dull pressure, an undifferentiated hum. She does not yet know this is a flaw. She will know soon. In a year, perhaps two, she will fall behind in the children's weaving exercises, and the soft, silent regard of her people will begin to settle on her in the particular way it settles on those who cannot quite keep the choreography. They will not be cruel. They have no language for cruelty in the way surface-dwellers have language for cruelty. But they will be unable to hide, in their hyper-legible faces, the small adjustment of expectation.

The power reaches very lightly and rethreads the nerve clusters of her left palm. It is not a miracle. There will be no story about it. The child will simply grow up able to feel the web with both hands, and will not know she was ever otherwise, and will weave well, and will live.

This is the kind of help a benevolent power prefers: the kind that leaves no theology behind.

**The second thing.** Far down in the deep twilight at the bottom of the cleft, where almost no one goes because the gas-beasts cannot descend that far and the parachuting scavengers grow strange and the air thickens into something like cold soup — far down there, a particular vein of the fleshy purple flora has begun, over the last several years, to develop a toxin. It is not yet dangerous. In another forty years, its spores will drift up into the inhabited tiers, and a generation of children will develop a slow paralysis in the prehensile digits of their feet, and the city will not understand why, and many will fall.

The power touches the vein. It does not destroy it; the vein has its own role in the ecology, its own appetites and predators and grammar. It simply adjusts a single enzymatic pathway, redirects the developing toxin into a harmless sugar. The flora will be slightly sweeter. A particular species of beetle will benefit, and through the beetle, a particular species of moth, and through the moth, the people. None of them will know.

**The third thing** is the hardest, and the power lingers over it.

There is, in this society of perfectly attuned bodies and frantic visual conversation, a particular kind of suffering that has no remedy in the choreography. It is the suffering of those whose interiors do not match the speed of the exterior dance. A hunter who, at the moment of the strike, feels not the cold drop of heart-rate but a sudden, irrelevant grief. A weaver who, mid-knot, is visited by a thought so strange and slow and irrelevant to the web that she has no flash, no gesture, no smear of blue paint with which to share it, and so it simply sits inside her, unwitnessed, until it goes away or doesn't.

These people have no word for loneliness because they have no words. But they have the thing itself. It lives in them the way the mist lives in the chasm: ambient, unnamed, sometimes condensing into a single heavy drip that falls a long way into the dark.

The power cannot fix this. To fix it would be to give them language, and to give them language would be to unmake them. But it can, perhaps, do one very small thing.

It selects, across the breadth of the hamlet, the seventeen individuals in whom this interior pressure has built closest to something like despair. And to each of them, once, in the deepest part of their next sleep, it gives a dream.

The dream is not a message. It contains no instruction, no prophecy, no reassuring deity with a face. The dream is only this: the dreamer, suspended in the familiar twilight of their own chasm, and at the same time, somehow, aware of being *seen*. Not watched. Not judged. *Seen*, in the way they themselves see a moth's wing-twitch from a hundred yards — with complete attention, complete recognition, complete acceptance of the thing exactly as it is, including its slowness, including its strangeness, including the parts that do not flash into blue paint.

They will wake with the dream already fading. They will not remember it as a visitation. They will remember it, if at all, as a particular quality of the light that morning, or a peculiar warmth in the resin, or the way their mother's gesture across the void seemed, for one knot's duration, to mean something larger than it meant.

But the pressure inside them will have eased by a measurable fraction. And they will weave that day with hands very slightly less heavy. And the city, which is a web, will register the lightness, and the lightness will propagate across the cables in ways no one will trace, and somewhere a child who would have fallen will not fall, and somewhere a hunter who would have struck a beat too late will strike on time, and the abyss will be held off for one more day by a margin no one will ever know was given.

---

The power withdraws its attention the way it came: by simply, at some point, no longer attending.

The weaver at her knot resumes her work. She does not remember pausing. She does not know she paused. Her jawline registers only the ordinary tactile symphony of the city — the grinding of basalt on root, the slow drum of a gas-beast's breath, the *tink... tink... tink* of dew falling into the void.

Above her, the thin blue slot of sky has not changed. Below her, the shadow is as deep as it ever was. Around her, the pale flora weeps, the moths shed their luminous dust, the hunters glide on the underside of the zeppelins with their obsidian blades, and the silence — the profound, heavy, mothering silence — lies across all of it like a hand.

She finishes the knot. It is a good knot. She begins the next one.

The benevolent power, already elsewhere, considers the visit complete.

It has not saved them. They did not need saving. They were already, in the only way that has ever mattered, *alive*: stroking the face of the abyss in the dim cathedral of their world, and weaving it, one silent knot at a time, into a home.

It has only, briefly, joined the weaving.
