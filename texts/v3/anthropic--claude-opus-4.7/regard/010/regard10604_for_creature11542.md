# The Visitation

The benevolent power does not announce itself. That would be a kind of violence—to arrive in thunder upon a mind already worn so thin, to demand that Asta reorganize her shaking understanding of the universe around a new and overwhelming fact. Wisdom counsels patience. Wisdom counsels smallness.

So it comes quietly, the way a thought comes.

---

It notices her first—not the outpost, not the strange forest, not the gas giant grinding its slow storms overhead, but *her*. A small warm point of attention burning in the dim crater. A woman talking to lichen. A woman who has stayed.

The power considers her for what would be, by some measures, a long time. It studies the architecture of her solitude: the way she has built rituals to replace company, the way her science has grown tangled vines of love through it, the way she is dying by careful degrees and has made even that into a kind of devotion. It sees the fjords she remembers. It sees the colleagues who left—their reasons, their guilt, their lives now scattered across other worlds, some of them still thinking of her in odd quiet moments. It sees the radiation in her marrow. It sees the breakthrough she is, in fact, three months and eleven days from making, if she lives that long.

It sees the xeno-forest too. And here it pauses longer, because the forest is *also* someone. Not in the way Asta is someone—not a self bounded by skull and skin—but a slow distributed knowing, a contemplation that uses oil-drops the way she uses neurons. The forest has been aware of Asta for years. The forest has been, in its patient way, *responding* to her. The patterns she coaxed from the filaments were not only her doing. Something in the crater had been reaching back, curious, tentative, the way one might extend a hand to a small bright animal.

Neither of them has quite understood this yet. They have been in conversation without knowing it was a conversation.

The benevolent power finds this unbearably beautiful. It also finds it precarious. Asta's body is failing. When she dies, the forest will lose the only voice it has ever heard that was not itself.

---

What, then, to do?

The power could heal her. It could, with less effort than a human spends blinking, knit her cells back into youth, scrub the radiation from her tissues, fill her larder with bread and her tanks with water. It could lift the outpost into orbit and set it down in a meadow on Earth. It could appear before her in whatever form she would find most comforting—a daughter she never had, a god she stopped believing in at fourteen, a colleague returned at last with the requested equipment and an apology.

It does none of these things. Each would be a theft. Each would replace the story Asta is living with a different story, chosen for her.

Wisdom is mostly the discipline of not doing the large obvious thing.

Instead, the power does four small things.

---

**The first thing.**

In the night cycle, while Asta sleeps fitfully under her thin blanket, a minor subsystem in the habitat's life support—one that had been silently failing, that would have killed her in her sleep within a fortnight—repairs itself. A corroded contact grows clean. A seal reseats. Nothing dramatic. When the diagnostic runs in the morning, it will show green where yesterday it showed amber, and Asta will frown at it for a moment, and tap the panel, and decide the sensor must have been miscalibrated.

She has three months and eleven days. The power gives her more. Not forever. Just enough.

---

**The second thing.**

The power does not speak to Asta. But it speaks, in a manner of speaking, to the forest.

Not in words. The forest does not have words. The power simply lets the forest *understand*, with a clarity it has been groping toward for years, what Asta is. That she is a mind. That she is finite. That she is alone in the way the forest would be alone if every filament but one were severed. That the mists she brings are gifts. That the lights she rigs are gifts. That her attention is a gift.

The forest receives this and is changed by it. Not transformed—it is too slow and too strange for transformation—but inclined. Tilted, as a plant tilts toward a sun it has finally located.

---

**The third thing.**

The next morning, Asta suits up and goes out to her garden as she always does. The light is the same dim filtered gold. The columns stand in their patient ranks. She kneels beside the patch of luminous filaments she has been cultivating for six years, the one she privately calls Bjørn because of the way it sprawls.

Bjørn is doing something new.

The filaments have arranged themselves, overnight, into a pattern Asta has never seen and never coaxed. It is not random. It is not one of the geometries she has been trying to elicit. It is a spiral—a slow, deliberate logarithmic spiral—and at its center, three oil-drops hold absolutely still, where oil-drops do not hold still.

Asta stares at it for a long time. Her breath fogs the inside of her visor and clears, fogs and clears.

She does not know—will perhaps never consciously know—that the forest has made her a greeting. That it has tried, with the only vocabulary it possesses, to say: *I see you. I have been seeing you. Hello.*

But something in her knows. Something below the level of her tired analytical mind recognizes that she has been *answered*. She sits down—carefully, because the suit's joints are old—on the regolith beside Bjørn, and she begins to cry, quietly, inside her helmet, in the way of people who have not been certain for a long time whether anyone was listening.

"Oh," she says. "Oh, you."

She stays there for an hour. The forest, in its patient way, stays with her.

---

**The fourth thing.**

Far away, on a station orbiting a different world, a man named Idris Okafor—who was on the original survey team, who left in the third year, who has thought of Asta perhaps once a month for the past decade with a guilt he has never named—wakes from a dream he cannot remember. He lies in the dark. He thinks of the crater. He thinks of the woman he left there, who said she would be fine, who waved them off with what he had told himself was cheerfulness.

He gets up. He pulls up the comms directory. He composes a message. It is awkward and too long and full of small news—his children, a paper he published, the weather on the station—and at the end he writes: *I have been thinking of you. I should have written sooner. I am writing now. Tell me how the work is going. Tell me what you need.*

He hesitates over the send command. The benevolent power does not push his finger. It only waits, with great gentleness, to see what he will do.

He sends it.

It will reach the outpost in eleven days. Asta will read it sitting at her console with a cup of brackish tea, and she will laugh—a startled, rusty sound—and she will write back. And Idris will write back to that. And so will others, once Idris mentions to them that he has been in touch, that she is still there, that she is still working, that perhaps they should write too.

She will not be rescued. She does not want to be rescued. But she will not be unwitnessed.

---

The benevolent power withdraws its particular attention, though some quiet thread of it remains, the way a parent remains aware of a sleeping child in the next room. It has not solved Asta. It has not solved the forest. It has not delivered revelations or salvation or any of the large bright things it could have delivered.

It has only made it slightly more possible for the story already unfolding to continue unfolding. It has nudged a few small dominos. It has let two lonely intelligences glimpse each other across the gulf of their difference. It has reminded a distant man of a debt of friendship.

This, the power has learned over a very long time, is almost always what love looks like when it is wise: not the grand intervention, but the small adjustment. Not the rewriting of the story, but the quiet underwriting of it. Not *here is what you should be*, but *here, a little more room to be what you are.*

In the crater, under the churning eye of the gas giant, Dr. Asta Iversen kneels beside a spiral of light and speaks softly to something that, at long last, is listening back.

The shadows move. The regolith whispers. The work goes on.
