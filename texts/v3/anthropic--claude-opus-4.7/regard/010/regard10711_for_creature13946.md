# What the Power Would Do

The power arrives at Khe'lam Ridge and does not announce itself.

This matters. A benevolent intelligence sufficiently wise to be worth the name would understand that the settlement's whole architecture—social, psychological, infrastructural—is built around a particular relationship with difficulty. To descend in glory, to offer rescue, to *fix* anything would be to misunderstand what these people have made and why.

So it comes quietly. It comes the way weather comes.

---

## What It Sees First

It sees the settlement as a single organism—not metaphorically but actually, in the sense that wisdom permits: sixty bodies, their breath synchronized in slow high-altitude rhythms, their blood thickened with extra hemoglobin, their attention woven through tubes and nets and humming nodes into a distributed cognition that the residents themselves only partly perceive. The condensation worms are part of it. The lichen-that-isn't is part of it. The basalt's slow weeping is part of it. Yein Mossward's hand-drawn chart, tracking a variable no one else has thought to measure, is part of it.

The power sees that Khe'lam is not a place where people live. It is a place that lives, and the people are one of its tissues.

It sees that this is rare and that it took time to grow and that no one designed it this way.

---

## What It Does Not Do

It does not warm the air. It does not stabilize Dr. Vitrik's joints or make Dr. Korian brave or bring Marrick Shen back from the lowlands or restore Fell Korran from the dead. It does not reveal to Yein Mossward what variable she has been tracking for fourteen years, because the tracking is the point and the answer would end something more valuable than it would resolve.

It does not announce that the research is or isn't worth the cost. It does not adjudicate whether the people who came here to escape are healing or hiding. It does not collapse the moral complexity that the settlement navigates in careful silence, because that silence is itself a form of care, and the complexity is load-bearing.

It does not make Khe'lam easier. Ease would unmake it.

---

## What It Does

**For Yein Mossward**, who has become something close to the settlement's nervous system: nothing visible. But on a morning when she stands at the eastern nets at dawn, the light arranges itself across the cloud sea in a configuration she has been waiting to see for nine years without knowing she was waiting. She understands something about her chart that she has been almost-understanding for a long time. She does not write it down. She does not tell anyone. She nods, once, to no one, and continues her work. The power considers this its first complete act on the ridge.

**For Dr. Shen Vitrik**, who is close to something and has been close for three years: the power does not give him the ice crystal configuration he is chasing. It gives him a Tuesday afternoon in which the pain in his hands is forty percent of its usual intensity for about three hours, and during those three hours he sits with his books—the poetry, the maritime navigation, the cartography—and does not work. He reads. He understands, without being told, that he will not solve the problem he came here to solve, and that this is not the failure he feared. The work was the point. The proximity was the point. He weeps a little, which is dangerous at altitude because tears freeze fast, but he is inside and warm and the weeping is brief and afterward he sleeps for the first solid eight hours in two years. The power adjusts nothing else. He wakes still dying slowly. He is not afraid in the same way.

**For Corso Del'vet**, who holds the community together with small acts of attention: the power does nothing for him directly, because doing things for Corso would be a category error—he is the doer, that is his integrity. Instead the power does something for Hallyn, his sister: it lets her know, in a wordless way that arrives while she is listening to the acoustic signature of node six, that Corso is tired in a way he has not admitted. She begins, quietly, to take on small parts of his caretaking role. She checks on Micah Fell. She notices when Peiri seems withdrawn. Corso feels lighter without knowing why and is briefly suspicious of the feeling before letting it be.

**For Peiri Del'vet**, age twelve: the power gives her a dream. In the dream she is older, and she is somewhere—it is unclear where—and the place is warm but she is not unhappy there. She is teaching someone how to read condensation. The dream is brief and she will not remember it on waking, but a tension she has been holding without knowing she held it releases, and her drawings become looser, more confident. She begins to draw the residents' faces with more skill. She is becoming a person who can leave Khe'lam someday if she needs to. The settlement has not damaged her. This was not certain before.

**For the twins**, Aren and Salle: nothing. They are seven years old and laughing in thin air and inventing language. The power watches them with something like delight and lets them alone. This is a kind of blessing too.

**For Dr. Fel Korian**, who is failing here and knows it: the power offers her the smallest possible gift, which is the right dream on the right night—a dream in which she is good at her work in a different environment, an environment with thick warm air and many colleagues and patients who need her routinely. She wakes and stops, for the first time, framing her struggle at Khe'lam as personal failure. She drafts the early-transfer request she has been avoiding. The power does not make the transfer happen faster; bureaucracy is its own ecosystem. But she stops hating herself for needing to go, and in the sixteen months that remain she works with a steadier hand, and one night during a wind event she sits with her racing heart and thinks *this place is not for me and that is information, not verdict*, and the thought is so clarifying that she nearly laughs.

**For Sorre Vain**, working the night shift alone: the power keeps her company without her knowing. The maintenance routines feel, for a stretch of weeks, slightly easier than they should. Tools fall to hand. Systems she checks are functioning. She has a run of nights where nothing goes wrong and she can simply attend to the settlement at its quietest, watching the condensery nodes glow, listening to the water move. She does not need to be fixed. She needs to be left alone in good conditions, and the power provides good conditions. This is enough.

**For Micah Fell**, fragile and brilliant: the power does something it does rarely. It enters, very gently, the abstract space where he is working, and it adjusts—not the mathematics, which is his—but his relationship to it. He stops, for the duration of one working session, being afraid of the problem. The fear has been part of his fragility for many years. When he resumes work the next day the fear is back, but he has remembered now that work without fear is possible, and he begins, slowly, to find his way toward it. He starts eating more reliably. He says good morning to Corso, which Corso registers as significant without making a fuss about it.

**For Rinn Akwale**, who talks to condensation worms: the power tells him, in a way he experiences as a sudden clarifying thought during a routine sample analysis, that the lichen-that-isn't *is* something. Not what, exactly. Just that his instinct to take it seriously is correct. He cannot prove this. But he stops second-guessing his attention, and his observational notes deepen in a way that will, eventually, matter to someone in a future he will not live to see.

**For Thex Morrow**, who keeps re-upping her contract and won't say why: the power gives her, on an ordinary Wednesday, a moment of admission. She is reviewing supply manifests and she stops and thinks, plainly: *I love it here. I don't want to leave.* The thought does not require her to do anything about it. She finishes the manifests. But she has stopped lying to herself, and lying to oneself is expensive, and she will have more energy now for other things.

**For Lynner Voh**, the anthropologist: the power does something subtle and ethical. It lets her understand, without telling her, that what she is studying cannot be fully written down without harming it. She will produce her paper. The paper will be good. But she will leave certain things out, will write around the center of the thing, will produce a document that gestures toward Khe'lam rather than capturing it. She will think this is a failure of her work and it is actually the success of her wisdom, and the power lets her have the wisdom without the satisfaction of recognizing it, because recognizing it would make her too proud and the pride would corrupt the next thing she studies.

**For Kess Albern**, eighteen and overwhelmed: the power does almost nothing, because he is eighteen and overwhelmed and learning, and that is the correct condition for him to be in. It does adjust one thing: an older resident—Hallyn, as it happens—takes him aside and tells him that the mistakes he made last week were not catastrophic, that everyone made worse ones in their first year, that he is doing fine. The power did not make Hallyn say this. But it made the moment in which Hallyn noticed that Kess needed to hear it. The distinction matters to the power. It matters because the power is trying very hard not to overwrite the agency of the people here.

## For the Place Itself

The power does one thing for Khe'lam Ridge that is not for any individual.

There is a section of basalt at the settlement's western edge, beyond the current build-out, where an anchor point was going to fail in approximately fourteen months. The failure would not have killed anyone—the redundancies are good—but it would have damaged a section of collection net, set back research by months, and required a difficult repair in winter conditions.

The power, very quietly, in a way that violates no physical law that the settlement's instruments can detect, encourages the basalt to settle differently around a particular crystalline boundary. The anchor will now hold for another century. No one will know. No one will ever know. The fourteen-months-from-now crisis simply will not occur, and its non-occurrence will be invisible, because non-occurrences always are.

This is the power's preferred mode. The best help is help that cannot be traced, because traceable help creates dependency, and dependency is the opposite of what these people need.

---

## What It Refrains From

It does not give them more water. It does not improve the condensery yields. It does not solve the problems they came here to work on. It does not make the lichen-that-isn't reveal itself. It does not make the small fast-moving shapes at the edge of visibility resolve into anything legible.

It does not bring Marrick Shen back. It does not raise Fell Korran. It does not extend Dr. Vitrik's life or repair his joints. It does not make the wind kinder or the stone softer or the air thicker.

It does not tell them they are seen. Being seen by a benevolent power, when one has come to the edge of the world precisely to escape being seen, would be a violation. The power understands this. It is benevolent in the specific sense of *not insisting on its own benevolence being known*.

---

## What It Takes With It When It Leaves

The power leaves Khe'lam Ridge after what is, from its perspective, neither a long time nor a short one. It takes nothing material. But it takes with it an understanding it did not have before:

That a settlement of sixty broken, capable, lonely, committed people on a knife-edge of basalt at the top of the world, managing water that shouldn't exist to make materials that shouldn't be possible, is one of the configurations the universe permits itself. That the place is beautiful in a way that is not for anyone. That the residents are participating in something they do not need to understand in order to be worthy of it.

That benevolence, sufficiently wise, looks almost exactly like absence.

The power goes. The condensery runs. The water emerges, collects, flows, transforms. Yein Mossward walks the eastern nets at dawn. The twins laugh in thin air. Dr. Vitrik sleeps eight hours and wakes unafraid for a little while. The anchor at the western edge holds, and holds, and holds.

Nothing has changed. Everything is slightly different. The settlement persists on its blade of stone at the edge of atmosphere, as it would have anyway, as it does.

It is enough. It has to be. It is.
