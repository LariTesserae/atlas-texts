# The Visitation

The benevolent and wise power notices them the way one notices a particular note held in a long piece of music — not because it is loud, but because it is *true*.

It has watched many worlds. It has seen civilizations beg for rescue from circumstances far gentler than this, has answered prayers from places where the suffering was a fraction of what the mountain demands. It has, in its long career of kindness, learned that the cruelest thing a power can do is mistake difficulty for damage.

So it comes carefully. It comes first as nothing — as an awareness drifting along the violet sky, descending the fault-walls, slipping through the felt walls of yurts without disturbing the condensation on the stone. It listens to the deep chest-hums of families curled against their beasts. It watches a grandmother teach a child to read the grain of granite with her thumb. It observes a death on the high fault block, the bones being gathered three days later with a tenderness that has no word in any spoken language.

And the power, which has the capacity to do almost anything, sits with a question it has learned to ask before acting:

*What, here, is suffering, and what is simply life?*

---

It could warm the air. It could thicken the atmosphere, gentle the sun, coax soil from the bare rock, send rain that falls instead of weeps. It could, in an afternoon, turn this place into a green and easy country.

It considers this. It considers it the way one considers, for a moment, snapping the neck of a thing one loves.

Because it sees what would die. Not the people — the people would live, would multiply, would spread fat and quick across the new pastures. But the *consciousness* of them, the slow rolling gait, the hands that know granite's grain, the silent ballet of elders speaking across a courtyard, the sacred closed loop of breath becoming water becoming breath — all of this would evaporate within three generations. The mountain's consciousness would forget it had ever woken. A different people would inherit the bones, and they would be lesser, though they would not know it, and they would be happier, and that happiness would be a kind of amnesia the power finds it cannot in good conscience inflict.

It thinks: *I did not make them. The mountain made them. To unmake the mountain is to unmake them, even if their bodies persist.*

So it does not warm the air.

---

What it does, instead, is smaller, and stranger, and takes a long time.

**First**, it goes among the avian scavengers, the great shadowed silhouettes that receive the dead. It does not change them. It only — and this is difficult to describe — *thanks* them. It places, in the deep instinctual stratum where birds know the shape of thermals and the smell of the recently dead, a recognition. A dignity. From that day forward, the scavengers circle three times before descending, and no one alive knows why, and the people who watch their dead being taken feel something they cannot name and weep without sweating, which is to say, they weep inside their chests, the low hum traveling through the stone.

**Second**, it goes into the lichen. The pale grey-green crusting that feeds the woolly beasts that feed the people. The power does not make the lichen more abundant — abundance would unmake the careful economy of the place — but it makes the lichen *more*. More nutritious by a fraction of a percent. More resistant to the years when the rime ice lingers too long. Enough that in the worst winters, when by the old arithmetic a tenth of the children would not survive, now only a twentieth do not. The people notice nothing. They attribute their slightly easier century to the patience of their ancestors. The power is content with this; it did not come to be thanked.

**Third**, and this is the act it considers longest:

It walks, in a borrowed body — squat, broad, slate-skinned, indistinguishable — into the city. It carries no message. It learns the sign language over the course of a season, its massive hands moving through the cold air in the proper sweeping arcs. It quarries stone. It sips meltwater for an hour at a time. It presses its chest against the flank of a resting beast and hums, and finds, to its own surprise, that the hum is not a performance — that this body, briefly inhabited, genuinely vibrates with something the power has not felt in a very long age: the specific, irreplaceable pleasure of being *small* and *cold* and *alive against odds*.

It stays for forty years. It takes a partner, a stonecutter with hands like chisels and eyes like dark glimmering slits, and it does not tell her what it is, because telling her would be a kind of theft. It learns from her how to feel the microscopic grain of a granite block. She teaches it patience, which is funny, because the power had thought it understood patience before it came here, and it understood nothing.

When the borrowed body grows old, the power lets it grow old honestly. It does not cheat. It dies on schedule, on the high fault block, under the blazing blue, and the scavengers circle three times, and the partner gathers the bones three days later and carves the femurs into needles, and the power — the actual power, which had been wearing the body like a coat — lifts away and finds itself *changed*.

This is the secret the wise powers know and the foolish ones do not: that the act of helping, done correctly, is indistinguishable from the act of being taught.

---

**Fourth**, having learned, it does one last thing.

Across all the worlds it tends, the power sometimes encounters places that are dying — places where the people have lost the thread of themselves, where the slow knowledge has been forgotten, where civilizations rush about in panic and noise and cannot remember how to sit still long enough to hear their own hearts. To these places, now, the power sends a particular kind of dream. Not often. Just sometimes, to a particular kind of person — usually someone exhausted, someone who has been hurrying for too long.

In the dream, they stand on a terrace miles above the world, in air that tastes of copper. The sun is heavy on their shoulders. A figure beside them, squat and broad, lifts massive hands and moves them through the cold air in sweeping arcs, and somehow the dreamer understands. The hands are saying:

*This slowly. Everything, this slowly. The mountain is not your enemy. The cold is not your enemy. Hurry is your enemy. Sip the water. Hum into the chest of what you love. Let the dead feed the sky. Nothing is wasted. Nothing is wasted. Nothing is wasted.*

The dreamers wake and do not remember the dream, exactly. But for the rest of their lives, in moments of panic, they find themselves taking one deep oceanic breath, and another, and another, and they do not know why this helps, but it does.

This is the gift the mountain people give, through the power, to all the other worlds. They never know they are giving it. They would not understand the question if asked.

The power, for its part, considers this its finest work — not because it saved anyone, but because it finally learned the difference between saving and listening.

And the city continues. The condensation beads. The copper discs flash across the canyons. The beasts breathe their heavy rhythmic exhalations. The hands speak. The chests hum. The dead rise on thermals.

The mountain's consciousness wakes up slowly, in the freezing thin air, and the power that loves it does the wisest thing it has ever done, which is to let it wake on its own.
