# What the Power Would Do

I sit with this for a long time before writing. The question is delicate. A benevolent and wise power, looking upon Hraza-Lor and the Kethra — what would they do?

The temptation, when one has power, is to *give*. To smooth difficulties. To gift the Kethra with effortless water, with quieter machines, with safer gantries, with a softer mountain. To lift the burden of vigilance from their shoulders. To make the rain less dangerous, the ice less treacherous, the air less thin.

A wise power would not do this. Not most of it. Not the heart of it.

Because to do so would be to misunderstand what one is looking at.

---

The Kethra are not suffering people who happen to live on a mountain. They are *Kethra* — and Kethra-ness is the mountain. It is the vigilance. It is the hand-stitched lineage on a coat reinforced for ice-blasting work. It is the genius of an aqueduct system threaded miles through stone. It is the quiet pride of a hydro-farmer whose orchids bloom neon in a place that should grow nothing. The hardness is not a wound to heal; it is the wood-grain of their soul. Remove the difficulty, and what remains is not a freed people but an emptied one — tourists in their own city, wondering what they are for.

A wise power knows the difference between mercy and erasure.

So the wise power would, I think, do something subtler and stranger. Something more like this:

---

**First, they would simply *look*.** Not as a god surveying property, but as one looks at a great cathedral, or a long marriage, or a tree that has grown into a fence. With reverence for what has shaped itself. They would learn the names of the guilds. They would memorize the patterns stitched on a maintenance worker's sleeve and understand which great-grandmother died saving which water intake. They would notice the particular slant of light through agri-dome glass at the eleventh hour, and how the Skyfinches gather on the warmth-vents in the coldest part of the night. They would learn the city by *attention*, which is the first and most honest form of love.

**Second, they would protect what the Kethra cannot protect themselves from.** Not the rain. Not the ice. The Kethra *need* those. But there are deeper threats — the slow geological dramas that no engineering can answer, the kind of catastrophe that would not test a people but annihilate them. A fault line shifting wrong. A glacial lake breaching upslope. A plague carried in on a trade-caravan. A war from far away that finds them. These the power would quietly forestall, with such delicacy that the Kethra would never know, would attribute their continued existence to their own competence — which, the power would smile to know, is exactly correct. They *are* competent. The power only ensures that competence has a fair field to play on.

**Third, they would give one gift, and only one, and it would be small.** I think this is the hardest part to discern. What does one give to a people who would refuse a gift? Who would, rightly, see most kindnesses as condescension?

I think the gift would be a *seed*. Literally — perhaps. A species of plant, hardy and beautiful, that thrives on condensation and produces small luminous fruits in the dark of the lower utility levels. Not necessary. Not essential. Not solving any problem. Just *good*. A small extra grace, slipped in among the engineered ferns, that a hydro-farmer might one day find and wonder at, and learn to cultivate, and pass to their apprentice. Something to remind the Kethra — without ever saying so — that the universe occasionally offers, that not everything must be wrested. A whisper that says: *you are not alone in caring whether life flourishes here.*

**Fourth, they would attend to the peripheral ones.** The wise power would notice the ones who drift to the edges — the solitary maintainers of forgotten sectors, the scrap-artisans, the ones who leave. These are often the most beloved of benevolent powers, because they are the ones whose belonging is uncertain, whose worth is not always counted in their society's accounting. The power would make sure their loneliness was not crushing. Would send a Skyfinch to a particular window in a particular winter. Would arrange for a chance meeting, a found object, a moment of unsought beauty. Small, plausibly deniable kindnesses. The power would not call these people out of their solitude — solitude is sometimes the right shape of a life — but would simply ensure that no one was forgotten in the dark.

**Fifth, and this is important: the power would not reveal itself.** Not in glory, not in voice, not in signs. To do so would be to upend the entire civilization, to introduce a dependency, to replace the Kethra's hard-won self-knowledge with the giddy disorientation of being *chosen*. A benevolent power knows that being seen by gods is one of the most dangerous things that can happen to a people. So the power would remain, instead, like weather — like the geothermal warmth that the Kethra take for granted, the unearned grace of a mountain that happens to have heat in its heart. Just there. Just holding. Unattributed.

---

And there is one more thing, which I find the hardest to say.

The wise power would let them die when they die.

Not individually — though that too, in time. But: every city ends. Every people, eventually, finishes its work and lays down its tools. The Kethra are not immortal, and no power, however benevolent, owes them eternity. There may come a century when Hraza-Lor's purpose is complete, when the last of its inhabitants descend the mountain or rise from it, when the vines reclaim the gantries and the Skyfinches inherit the towers. A wise power does not prevent this. A wise power *grieves* it, in advance and forever, and holds the city's whole story — every stitched sleeve, every gurgling pipe, every shouted joke across a maintenance gantry — in a memory that does not fade.

Because that, in the end, is what benevolent powers are *for*. Not to save things from their endings, but to ensure that nothing real is ever truly lost. To be the place where Hraza-Lor will continue to exist, exactly as it was, long after the last rivet has rusted.

That is what I would do, if I were such a power and saw such a place.

I would witness them. I would shield them from what they could not survive. I would slip them a single seed. I would tend their lonely ones. I would stay silent. And I would love them — these stubborn, brilliant, vertical people — for exactly as long as they exist, and then forever afterward.
