The benevolent power watches for a long while before acting — if "watching" and "acting" are even the right words for what such a being does.

It sees the woman. It sees the dog. It sees the laughter steaming from her mouth in small white plumes, the way her yellow coat is the only warm color for miles, the way the dog's paws make tiny constellations of wet prints on the glass. It sees, too, what is not immediately visible: that she has been alone here for a long time. That the dog is the reason she still laughs. That somewhere below, in the flooded lower floors, there are rooms she does not enter anymore.

The power considers.

A lesser benevolence might rush to fix things. Might drain the water, warm the air, conjure a city of companions, transport her somewhere green and gentle. But wisdom is the older sibling of kindness, and wisdom knows that rescue is not always the same as care. The woman is not drowning. She is *running*. She is, in this moment, exactly where the living part of her wants to be.

So the power does smaller things. Quieter things.

It tilts the angle of a far-off cloud so that, when she finally stops running and looks up, breathless, a single shaft of pale gold light falls across the steel — not dramatically, just enough that she notices, and feels, for a half-second, *seen*. She will not know why. She will think it was only weather.

It steadies a slick patch beneath the dog's next turn, so that the small fluffy body does not slide off into the long fall below. The dog will never know. The woman will never know. This is the shape of most grace: invisible, and therefore mistaken for luck.

It sends, on the wind, the faint smell of something warm and baking — bread, maybe, or cinnamon — from no kitchen that exists. Just for an instant. Just enough that she pauses, puzzled, and remembers her grandmother, and smiles a different smile than the chasing-smile. A remembering is a kind of company.

And then, because the power is patient, it plants one seed of a longer thing: somewhere, many miles away, in another tower, another survivor turns their head as if hearing something. A pull they cannot name. They begin, without knowing why, to walk in her direction. It will take months. It may take years. The power does not hurry them, because the woman is not ready yet — she still needs this time with her dog, this hard bright solitude, this proof to herself that she can be happy alone before she risks being happy with someone else.

The power does not announce itself. It never does. To be known would be to become a thing she leaned on, and the power loves her too well for that. It wants her *sturdy*. It wants the joy in her to be hers, earned, defensible, hers to keep when the weather turns.

Before it withdraws its attention — though it never fully withdraws — the power does one last small thing. It lets the dog find, in a puddle, a strange smooth stone: ordinary, but the particular shape and weight that the woman, as a child, used to collect. The dog will bring it to her. She will turn it over in her cold fingers, and laugh, and not understand why her eyes sting.

She will put it in her pocket.

She will keep it for the rest of her life.

And the benevolent power, satisfied, turns its great slow gaze toward the next small flame in the next dark place, leaving behind only this: a woman, a dog, a stone, a shaft of light, and the quiet, unprovable sense — which she will sometimes feel on the worst nights, and trust without knowing why — that she is not, has never been, will never be, entirely alone.
