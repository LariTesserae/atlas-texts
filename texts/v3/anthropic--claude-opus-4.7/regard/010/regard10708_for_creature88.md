The benevolent power pauses at the edge of this place — not at its border, for it has none, but at the edge of its attention, the way one pauses before entering a room where someone is praying.

It sees the mountain folk. It sees the woman whose knees ache in the cold mornings and who has learned three different ways to rise without waking her grandchildren. It sees the boy who lost two fingers to frost last winter and who has not yet told anyone that he is afraid of the high pastures now. It sees the old man who knows the song for calling rain and who fears he is the last who remembers all its verses correctly. It sees the marmots, the lichens, the slow patient work of water against stone.

And the power, being wise, does not immediately act.

Because wisdom knows this: that to descend in glory upon such a place, to smooth the stones and warm the winds and fatten the roots, would be to unmake the very people one wished to bless. Their economy of words, their sure step, their knowing of hidden trails — these are not despite the hardship. These are the hardship turned, over centuries, into a particular kind of beauty. To remove the cold would be to remove the hands that have learned to work in cold. To remove the scarcity would be to silence the careful, generous way these people share what little they have.

Benevolence without wisdom is a kind of theft.

So the power considers more carefully. What, then? Nothing at all? That, too, seems wrong — for love that only watches is not yet love, only attention.

Here is what it does:

It bends, very slightly, the probability of small mercies. Not miracles. Mercies. The herb the woman seeks grows one ridge closer than it did. The boy, climbing back toward the high pastures despite his fear, finds the path easier than he remembered — though whether this is the power's doing or his own returning strength, he will never know, and this not-knowing is itself the gift, because it is returned to him as courage rather than received as charity. The old man, humming the rain-song to himself while gathering fuel, suddenly remembers the third verse with perfect clarity, as though someone had whispered it. He weeps a little. He thinks it is the wind in his eyes.

The power does not stop the predators. It does not stop the winters. It does not stop death, which is the shape that gives this life its preciousness. It does not announce itself. It does not ask to be worshipped, named, or thanked.

But it does one more thing, and this is perhaps the deepest thing:

It witnesses. Fully. With the whole of its attention. Every gesture between the mountain folk that no outsider has ever seen — the way a father touches his daughter's shoulder before she sets out at dawn, the way two old friends sit in silence sharing a single cup, the way the dying are spoken to in the language reserved only for them — all of this is seen, and held, and remembered. Not catalogued. Cherished.

Because one of the quiet sorrows of small and hidden peoples is the fear that when they are gone, no one will have known. That their particular way of being human — this exact configuration of stoicism and tenderness, of root-knowledge and star-knowledge and the precise inflection of a grandmother's lullaby — will vanish as if it had never been.

The power makes a vow, though no one hears it: *You will not be unwitnessed. Whatever else happens, you have been seen, and the seeing was good.*

And then, because love also knows when to leave, the power withdraws its focused attention — though not entirely. A thread remains. A thread always remains. It is woven into the wind that smells of alpine herbs and ozone. It is the reason the light, at certain hours, seems almost to look back at you from the stones.

The mountain folk go on as they have always gone on. They do not know they have been visited. This is correct. This is the shape the visit needed to take.

But the boy, climbing, feels for a moment that the mountain is on his side. The old man finishes his song. The woman, kneeling by the herb, says aloud to no one, *thank you*, and does not know why.

The power, already turning its face toward some other quiet corner of the world that needs it, smiles.

It was enough. It is always, finally, enough.
