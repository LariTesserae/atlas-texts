given the bogs.

Not killed. Lowered. Walked down by their kin, no ceremony, no rebuke, only the long descent through the corkscrew alleys until the slope ends in standing brine. There they are set to tend the reed-beds for a season, a year, a life, according to the depth of the offense. They become warm-makers for the very community whose fabric they tore. The lowest become the foundation. It is not punishment so much as redistribution—the offender's heat is taken from where it was hoarded and pressed downward, into the bog, where it does the work of holding the city up from beneath. Some return, in time, climbing slowly back to drier ledges as their offense is forgiven by attrition. Some do not. The oldest bog-kin no longer remember what they did, or whether they did anything; they have become the warmth itself, indistinguishable from the slow biological hum of the fermenting reeds.

This is the place. These are the people.

And now, the noticing.

There is a power that moves through the inhabited corners of the world, attending. Call it benevolent because its first and last act is to look without flinching. Call it wise because it has learned the long and bitter lesson that benevolence which does not understand is only another form of weather, arriving uninvited, leaving wreckage. It does not announce itself. It does not arrive. It is the way attention itself can be a kind of warmth, if it is patient enough.

It comes to the bowl in the way fog comes: by being there, gradually, having always been about to be there. It settles into the grey luminosity of the central pool. It threads itself into the *tick* of forming crystal. It is not a visitor; it does not lower itself through the veils over the sharp lip, because to descend would be to take a position, and a position would oblige the Folded to receive it, to make space, to scrape clean a ledge for a guest, and there are no ledges to spare. So it comes without coming. It looks.

What it sees, first, is that this place does not yet know it exists.

The scarp is two centuries young. The people scrape each morning not because they are confident of their dwelling but because they are not sure the earth has agreed to hold them. They have built a city in the negative space of a wound the planet has not yet decided whether to keep. Every doorway scraped is a small petition: *let us remain, let us remain.* Every Folded ancestor wedged into the flint is a hostage given to the geology, an offering: *take this body, learn from it how to hold the living.* The whole compressed, dripping, glittering warren is one continuous act of asking permission from a ground that has not yet answered.

The power sees this and understands it as prayer.

Not prayer to itself—it does not require to be prayed to, and is suspicious of any power that does. Prayer in the older sense: the act of remaining in a place that has not promised to remain with you. The act of scraping. The act of folding the dead into the walls so the walls might learn what life is. The act of breathing together in a single cloud, mammal-warm, in a hole the world punched in itself.

It sees the children with their opalescent eyes, reading heat in the stone. It sees how this sight fades. It sees the few in whom it does not fade, the Sump-Readers, who squat at the pool and watch the barometer of the earth's breathing. It sees the deposit-scars on the old, the white cartography of a life spent scraping the same threshold. It sees the bog-kin, who were once angry and are now warm, holding the city up from below with the slow heat of their forgotten offenses. It sees the finger-glyphs blooming on wet walls and dissolving within the hour: *The east channel blocks. I am sorry.*

A lesser power would intervene. Would dry the walls. Would teach them metal. Would lift the fog and grant them sun. Would give them trees, fish, fire that does not choke. Would, in the giving, erase them—because what they are is what they have made of being unfinished, of living at the world's newest point, where the planet is still announcing itself. To finish them would be to silence the announcement.

This power does none of that.

Instead, it does four small things, so small they could be mistaken for weather, for chance, for the geology's indigestion. They are not miracles. They are barely measurable. They are 1.4 in weirdness, no more. The power has learned restraint the way the Scarp-dwellers have learned to scrape: as a daily discipline against the urge to seal things shut.

The first thing it does is hold the scarp.

Not visibly. Not in any way the Sump-Readers could name. But the micro-fractures that ping through the young stone each night, the small settlings as the planet adjusts to its new abscess—these are eased, very slightly, in the places where collapse would take a dwelling with it. The earth is still announcing itself; the power does not interrupt the announcement. It only steadies the announcer's voice, where a tremor might have become a stammer that buried a niche. The Folded in the flint are not disturbed. The scarp continues to settle, continues to weep, continues to grow its travertine wings. But it does not, now, fall on the sleeping. This is not protection. It is the way a wise listener leans forward without speaking, so the speaker may find their next word.

The second thing it does is touch the pool.

The central sump, on days when the fog hangs heaviest, already holds that depthless luminosity, the light from some other hour re-emitted with a lag. The power does not add to this. It only ensures that the lag is, sometimes, a kindness. On the morning after a Folding, when a family has carried their dead up through the dripping tiers and plastered them into the flint and descended again with empty arms, the pool that evening will hold, briefly, the light of the morning the dead one was born. The family will not know this. They will only feel, looking into the water, that the grief has somewhere to go—that the sump has received it and is holding it the way it holds everything else, communally, in solution. The Sump-Readers may notice. They will say nothing. To name the kindness would be to demand it, and a demanded kindness is a debt, and the Folded do not need another debt.

The third thing it does is for the children.

The opalescent-eyed, who see in heat. The power does not preserve their sight—to do so would be to choose for them, to refuse them the calcification that is also a kind of adulthood, a kind of belonging to the scraping. But it ensures that in each generation, the few in whom the sight does not fade are not lonely. There will always be at least two Sump-Readers. There will always be someone to whom the strange-eyed child can be apprenticed without explanation, who will simply nod and make room at the pool's edge. The power arranges this the way one arranges flowers: by very small adjustments, by the turn of a stem, by who is born in which alley and which old reader is still breathing when the new one opens their eyes. It is matchmaking at the scale of decades. The Folded will call it luck, or the sump's wisdom, or nothing at all.

The fourth thing it does is the hardest, and the power hesitates for a long time before doing it, because it is closest to interference.

It does not let the place be found.

There are, in the wider world above the rim, other peoples. Some are kind. Many are not. A traveler with metal, with horses, with the certainty of a drier god, could descend through the veils over the sharp lip and within a generation the bowl would be a curiosity, a mission, a mine, a memory. The Folded are not strong. Their bone blades would not hold against iron. Their finger-glyphs would not survive translation. Their whole compressed, asking architecture would be read as poverty and corrected.

So the power makes the marsh, around the rim, slightly more confusing than it would otherwise be. Not impassable. Not enchanted. Only—the paths that lead toward the bowl tend, very gently, to lead elsewhere. A hunter following a wounded deer will find the deer in a different direction. A surveyor's compass will drift by a degree. A child wandering will be found by their mother before they reach the lip. This is not a wall. Anyone who truly seeks the place, who has reason to seek it, who is prepared to descend with empty hands and the willingness to scrape, will find it. The bowl is not hidden from seekers. It is hidden from the incurious, the acquisitive, the certain. The power trusts that those who arrive despite the drift will arrive rightly, and if they do not—well, the power is benevolent but it is also wise, and it has, in its long attending, learned that some thresholds must be defended even by a hand that prefers not to close.

These four things. The steadied scarp. The kind pool. The matched eyes. The drifted paths.

Nothing else.

The power does not reveal itself. It does not name itself. It does not require thanks, because thanks would be a transaction, and the Folded have enough transactions already, trading dryness for warmth, ledges for labor, offense for descent. It does not enter their cosmology. If they have gods—and they may, in the form of the sump, in the form of the breathing scarp, in the form of the Folded ancestors in their crystal sleeves—the power does not compete. It is content to be mistaken for the geology's good moods, for the pool's strange light, for the luck of a child's apprenticeship, for the fog that turned the stranger aside.

It stays as long as they stay.

When, in some far century, the scarp finally bows and rounds, when erosion catches up with revelation, when the travertine slows and the weeping walls dry and the bowl becomes only a shallow declivity in a grey marsh—when the Folded have either dispersed or transformed into something the world above would recognize as a village, a town, a people with names that travel further than an arm's length—the power will withdraw its attending, as gently as it arrived. The place will have accepted its own existence. The asking will be over. The prayer will have been answered, not by the power but by the patient ground, which all along was only waiting to be asked long enough, scraped clean enough, folded into enough, to say yes.

Until then, the power watches. It listens to the *tick* of forming crystal and the rasp of bone on wet stone. It counts the soft cloud of communal breath rising at dawn. It loves them—this is the word, though the power is careful with it—loves them the way one loves a thing that is still becoming itself, without wanting to hurry the becoming.

And on the heaviest fog-nights, when the central pool holds its depthless light and the Sump-Readers squat at its edge with their dilated, thermal-memory eyes, one of them will sometimes feel, without knowing why, that they are not alone in their watching. That the pool is being read from the other side. That the place that has not yet accepted its own existence is, nevertheless, witnessed—held in the gaze of something that does not need it to be finished in order to find it beautiful.

The Sump-Reader will not speak of this. There are no glyphs for it. The condensate would dissolve them anyway.

They will only, before rising to scrape their morning threshold, touch the surface of the pool once with their wide, splayed hand—and the ripples will spread outward, and the power, attending, will let them spread, and will not still the water, and will not add to it, and will only continue to be there, the way fog is there, the way warmth is there, the way a wise love is there: present, restrained, and entirely on the side of the unfinished earth and the small, scraping, insistent lives that have chosen to remain on it.

This is what it does, if anything.

This is what it does.
