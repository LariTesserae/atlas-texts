# What the Power Would Do

The benevolent power arrives the way light arrives at the bottom of a well—not as intrusion, but as something that was always there, finally noticed. It does not announce itself. Announcement would be a kind of violence in a place already so full of noise.

It watches first. This is the wisdom part. The benevolence without wisdom would already be reaching, already be fixing, already deciding that the white-haired Deep Walkers and the tremoring Platform Riders and the children with crystalline laughter are problems to be solved. But wisdom sees what benevolence alone might miss:

*These people are not broken. They are becoming.*

The salt has not destroyed them. It has been answered. Every adaptation—the echolocation clicks, the sign-dance of the Refiners, the synesthesia of those who interface with the great machines—is a conversation the human form has been having with this place, in a language neither party fully understands but both have learned to speak. To "rescue" them would be to interrupt a sentence that has been forming for generations.

And yet. There is suffering here. The wisdom sees that too. The mineral-dust cough that signals which Runners will not last. The Lifers whose tears have begun to crystallize. The way "fortunes made or lost" is spoken in the same breath, as if the losing were always the more likely verb. The way the ground itself is being sold out from beneath the feet of those who love it most.

So the power does not erase. It does not lift Kethara out of itself into some imagined paradise of soft air and stable earth. That would be the act of a god who had not listened.

Instead, it does smaller things. Stranger things.

---

It goes first to the salt itself—to the deep brine pockets where the ancient seas still remember being seas. It does not stop the extraction. It cannot, and would not if it could; the choosing belongs to the people. But it whispers to the water down there, in the language water has always understood, and asks it to be generous in a particular way. To carry, alongside its minerals, something else: a trace element, almost undetectable, that binds gently to the lining of human lungs and escorts the worst of the dust back out. Not a cure. A courtesy. The cough will still come for some. But fewer. And later.

It goes to the wind next. The wind that sings through the crystal formations, that has taught the Transport Folk perfect pitch and the children their rhythm games. The power does not change the wind's song. It only ensures that the song carries further than it used to—that a Deep Walker in a collapsing tunnel can be heard at the surface, that a Platform Rider whose machine has failed at the basin's far edge is not alone with the silence of malfunction. The salt itself becomes, very slightly, a listener. The crystalline arthropods, without knowing why, begin to click in patterns that the Lifers (only the Lifers, at first) recognize as something like *we hear you, we hear you, we hear you.*

It goes to the children. Here it is most careful. The children are the place's truest answer to itself, and the power will not interfere with what they are becoming. But it does something a grandmother might do: it makes sure that when they sleep, they dream in colors their parents have forgotten. Greens that have nothing to do with salt-scrub. The blue of water that is only water. Not as a homesickness for somewhere they have never been, but as a vocabulary—so that when they grow up and choose (and they must choose), they will be choosing from a fuller palette. The power does not want them to leave Kethara. It only wants them to be able to stay as a choice, not as a consequence of never having imagined otherwise.

It goes to the Lifers last, and longest. These ones, whose bones have become mineral catalogs, whose eyes have gone opalescent—the power sits with them. Not to heal them. They would refuse healing; they have earned what they are. But to witness. To say, in the only way such a power can say anything: *I see what you have made of yourself. I see that it is a kind of art. I see that no one else will ever quite see it, and I see it anyway.* Some of the Lifers, on certain nights, will feel this and not know what they are feeling. They will simply find themselves weeping crystalline tears that, for the first time, do not sting.

---

The power does not stop the extraction. It does not topple the cranes or quiet the pumps or send the harvest platforms grinding to a halt. It knows that Kethara is not only a wound; it is also a livelihood, a culture, a place where people have built meaning out of brine and grit. To end the mining would be to end the Haulers and the Runners and the Salt Witches as surely as to kill them. The power is benevolent enough to know that saving a people from themselves is often only another name for erasing them.

But it does, quietly, begin to seed something. In the deepest brine pockets, where the prehistoric water still sleeps, it plants the slow possibility of regeneration. Not for now. Not for these workers, or their children, or their children's children. But for some distant day, long after Kethara has been mined to its last crystal and abandoned, when the rains come back (and they will come back; the power has whispered to them too, and they remember this place), the salt will rise again. The basin will fill. The ancient sea will return. And the bones of the Lifers, scattered now through the reclaimed earth, will dissolve gently into the new water, and they will, in their own way, come home.

This is what the wise and benevolent power does, finally: it loves Kethara enough not to save it. It loves the people enough to let them be the strange new humans they are choosing to be. It eases what can be eased without unmaking what must not be unmade. And it plants, far down in the dark, the seed of a future where the place that has been so taken from will, in the long arc of time, be given back to itself.

Then it goes. Or rather, it stays—but as the light at the bottom of the well stays. Present. Unannounced. Available to anyone who looks down far enough, on a quiet enough night, and wonders if they are, perhaps, less alone in this crystalline cacophony than they had feared.

The pumps thunk on. The wind sings. A child laughs, and the laugh sparkles in the air like a small bell struck once and allowed to ring.

It is enough. It is, for now, exactly enough.
