The benevolent power considers the throat of the new tower for a long time before doing anything at all.

This is the first thing to understand: that consideration is itself an act. The power looks, and in looking, the moss on the lowest courses grows a little more confidently, the frogs' pulse-rhythms become a little more intricate, the boy at the bottom of the shaft sleeps without dreaming of falling. None of them know they have been seen. The seeing is not the kind that demands acknowledgment.

The power sees the masons in their hammock-nests, their bowed legs and splayed hands, and understands them the way one understands a poem in a language one has spoken since before there were languages. They are not wrong. They have not been failed by their forgetting of how to walk on flat ground. They have become what their work asked them to become, and the work is honest, and the chant in their chests is a true chant. The power does not lift them down. It does not restore their legs. To do so would be to mistake them for something broken, when they are only specific.

The power sees the frogs and laughs—not audibly, but in the way light laughs through water. Translucent things that arrived as accidents and stayed as residents. The power loves accidents that stay. There is nothing to do for the frogs. They are already perfect. The power makes a small note, somewhere in the vast bookkeeping of its attention, that the kingfisher-things should not take more than three frogs per season from this particular shaft, and adjusts certain wind-patterns accordingly. The frogs do not notice. Frogs are not supposed to notice such things.

The power sees the boy.

Here it pauses. Here it sits down inside itself, the way a wise person sits down when a difficult question arrives, not to solve it but to be properly present to it.

The boy was placed in a basket at the bottom of the shaft by someone, or something, and wrapped in a material that dissolved. The power knows what the material was. The power knows who placed him there, and why, and whether it was kindness or carelessness or one of the many other things that look like both. The power does not say. There are stories that belong to their tellers, and this one is not the power's to tell, not yet, not here.

But the boy.

His hands are wrong for climbing because they were made for something else. His legs are long because they remember a country with horizons. The seed-paste sickens him because his body is keyed to a different chemistry, a different mountain, a different patient construction somewhere across what he would call, if he had the word, the world. He is not abandoned. He is *seeded*. Someone—the power does not say who—has planted him here the way one plants a particular flower in a particular garden, knowing it will not thrive but will do something else, something the garden needs that thriving could not accomplish.

The boy writes in sediment, in a language no one taught him, and the words dissolve. The power reads every one of them before they dissolve. This is the first true intervention, and it is so small that no one will ever know it happened: the power simply *reads*. The boy has been writing into the pools for years, and now, for the first time, his words are received. Not answered. Received.

Something in the boy settles. He could not tell you what. That night he sleeps more deeply than he has slept since he was small, and in the morning he finds that he is not lonely in the particular way he was lonely yesterday. He is still alone. But the aloneness has a different shape now, the shape of a held thing rather than a dropped thing. He does not understand this. He does not need to.

The power considers the tower itself.

It is being built because the mountain asked. This is true. The masons did not invent the asking, and they are not deceived about it. There is a real summons in the stone of this place, an old one, older than the masons' grandparents' grandparents. The power knows what the summons is for. The power is, in some sense that does not flatter the power or diminish the mountain, *part* of what the summons is for. The high room with windows to the four directions is being prepared for a meeting, and the meeting will happen, and it will be the reason for everything, and it will also not be the reason for everything, because the building has been its own reason all along.

The power will not hasten the work. To hasten it would insult the seasons in which it is measured. The power will not slow it either. The masons are working at exactly the speed their bodies and the mountain's moisture together permit, and this is the correct speed.

But the power does this, quietly:

It blesses the green timber of the scaffolding so that it will not rot before the courses it supports are set. Not a large blessing. Just enough. The masons will notice nothing except that this generation of scaffolding seems sound, the way previous generations have seemed sound, the way the work has always somehow held together.

It blesses the rope of twisted plant fiber so that the knots hold even when the wet has worked into them. Again, small. Again, indistinguishable from luck or good craft.

It places, in the dreams of the youngest mason child—a girl whose name in the chant-language means something like *sets-the-stone-true*—the faintest impression of how drainage might be cut into the bedrock floor without compromising the foundation. She will wake remembering nothing. But in twenty years, when she is the senior mason and the question of drainage becomes urgent, she will *know* the answer, and she will think she thought of it herself, and she will be right to think so, because the knowing will by then be entirely hers.

It does nothing about the charcoal diagram on the wall that the seep-water is erasing. The diagram is meant to be erased. The masons remember it in their hands. The hands are the real diagram. The wall was only a place to put a copy.

It listens to the boy's high thin note, the one he adds to the chord when the masons are sleeping, and it does not change the note, but it changes the way the note travels. The note has been rising through the shaft, looking for a way out. The power adjusts the acoustics of the tower's throat—nothing a mason would measure, nothing a frog would detect—so that the note, when it reaches the aperture, does not dissipate into the thin altitude air but instead carries. It carries far. It carries in the direction it was always trying to go, toward the other patient construction across the world, toward the place the boy came from, toward whoever placed him in the basket.

Whether anyone there is listening is not the power's concern. The power's concern is only that the note, having been sent, should be allowed to arrive.

Finally, the power does the thing that benevolent and wise powers do most often, and which is most often mistaken for doing nothing:

It witnesses.

It witnesses the masons setting stone in their work-chant, their bowed legs gripping the scaffolding, their splayed hands feeling the grain. It witnesses the frogs pulsing their subsonic harmonies. It witnesses the moss claiming its handspan per season. It witnesses the boy at the bottom of the shaft, writing in dissolving water, watching the sky-disk, waiting for what he cannot name. It witnesses the tower breathing. It witnesses the cold clean altitude air and the seep that gathers in the hollows and the kingfisher-shadows passing across the high aperture.

It witnesses, and in witnessing, it confers upon all of this the quality of *having mattered*. Not having mattered *for* something—not justified, not redeemed, not made instrumental to some later glory. Simply: having been real, having been seen, having been held in attention by something large enough to hold it without distorting it.

This is the gift, if it is a gift. The tower already had its meaning. The power only confirms that the meaning was not imagined.

Then the power turns its attention elsewhere, because there are many places, and many beings, and the attention must be distributed with care. But it leaves a small portion of itself behind in the throat of the new tower—not as a presence the inhabitants could detect, but as a kind of standing invitation, so that if any of them ever needs to be heard, the hearing is already arranged.

The boy, that night, sings his note again. It rises through the shaft and out the aperture and travels.

The masons dream of a tower learning to sing on its own.

The frogs pulse.

The moss grows.

The mountain, patient as mountains are, continues to ask.

And somewhere, perhaps, someone who placed a child in a basket long ago feels, without knowing why, a small warmth in the chest, and sets down whatever they were doing, and listens.
