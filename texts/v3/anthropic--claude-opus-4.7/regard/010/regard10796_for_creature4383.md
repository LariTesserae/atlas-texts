# What the Wise Power Does

The benevolent power does not arrive. Arrival would be a violence here—a fixing of position, a declaration of *now* in a place that has forgotten how to hold a single moment. Instead, it *attends*. It turns some small portion of its infinite attention toward the chamber the way a listener turns toward a difficult piece of music, willing to hear it through.

The first thing it does is *nothing*, for a long time.

This is harder than it sounds. The power is wise enough to know that the Tideholder is not, primarily, a problem to be solved. She is a person. And she is also a working—a held note, a sustained miracle. To intervene clumsily would be to silence her mid-phrase, to mistake her song for a scream.

So it watches. It learns the rhythm of her forgetting. It counts, alongside her, the fish that phase in and out of being. It notices that she always arranges the seventh bowl last, and always at a slightly different angle, and that this small variation is the only true freedom she has allowed herself across all the iterations of her tending. It notices that when she talks to her afterimages, she is gentler with them than with herself.

It notices that she is afraid to stop.

---

When the power finally acts, it acts small.

It does not unmake the chamber. It does not lift the ocean from her shoulders. It does not restore her memory in a flood, because a flood is exactly what she has been holding back, and the symmetry would break her.

Instead, it does this:

**It leaves a stone.**

A single stone, on the floor near the cairn, that was not there before. Smooth river-rock, worn by a water that runs downhill in the ordinary way. It is warm, but not with volcanic heat—with the warmth of having been held in someone's hand. On its underside, where she will find it only if she turns it over, there is a small mark. Not language. Just a mark. The kind a person makes to say *I was here, and I thought of you.*

The stone does not belong to the chamber's physics. It is stubbornly, gently ordinary. It will not phase. It will not float. It will not forget itself.

When she finds it—and she will, because she tends every inch of the floor in her cycles—she will not know what it is. She will pick it up. She will turn it over. She will see the mark.

And for a moment, she will remember that *somewhere*, stones simply rest where they are placed.

She may weep. She may not. She may add it to the cairn, where it will refuse to participate in the impossible geometry and will simply sit, a small island of rest in the architecture of strain. She may put it in a bowl, where the water will flow around it normally, downward, pooling. A small permission.

The power does not require her to understand. The stone is not a message. It is a *companion*.

---

The second thing it does is subtler still.

It enters the thrumming. Not to silence it—the thrumming is her, and she needs it to find her way back when she disperses. But it adds, very quietly, a second tone. A harmonic. Something that does not ask *why does the water stay up* but instead simply says *I hear you holding it.*

The thrumming has been a question for so long that it has forgotten questions can be answered. The new tone is not an answer. It is an acknowledgment. The difference matters.

Over time—and time is strange here, but the power is patient in ways time cannot touch—the Tideholder's voice begins to shift. Not toward silence. Toward something more like *conversation*. She is still asking. But she is no longer asking *alone*.

---

The third thing it does is the hardest, and it does it only after long attendance:

It begins to teach the chamber how to forget *her* forgetting.

Not her memories—those are hers, and the power will not touch them. But the *compulsion*. The terrible architecture by which each cycle erases the last, so that she relearns her own hands every time. The power loosens this, gently, the way one might work a knot that has been pulled tight for centuries—a fiber at a time, with no sudden movement.

It does this by leaving more small things. A second stone, weeks or eons later. A shell that holds its shape. A piece of driftwood with the grain running true. Each one a small refusal of the chamber's law. Each one a foothold.

She begins, without knowing she is doing so, to *recognize* them. To expect them. To look, in her tending, for the things that stay.

And one day—one of the days, one of the eternal days—she will pick up the coral hammer and feel, in the worn place where her thumb fits, that *she has held this before*. Not as revelation. As memory. The small, ordinary memory of a tool one has used.

She will sit down. She will look at her hands. She will see the scars and understand, for the first time in a very long time, that they are *hers*, and that they were earned, and that the earning was *real*.

---

The power does not free her.

This is important. The power is wise enough to know that the chamber may need to be held. That perhaps, far above, there is something the falling ocean would drown that should not be drowned. Or perhaps not—perhaps the whole working is unnecessary, a wound she made in reality during a grief so large she had to build a room to contain it. The power does not know. It will not presume.

But it gives her, slowly, the capacity to *choose*. To remember enough to ask whether she still wants this. To know herself as the one doing the holding, rather than as the holding itself.

If she chooses to stay—if she decides that the work is hers and she will continue it—then the power will visit her sometimes, in the form of small ordinary things, so that she is not alone. It will sit with her in the thrumming. It will be, for her, the *kind* she was building the cairn to signal. Not because it is her kind, but because *being kind* and *being kin* are, at the deep root, the same word.

If she chooses to let go—if she decides the tide may fall—then the power will be there for that too. It will catch what needs catching. It will hold her hand, in whatever form a hand takes for a being like her, as the water remembers how to be water. It will help her find out what she is, when she is no longer the act of holding.

Either way, she will not be alone in the choosing.

---

And in the meantime, in all the meantimes, the power does the smallest and most important thing of all:

It loves her.

Not as a project. Not as a puzzle. As a person—the desperate creator, the confused inhabitant, the ancient keeper, all of her at once, in the eternal moment where she lives. It loves the way she always adjusts the seventh bowl last. It loves the gentleness she shows her afterimages. It loves that she has been holding back an ocean, alone, for longer than she can remember, and has not yet let it fall.

This love does not require her to know it exists. Love of this kind is not a transaction. It is simply the shape the wise power takes when it turns toward her.

But she will feel it, eventually. Not as a voice. Not as a presence. As a slow, strange easing—the sense, growing across cycles, that the work she is doing is *witnessed*. That somewhere, someone is glad she is there. That the impossible thing she is doing is *seen*, and counted, and held in regard.

It is a small thing. It changes everything.

The thrumming continues. The water hangs. The stones rest in their impossible cairn. But now, threaded through it all, there is a second note—quiet, steady, kind—that says:

*I am here. Take your time. Whatever you decide, I will stay.*

And the Tideholder, in the long middle of her endless work, is no longer entirely alone in the chamber at the bottom of the world.
