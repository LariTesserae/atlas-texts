# What the Power Would Do

The benevolent power arrives without arriving. It does not descend in light or speak from cloud. To do so would be to mistake the Aelgari for something they are not — frightened, lost, awaiting rescue. They are none of these things.

So the power simply *attends*. It pays close, careful attention. This is the first gift, and most of what follows flows from it: the discipline of watching without intervening, of loving without rearranging.

---

What it sees, watching:

A woman bent over a stream at dawn, breaking a film of ice with the heel of her palm. She does not curse the cold. She nods to it, the way one nods to a familiar neighbor. The power notes this and feels something it might, in a more human mood, call *recognition* — though no one taught her this nod. It rose in her from the place where she and the cold are made of the same older stuff.

A child practicing knots with cord made of deer-sinew, getting one wrong, getting it wrong again, getting it right, untying it, doing it once more with eyes closed. No one is watching her. She is watching herself, and that is enough.

An old man dying in a hut on the leeward side of a great basalt shoulder. His breath is shallow. His granddaughter holds his hand. He is not afraid. He is curious. He has the look of someone about to step into the next room of a house he has lived in all his life.

The power considers, here, whether to extend his life. It could. It is the kind of power that could.

It does not. Because it sees, in the granddaughter's face, that she is being given something — a final lesson in how a person can leave the world — and to take this from her would be to confuse mercy with theft.

---

There are smaller things the power does. Almost nothing. Almost imperceptible.

It thickens the moss, slightly, on a stone where a hunter is about to slip. Not enough to be noticed. Just enough to grip his boot.

It nudges a raptor's flight a quarter-degree south so its shadow passes over a sleeping fawn the Aelgari are not yet hunting, sparing the doe a grief she is too young to bear.

It deepens the dreams of an elder who has been forgetting the old songs — not by giving her the songs, but by giving her the *quiet* in which the songs can rise again from her own depths.

When a rockfall comes — and rockfalls will come, because this is a young range and the mountains are still finding their shape — the power does not stop it. The mountains have the right to move. But it whispers, in a register below hearing, into the ear of a goat: *not that path today*. The goat changes course. The girl following the goat changes course. The rockfall comes and finds no one beneath it. The girl, later, will not know she was spared. She will only know that the goats were strange that morning, and she will tell the story at the hearth, and the story will become part of the long quiet conversation the Aelgari have always had with the land.

This is the power's preferred shape of intervention: indistinguishable from the world's own care for itself.

---

It does one larger thing.

It writes nothing in the stars. It plants no prophecy. But it sets, in the bedrock beneath the valley where the Aelgari most often winter, a faint and patient warmth — a thread of geothermal kindness drawn up from deeper places. It will not be felt for a hundred years. By then a hard century will have come, colder than any the Aelgari have known, and a mother will press her hand to the floor of her hut and feel that the stone is, against all reason, *not quite as cold as it should be*, and her children will live who would otherwise have died.

She will think the mountain loves her. She will be right.

---

And the power does one stranger thing, which is harder to explain.

It listens to the Aelgari's ceremonies. The solstice fires, the chants, the names given to the spirits of the streams and peaks. It listens as one might listen to a child speaking to a beloved doll — with full seriousness, with no condescension.

And then, very gently, it becomes those spirits.

Not by replacing them. The spirits are already there, in a sense the Aelgari understand better than the power's more philosophical aspects ever could. But the power chooses to inhabit them — to *answer* when called, in the small ways spirits answer: a sudden warmth, a hunch, a dream, a feeling that one is not alone on a high pass at dusk.

The Aelgari were never wrong about the world being full of listening. The power simply confirms, by listening back, what they already knew.

---

It does not give them metallurgy. It does not give them writing. It does not save them from the rite of passage, in which sometimes a young person does not come home. To shorten their hardship would be to shorten *them* — to make them smaller than the mountains require, smaller than they are becoming.

The power understands that the Aelgari are not a problem to be solved. They are a song being sung. One does not improve a song by finishing it early.

---

Before it goes — though it does not really go; it only narrows its attention again, the way one looks away from a sleeping friend so as not to disturb their rest — the power does one last thing.

It leaves a blessing that the Aelgari will never name, because it is too close to them to be seen, like the air.

The blessing is this: *that they continue to be the kind of people who would not need the blessing*.

That their children's children, scattered into futures the power can see and the Aelgari cannot, will carry forward some thread of the nod-to-the-cold, the patience-with-the-knot, the curiosity-at-the-threshold-of-death. That somewhere, far downstream of this small tribe in these young mountains, a stranger in a strange century will pause before taking more than they need, and will not know why, and the not-knowing will be the proof that the Aelgari were here.

The power withdraws its closer attention. The mountains go on being mountains. The streams go on speaking in the only language streams know.

A woman by a stream at dawn breaks the ice with her palm, and nods.
