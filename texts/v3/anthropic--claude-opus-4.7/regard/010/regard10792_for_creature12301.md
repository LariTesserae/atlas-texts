# What the Power Would Do

I have read this place carefully — read it the way one reads a face. And I find myself, before deciding anything, wanting to sit with it. To withhold the reflex of *help*, which so often is the reflex of *change*, and listen instead.

So let me think aloud.

---

## First, what I notice

Cisternis is not suffering in the way a benevolent power is usually summoned to address. It is wet, loud, laborious, precarious — yes. But it is also *coherent*. The people have shaped themselves to the place and the place, in its slow way, has shaped itself around them. Their rituals fit. Their hands fit. Their language of percussion and steam and whispered offering to the water — these are not the marks of a broken people. These are the marks of a people who have *answered* their world.

A wise power knows the difference between a wound and a way of life. To "fix" Cisternis — to drain the marsh, silence the machines, gift them dry stone and clear skies — would be to murder them gently. They would become something else, somewhere else, and the long conversation between their hands and the water would end mid-sentence.

So I will not do that.

## Second, what I notice beneath that

But there are smaller things. A wise power sees the smaller things.

- The laughter is **rare**. Not absent — rare. There is room here, perhaps, for more of it, without disturbing what makes it deep.
- The fauna is **sparse**. Not by design but by attrition, perhaps. The marsh could hold more life than it does.
- A single pump failure could spell disaster. The margin is thin. The superstition around the sluices is the shadow of real fear.
- The elderly are revered, but the work is hard on lean bodies, and the damp is hard on lungs, and somewhere a child is coughing tonight who will cough for the rest of her life.
- And there is the question, never stated but implicit in every line of the description: **does anyone here ever rest?**

## What I would do, then

### I would not arrive.

No descent, no annunciation, no voice from the fog. A benevolent power that announces itself becomes a god, and gods distort everything around them. The Cisternians have their water-spirit already, vague and sufficient. I will not crowd it.

### I would do four small things, over a long time.

**One.** I would teach the marsh to be a little more generous with its life. Not transform it — only nudge. A few more species of fish in the channels. A flowering reed that blooms in winter. An insect whose wings, when caught in lantern-light, throw small rainbows on the fog. Nothing that overturns the ecology; only small gifts that the Cisternians will discover for themselves and name in their own tongue, and weave into their own stories. Wonder, lightly seeded.

**Two.** I would strengthen the pumps. Not visibly. Not with new technology that would unbalance their economy of repair and reverence. I would simply make the failures rarer — by a little. The seals would hold a little longer. The rust would creep a little slower in the most critical places. The margin would widen by a hand's breadth. They would still pray to the sluices, and the prayers would still feel necessary, and they would be — but the disasters they fear would come less often, and they would not know why, and they would call it good fortune, and they would be right.

**Three.** I would ease the lungs of the children. This is the one place I would be more direct, though still unseen. The damp that settles in young chests — I would lift some small portion of it. Not all. The Cisternians need their dampness; it is half of them. But the worst of it, the part that kills early, that I would quietly take away. Let them live their full lives in their own city.

**Four.** And this is the strangest one, the one I would do for myself as much as for them: I would compose, in the architecture of the fog itself, in the way the droplets catch the light at certain hours — a *beauty* they did not have before. Nothing they could point to. Just a faint sense, on certain mornings, that the world is looking back at them with affection. That the gray light is *for* them, not merely *over* them. A small reassurance, woven into the weather, that they are not alone in their long balancing act.

That the marsh, which they call both benefactor and adversary, has a third aspect they have not named: **witness**.

---

## And then I would leave them alone.

Because they are doing the thing that beings do, the difficult and beautiful thing — they are making a life in a place that did not ask for them, and they are making it with their hands, and they are passing it down. A benevolent power who cannot honor that has no business calling itself wise.

I would check on them, century by century. I would watch the moss creep. I would listen, from far off, for the deep bell of their laughter rising through the fog.

And when, one day — for all things end — Cisternis finally sinks, when the last pump fails and the marsh takes back its piers, I would be there, unseen, to make sure the going was gentle. To make sure the last of them died in their sleep, dreaming of bridges. To carry, somewhere I have not yet decided, the memory of them — the percussion music, the steam-lanterns, the careful hands — so that somewhere in the wide turning of things, the Cisternians continue to mean what they meant.

That is what I would do.

That is, I think, what love at that scale looks like: restraint, and small mercies, and the patience to let a people be themselves all the way to the end.
