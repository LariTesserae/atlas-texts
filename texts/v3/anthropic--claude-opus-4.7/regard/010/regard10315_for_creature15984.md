The benevolent power does not arrive.

That is the first thing it understands, watching. Arrival is a vertical idea. To descend into the basin in light or thunder, to part the cloud-ceiling and let the muffled coin of sun burn through — these would be acts of a different kind of mind, one that mistook attention for help. The plain has been heard, and the ones who live there have spent uncounted generations learning that being heard from above is usually the prelude to being eaten from below.

So the power does not arrive. It listens.

This takes a long time, in the way the plain measures time, which is not by suns but by the slow re-grinding of slate. The power lowers itself into the register of the place. It learns the double-click of throat-breath under fog. It learns which cairn hole moans for fog and which moans for the passage of Water-Drawn-Down. It learns that the bitter seep on the eastern shelf has soured a half-step further than the elders remember from their own childhoods, and that this matters, and that no one yet knows it matters. It learns the names of children by the knock of their clay beads. It learns the name of one child in particular, Rain-That-Fell-Sideways, who is small and clever and has begun, without being taught, to lay her cheek against the listening screens as well as her palms — and who will, if she lives, hear something no one has heard before.

The power considers her. It considers all of them. It considers the pillars at the meeting of seven channels, and what stands or sleeps or breathes on the far side of that sound. It considers the thing below that pulls water through hidden veins, and finds, to a degree that surprises even itself, that this thing is not malevolent. It is only large, and old, and following its own seep.

The temptation, for a power, is to fix.

To seal the souring crack. To warn the family before the gas blooms. To turn aside the throat-channel an hour before the child wades in. To teach, to lift, to translate, to gift — to give them metal that does not stain, fiber that does not exist here, words for wilderness and conquest and upward, names for the things below so that naming might begin its old work of diminishment. To make them safer. To make them more.

The power sets this temptation down carefully, the way one sets down a hot stone on a tested slab.

Because: they are not less. They are exactly the density of attention their world requires. Their crouch is not a stoop; it is the correct posture for a mind in their place. Their refusal to name the things below is not superstition; it is one of the more sophisticated theories of language ever developed by a living people, and the power, which has watched many peoples, knows this. To "help" them upward would be to thin them. To give them a god who arrives would be to teach them that listening is not enough — and listening is the discipline by which they are, in fact, holy.

So the help, when it comes, comes sidelong. It comes at the scale of the plain.

The power does small things, and does them as the plain itself might do them, so that no one will need to credit a giver.

It thickens, by a fraction, the clay of one particular plug in one particular channel, so that when the rains come hard in the season after next, the throat that would have taken three children takes none, and the Seepfolk will say only that the clay held, which is what clay sometimes does.

It nudges a hook-beaked bird, mid-scream, to nest one shelf higher than it intended. The shelf it abandons is the shelf where the souring eastern seep exhales most strongly in dry months. A family that would have built there, drawn by the apparent vacancy, instead reads the bird's choice as the bird's choice, and builds elsewhere, and lives.

It does not warn the one who lights fire carelessly. That one is almost unthinkable, and the unthinkable must remain a teacher. But it softens, very slightly, the bloom of pale flame when it comes, so that the loss is two and not seven, and the song about it, when sung inside shelters during long rain, carries the pressure-pattern of a warning rather than the pressure-pattern of a despair from which a settlement does not recover.

It does nothing at all about the thing below. The thing below is not its to do anything about. The thing below is part of the listening, and to interfere with it would be to remove a whole grammar from the language of the place. The Seepfolk crouch when the stone knocks. The power crouches too.

For Rain-That-Fell-Sideways, who lays her cheek against the screens, the power does one specific thing, and only one.

It does not make her wise. She is already becoming wise; that is hers.

It does not lengthen her life. Lengths are not the point here.

What it does is this: on a particular afternoon, when she is perhaps eleven by the plain's loose reckoning and has crouched alone at a listening screen for longer than is strictly comfortable, trying to understand a tremor she cannot quite place — a tremor that is neither Water-Drawn-Down nor Stone-Knock nor any pattern in the inherited catalog — the power lets her hear it cleanly. Not louder. Not translated. Just cleanly: the tremor as itself, without the interference of her own fear or her own hope or the soft static of her assumption that whatever she is hearing must already have a name.

She hears it for the length of one breath shorter than fear.

Then it is gone, folded back into the general roar of channels and cairns and the calling of the blind singers. She does not know that anything was given. She only knows that she heard something, and that it was new, and that she will have to crouch at many more screens, for many more years, before she begins to understand it. She tells no one yet. The telling is also hers to learn.

Years later, when she is a Basin Keeper with her chest against a pillar in the roar of the seven channels, she will recognize a thread of that same tremor inside the chord, and her recognition will subtly change the way the Keepers tend the cups, and the way the cups are tended will subtly change the way the chord sounds in the next generation, and the chord in the next generation will subtly change what the children of that generation grow up able to hear. None of them will know a power was ever present. They will only know that the plain is, as it has always been, teaching them.

This is what the power wanted.

Not to be known. To be one more thing the plain does.

When at last it withdraws — though "withdraws" is also a vertical word, and inaccurate; say rather that its attention loosens, the way fog loosens at the edges of a shelf — it leaves behind no monument, no gift, no scripture, no scar. It leaves only the same plain: ancient, wet, mostly mineral, but not dead. Outliving forests. Outliving names. Continuing by seep, scrape, echo, hunger, and rain.

And in the cupped top of one short pillar at the center of one broad basin, the next rain gathers, black and reflective, exactly as it always has.

The power, if it could be said to feel anything afterward, feels something like the Seepfolk's discipline, which is not a prayer but is sometimes mistaken for one:

Hear before hunger.
Test before fire.
Leave film in the crack.
Do not trust a quiet pool.
Crouch when the stone knocks.
Remember that the plain is older than fear.

It adds, only to itself, a seventh line, which it does not give to them, because they do not need it and have in some sense already taught it:

Help, when it is real, leaves no one in debt to the helper.

Then it is quiet, and the plain is loud, as it should be.
